> Flashcards covering the **application** (banking, credit risk, risk management) and **regulation** (BIS/Basel, IFRS 9, FSB, South Africa) folders.
> Organised into three difficulty tiers: **Easy → Medium → Hard**.

## ⬜ EASY — Simple Facts & Definitions

What does **BIS** stand for?
?
Bank for International Settlements.

---

In what year was the BIS established, and what was its original purpose?
?
Established in **1930** to manage German reparations payments following the First World War; it later evolved into a cooperative forum for central banks.

---

What does **BCBS** stand for?
?
Basel Committee on Banking Supervision.

---

How many member institutions does the BCBS have, and across how many jurisdictions?
?
**45 members** from **28 jurisdictions**.
<!--SR:!2026-03-30,1,230-->

---

What is the oversight body of the BCBS?
?
The **Group of Governors and Heads of Supervision (GHOS)**, which comprises central bank governors and non-central bank heads of supervision from BCBS member jurisdictions.

---

Does the BCBS have supranational legal authority?
?
**No.** BCBS decisions do not have legal force. Standards must be incorporated into each jurisdiction's national legal framework to become binding.

---

What does **CET1** stand for, and is it going-concern or gone-concern capital?
?
**Common Equity Tier 1** — it is **going-concern** capital (loss-absorbing while the bank is still operating).

---

What does **AT1** stand for, and what is one of its defining features?
?
**Additional Tier 1** — also going-concern capital. A key feature is that it is **perpetual** (no maturity date) and coupons are **discretionary and non-cumulative**.

---

What is **Tier 2 (T2)** capital also known as?
?
**Gone-concern capital** — it absorbs losses only upon failure/resolution of the bank, not during normal operations.

---

What are the minimum capital ratios under **Basel III** (RBC20)?
?
- **CET1**: ≥ 4.5% of RWA
- **Tier 1**: ≥ 6% of RWA
- **Total Capital**: ≥ 8% of RWA

---

What does **RWA** stand for?
?
**Risk-Weighted Assets** — the denominator used in Basel capital ratio calculations, reflecting the risk profile of a bank's exposures.

---

What does **PD** stand for in credit risk?
?
**Probability of Default** — the likelihood that a borrower will fail to meet its credit obligations within a given time horizon.

---

What does **LGD** stand for?
?
**Loss Given Default** — the proportion of an exposure that a bank expects to lose if a borrower defaults (after recovery of collateral etc.).

---

What does **EAD** stand for?
?
**Exposure at Default** — the total outstanding exposure (including drawn and undrawn amounts) at the time of default.

---

What does **ECL** stand for?
?
**Expected Credit Loss** — the probability-weighted estimate of credit losses, central to IFRS 9 impairment calculations.

---

What standard did **IFRS 9** replace, and when did it become effective?
?
IFRS 9 replaced **IAS 39**. It became effective for annual periods beginning on or after **1 January 2018**.

---

Name the **three stages** of IFRS 9 impairment.
?
- **Stage 1**: Performing — recognise **12-month ECL**
- **Stage 2**: Significant increase in credit risk (SICR) — recognise **lifetime ECL**
- **Stage 3**: Credit-impaired / defaulted — recognise **lifetime ECL**; interest calculated on **amortised cost**

---

What does **DPD** stand for, and at what threshold is an account classified as in default?
?
**Days Past Due**. An account is classified as in default when **DPD ≥ 90** (i.e. 90 days past due on any material credit obligation).

---

What does **TLAC** stand for?
?
**Total Loss-Absorbing Capacity** — a minimum requirement for G-SIBs to hold sufficient loss-absorbing and recapitalisation instruments to support orderly resolution.

---

What does **G-SIB** stand for?
?
**Global Systemically Important Bank** — banks whose failure could pose systemic risk to the global financial system.

---

What does **FSB** stand for?
?
**Financial Stability Board** — coordinates national authorities and international standard-setters to promote global financial stability.

---

What does **maturity transformation** mean in banking?
?
The simultaneous act of **lending long** (e.g. mortgages) while **borrowing short** (e.g. instant-access deposits). It creates a structural mismatch that drives the need for both capital and liquidity management.

---

What does **BCBS 239** address?
?
**Principles for Effective Risk Data Aggregation and Risk Reporting** — it sets out 14 principles to ensure banks can accurately, completely, and quickly aggregate risk data across the firm.

---

What are the **three categories** used to classify capital instruments under Basel?
?
1. **Permanence** (stability/long-term nature)
2. **Freedom** (ability to absorb losses on an ongoing basis)
3. **Subordination** (ranking relative to depositors and other creditors)

---

What minimum external TLAC requirement (as % of RWA) applies to G-SIBs from January 2022?
?
**≥ 18% of RWA** (plus applicable Basel III capital buffers, which are additional).

---

How many BIS committees are hosted by the BIS under the Basel Process?
?
**Six committees**, overseen by three senior groupings: the Global Economy Meeting (GEM), the All Governors' Meeting, and the Group of Governors and Heads of Supervision (GHOS).

---

What was Basel I's minimum capital ratio, and in what year was it implemented?
?
**8% of risk-weighted assets**, to be implemented by end of **1992**.

---

What is a **transactor** exposure under CRR/IRB definitions?
?
A revolving facility where the obligor has **repaid the balance in full** at each scheduled repayment date over the previous 12 months.

---

What does **LTV** stand for in collateral context?
?
**Loan-to-Value** — the ratio of the loan amount to the value of the collateral pledged. Banks take a "haircut" by lending up to a percentage of collateral value to protect against value declines.

## 🟡 MEDIUM — Nuanced Understanding

What are the **three IFRS 9 classification categories** for financial assets, and the key conditions for each?
?
1. **Amortised Cost**: held to collect contractual cash flows (SPPI test — solely payments of principal and interest) with no intent to sell.
2. **Fair Value through Other Comprehensive Income (FVOCI)**: held to both collect cash flows and sell; SPPI test met.
3. **Fair Value through Profit or Loss (FVPL)**: all other assets (trading, derivatives, non-SPPI); changes in fair value go directly to the income statement.

---

What are the **three Basel pillars** and what does each cover?
?
- **Pillar 1 — Minimum Capital Requirements (MCR)**: quantitative minimum capital for credit, market, and operational risk.
- **Pillar 2 — Supervisory Review Process (SRP)**: supervisors assess whether a bank's capital is adequate for its full risk profile (ICAAP/SREP). Includes economic capital and stress testing.
- **Pillar 3 — Market Discipline (Disclosure)**: mandatory public disclosure to allow market participants to assess a bank's risk profile and capital adequacy.

---

What were the key **shortcomings of Basel I** that motivated the development of Basel II?
?
1. **Lack of risk differentiation**: all corporate loans carried a blanket 100% risk weight, regardless of borrower credit quality.
2. **Limited risk scope**: focused almost entirely on credit risk; market risk was added later (1996 amendment), and operational risk was not addressed.
3. **Regulatory arbitrage**: banks could structure transactions to minimise capital requirements without reducing actual risk.

---

What are the qualifying criteria for **Additional Tier 1 (AT1)** capital?
?
- Subordinated to depositors, general creditors, and subordinated debt
- **Perpetual** (no maturity date, no step-ups or redemption incentives)
- Coupons are **discretionary and non-cumulative**
- Subject to **write-off or conversion to equity** at the Point of Non-Viability (PONV)
- Neither secured nor covered by a guarantee enhancing seniority

---

What are the qualifying criteria for **Tier 2** capital?
?
- Subordinated to depositors and general creditors
- Minimum original maturity of **at least 5 years**
- Recognition in regulatory capital in the final 5 years **amortised on a straight-line basis**
- Subject to write-off or conversion at PONV
- Coupons are **compulsory** (unlike AT1)

---

Describe the **three Lines of Defence** in bank risk management.
?
- **1st Line (Risk Ownership)**: revenue-generating business units — they own and manage risk at the transaction level.
- **2nd Line (Risk Control)**: independent risk management and compliance functions (e.g. Credit Risk Committee, ALCO, Model Risk Committee) — they monitor, challenge, and report.
- **3rd Line (Independent Assurance)**: internal audit and the board — they provide independent assurance on the adequacy of controls.
*(A 4th line — external auditors and regulators — also exists.)*

---

What is a **Risk Appetite Statement (RAS)** and what does it typically contain?
?
A formal document approved by the board that defines the level and types of risk the bank is willing to accept in pursuit of its strategy. It includes:
- **Quantitative metrics** (e.g. minimum CAR, maximum earnings volatility, concentration limits, RAROC hurdle rates, LCR/NSFR thresholds)
- **Qualitative metrics** (e.g. zero tolerance for fraud, customer conduct standards, climate commitments)
- Breach triggers and escalation procedures

---

What is the **definition of default (DoD)** under Basel/CRR, and what are the two main triggers?
?
An obligor is in default if **either**:
1. **Days Past Due ≥ 90** on any material credit obligation (absolute threshold: £0/€0 materiality for retail under CRR)
2. **Unlikeliness to Pay (UTP)** — the bank considers the obligor unlikely to meet obligations without recourse to collateral, e.g. due to bankruptcy, forbearance, charge-off, fraud, or death.
The default returns after a **12-month probation period** (cure period).

---

What is the difference between **regulatory capital** and **economic capital**?
?
- **Regulatory capital**: prescribed by regulators (Basel/CRR) — a standardised, conservative measure of the capital a bank must hold.
- **Economic capital**: the bank's own internal estimate of capital needed to remain solvent at a chosen confidence level. Usually **lower** than regulatory capital as it incorporates portfolio diversification effects, but it may be higher if the bank's internal risk view is more conservative in certain areas.

---

What are the **five risk data aggregation principles** of BCBS 239?
?
1. **Governance** — strong board/senior management oversight of data quality
2. **Data Architecture & IT Infrastructure** — robust systems supporting aggregation in normal and stressed conditions
3. **Accuracy & Integrity** — reliable, reconciled data with a single authoritative source
4. **Completeness** — capture all material risks across all business lines, legal entities, and geographies
5. **Timeliness** — produce aggregate risk data within required timeframes
*(Plus Principle 6: Adaptability — flexible enough to meet ad hoc and stress scenario requests.)*

---

What additional requirements do **G-SIBs** face beyond standard Basel III capital minimums?
?
1. **Higher CET1 capital buffer** (ranging from 1.0% to 3.5% depending on bucket, applied on top of the capital conservation buffer)
2. **TLAC requirement** (minimum 18% of RWA from 2022)
3. **Enhanced resolvability**: group-wide resolution plans and resolvability assessments
4. **Higher supervisory expectations**: on risk management functions, data aggregation (BCBS 239), risk governance, and internal controls

---

What is the **ICAAP** and how does it relate to Basel Pillar 2?
?
The **Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP)** is a bank's own assessment of its capital needs under Pillar 2. It goes beyond Pillar 1 by considering risks not fully captured (e.g. interest rate risk in the banking book, concentration risk, strategic risk). The regulator reviews the ICAAP through the **Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process (SREP)** and may impose additional capital requirements (Pillar 2A/2B add-ons).

---

What are the **three Basel capital buffer types** under Basel III (beyond the minimum)?
?
1. **Capital Conservation Buffer (CCB)**: 2.5% of CET1 — restricts distributions if breached
2. **Countercyclical Capital Buffer (CCyB)**: 0–2.5% of CET1 — set by national authorities during credit booms
3. **G-SIB Surcharge**: 1.0–3.5% of CET1 — applied to global systemically important banks based on their bucket

---

What is the role of the **ALCO (Asset-Liability Committee)** in a bank?
?
The ALCO (or Treasury Committee) sits at executive level and oversees **all aspects of balance sheet structure**, including:
- Capital and asset/liability management
- Raising of funds (money market, capital, long-term debt)
- Monitoring structural liquidity, interest rate risk, and funding mismatches
It reports to a board-level committee (e.g. Group Risk and Capital Management Committee).

---

How does **IFRS 9** handle **interest revenue** differently across its three stages?
?
- **Stage 1 & 2**: Interest is calculated using the **effective interest rate (EIR) on the gross carrying amount** (i.e. before deducting the ECL allowance).
- **Stage 3**: Interest is calculated using the **EIR on the amortised cost** (i.e. gross amount minus the loss allowance).
- If an asset is **purchased or originated as credit-impaired**, a **credit-adjusted EIR** is used on the amortised cost from inception.

## 🔴 HARD — Connecting Ideas Across Topics

How does the **IFRS 9 ECL framework** relate to the **IRB capital models** under Basel, and where do they diverge?
?
Both use the same fundamental credit risk components — **PD, LGD, EAD** — and share the concept of expected loss. However, they diverge in important ways:
- **Time horizon**: Basel IRB uses a **1-year** PD (through-the-cycle for capital), while IFRS 9 ECL uses **point-in-time** PDs over **12 months (Stage 1)** or a **lifetime horizon (Stage 2/3)**.
- **Conservatism**: Basel IRB models include regulatory floors and margins of conservatism (e.g. downturn LGD, long-run average PD); IFRS 9 requires a **probability-weighted, unbiased** estimate incorporating forward-looking information.
- **Purpose**: IRB drives **minimum regulatory capital** (Pillar 1); ECL drives **accounting provisions** (balance sheet).
- A bank may have different model calibrations for each purpose even if the same data infrastructure is shared (see IFRS 9 vs IRB note).

---

How does the evolution from **Basel I → Basel II → Basel III** reflect progressively more sophisticated risk measurement?
?
- **Basel I (1988)**: crude risk weights (0%, 20%, 50%, 100%); all corporate loans treated equally; only credit risk addressed. Problem: no differentiation; regulatory arbitrage.
- **Basel II (2004)**: introduced **three pillars** (MCR, SREP, Disclosure) and **three approaches** for credit risk (Standardised, Foundation IRB, Advanced IRB) allowing internal models for PD/LGD/EAD; added operational risk capital.
- **Basel III (2010–2022)**: responded to the 2007–08 GFC by: (a) increasing quantity/quality of capital (higher CET1 minimum, CET1-only buffers), (b) introducing **leverage ratio** (non-risk-based backstop), (c) introducing **LCR and NSFR** (liquidity requirements), (d) adding G-SIB surcharges and TLAC.
- **Basel 3.1 / CRR3**: constrained model outputs with an **output floor** (72.5% of SA RWA) to reduce variation across banks.

---

How does **maturity transformation** simultaneously create the need for both **capital regulation** and **liquidity regulation**?
?
Maturity transformation creates two distinct vulnerabilities:
1. **Solvency risk** (→ capital): If long-dated assets (e.g. loans) lose value through defaults, the bank's equity can be wiped out. Capital (CET1, AT1, T2) is required to absorb these losses before depositors are harmed. Basel's Pillar 1 MCR and Pillar 2 ICAAP address this.
2. **Liquidity risk** (→ liquidity regulation): If short-term depositors withdraw funds faster than long-dated assets mature (a "bank run"), the bank becomes illiquid even if solvent. The **LCR** (30-day stress survival) and **NSFR** (stable funding over 1 year) address this.
These are distinct but interconnected: a capital loss can trigger a liquidity crisis (confidence), and a liquidity crisis can trigger insolvency.

---

How do **TLAC, MREL**, and **Basel III capital buffers** interact in a bank's loss-absorption hierarchy?
?
They form a layered "waterfall" of loss absorption:
1. **Basel III minimum capital** (4.5% CET1 + 6% Tier 1 + 8% Total) — first line of defence in going concern
2. **Capital buffers** (CCB 2.5%, CCyB 0–2.5%, G-SIB surcharge 1–3.5%) — restrict dividends/AT1 coupons if breached, acting as a pre-resolution buffer
3. **TLAC/MREL** — these sit on top of minimum regulatory capital and consist of instruments that can be written down or converted in **resolution** (gone-concern). TLAC is the FSB's international standard for G-SIBs (≥18% RWA); **MREL** is the EU/UK equivalent applied more broadly by resolution authorities.
Key point: CET1 used to meet minimum capital requirements **cannot simultaneously** count toward capital buffers under the TLAC framework.

---

How do the **BCBS 239 risk data aggregation principles** connect to G-SIB governance requirements, ICAAP, and Pillar 2?
?
BCBS 239 was directly motivated by the 2007–08 GFC, where G-SIBs could not aggregate risk exposures quickly enough to manage the crisis. The connection runs as follows:
- **G-SIB requirements**: G-SIBs face heightened supervisory expectations specifically referencing BCBS 239 data capabilities as part of the G-SIB framework.
- **ICAAP (Pillar 2)**: Accurate risk data aggregation is a **prerequisite** for a credible ICAAP — if a bank cannot reliably aggregate exposures, its internal capital assessment is unreliable.
- **Resolution**: BCBS 239 improves **resolvability** — resolution authorities need accurate, timely firm-wide data to execute resolution plans (aligned to FSB Key Attributes).
- **Risk Appetite monitoring**: KRIs in the Risk Appetite Statement can only be monitored if data is accurate, complete, and timely — directly linking BCBS 239 to the risk management framework.

---

How does the **IFRS 9 staging framework** create different financial reporting outcomes, and what are the **model risk** implications of the SICR (Stage 1 → Stage 2) boundary?
?
**Financial reporting impact**:
- Moving from Stage 1 to Stage 2 dramatically **increases provisions** (from 12-month ECL to lifetime ECL) — this is often called the "cliff edge" effect. For long-dated products (mortgages), lifetime ECL can be many multiples of 12-month ECL.
- Stage 3 also changes interest income recognition (on amortised cost, not gross) — reducing interest income on non-performing assets.

**Model risk at the SICR boundary**:
- The SICR determination is inherently judgemental and model-dependent. Small changes in the SICR threshold (e.g. PD doubling, or 30 DPD backstop) can move large portfolios between stages.
- This creates **pro-cyclicality** risk: in a downturn, mass migrations to Stage 2 can amplify reported losses and constrain lending precisely when credit is needed.
- Regulators (PRA, ECB) scrutinise SICR models carefully (SS3/24, EBA Guidelines) to ensure they are not too delayed in recognising deterioration.

---

How does the **three lines of defence model** map onto Basel's **Pillar 2 SREP** framework, and what happens if the model breaks down?
?
**Mapping**:
- **1st Line** provides the raw risk data and manages risk day-to-day → feeds into ICAAP metrics and Pillar 2 quantification
- **2nd Line** (Risk Control, Model Risk Committee, ALCO) independently assesses and challenges the adequacy of capital and risk measurement → produces the ICAAP
- **3rd Line** (Internal Audit, Board) provides independent assurance on the ICAAP process
- **Regulator** (PRA/ECB via SREP) acts as an **external 4th line**, reviewing the ICAAP and potentially imposing Pillar 2A or 2B capital add-ons

**If the model breaks down**:
- Weak 1st-line risk culture (moral hazard) may generate mis-stated exposures
- An insufficiently independent 2nd line fails to catch errors → ICAAP is unreliable
- Regulators identify this through SREP and can: require higher capital, restrict dividends, or initiate supervisory action
- BCBS 239 failures compound this: poor data aggregation means the 2nd line is monitoring inaccurate figures

---

How does **collateral** (LTV, type, and volatility) affect **LGD** and flow through to both **IRB capital requirements** and **IFRS 9 provisions**?
?
**Mechanism**: LGD = 1 − Recovery Rate. Recovery depends on the value and liquidity of collateral post-default.
- **High-quality collateral** (e.g. cash, first-charge residential property in a low-LTV mortgage): high recovery → low LGD → lower EL (= PD × LGD × EAD) and lower unexpected loss → lower Pillar 1 RWA and lower IFRS 9 ECL provisions.
- **Downturn LGD** (Basel requirement): IRB models must use LGD estimates reflecting stressed collateral values (e.g. property price falls), introducing conservatism into capital vs. IFRS 9's point-in-time LGD with forward-looking macro scenarios.
- **LTV monitoring**: lenders monitoring LTV triggers can require additional collateral ("margin calls") or classify accounts differently (Stage 2 SICR trigger), connecting collateral management to both Pillar 1 model inputs and Pillar 2 stress testing.
- **IFRS 9 vs IRB divergence**: IFRS 9 LGD models incorporate forward-looking economic scenarios (FLI adjustments), while Basel LGD must use long-run downturn estimates — the same collateral data feeds both but with different adjustments.
