Banking regulation

A thirty-lecture sweep through the institutions and standards that govern banks worldwide. Two parallel narratives: at the top, the supranational standard-setters (Basel Committee, FSB, IFRS Foundation) writing the rules; below them, the jurisdictional regulators (EU, UK, South Africa, USA) adopting, adapting, and enforcing those rules. Every lecture treats the regulator as a living institution, with a history, a mandate, a publication style, and a way of engaging with the banks it watches.

How to use this

Lectures are sequenced top-down: start with the global standard-setters, then move through the jurisdictional implementations. Regulator lectures introduce an institution; standard lectures unpack a specific rulebook. The two interleave, since most standards make little sense without knowing who wrote them and why. Status badges show what is built (built) or planned.

How global standards become local rules

The Basel Committee, the FSB, and the IFRS Foundation produce standards: agreed-upon minimum requirements that members commit to implement. Standards are not law. They become law when each jurisdiction's parliament passes them into statute and each jurisdiction's regulator writes detailed rules. The EU does this through the CRR and CRD; the UK through the PRA rulebook; South Africa through the Banks Act and SARB directives; the US through Federal Reserve regulations, OCC rules, and FDIC supervision. The same Basel III capital ratio becomes a CRR article in Brussels, a PRA rule in London, a directive note in Pretoria, and a Final Rule in Washington. The arithmetic is identical; the texture of each adaptation is local.
