Federal Reserve System: monetary policy and bank supervision

The Federal Reserve is the central bank of the United States and the world's most consequential monetary authority. It is also the consolidated supervisor of every US bank holding company, every state-chartered bank that has chosen to join the system, every foreign banking organisation operating on US soil, and every systemically important non-bank financial institution that has been brought into its perimeter. No other major central bank combines these two roles to the same degree, and no other supervisor has shaped global stress testing, capital tailoring, or the post-crisis prudential architecture as decisively. This lecture unpacks both halves of the institution and the relationship between them.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The Federal Reserve System is the central bank of the United States, established by the Federal Reserve Act of 1913 after the Panic of 1907 exposed the fragility of a country without a central monetary authority. It has a two-level structure: the Board of Governors in Washington DC (seven members appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate, each for a 14-year staggered term) and twelve regional Federal Reserve Banks (Boston, New York, Philadelphia, Cleveland, Richmond, Atlanta, Chicago, St Louis, Minneapolis, Kansas City, Dallas, San Francisco) each with its own President, Board of Directors, and supervisory staff. Monetary policy is set by the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC), a body of twelve voting members combining the seven Governors with the New York Fed President (permanent voter) and four of the remaining eleven Reserve Bank Presidents rotating annually. The FOMC sets the target range for the federal funds rate, manages the Fed's balance sheet, and guides the broader monetary stance.

On the supervisory side, the Fed is the consolidated supervisor of bank holding companies (BHCs), financial holding companies (FHCs), state member banks, foreign banking organisations (FBOs), and (since the Dodd-Frank Act 2010) savings and loan holding companies and designated non-bank systemically important financial institutions. Supervision is tailored by firm size and complexity into four categories under the post-EGRRCPA 2018 framework, with the largest US firms (Category I G-SIBs) subject to the full Comprehensive Capital Analysis and Review (CCAR) and Dodd-Frank Act Stress Test (DFAST), and smaller community banks subject to a much lighter regime. The Fed's dual mandate, formalised by the Humphrey-Hawkins Full Employment and Balanced Growth Act of 1978, is maximum employment and stable prices. By assets supervised it is the largest single bank supervisor in the world.

Feynman test Imagine a country where every bank used to be on its own, and once in a while one would run out of cash and topple, and a few others would topple after it. The country eventually decides to build a single back-stop. It puts a small board in the capital city to write the rules, and twelve regional banks scattered around the country to do the day-to-day work of holding everyone's cash, lending against good collateral when needed, and watching the local banks. The board and the regional banks share a name and an institutional identity but they argue with each other about the right interest rate, and that argument, eight times a year, around a big oval table, is how the country sets the price of money. The same institution that argues about interest rates also reads the audit files of the biggest banks and tells them how much capital they need to survive a recession. That is the Federal Reserve.

Step 2

Why this matters

The Federal Reserve is the issuer of the world's reserve currency. Its policy rate is the global risk-free benchmark against which almost every other asset price is calibrated, and its balance sheet is the largest single counterparty in international financial markets. When the FOMC moves, every emerging-market central bank, every European pension fund, and every commodities desk recalibrates. For an actuary working anywhere outside the United States, the Fed's monetary decisions still flow through into the discount curves used for liability valuation, the funding cost of overseas branches, and the dollar leg of any cross-currency hedge.

On the supervisory side, the Fed is the consolidated supervisor for roughly half of US banking-sector assets, with the remainder split between the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC), the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC), and the state banking departments. This split exists because the United States has a dual banking system: a bank can choose a federal (national) charter regulated by the OCC, or a state charter regulated by the relevant state plus, if it joins the Federal Reserve System, by the Fed. The choice of charter is consequential and is one of the few places in modern bank regulation where the firm picks its primary supervisor.

The Fed's stress-testing programme (CCAR and DFAST) has become the global template. The European Central Bank's SREP stress tests, the Bank of England's annual cyclical scenario, and the supervisory stress tests in Canada, Australia, and Japan all borrow from the CCAR design. The Fed pioneered the practice of publicly disclosing supervisory loss projections under a common scenario, and the Stress Capital Buffer mechanism (introduced 2020) is the most ambitious attempt to integrate stress losses directly into binding capital requirements. If you understand CCAR you can read every other major supervisory stress test on the planet.

Finally, the Fed's tailoring framework matters because it is the live laboratory for the debate about how much regulation systemically important banks actually need. The Economic Growth, Regulatory Relief and Consumer Protection Act of 2018 (EGRRCPA, often called S.2155) raised the threshold for "systemic" treatment from $50 billion to $250 billion in consolidated assets and instructed the Fed to tailor enhanced prudential standards by firm category. The March 2023 collapses of Silicon Valley Bank and Signature Bank, both Category IV firms under the tailoring framework, reopened that debate and continue to shape the design of the Basel III endgame rule and its post-2024 revisions.

Step 3

The mental model

The Fed has two operational structures that share a single institutional identity. The monetary policy structure runs from the Board of Governors and the FOMC down to the New York Fed's open market trading desk, which executes the FOMC's decisions in the market. The supervisory structure runs from the Board of Governors and the Division of Supervision and Regulation down to the twelve regional Reserve Banks, each of which fields its own teams of examiners assigned to the BHCs and state member banks in its district. Both structures meet in the Board, which is the only point at which the Fed acts as a single legal and political entity.

Three flows of authority connect the two structures. Monetary decisions flow down from the FOMC to the New York Fed's trading desk and then into wholesale funding markets, where they alter the cost of funds for every supervised firm. Supervisory findings flow up from the Reserve Bank examination teams to the Board, where they inform policy on capital, liquidity, and resolution. And the Vice-Chair for Supervision, a position created by the Dodd-Frank Act 2010 and first filled in 2017, gives the Board a designated chief supervisor with statutory authority to direct the supervisory programme. The Vice-Chair for Supervision is, by reach, the most powerful single bank supervisor in the world.

Step 4

The detail

History: from the Panic of 1907 to the Vice-Chair for Supervision

The United States had no central bank between 1836, when President Andrew Jackson vetoed the recharter of the Second Bank of the United States, and 1913. In the intervening seventy-seven years the country relied on a fragmented system of state banks, national banks (created by the National Bank Act 1864), and clearing-house associations, with no lender of last resort. Banking panics were routine. The Panic of 1907, triggered by an attempted corner of United Copper stock and resolved only by J.P. Morgan personally underwriting a private liquidity facility, persuaded Congress that a public lender of last resort was unavoidable.

The Federal Reserve Act, signed by President Woodrow Wilson on 23 December 1913, was the political compromise. Rural and progressive Democrats wanted a single public central bank; Wall Street and the eastern banks wanted a private one; the Republicans wanted no central bank at all. The Act delivered a hybrid: a federal Board in Washington (public, presidentially appointed) and twelve regional Reserve Banks (quasi-private, with stock held by member commercial banks). The compromise has held for more than a century, although the balance has shifted decisively toward the Washington Board over time.

Several Acts since 1913 have rebuilt the institution:

Banking Act 1935
Renamed the Federal Reserve Board the Board of Governors, stripped the Treasury Secretary and Comptroller of the Currency of their ex-officio seats, gave the Board control over the Reserve Banks' discount rates and reserve requirements, and created the FOMC in its modern form. Effectively the founding statute of the Fed as it operates today.
Bank Holding Company Act 1956
Gave the Fed jurisdiction over companies that owned banks (bank holding companies). Prior to 1956, holding-company structures had been used to skirt the McFadden Act's prohibitions on interstate branching. The Act established the Fed as consolidated supervisor of every BHC and remains the legal basis for Reg Y.
International Banking Act 1978
Brought foreign banks operating in the United States under federal supervision and gave the Fed authority over foreign banking organisations (FBOs). The FBO regime was later substantially rebuilt by the Dodd-Frank Act and the Board's 2014 Reg YY enhancements.
Humphrey-Hawkins Act 1978
The Full Employment and Balanced Growth Act. Formalised the Fed's "dual mandate" of maximum employment and stable prices and required the Fed Chair to deliver semi-annual monetary policy testimony to Congress. The dual mandate phrasing remains the statutory anchor for FOMC communications.
Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act 1999
Repealed the Glass-Steagall separation of commercial and investment banking. Created the financial holding company (FHC) status, allowing eligible BHCs to engage in merchant banking, insurance underwriting, and a broader range of financial activities. Made the Fed the umbrella supervisor of all FHCs.
Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act 2010
The post-2008 reconstruction. Created the Vice-Chair for Supervision; expanded Fed authority to designated non-bank SIFIs; gave the Fed responsibility for enhanced prudential standards on the largest BHCs; introduced DFAST; codified the Fed's resolution-planning role; and (Section 13(3)) restricted the Fed's emergency-lending authority to broad-based facilities rather than rescues of individual firms.
EGRRCPA 2018 (S.2155)
The Economic Growth, Regulatory Relief and Consumer Protection Act. Raised the threshold for automatic enhanced prudential standards from $50bn to $250bn in consolidated assets and instructed the Fed to tailor requirements between $100bn and $250bn. The legal basis for the four-category tailoring framework now in force.

Governance: the Board, the Reserve Banks, and the FOMC

The Board of Governors has seven members appointed by the President of the United States with the advice and consent of the Senate. Each Governor serves a non-renewable fourteen-year term, with terms staggered so that one expires every two years. This design is meant to insulate the Board from short-term political pressure: no president serving a single term can appoint a majority of Governors. The Chair and Vice-Chair are designated by the President from among the sitting Governors for renewable four-year terms. The Vice-Chair for Supervision, established by Dodd-Frank Section 1108, is similarly designated for a renewable four-year term and is the Board member primarily responsible for the supervisory programme.

Each of the twelve Reserve Banks has its own President, appointed by the Reserve Bank's Board of Directors with the approval of the Board of Governors, for a renewable five-year term. The Reserve Bank Boards have nine directors split between three classes: three Class A directors elected by the member banks to represent the banks themselves; three Class B directors elected by the member banks to represent the public; and three Class C directors appointed by the Board of Governors to represent the public. The Chair of the Reserve Bank Board is always a Class C director, which gives the public-interest representatives the chair role at every Reserve Bank. The Reserve Banks are technically corporations whose stock is held by the member banks in their districts, but the dividend on that stock is statutorily capped and the Reserve Banks remit substantially all of their profits to the US Treasury.

FOMC composition
Twelve voting members: the seven Governors plus the New York Fed President (permanent voter, reflecting the NY Fed's role in market operations) plus four of the remaining eleven Reserve Bank Presidents rotating on a one-year cycle. All twelve Reserve Bank Presidents attend every meeting and participate in the discussion; only the four rotating Presidents have a vote at any given meeting. The Chair of the Board is by convention the Chair of the FOMC; the Vice-Chair of the FOMC is by convention the President of the New York Fed.
FOMC meeting cycle
Eight scheduled meetings per year, roughly every six weeks. Each meeting produces an FOMC statement (released at the conclusion), a Summary of Economic Projections (released quarterly, including the "dot plot" of individual participants' rate projections), and minutes (released three weeks later). Out-of-cycle meetings can be convened for policy emergencies.
Vice-Chair for Supervision
A Board position created by Dodd-Frank 2010, first filled by Randal Quarles in 2017, then Michael Barr from 2022. The Vice-Chair for Supervision sets the direction of the Fed's supervisory programme and represents the Board on the BCBS and FSB. Statutorily required to testify before Congress on supervisory policy twice a year.

Bank supervisory perimeter

The Fed's supervisory remit is structured by entity type rather than by firm size. Five entity categories are relevant.

Bank holding companies (BHCs)
Companies that own one or more US banks. The Fed is the consolidated supervisor of every BHC, with authority over the holding company and its non-bank subsidiaries. Approximately 3,500 BHCs in the United States, ranging from JPMorgan Chase (consolidated assets over $4 trillion) to small one-bank holding companies. The legal basis is the Bank Holding Company Act 1956 and Reg Y.
Financial holding companies (FHCs)
A subset of BHCs that have elected FHC status under Gramm-Leach-Bliley. FHCs may engage in merchant banking, insurance underwriting, and other "financial in nature" activities beyond the traditional BHC permissions. All US universal banks operate as FHCs. The legal basis is Section 4 of the Bank Holding Company Act and Reg Y Subpart I.
State member banks
State-chartered banks that have opted to join the Federal Reserve System. Approximately 700 to 850 institutions, ranging from large regional banks to community banks. The Fed supervises them jointly with the relevant state banking department under Reg H, which sets membership conditions, permissible activities, and reporting requirements. State-chartered banks that have not joined the System are supervised by the FDIC and the state.
Foreign banking organisations (FBOs)
Non-US banks with US branches, agencies, representative offices, or US bank subsidiaries. The Fed is the umbrella supervisor of every FBO's combined US operations. FBOs with substantial US footprint (typically over $50bn in US non-branch assets) are required to operate through an intermediate holding company (IHC) and meet US capital, liquidity, and resolution standards on an IHC-consolidated basis. The legal basis is the International Banking Act 1978 and Reg YY Subpart O.
Savings and loan holding companies, designated non-bank SIFIs
Dodd-Frank transferred consolidated supervision of savings and loan holding companies from the now-abolished Office of Thrift Supervision to the Fed. The Financial Stability Oversight Council (FSOC) may also designate non-bank financial firms as systemically important, bringing them under Fed supervision. Designations have been used sparingly and most have been rescinded; AIG, GE Capital, MetLife, and Prudential were all designated and de-designated between 2013 and 2018.

The tailoring framework: four categories

The Board's October 2019 final rules implementing EGRRCPA established four categories of supervised firm based on size and complexity. The thresholds use consolidated assets and four risk-based indicators: cross-jurisdictional activity, weighted short-term wholesale funding, non-bank assets, and off-balance-sheet exposure. The framework applies to US BHCs and the IHCs of large FBOs; community banks below the Category IV threshold sit outside the framework on a separate, simpler regime.

Category I: US G-SIBs
The eight banks designated as US global systemically important banks under the FSB methodology. Subject to the full Basel III standards as implemented in the United States, the G-SIB surcharge, the supplementary leverage ratio, full LCR and NSFR at the bank and consolidated level, single-counterparty credit limits, annual CCAR and DFAST, and full resolution planning under Title I of Dodd-Frank.
Category II
Non-G-SIB firms with $700bn or more in consolidated assets, or $75bn or more in cross-jurisdictional activity. Subject to substantially the same standards as Category I, less the G-SIB surcharge. Annual CCAR and DFAST.
Category III
Firms with $250bn or more in consolidated assets, or $75bn or more in weighted short-term wholesale funding, non-bank assets, or off-balance-sheet exposures. Reduced LCR and NSFR requirements (85 per cent rather than 100 per cent). Annual CCAR and DFAST. The supplementary leverage ratio applies.
Category IV
Firms with $100bn to $250bn in consolidated assets that do not meet any Category III risk-based threshold. Pre-2023: no LCR, no NSFR, biennial CCAR (in alternating years), limited resolution planning. SVB and Signature Bank were Category IV firms at the time of their failures. Post-2023 the Fed has signalled tighter Category IV standards but the rule changes are still in train.
Community banking organisations
Below $100bn in consolidated assets. Not subject to enhanced prudential standards. Subject to the Community Bank Leverage Ratio framework (an optional 9 per cent leverage ratio that, if met, deems the firm compliant with risk-based capital rules) and a tailored examination cycle. Most of the United States' approximately 4,500 commercial banks fall into this group.

CCAR, DFAST, and the Stress Capital Buffer

The Comprehensive Capital Analysis and Review (CCAR) is the Fed's annual capital adequacy examination for the largest BHCs. It began in 2011 as a successor to the 2009 Supervisory Capital Assessment Program (the "stress test" that helped end the acute phase of the global financial crisis). CCAR has two components: a quantitative assessment of projected capital ratios under a Fed-supplied severely adverse macroeconomic scenario, and a qualitative assessment of the firm's capital planning processes. Until 2020 the Fed could "object" to a firm's capital plan on either basis, prohibiting the planned distributions until the firm resubmitted.

The Dodd-Frank Act Stress Test (DFAST) is a parallel statutory exercise mandated by Section 165(i) of Dodd-Frank. It uses the same severely adverse scenario as CCAR but excludes any management actions during the stress period (a "no-actions" baseline), producing a more conservative projected capital trajectory. The Fed publishes DFAST results for every covered firm under a common scenario, in a single annual disclosure document that has become a fixture of the US bank-investor calendar.

Stress Capital Buffer (SCB)
Introduced in 2020, the SCB replaced CCAR's qualitative objection and quantitative cap with a buffer mechanism integrated into ongoing capital requirements. The SCB equals the maximum projected decline in the firm's CET1 ratio under the severely adverse scenario over the nine-quarter projection horizon, plus four quarters of planned common dividends, floored at 2.5 per cent. The SCB replaces the static 2.5 per cent capital conservation buffer for firms subject to CCAR. A firm whose actual CET1 falls below its [4.5% minimum + SCB + G-SIB surcharge + countercyclical buffer] sum faces automatic restrictions on capital distributions and discretionary bonuses.
Scenarios
The Fed publishes three scenarios annually (baseline, adverse, severely adverse) in early February. The severely adverse scenario typically combines a deep US recession (unemployment rising 4 to 6 percentage points, equity prices falling 50 per cent or more, house prices falling 25 to 30 per cent) with a global slowdown. Trading-book firms additionally receive a Global Market Shock and a Largest Counterparty Default scenario.

The Fed's regulations: a quick reference

The Board's regulations are organised by letter (Reg A through Reg ZZ). The ones most relevant to bank supervision are these.

Reg A
Extensions of credit by Federal Reserve Banks. Governs the discount window: primary credit (for sound institutions), secondary credit (for less sound institutions), seasonal credit (for small banks with predictable seasonal needs), and emergency credit under Section 13(3).
Reg H
Membership of state banking institutions in the Federal Reserve System. Sets out the conditions for state member bank status, permissible activities, real-estate lending standards, and reporting.
Reg K
International banking operations. Governs the foreign activities of US BHCs (Edge Act and Agreement corporations) and the US activities of foreign banks. Sister regulation to Reg YY for FBO matters.
Reg LL
Savings and loan holding companies. The Fed's consolidated supervision regime for thrift holding companies, inherited from the Office of Thrift Supervision under Dodd-Frank.
Reg Q (capital adequacy)
The current Reg Q (2013) implements Basel III capital standards in the United States: minimum CET1, Tier 1, and total capital ratios, the capital conservation buffer (now the SCB for CCAR firms), the countercyclical buffer, and the supplementary leverage ratio. Note: the original Reg Q (interest-rate ceilings on deposits) was repealed in 2011; the letter was reused for the post-Basel III capital rule.
Reg WW
Liquidity standards. Implements the Liquidity Coverage Ratio (full LCR for Category I, II, and III; reduced LCR for some Category III; no LCR for Category IV pre-2023) and the Net Stable Funding Ratio.
Reg Y
Bank holding companies and change in bank control. The core regulation implementing the Bank Holding Company Act 1956 and Gramm-Leach-Bliley FHC provisions. Governs permissible activities, acquisitions, and divestitures.
Reg YY
Enhanced prudential standards for large BHCs and FBOs. Implements Dodd-Frank Section 165 and the tailoring rules. Covers risk management, liquidity risk management, single-counterparty credit limits, stress testing for large firms, and the IHC requirement for FBOs.

Monetary policy tools

The FOMC's primary tool is the target range for the federal funds rate, the overnight rate at which depository institutions lend reserve balances to one another. The Fed implements the target through several administered rates and market operations.

Interest on Reserve Balances (IORB)
The interest rate the Fed pays depository institutions on the reserves they hold at the Fed. Sets a floor for the federal funds rate because no bank should lend reserves below the rate it could earn risk-free at the Fed. IORB authority dates from 2008.
Overnight Reverse Repo (ON RRP) rate
The rate the Fed pays counterparties (money market funds, GSEs, banks) on overnight reverse repos. Sets a soft floor below IORB for non-bank cash investors who cannot earn IORB. Together with IORB, defines the lower bound of the fed funds target range.
Discount window
Standing collateralised lending facility for sound depository institutions. Primary credit is offered at the discount rate (typically 25 to 50 basis points above the upper bound of the fed funds target range). The discount window is the Fed's principal lender-of-last-resort tool for individual banks.
Open market operations (OMO)
Outright purchases and sales of Treasury and agency securities by the New York Fed's trading desk, expanding or contracting the supply of reserves. The 2008 to 2014 large-scale asset purchase programmes (QE1, QE2, QE3) and the 2020 COVID-era purchases were OMO at an unprecedented scale; balance-sheet runoff since 2017 (and again from 2022) reverses the process.
Section 13(3) emergency lending
Authority under Section 13(3) of the Federal Reserve Act for the Fed to extend credit to non-depository institutions in "unusual and exigent circumstances". Used heavily in 2008 (AIG, Bear Stearns, the Primary Dealer Credit Facility, the Commercial Paper Funding Facility) and again in 2020 (Main Street Lending Program, Municipal Liquidity Facility, the SVB-era Bank Term Funding Program). Dodd-Frank narrowed the authority so that Section 13(3) can only be used to support broad-based facilities, not individual firms.

The 2023 failures and the Bank Term Funding Program

On 10 March 2023 Silicon Valley Bank (SVB), a Category IV firm with $209bn in consolidated assets, was closed by the California Department of Financial Protection and Innovation following a deposit run triggered by the disclosure of large unrealised losses on its securities portfolio. Two days later Signature Bank (also Category IV, $110bn in assets) was closed by the New York Department of Financial Services. The Fed, the FDIC, and the Treasury jointly invoked the systemic risk exception under the Federal Deposit Insurance Act to guarantee all deposits at both banks, including the substantial uninsured deposits. The Fed simultaneously announced the Bank Term Funding Program (BTFP) under Section 13(3): a facility offering one-year loans to depository institutions against high-quality collateral valued at par, rather than market value. The BTFP closed to new borrowing in March 2024.

The Fed published an internal review of the SVB supervisory failure in April 2023, led by Vice-Chair for Supervision Michael Barr. The Barr Review concluded that SVB had failed in part because of weaknesses in its own management, in part because of the Category IV tailoring that had exempted it from LCR, NSFR, and full CCAR, and in part because of supervisory failures by the San Francisco Reserve Bank examination team. The Review recommended tightening the Category IV regime, accelerating the application of LCR and NSFR to firms above $100bn, and bringing back qualitative supervisory expectations on capital planning. Several of those recommendations have been incorporated into rule proposals, including the 2023 Basel III endgame proposed rule.

Basel III endgame and the 2024 to 2025 outlook

In July 2023 the Federal Reserve, OCC, and FDIC jointly proposed a rule implementing the BCBS d424 finalisation package in the United States, widely known as the "Basel III endgame". The proposal would have raised aggregate capital requirements at the largest BHCs by roughly 16 to 19 per cent according to the Fed's own impact analysis, principally through changes to credit-risk standardised approaches, the introduction of the standardised market-risk approach (FRTB SA), the standardised CVA framework, and the 72.5 per cent output floor. The proposal was politically contentious; industry argued the calibration was excessive and Vice-Chair for Supervision Barr announced in September 2024 that a "broad and material" re-proposal was forthcoming, with lower aggregate capital impact. The rule remains under development at the time of writing and will not finalise before 2025 at the earliest; implementation is currently targeted for 2026 to 2028.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The Fed is unusual among central banks in being a prudential supervisor at scale

Most major central banks either have no bank-supervisory role (the European Central Bank had none until 2014 and even now operates through the Single Supervisory Mechanism rather than as a unitary supervisor), or have a supervisory role that is operationally separate from monetary policy (the Bank of England's PRA is institutionally a subsidiary). The Fed combines both at the Board level. The advantage is information flow: the same Governors who set the policy rate see the supervisory portfolio reviews. The disadvantage is conflict: monetary loosening at a time of fragile bank balance sheets is sometimes read as supervisory forbearance. The Vice-Chair for Supervision role is a partial answer; it gives the Board a designated chief supervisor whose career incentives are aligned with supervisory rigour rather than with low rates.

The regional Reserve Bank structure is a uniquely American supervisory architecture

The twelve Reserve Banks are quasi-private corporations whose Presidents are not Senate-confirmed and whose Boards include local commercial bankers. They run the day-to-day examination programmes for the BHCs and state member banks in their districts. This gives the Fed a permanent regional supervisory presence that no other major central bank has. It also produces variation in supervisory culture: a BHC headquartered in San Francisco interacts with a different examination team, with different priorities, from one headquartered in Atlanta. Post-SVB the Board has tightened central oversight of regional examination programmes to address this variation.

CCAR and the SCB are the global supervisory stress-testing benchmark

The CCAR scenarios, the nine-quarter projection horizon, the public disclosure of supervisor-projected losses, and the SCB integration of stress losses into binding capital requirements have all been copied or adapted by other major supervisors. The European Central Bank's SREP stress tests, the Bank of England's annual cyclical scenario, and the supervisory stress tests in Japan, Canada, Australia, and Singapore all derive from CCAR. For a credit risk modeller, this means that the modelling techniques used to produce CCAR submissions (loss-PPNR-RWA projection on a quarter-by-quarter basis under a path of macroeconomic variables) are now the global state of practice, not a US-specific peculiarity.

Watch out The 2018 EGRRCPA tailoring framework was politically popular with the regional banks at the time but is now widely seen as having contributed to the 2023 failures. Whether the post-2023 supervisory response permanently reverses tailoring or merely tightens Category IV requirements is the live question. An actuary at a Category IV or Category III firm should expect a meaningful tightening of LCR, NSFR, resolution-planning, and CCAR requirements over the 2025 to 2027 window, even if the headline Basel III endgame is recalibrated downward.

The Vice-Chair for Supervision is the most powerful single bank supervisor in the world

By assets supervised the Vice-Chair for Supervision oversees more bank balance sheet than the ECB's chair of the Supervisory Board, the PRA chief executive, or the chair of the Bank of Japan. The post is also the principal US voice on the BCBS and the FSB. Watching who fills the role and what their stated priorities are is a leading indicator of where US bank regulation is going. Quarrels between the Vice-Chair for Supervision and the Chair of the Board are unusual but, when they happen, are politically consequential; the 2023 to 2024 debate over the Basel III endgame proposal was in part a public disagreement between Vice-Chair Barr and other Governors over calibration.

Read Fed speeches before you read Fed rules

The Fed's most important supervisory signals are in speeches and testimony, not in final rules. By the time a rule is finalised, the policy debate is closed. The policy debate itself, including the Fed's likely calibration choices, plays out in speeches by the Chair, the Vice-Chair for Supervision, and the New York Fed President. The Board's website publishes every Governor speech; reading them in chronological order gives you the supervisory narrative in real time, months ahead of the official rule. The same is true for FOMC monetary policy: the Chair's press conference at the conclusion of each FOMC meeting is usually more informative than the statement.
