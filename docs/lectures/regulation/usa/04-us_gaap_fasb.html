US GAAP, FASB, and CECL: the American accounting framework

United States accounting standards are written by the Financial Accounting Standards Board, a private-sector body recognised by the Securities and Exchange Commission as the authoritative source of US GAAP for public companies. Since 2009 every requirement sits in a single Accounting Standards Codification. The most consequential standard for banks is ASC 326, the Current Expected Credit Losses model, which replaced the incurred-loss approach in 2020 and now governs how every US bank measures impairment on every loan, every held-to-maturity security, and every off-balance-sheet credit exposure.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB), founded in 1973 to replace the Accounting Principles Board, is the private-sector body that writes US accounting standards. Its parent is the Financial Accounting Foundation (FAF), an independent private foundation that also oversees the Governmental Accounting Standards Board for state and local government reporting. The Securities and Exchange Commission has statutory authority over financial reporting by public companies under the Securities Act of 1933 and the Securities Exchange Act of 1934; it has delegated standard-setting to FASB while retaining the legal right to override, a right it has never exercised. Since 2009, every piece of authoritative US GAAP lives inside a single document, the Accounting Standards Codification (ASC), and is amended through numbered Accounting Standards Updates.

For banks, the most consequential FASB output of the past decade is ASC 326, Financial Instruments, Credit Losses, issued in 2016 as Accounting Standards Update 2016-13 and effective for large public banks from 1 January 2020. ASC 326 introduced the Current Expected Credit Losses model, known universally as CECL. CECL replaced the old incurred-loss approach in which a loss could only be booked once it had probably been incurred. Under CECL a bank must, from the moment a loan is originated, hold an allowance equal to the expected credit loss over the entire remaining life of the instrument, taking into account past events, current conditions, and reasonable and supportable forecasts of future conditions. It is the US analogue of IFRS 9 expected credit losses, but with one critical difference: CECL is lifetime ECL from day one with no probability threshold and no staging.

Feynman test Two banks are sitting next to each other in a marketplace. The London bank works under IFRS. When it lends £100, it puts aside a small amount, maybe 50p, equal to one year's worth of expected losses. Only if the borrower starts looking worse does it bump the reserve up to the full life-of-loan loss. The New York bank works under US GAAP. When it lends $100, it puts aside the entire lifetime expected loss on day one, maybe $4, even if the borrower looks perfectly healthy. From day two both banks track the loan the same way, but the New York bank started with a bigger hole. The London bank has stages. The New York bank has one stage, called "all". That is the difference between IFRS 9 and CECL: same idea, expected loss, different starting point. Everything else in this lecture is who decides this stuff and how they wrote it down.

Step 2

Why this matters

US banks report under US GAAP, not IFRS. The Federal Reserve, the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, and the National Credit Union Administration all rely on GAAP financial statements as the starting point for prudential reporting. If you cannot read a US bank's 10-K and locate the allowance for credit losses, the trading versus available-for-sale versus held-to-maturity split on the investment portfolio, and the hedge-accounting disclosures, you cannot understand the bank's risk profile or its regulatory capital.

The IFRS 9 to CECL divergence creates real measurement differences. The same loan to the same borrower, originated on the same day, will carry a different allowance depending on which framework the lender uses. For a cross-border bank, or for an analyst comparing US and European banks, those differences are systematic, not noise. On day one a CECL allowance is materially higher than an IFRS 9 stage 1 allowance because CECL booked the entire lifetime expected loss while IFRS 9 booked only the next twelve months. As the loan deteriorates, the gap narrows because IFRS 9 catches up at the stage 1 to stage 2 transition. At the point of credit impairment both frameworks converge on something close to the lifetime loss.

The CECL transition was material in absolute terms. The day-one impact for large US banks averaged a 25 to 50 per cent increase in the allowance for credit losses when CECL replaced the incurred-loss model on 1 January 2020. The 2020 COVID shock then drove a further surge in reserves in the first quarter, followed by reserve releases through 2021 and 2022 as the macroeconomic forecast improved. The procyclicality of CECL provisioning has been one of the most debated features of the standard ever since.

For an actuary doing credit-risk modelling at a US-domiciled bank, CECL is the controlling rule for financial-statement allowance. A separate set of regulatory rules under Basel and the US implementation through 12 CFR Part 217 determines regulatory capital. The two frameworks talk to each other through the capital transitional rule and through the deduction of expected loss shortfall, but they are conceptually distinct. The financial statement number is CECL. The regulatory capital number is Basel. The modelling team usually builds for CECL first and adapts for capital second, because CECL has the more demanding documentation and audit requirements.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture the US accounting universe as two stacked diagrams. The top diagram is institutional: who writes the rules and who signs off on them. The Financial Accounting Foundation sits at the apex as the private parent. Beneath it sit two operational boards, FASB for the private sector and GASB for state and local government. Around the side sits the SEC, with statutory authority but a long-standing delegation arrangement. The bottom diagram is documentary: every piece of authoritative US GAAP is filed in a single Codification, organised into Areas, then Topics, then Subtopics, then Sections.

Three flows of authority matter. Standards flow down from FASB into the Codification, then into entity financial statements, where auditors verify compliance with reference to the topic citations. Enforcement flows sideways from the SEC for public companies and from state boards of accountancy or the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PCAOB) for the audit profession. Interpretive guidance flows back up into FASB through the Emerging Issues Task Force and through SEC Staff Accounting Bulletins, which the SEC issues to interpret GAAP in specific public-company contexts.

Step 4

The detail

FASB history

FASB was created in 1973 by the Financial Accounting Foundation to replace the Accounting Principles Board (APB), which had run accounting standard-setting under the American Institute of Certified Public Accountants since 1959. The APB had been criticised for slow output and for being too close to the profession it was meant to regulate. The Wheat Committee, a 1971 study group, recommended replacing it with an independent body funded outside the AICPA. FASB was the result. It is based in Norwalk, Connecticut, and has a seven-member Board, all serving full-time and required to sever ties with their former employers on appointment. Standards are passed by simple majority vote.

From 1973 to 2009, FASB pronouncements took the form of numbered Statements of Financial Accounting Standards (FAS or SFAS), Interpretations, Technical Bulletins, and similar instruments. The pre-2009 vocabulary still surfaces in older textbooks and in conversation: FAS 5 was the incurred-loss impairment standard that CECL eventually replaced; FAS 114 was the impaired-loan accounting standard; FAS 133 was the original derivatives and hedging standard, now ASC 815; FAS 157 was the fair-value-measurement standard, now ASC 820. In 2009 FASB consolidated the entire body of guidance into the Accounting Standards Codification, retiring the old citation system in favour of ASC topic numbers.

FAF (Financial Accounting Foundation)
An independent private-sector organisation that funds and oversees FASB and GASB. Trustees are appointed from a defined set of nominating organisations including the AICPA, Financial Executives International, the Institute of Management Accountants, the CFA Institute, the National Association of State Auditors, and the Securities Industry and Financial Markets Association. The FAF appoints FASB Board members and the FASB chair, sets the strategic direction, and approves the operating budget. Crucially it does not vote on standards: the Board operates with technical independence.
GASB (Governmental Accounting Standards Board)
FASB's sibling under the FAF, established 1984. Writes accounting standards for US state and local government entities. Not relevant to banks except to the extent that banks lend to municipalities, whose financial statements are prepared under GASB rather than FASB rules.
FASB Board composition
Seven full-time members appointed by the FAF, each serving five-year terms (renewable once). Members must sever ties with prior employers. The chair is a Board member designated by the FAF. Voting requires a simple majority of four for adoption of a final standard.
FASAC (Financial Accounting Standards Advisory Council)
FASB's principal advisory body, drawn from preparers, auditors, and users. Advises on technical agenda and on priorities. Meets quarterly. Influential but non-voting.

The SEC relationship

The Securities and Exchange Commission has statutory authority over financial reporting by public companies under Section 19(a) of the Securities Act of 1933 and Section 13(b) of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, which together require public companies to file periodic financial statements prepared in accordance with such accounting principles as the SEC may prescribe. In 1973 the SEC issued Accounting Series Release 150 (now ASR 150, later reaffirmed in Financial Reporting Release 1) recognising FASB pronouncements as authoritative for SEC filings, on the condition that the SEC retained the right to override or reject any standard it disagreed with. That right has never been used to overturn a FASB standard, although the SEC has frequently nudged FASB through Staff Accounting Bulletins, comment letters, and public statements.

For non-public companies the legal route is different. State boards of accountancy and the AICPA's code of professional conduct require that financial statements presented as being in conformity with US GAAP comply with FASB pronouncements. The result is the same: FASB pronouncements are the de facto and de jure source of GAAP for essentially every entity outside government.

The SEC supplements FASB guidance with its own interpretive output for public companies. Staff Accounting Bulletins (SABs) clarify how a FASB standard should be applied in an SEC filing context. SAB 119, issued in November 2019, provided transition relief and interpretive guidance for CECL adoption. Financial Reporting Manual entries and Compliance and Disclosure Interpretations provide further detail. None of this is GAAP itself, but for an SEC registrant it is binding.

The FASB standard-setting process

A FASB project typically takes three to six years from agenda decision to final standard. The stages are:

  1. Pre-agenda research. Staff research and FASAC consultation identify a topic worth pursuing.
  2. Agenda decision. The Board formally adds the project to its technical agenda and assigns staff.
  3. Initial deliberations. Staff develops the technical proposal through public Board meetings, with input from working groups and the EITF where relevant.
  4. Exposure Draft. A formal proposed standard is issued for public comment, usually with a 60- to 120-day comment period. Comment letters are public and substantive.
  5. Redeliberation. The Board reads the comment letters and reconsiders provisions of the draft. Substantive changes can occur at this stage.
  6. Final Accounting Standards Update (ASU). Issued as an amendment to the Codification, numbered by year and sequence (e.g. ASU 2016-13 for CECL). Includes the effective date and transition provisions. Once issued, the ASU is folded into the relevant Codification topic.

The Accounting Standards Codification

The Codification launched on 1 July 2009 as the single authoritative source of US GAAP for non-governmental entities. Before the Codification, GAAP was scattered across more than 2,000 separate pronouncements issued by FASB, the APB, the AICPA, the SEC, and the Emerging Issues Task Force, organised by source and date. Researching a particular issue required consulting multiple documents in different hierarchies. The Codification consolidated all of this into a single structure organised by topic, with one section addressing each issue.

The structure is hierarchical. Areas group topics by subject (e.g. Area 300 covers Assets). Topics are the principal unit, numbered with three-digit codes (e.g. ASC 326 for Credit Losses). Subtopics subdivide a topic by transaction type (e.g. ASC 326-20 for measured-at-amortised-cost financial assets, ASC 326-30 for available-for-sale debt securities). Sections address aspects of the subtopic in a standardised order (10 Overall, 15 Scope, 20 Glossary, 25 Recognition, 30 Initial Measurement, 35 Subsequent Measurement, 40 Derecognition, 45 Other Presentation, 50 Disclosure, 55 Implementation, 60 Relationships, 65 Transition, 70 Grandfathered Guidance, 75 XBRL Elements). A full citation looks like ASC 326-20-30-2: Topic 326, Subtopic 20, Section 30 (Initial Measurement), paragraph 2.

Key ASC topics for banks

ASC 320, Investments, Debt Securities
Classification and measurement of debt securities into trading (fair value through earnings), available-for-sale (fair value through other comprehensive income with realised gains and losses through earnings), and held-to-maturity (amortised cost). Inherited from FAS 115. For banks, ASC 320 determines whether unrealised gains and losses on the bond book hit earnings, OCI, or neither. The shape of the bond book and the AFS versus HTM split was central to the 2023 regional-bank failures.
ASC 326, Financial Instruments, Credit Losses (CECL)
The current expected credit losses model, issued as ASU 2016-13. Replaces the incurred-loss model for loans held for investment, held-to-maturity debt securities, lease receivables, reinsurance receivables, and off-balance-sheet credit exposures, plus a separate impairment model for available-for-sale debt securities in subtopic 326-30. Discussed in detail below.
ASC 815, Derivatives and Hedging
Successor to FAS 133. Defines what is a derivative for accounting purposes, measures derivatives at fair value through earnings unless hedge accounting is elected, and sets out the three permitted hedge designations: fair-value hedges, cash-flow hedges, and net-investment hedges of foreign operations. Hedge accounting documentation requirements are stringent. ASU 2017-12 and subsequent amendments simplified the hedge accounting model but did not change the core fair-value-through-earnings default.
ASC 825, Financial Instruments
Permits an entity to elect fair-value measurement for eligible financial instruments on an instrument-by-instrument basis (the "fair value option"). Once elected, the election is irrevocable for that instrument. Often used by banks for hybrid instruments to avoid bifurcation.
ASC 842, Leases
Effective for public business entities in 2019 and others in 2022. Brings essentially all leases onto the lessee balance sheet as right-of-use assets and lease liabilities. The US analogue of IFRS 16, but with two lessee classifications (finance and operating) where IFRS 16 has only one. Banks are usually small lessees but large lessors of equipment-finance and real-estate-finance leases.
ASC 606, Revenue from Contracts with Customers
Effective 2018, jointly developed with the IASB (which issued IFRS 15). Five-step revenue recognition model based on transferring control of goods and services to customers. For banks, ASC 606 governs fee revenue (deposit fees, card interchange, asset-management fees) but does not apply to interest income on financial instruments, which is in ASC 310 and ASC 320.

The CECL model: ASC 326-20

CECL is the most consequential FASB output for banks in the past decade. It replaces the incurred-loss model that had governed US bank impairment accounting under FAS 5 (general allowance) and FAS 114 (impaired loans) since the mid-1970s. The incurred-loss model had been criticised after the 2008 crisis for delaying loss recognition: banks could only book a loss when a loss event had probably occurred. CECL removes the probability threshold entirely.

The scope of ASC 326-20 includes loans held for investment, held-to-maturity debt securities, net investment in leases, reinsurance receivables, trade receivables, and off-balance-sheet credit exposures such as unfunded loan commitments and standby letters of credit. Available-for-sale debt securities have a separate impairment model in ASC 326-30 (described below). Trading securities and loans held for sale at lower of cost or fair value are not in scope.

The measurement objective is the expected credit losses over the contractual life of the asset, adjusted for prepayments. The measurement must reflect three sets of information:

  1. Past events. Historical loss experience for assets with similar risk characteristics.
  2. Current conditions. Adjustments to historical loss rates to reflect differences between historical and current portfolio composition, underwriting, and economic conditions.
  3. Reasonable and supportable forecasts. Forward-looking adjustments over a period the entity can reasonably and supportably forecast. Beyond that period, the entity reverts to historical loss information on a straight-line or other rational basis. The forecast period is typically one to three years in practice.

Critically, there is no probability threshold and no staging. From the day a loan is originated, the bank must hold an allowance equal to the lifetime expected credit loss on that loan. There is no concept of a "12-month ECL" or a "stage 1" pool. The only conditional measurement applies to financial assets that are purchased credit-deteriorated (PCD), for which a gross-up approach is used at acquisition so that the initial allowance is added to the purchase price rather than expensed.

CECL methodologies in practice

ASC 326 is method-agnostic. It tells the entity what the answer must represent (lifetime expected credit losses) but not how to compute it. In practice US banks have implemented one or more of the following approaches, often a different approach per portfolio:

PD / LGD / EAD
The same framework used for A-IRB and IFRS 9, with the modification that PDs and EADs are projected over the full remaining contractual life of each loan, not just twelve months. Familiar to actuarial teams. Standard at the largest banks for commercial portfolios where there is enough data to support segment-level PD term structures.
Vintage analysis
Group loans by origination cohort and track cumulative loss curves by age. The lifetime expected loss for a new cohort is read off the historical loss curve, adjusted for current portfolio characteristics and forecast macro conditions. Common for retail portfolios with long historical data.
Roll-rate / migration matrices
Estimate transition probabilities between delinquency buckets (current, 30 days past due, 60 dpd, charge-off) and chain them forward over the loan's remaining life. Common for credit cards and consumer instalment portfolios where the migration data is plentiful.
Discounted cash flow (DCF)
Project contractual cash flows, deduct expected credit losses, and discount at the effective interest rate. The allowance is the difference between the amortised cost and the discounted expected cash flows. The most theoretically grounded method but data-intensive.
WARM (Weighted Average Remaining Maturity)
A simplified method permitted by an October 2018 FASB Q&A. The annual historical loss rate is multiplied by the weighted average remaining maturity of the portfolio to approximate lifetime loss. Commonly used by community banks and credit unions with limited data infrastructure.
Loss-rate (snapshot or open-pool) methods
Apply a historical lifetime loss rate to the current balance, adjusted for portfolio mix and forecast conditions. Simpler than vintage analysis; sometimes used for smaller homogeneous pools.

Available-for-sale impairment: ASC 326-30

Available-for-sale debt securities are not in the CECL scope of ASC 326-20. They have their own impairment model in ASC 326-30, which retains some elements of the pre-2020 other-than-temporary-impairment (OTTI) approach but reframes the result as an allowance rather than a permanent write-down. If the fair value of an AFS security is below amortised cost and the entity does not intend to sell, the entity must estimate whether any of the unrealised loss is attributable to credit. The credit-related portion is recognised through an allowance for credit losses with a corresponding charge to earnings; the remainder stays in OCI as a temporary mark-to-market move. Because it is an allowance rather than a write-down, it can be reversed if credit conditions improve. This was a notable departure from the OTTI model, where credit-related write-downs were permanent.

CECL versus IFRS 9 ECL

CECL and IFRS 9 expected credit losses are sibling standards. Both are forward-looking, both replace incurred-loss frameworks, and both were the joint output of a FASB-IASB convergence project that began in 2008. The convergence project ran from 2008 to 2014 but ultimately failed: the two Boards could not agree on a single model. FASB landed on lifetime ECL from day one. IASB landed on a three-stage model with a 12-month ECL allowance until a significant increase in credit risk triggers the move to lifetime ECL. The result is that essentially the same conceptual model produces different numbers on the same loan.

CECL: one stage, lifetime ECL from day one
On origination, the allowance equals the lifetime expected credit loss. No probability threshold to cross. As the loan ages, the allowance is remeasured each reporting date to reflect the current lifetime expected loss given current conditions and forecasts. The allowance can be released as the loan amortises or as conditions improve.
IFRS 9: three stages, conditional measurement
Stage 1, the default at origination, holds 12-month ECL. Stage 2, triggered by a significant increase in credit risk (SICR), holds lifetime ECL. Stage 3, triggered by objective evidence of credit impairment, also holds lifetime ECL and recognises interest revenue on the net carrying amount. The SICR test is the lever that controls the stage 1 to stage 2 transition.
Day-one comparison
For a healthy loan at origination, CECL gives a larger allowance than IFRS 9 stage 1, because CECL books the full lifetime expected loss while IFRS 9 books only the next twelve months. Empirically the gap is often two to five times the IFRS 9 stage 1 allowance.
Reactivity comparison
As a loan deteriorates without yet defaulting, IFRS 9 recognises a sharp step-up at the stage 1 to stage 2 transition (12-month ECL jumps to lifetime ECL). CECL recognises a smoother build, because it was already on lifetime ECL. CECL is less reactive to credit deterioration. IFRS 9 has cliff-edges.

Implementation, effective dates, and the CARES Act deferral

ASU 2016-13 was issued in June 2016. The effective dates were staggered by entity type, designed to give smaller institutions extra preparation time:

  • Public business entities that are SEC filers (excluding smaller reporting companies): fiscal years beginning after 15 December 2019, i.e. 1 January 2020 for calendar-year filers. This is the date the four largest US banks adopted CECL.
  • Other public business entities (including smaller reporting companies): fiscal years beginning after 15 December 2022, i.e. 1 January 2023.
  • Private companies, not-for-profits, and employee benefit plans: fiscal years beginning after 15 December 2022, i.e. 1 January 2023.

Transition was modified retrospective, with a cumulative-effect adjustment to retained earnings on the adoption date. The day-one impact at large US banks averaged a 25 to 50 per cent increase in the allowance for credit losses, with significant variation by portfolio mix. Banks with heavy credit-card and consumer-lending exposure saw the largest increases; banks with predominantly short-dated commercial portfolios saw smaller increases.

The Coronavirus Aid, Relief, and Economic Security (CARES) Act, signed in March 2020, gave covered banking organisations an option to defer CECL adoption through the earlier of 31 December 2020 or 60 days after the official end of the COVID-19 national emergency. Most large banks declined the deferral, having already adopted CECL on 1 January 2020 and having no operational appetite to revert to the incurred-loss model and then re-adopt. Some smaller banks took the deferral. The deferral expired and all in-scope entities had transitioned by their respective effective dates.

The federal banking-agency capital transition rule

CECL is an accounting standard, but it has direct regulatory-capital consequences because the allowance for credit losses sits in the capital stack: the portion of the allowance up to a fixed cap is includable in Tier 2 capital, and the unallocated portion is deducted from CET1 to the extent it exceeds the eligible regulatory expected loss. The day-one CECL impact would therefore have caused a sudden CET1 reduction for many banks on 1 January 2020.

The Federal Reserve, OCC, and FDIC jointly adopted a CECL capital transitional rule allowing covered institutions to phase in the CET1 impact of CECL adoption over a three-year period (later extended to five years for institutions that elected the COVID-era extended option introduced under the 2020 interim final rule). The phase-in adds back a portion of the day-one allowance impact and 25 per cent of subsequent CECL provisioning to CET1, declining linearly over the transition window. By 2025 essentially all large banks are out of the transition and reflect the full CECL impact in regulatory capital.

Federal Reserve supervisory guidance

The Federal Reserve issued a series of supervisory letters in support of CECL implementation, the most important being SR 19-8 (joint with the OCC, FDIC, and NCUA), which describes supervisory expectations for the bank's CECL methodology, governance, validation, and documentation. The Joint Statement on the New Accounting Standard on Financial Instruments, Credit Losses, issued in June 2016 and updated in 2019 and 2020, provided cross-agency guidance on the interaction between CECL and regulatory reporting. SR 11-7, the older but still controlling guidance on model risk management, applies fully to CECL models. The result is that CECL methodologies are subject to the same independent validation, governance, and back-testing requirements as IRB and stress-testing models.

Worked example: CECL versus IFRS 9 on the same loan

Consider a small business loan originated on 1 January 2024. Principal $1,000,000, contractual maturity five years, annual interest rate 6 per cent. At origination the lender's one-year probability of default is 0.5 per cent, the loss given default is 40 per cent, and the lifetime cumulative probability of default over five years is 2.3 per cent. The effective interest rate equals the contract rate. Ignore prepayment for simplicity.

Step 1: IFRS 9 stage 1 ECL on day one. Under IFRS 9 the lender books the 12-month expected credit loss for a stage 1 exposure. The 12-month ECL approximation, discounted one year at 6 per cent, gives:

ECL_12m = PD_1y * LGD * EAD * df_1y
        = 0.005 * 0.40 * 1,000,000 * (1 / 1.06)
        ~= 1,887

The IFRS 9 allowance on origination is approximately $1,887.

Step 2: CECL allowance on day one. Under ASC 326-20 the lender must book the lifetime expected credit loss. Using a simple PD / LGD / EAD lifetime construction, with the five-year cumulative PD of 2.3 per cent and an average discount factor over the life of the loan of approximately 0.84:

ECL_lifetime = PD_cumulative * LGD * EAD * df_avg
             = 0.023 * 0.40 * 1,000,000 * 0.84
             ~= 7,728

The CECL allowance on origination is approximately $7,728. Day-one CECL is roughly four times day-one IFRS 9 for the same loan. The CECL bank books a $7,728 impairment expense in its first day's profit and loss; the IFRS 9 bank books $1,887.

Step 3: deterioration without default (1 January 2025). Suppose at the first anniversary the borrower's financial position has deteriorated. The lender now believes the one-year forward PD has risen to 4 per cent and the residual four-year cumulative PD is 9 per cent. The lender concludes the loan has experienced a significant increase in credit risk and migrates it to IFRS 9 stage 2.

IFRS 9 stage 2 ECL_lifetime = 0.09 * 0.40 * 1,000,000 * 0.85
                            ~= 30,600

CECL lifetime ECL          = 0.09 * 0.40 * 1,000,000 * 0.85
                            ~= 30,600

At stage 2 the two frameworks converge on essentially the same lifetime loss. The IFRS 9 bank books a sharp impairment expense of roughly $30,600 minus $1,887, around $28,700, at the stage transition. The CECL bank books an impairment expense of $30,600 minus $7,728, around $22,900. The CECL bank had already absorbed a larger day-one hit and therefore has less to add at the deterioration event.

Step 4: aggregate cumulative impairment expense to the deterioration point. Cumulative P&L impact across origination and stage migration is approximately $30,600 under both frameworks. The path differs: CECL front-loads the loss; IFRS 9 back-loads to the SICR trigger. Lifetime loss is the same. The earnings volatility profile is not.

The worked example illustrates the central point. Both standards produce the same lifetime expected loss but front-load versus back-load the recognition. For a stable healthy portfolio the CECL allowance is larger; for a deteriorating portfolio the IFRS 9 allowance steps up sharply at the SICR trigger. For an analyst comparing US and European banks, the difference is systematic and predictable.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

CECL is the residue of a failed convergence project

FASB and IASB spent seven years between 2008 and 2014 trying to write a common impairment standard. They wrote a joint Discussion Paper in 2009 and a joint Exposure Draft in 2013. The Exposure Draft proposed a three-bucket model not unlike IFRS 9's eventual three stages. FASB redeliberated and concluded that the three-bucket model was too operationally complex and too prone to manipulation at bucket boundaries. IASB persisted with the staged model. The two Boards went their separate ways in 2014. CECL was published in 2016, IFRS 9 in 2014. The split is the most consequential failure of FASB-IASB convergence in the last fifteen years.

CECL is more conservative on day one but less reactive afterwards

Because CECL books lifetime expected loss from origination, the day-one allowance is large but the subsequent path is smoother. IFRS 9 books a small day-one allowance but then steps up sharply at the SICR trigger. Banks adopting CECL therefore see a one-time hit to retained earnings at transition, followed by lower earnings volatility from impairment swings. IFRS 9 banks see a smaller hit at transition but more impairment volatility through the cycle. Investor analysis that treats the two frameworks as equivalent misses both differences.

The procyclicality debate is unresolved

Critics of CECL argue that requiring lifetime expected loss from day one makes lending procyclical: at the onset of a downturn, banks must immediately recognise the full lifetime impact of the worsened outlook, which builds reserves at exactly the moment new lending is most needed. The 2020 experience was a vivid illustration: large US banks recognised tens of billions of dollars of CECL provisions in the first and second quarters as the COVID forecast deteriorated, then released large portions of those reserves through 2021 as the forecast improved. Federal Reserve research has examined whether this pattern is more or less procyclical than the incurred-loss model it replaced, with mixed conclusions. FASB has not revisited the framework.

Watch out Do not confuse the CECL allowance with the regulatory-capital expected loss number. They are computed under different rules, with different scope, different discounting conventions, and different definitions of default. The CECL allowance is a financial-statement number under ASC 326. The regulatory expected loss is a Basel/Federal Reserve construct under 12 CFR Part 217. The difference between them feeds into a CET1 adjustment via the EL-versus-allowance comparison. A bank can have a CECL allowance materially larger or smaller than its regulatory EL, and both numbers are correct on their own terms.

The "reasonable and supportable forecast" period is the key judgement

ASC 326 requires entities to incorporate forecasts of future conditions but only over a period they can reasonably and supportably forecast. Beyond that horizon the entity reverts to historical loss information. In practice the choice of forecast horizon, typically one to three years, is the single largest source of variability in CECL outputs across banks. A bank using a one-year forecast horizon will under-react to a deep multi-year downturn; a bank using a three-year horizon will over-react if the downturn proves short. The reversion to historical loss experience can be done on a straight-line basis, a step-down basis, or any other rational basis. Auditors and supervisors have been deferential to entity-specific judgements provided the methodology is documented and applied consistently.

The CECL versus IFRS 9 divergence is the most material accounting difference for cross-border bank comparisons

For an analyst comparing a US bank with a European or UK bank, the impairment frameworks alone cause systematic measurement differences before any portfolio composition or economic environment effects. Adjusting for the framework difference is non-trivial because it requires reconstructing the IFRS 9 stage 1 and stage 2 allowances from CECL disclosures, or vice versa, neither of which is publicly disclosed at sufficient granularity. The closest approximation is the historical stage 1 to stage 2 ratio reported by IFRS 9 banks, which can be applied to a CECL bank's lifetime allowance as a rough cross-walk. The exercise is dirty but defensible for high-level comparisons.

FASB convergence with IFRS is unlikely to be revived

FASB has signalled openness to convergence in principle since the 2014 split but has not initiated any active project to revisit CECL. The IASB has, separately, said it has no plans to revisit IFRS 9 staging. Both Boards are aware that re-opening either standard would impose another wave of multi-year implementation cost on banks that have already absorbed the original transition. The practical position for the foreseeable future is that US banks will report under CECL and the rest of the world will report under IFRS 9, with the divergence baked into every cross-border comparison.
