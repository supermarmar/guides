Dodd-Frank Act: the post-2008 US rebuild

The Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010 is the largest single restructuring of US financial regulation since the New Deal. It rewrote the supervisory perimeter, created entirely new federal agencies (FSOC, OFR, CFPB), prohibited proprietary trading at insured depositories, dragged the over-the-counter derivatives market into central clearing, and built a parallel resolution regime for systemically important firms. Most of its design choices remain politically contested fifteen years on.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act (Public Law 111-203), signed by President Obama on 21 July 2010, runs to 848 pages and 16 Titles. Its purpose, as the long title puts it, is "to promote the financial stability of the United States by improving accountability and transparency in the financial system, to end 'too big to fail', to protect the American taxpayer by ending bailouts, to protect consumers from abusive financial services practices, and for other purposes". The core architecture is built on five pillars. Title I creates the Financial Stability Oversight Council (FSOC) and the Office of Financial Research (OFR), and imposes Enhanced Prudential Standards on large bank holding companies and FSOC-designated nonbank SIFIs. Title II creates the Orderly Liquidation Authority (OLA), allowing the FDIC to act as receiver for a failing systemically important firm outside the normal bankruptcy code. Title VI contains the Volcker Rule, which prohibits insured depositories and their affiliates from proprietary trading and restricts their ability to sponsor or invest in hedge funds and private equity funds. Title VII pulls the over-the-counter derivatives market under CFTC and SEC oversight and into central clearing. Title X creates the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB).

Around these five pillars sit eleven further Titles covering executive compensation and corporate governance (Title IX), mortgage reform (Title XIV), credit rating agencies (Title IX subtitle C), insurance (Title V), securitisation risk retention (Title IX subtitle D), and access to financial services (Title XII). Dodd-Frank also created a new senior Federal Reserve role, the Vice-Chair for Supervision, and required large banks to file resolution plans (living wills) under section 165(d). Eight years later, the Economic Growth, Regulatory Relief, and Consumer Protection Act (EGRRCPA, 2018) raised the automatic SIFI threshold from $50 billion to $250 billion and introduced a tiered "tailoring" framework for prudential requirements. The March 2023 failure of Silicon Valley Bank, a $209 billion bank holding company, reopened the question of whether the 2018 tailoring went too far.

Feynman test Imagine the United States in late 2008. Lehman has collapsed. AIG has taken $182 billion of taxpayer support. Fannie and Freddie are in conservatorship. The Treasury and Fed are improvising rescue programmes (TARP, TALF, PDCF, AMLF) on a weekly basis because there is no peacetime statute that lets the federal government wind down a non-bank financial firm in an orderly way. Congress responds with one enormous bill. The bill says four things. First, somebody needs to be in charge of watching the whole financial system, not just the banks: that is the FSOC, with the Treasury Secretary in the chair and every other regulator at the table. Second, if a big financial firm fails again, the FDIC, not the bankruptcy court, will wind it down: that is OLA. Third, banks should not be using insured deposits to gamble in financial markets: that is the Volcker Rule. Fourth, ordinary Americans need a regulator whose entire job is to police mortgages, credit cards, and overdraft fees: that is the CFPB. Everything else in the 848 pages is detail.

CFPB in 2024 on funding); and the simplifications of the Volcker Rule ("Volcker 2.0", 2019 to 2020).
Step 2

Why this matters

Dodd-Frank is the framework on which almost every contemporary US prudential and conduct rule is hung. The Federal Reserve's Regulation YY, which contains Enhanced Prudential Standards, is a Dodd-Frank Title I implementation. The CCAR and DFAST stress tests, run annually since 2011, are mandated by sections 165(i) and 165(i)(2). The Volcker Rule lives in section 13 of the Bank Holding Company Act (added by section 619 of Dodd-Frank) and in implementing regulations from five agencies (Fed, OCC, FDIC, SEC, CFTC). The derivatives clearing mandate sits in section 723 of Dodd-Frank and in CFTC Part 50 / SEC Reg SBSR. Consumer mortgage rules, including the Ability-to-Repay rule and the Qualified Mortgage definition, live in Title XIV. If you are reading a US bank's regulatory disclosures, you are reading Dodd-Frank.

The Act is also the closest analogue in US law to the post-crisis Basel III package, and the two were negotiated in parallel. The Vice-Chair for Supervision role was created so that the Federal Reserve had a single dedicated senior official representing the US at the Basel Committee and the FSB. Many Dodd-Frank concepts, including the SIFI designation, the resolution plan, the surcharge for systemic importance, the leverage ratio buffer for G-SIBs, and the orderly liquidation framework, map directly to international standards (FSB Key Attributes for resolution, BCBS G-SIB framework). Where Dodd-Frank diverges from Basel, it is usually because the US went further or earlier: the Volcker Rule has no Basel equivalent, the OLA has no Basel equivalent, the CFPB has no Basel equivalent.

For an actuary working in or with US banks, three working facts matter. First, the Federal Reserve's stress-testing process, born of Dodd-Frank, is the central planning event of the US banking calendar; the supervisory severely adverse scenario announced in February each year drives capital plans, dividend approvals, and stress-test reserve disclosures for the next 12 months. Second, the SIFI / tailoring framework determines which prudential and resolution requirements a given bank actually faces, and the boundary lines (Category I to IV) sit at carefully chosen size and complexity thresholds. Third, the Volcker Rule remains a live constraint on the kinds of activities a US-affiliated trading desk can carry on, and its definition of "proprietary trading" includes presumptions and exemptions that take genuine work to navigate.

Step 3

The mental model

Dodd-Frank is enormous, but its sixteen Titles fall into five thematic groups. Picture the Act as five buckets sitting side by side. The first bucket is systemic risk and macroprudential oversight: Titles I, II, and VIII. The second is bank conduct and bank governance: Titles VI and IX. The third is derivatives and market infrastructure: Title VII. The fourth is consumer protection: Title X and the mortgage parts of Title XIV. The fifth is everything else: insurance, securitisation risk retention, executive pay, access, and miscellany.

Three flows of authority cut across the diagram. Prudential authority over large bank holding companies flows from Title I through the Federal Reserve's Regulation YY and through CCAR/DFAST. Resolution authority for a failing systemically important firm flows from Title II to the FDIC as receiver. Consumer authority over financial products flows from Title X to the CFPB. The FSOC sits above all of this as a coordinating body, but with limited direct rule-making authority of its own; it works by recommending action to the Fed, by designating firms, and by designating financial market utilities.

Step 4

The detail

Political context: how the bill happened

The crisis sequence that produced Dodd-Frank ran from spring 2007, when the asset-backed commercial paper market seized up, through the spectacular weekend of 12-14 September 2008 (Lehman bankruptcy, Bank of America acquires Merrill Lynch, Fed bailout of AIG announced the following Tuesday), to the Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP) signed into law on 3 October 2008. Henry Paulson's Treasury, the Federal Reserve under Ben Bernanke, and the FDIC under Sheila Bair improvised over a dozen new lending and guarantee programmes during late 2008 and early 2009. By the time Barack Obama took office in January 2009, the immediate crisis was abating but the architectural questions remained open: why had the Fed not seen this coming, why had the rating agencies signed off on the mortgage securitisations, why was there no statute that allowed the orderly wind-down of a non-bank financial firm, and what should be done about the consumer protection failures that had fed the subprime boom?

The legislative answer was drafted under the political leadership of two figures. Senator Chris Dodd (D-CT) chaired the Senate Banking Committee. Representative Barney Frank (D-MA) chaired the House Financial Services Committee. The Treasury's June 2009 white paper, "Financial Regulatory Reform: A New Foundation", set out the administration's blueprint. The House passed its version of the bill in December 2009; the Senate passed its version in May 2010 (with three Republican votes); the conference committee reconciled the two and the bill was signed on 21 July 2010. The Act was widely understood at the time to be a compromise between an aggressive reform agenda and bank-lobby resistance; consumer-protection advocates considered Title X a major win, while many of the systemic-risk and resolution provisions were left for the regulators to flesh out in implementing rules.

Title I: Financial Stability

Title I is the constitutional core of the new architecture. It creates two new bodies and rewrites the prudential rulebook for the largest banks.

Financial Stability Oversight Council (FSOC)
A council of 10 voting members chaired by the Treasury Secretary, with the Fed Chair, OCC, FDIC, SEC, CFTC, FHFA, NCUA, and CFPB Director as the other agency seats, plus one independent member with insurance expertise appointed by the President. Five non-voting members (state banking, state securities, state insurance, OFR Director, FIO Director) round out the membership. The FSOC's statutory functions are to identify risks to financial stability, to respond to emerging threats, and to promote market discipline. Its most consequential power is to designate nonbank financial companies as systemically important and place them under Federal Reserve supervision.
Office of Financial Research (OFR)
An independent unit within the Treasury, funded by assessments on large bank holding companies and nonbank SIFIs. Provides data, research, and analysis to the FSOC. Publishes the Financial Stability Report annually and a suite of systemic-risk monitors. Its early years were politically embattled; it has been a smaller and less assertive body than the original drafters envisaged.

SIFI designation works in two ways. Bank holding companies with consolidated assets above the threshold are automatically subject to enhanced supervision. The original threshold was $50 billion (set in Section 165(a)); EGRRCPA raised it in 2018 to $250 billion, with the Fed retaining discretion to apply enhanced standards to firms between $100 billion and $250 billion. Nonbank financial companies can be designated by FSOC on a two-thirds vote of the voting membership including the affirmative vote of the Chair, after a multi-stage analysis. Once designated, the firm is supervised by the Federal Reserve under the same Enhanced Prudential Standards as a SIFI bank holding company. Four nonbanks have been designated in the history of the framework: AIG (2013, dedesignated 2017), GE Capital (2013, dedesignated 2016), Prudential Financial (2013, dedesignated 2018), and MetLife (2014, dedesignated by federal court 2016 before appeal was dropped). As of writing, no nonbank financial companies are designated.

Enhanced Prudential Standards (EPS) are the substantive content of Title I supervision. Section 165 directs the Federal Reserve to apply heightened standards to large banking organisations and to nonbank SIFIs. The principal EPS components are:

Risk-based and leverage capital
Higher capital ratios than the baseline Basel III minimums; the supplementary leverage ratio of 3 per cent (5 per cent for G-SIB bank holding companies, 6 per cent for their insured-bank subsidiaries) sits here.
Liquidity standards
The US implementation of LCR and NSFR (Federal Reserve Reg WW) is mandated by Section 165(b)(1)(A)(ii). Internal liquidity stress tests with 30-day, 90-day, and one-year horizons.
Stress testing (CCAR and DFAST)
Section 165(i) requires the Fed to conduct annual supervisory stress tests of large bank holding companies. DFAST (Dodd-Frank Act Stress Tests) is the quantitative results disclosure; CCAR (Comprehensive Capital Analysis and Review) is the qualitative assessment of capital plans. The supervisory severely adverse scenario is published each February and runs over a nine-quarter projection horizon. Banks must also conduct an annual company-run stress test, and (formerly) a mid-cycle stress test.
Risk committee
Public bank holding companies with assets above the threshold must have a board risk committee with at least one risk-management expert, meeting at least quarterly.
Single counterparty credit limits
Section 165(e). Caps a covered company's credit exposure to a single counterparty at 25 per cent of Tier 1 capital, tightening to 15 per cent for major covered companies' exposure to other major counterparties.
Resolution plans (living wills)
Section 165(d). Large bank holding companies and nonbank SIFIs must file a plan describing how the firm could be resolved in bankruptcy without taxpayer support or destabilising the financial system. Filed jointly with the Fed and FDIC. Both agencies have a "credibility" determination; a plan judged not credible can trigger remedial action up to and including a divestiture order.

Title II: Orderly Liquidation Authority

The Orderly Liquidation Authority (OLA) is the resolution regime for systemically important financial firms that cannot be safely resolved under ordinary bankruptcy. It is the US implementation of the FSB Key Attributes for resolution. The architecture has several distinctive features.

The decision to invoke OLA requires a three-step process: a recommendation by the Fed and the FDIC (in the case of a bank holding company) or the Fed and the SEC (in the case of a broker-dealer), then a determination by the Treasury Secretary in consultation with the President, then judicial review by the DC District Court on a one-day timetable. Once OLA is invoked, the FDIC becomes receiver for the failing firm and is granted broad powers to transfer assets and liabilities to a bridge financial institution, to write down equity and unsecured debt, and to repudiate or modify contracts.

Funding comes from an Orderly Liquidation Fund (OLF) within the Treasury, but the OLF is paid back through assessments on financial firms after the fact, not through taxpayer money in the conventional sense. The statute prohibits any losses to taxpayers; if the receivership recovery is insufficient, the FDIC must levy assessments to make up the shortfall.

OLA has never been used. The FDIC's preferred resolution strategy for a US G-SIB, the single point of entry (SPOE) approach, would put only the top-tier holding company into receivership and recapitalise the operating subsidiaries through the conversion of internal total-loss-absorbing capacity (TLAC) into new equity. The result is that the operating banks, broker-dealers, and asset managers continue functioning while the holding company's old equity and debt absorb the losses. The required internal TLAC and external long-term debt requirements for US G-SIBs (Federal Reserve Reg YY subparts I and J) operationalise the SPOE strategy.

Title VI: the Volcker Rule

Named after former Federal Reserve Chair Paul Volcker, the Volcker Rule was introduced into the bill late in the legislative process. Volcker himself argued that allowing federally insured banks to engage in speculative trading was inconsistent with the purpose of deposit insurance. The rule is codified as section 13 of the Bank Holding Company Act and was added by section 619 of Dodd-Frank.

The prohibition
A "banking entity" (essentially, an insured depository, a company controlling one, and any affiliate) may not engage in "proprietary trading". Proprietary trading is defined as engaging as principal in any purchase or sale of a financial instrument for the trading account. The trading account is defined by purpose (short-term resale or arbitrage) or by membership of certain prudential categories (market-risk capital trading book, dealer accounts).
Covered fund restriction
A banking entity may not sponsor or invest in a "covered fund", broadly defined to include hedge funds, private equity funds, and certain other private funds. Two cap exemptions apply for sponsored funds that satisfy organisational and conflict-of-interest conditions: investment in a single fund must not exceed 3 per cent of the fund's value, and aggregate investments in all sponsored funds must not exceed 3 per cent of the banking entity's Tier 1 capital ("the 3 per cent / 3 per cent caps").
Permitted activities (exemptions)
Underwriting, market-making, risk-mitigating hedging, trading in US government and agency securities, trading on behalf of customers, transactions by foreign banking entities solely outside the United States. The market-making exemption is the most consequential and the most contested in practice; it permits inventory that is "reasonably expected near-term demand of clients, customers, or counterparties" (the RENTD principle).

Implementation was contentious. The first Volcker Rule implementing regulation was issued jointly in December 2013 by five agencies (Fed, OCC, FDIC, SEC, CFTC) and was over 950 pages including preamble. The rule was simplified twice in 2019 to 2020 ("Volcker 2.0") under the Trump administration's first term, narrowing the trading-account definition, easing compliance obligations for smaller firms, and relaxing the covered-fund definition. The simplifications were criticised by Volcker himself before his death in December 2019. The rule remains in force in essentially its current form.

Title VII: derivatives

Title VII is the US implementation of the G20 Pittsburgh commitments of September 2009 on the OTC derivatives market. Before Dodd-Frank, swap contracts were almost entirely uncleared, unreported, and bilateral; the AIG crisis was substantially a credit-default-swap problem that no regulator could see in real time. Title VII makes four changes.

First, swap dealers and major swap participants must register with the CFTC (for swaps) or the SEC (for security-based swaps). Registration triggers business-conduct standards, capital and margin requirements, and chief-compliance-officer requirements. Second, swaps subject to a CFTC or SEC clearing determination must be cleared through a derivatives clearing organisation (DCO) or central counterparty. The bulk of the interest-rate-swap market and the index-CDS market is now cleared. Third, cleared swaps subject to a trade-execution determination must trade on a swap execution facility (SEF) or a designated contract market. Fourth, every swap (cleared or uncleared) must be reported to a swap data repository (SDR), providing regulators with a transaction-level dataset.

For uncleared swaps, the Title VII margin rules (issued jointly by the prudential regulators and the CFTC in 2015 and 2016, with phased implementation through 2022) require both initial margin and variation margin to be exchanged between in-scope counterparties. The Section 716 "push-out rule", which would have forced swap dealing activity out of insured depositories, was largely repealed by the FY2015 omnibus appropriations bill (the "Cromnibus") in December 2014; only equity, certain commodity, and uncleared credit-default swaps remain pushed out.

Title X: the CFPB

Title X creates the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau as an independent agency housed within the Federal Reserve System. The CFPB's statutory mission is to ensure that markets for consumer financial products and services are fair, transparent, and competitive. The Bureau consolidates consumer-protection authority previously dispersed across seven federal agencies (Fed, OCC, FDIC, OTS, NCUA, HUD, FTC) into a single agency.

Independence design
The CFPB is funded directly through transfers from the Federal Reserve, not through congressional appropriations, with a statutory cap (12 per cent of Fed operating expenses, around $700 million per year). It is led by a single Director with a fixed five-year term. The funding mechanism survived constitutional challenge in CFSA v. CFPB (Supreme Court, May 2024).
Rule-making authority
The CFPB inherits and administers eighteen "enumerated consumer laws" including the Truth in Lending Act, the Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act, the Fair Credit Reporting Act (subject to FTC overlap), the Equal Credit Opportunity Act, the Home Mortgage Disclosure Act, and the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act. The Bureau also has authority over "unfair, deceptive, or abusive acts or practices" (UDAAP), a category that adds "abusive" to the older "unfair or deceptive" FTC framework.
Supervisory authority
Examination authority over insured depositories with consolidated assets above $10 billion, all non-bank mortgage originators and servicers, all payday lenders, all private student lenders, and "larger participants" in markets the Bureau designates by rule (debt collectors, consumer-reporting agencies, international money-transfer providers, auto-finance companies above thresholds).

The CFPB's enforcement record includes high-profile actions against Wells Fargo (the 2016 sales-practices case and a 2022 follow-up settlement totalling $3.7 billion in penalties and restitution), Bank of America, JPMorgan Chase, Citigroup, Capital One, and a long list of payday and instalment lenders. The Bureau's political history has been turbulent: every administration of the opposite party from its founding has sought to constrain or restructure it, and Seila Law v. CFPB (Supreme Court, June 2020) held that the Director must be removable at will by the President, replacing the original for-cause-only design.

Title IX: investor protection and SEC reform

Title IX covers a range of SEC and securities-market reforms. The headline provisions are the whistleblower programme (Section 922), which authorises the SEC to pay awards of 10 to 30 per cent of monetary sanctions over $1 million to whistleblowers who provide original information leading to a successful enforcement action; the credit rating agency reforms (subtitle C), which created the SEC Office of Credit Ratings, required rating agencies to maintain internal controls, and ended the safe harbour for rating agencies under Securities Act Section 11; and risk retention (Section 941), which requires the sponsor of an asset-backed securitisation to retain at least 5 per cent of the credit risk ("skin in the game"). The "say on pay" advisory shareholder vote on executive compensation also sits in Title IX.

Other Titles

The remaining Titles fill in around the edges. Title III abolished the Office of Thrift Supervision and divided its supervisory portfolio between the OCC (federal thrifts) and the FDIC (state thrifts); the OTS's holding-company supervision moved to the Federal Reserve. Title IV (the "Private Fund Investment Advisers Registration Act") brought hedge fund and private equity advisers within Investment Advisers Act registration. Title V created the Federal Insurance Office (FIO) within Treasury, the first federal voice on insurance regulation in a state-regulated industry. Title XI made changes to the Federal Reserve's emergency lending authority under Section 13(3), tightening eligibility to broadly available facilities rather than rescues of individual firms. Title XII addressed access to mainstream financial services for low- and moderate-income households. Title XIV is the mortgage reform package: the Ability-to-Repay rule and the Qualified Mortgage definition (CFPB Reg Z), HOEPA expansion, escrow requirements for higher-priced mortgages, and the loan officer compensation rule. Titles XV and XVI are miscellaneous provisions including conflict-minerals disclosure (Section 1502) and resource-extraction disclosure (Section 1504).

EGRRCPA 2018: the tailoring amendment

The Economic Growth, Regulatory Relief, and Consumer Protection Act, signed by President Trump on 24 May 2018 (Public Law 115-174), is the only substantive amendment to Dodd-Frank's prudential architecture. It raised the automatic SIFI threshold from $50 billion to $250 billion, granted the Fed discretion over the $100 billion to $250 billion range, and directed the Fed to "tailor" prudential requirements based on risk profile.

The implementing rule, the Tailoring Rule of October 2019, established four categories of bank holding company:

Category I: US G-SIBs
JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, Citigroup, Wells Fargo, Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, Bank of New York Mellon, State Street. Full suite of EPS, daily LCR, annual CCAR with stress capital buffer, annual resolution plan, supplementary leverage ratio surcharge, G-SIB surcharge, internal TLAC, advanced approaches capital.
Category II
Non-G-SIB banks with $700 billion or more in assets, or $75 billion or more in cross-jurisdictional activity. Substantially full EPS, daily LCR, annual stress test, biennial resolution plan, advanced approaches capital. Threshold currently captures no banks since Northern Trust and US Bancorp do not meet the cross-jurisdictional test.
Category III
Banks $250 billion to $700 billion in assets, or $75 billion or more in weighted short-term wholesale funding, non-bank assets, or off-balance-sheet exposure. Reduced LCR (85 per cent), annual stress test, triennial resolution plan, no advanced approaches.
Category IV
Banks $100 billion to $250 billion not meeting any Category III trigger. Biennial supervisory stress test, no company-run stress test, no daily LCR (reduced or none), reduced resolution-plan requirements. This is where SVB sat at the time of its March 2023 failure.

The 2023 stress event and its aftermath

Silicon Valley Bank, a $209 billion Category IV institution, failed on 10 March 2023 after a deposit run triggered by losses on its held-to-maturity Treasury and agency portfolio. Signature Bank, a $110 billion New York commercial bank with crypto-focused deposit clients, failed two days later. First Republic Bank, a $229 billion private-banking franchise, failed on 1 May 2023 and was sold to JPMorgan Chase. The FDIC and Federal Reserve invoked the systemic-risk exception to protect uninsured depositors at SVB and Signature, and the Fed introduced the Bank Term Funding Program to provide one-year liquidity against par-value pledged Treasuries.

The Federal Reserve's April 2023 review (the "Barr Review", led by Vice-Chair for Supervision Michael Barr) identified four contributing factors: idiosyncratic risk management failures at SVB, supervisory weaknesses in pace and forcefulness, regulatory standards that had been tailored downward under EGRRCPA, and the broader impact of social-media-driven runs at digital speed. Subsequent rulemakings have re-tightened parts of the framework: a 2024 proposal would require long-term debt for Category II to IV banks (extending the TLAC concept), and stress-test scenarios have been broadened.

Worked example: a $200 billion US bank holding company under tailoring

Consider a US bank holding company with $200 billion in consolidated assets, $30 billion in cross-jurisdictional activity, and $20 billion in weighted short-term wholesale funding. Where does it sit, and what does it have to do?

Category determination. Below $250 billion in assets, so not Category III by size. Cross-jurisdictional activity $30 billion, below the $75 billion Category III trigger. Short-term wholesale funding $20 billion, below the $75 billion Category III trigger. Off-balance-sheet exposure assumed below $75 billion. Above $100 billion in assets, so within Category IV scope. Outcome: Category IV.

Capital. Standardised approaches capital (Basel III standardised), CET1 minimum 4.5 per cent, Tier 1 6 per cent, total capital 8 per cent, capital conservation buffer 2.5 per cent. Stress capital buffer replaces the static conservation buffer for stress-tested firms. Supplementary leverage ratio applies. No G-SIB surcharge, no countercyclical buffer (currently 0 per cent in the US in any event).

Liquidity. No daily LCR. Reduced LCR (85 per cent of full requirement) or none, depending on weighted short-term wholesale funding. Internal liquidity stress testing on the monthly cycle.

Stress testing. Supervisory DFAST every two years (not annual). Company-run stress test no longer required for Category IV firms. CCAR qualitative assessment still applies.

Resolution. Triennial reduced resolution plan, not the full plan required of larger firms.

Risk governance. Board risk committee with risk-management expert. Single counterparty credit limits apply (25 per cent of Tier 1 to any one counterparty).

Volcker Rule. A banking entity, so subject to the full Volcker Rule. Trading-desk metrics and compliance programme required, though simplified under the 2019 to 2020 amendments.

CFPB. Subject to direct CFPB examination authority (over $10 billion in consolidated assets).

This is roughly the regulatory stack SVB faced at the time of its failure. The post-2023 reforms would substantively tighten the stress-testing and resolution-plan elements for a firm of this size.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Dodd-Frank is principles plus rulemakings, not a single document

The 848-page statute is in many places a delegation. Section 165 says "the Board shall establish prudential standards" and then lists the topics; the substantive rules are in the Federal Reserve's Regulation YY. Section 619 is two pages on proprietary trading; the implementing rule is over 200 pages. When citing Dodd-Frank in regulatory work, you usually want the implementing rule, not the statute itself. The exceptions are provisions that Congress wrote in detail (the 3 per cent fund-investment caps, the $50 billion / $250 billion thresholds, the 5 per cent risk-retention requirement), which sit in the statute itself.

The CFPB's existence has survived multiple existential threats

The Bureau has been the subject of two Supreme Court cases (Seila Law 2020 on at-will removal of the Director, CFSA v. CFPB 2024 on the funding mechanism), a number of failed legislative attempts to defund or restructure it, and waves of acting directors during transitions. It has nonetheless built a body of consumer-protection rulemaking and enforcement that is now embedded in the operations of every US consumer-finance business. Its UDAAP authority is broad enough that even non-bank firms not formally supervised by the Bureau watch its enforcement actions for guidance on what counts as "abusive".

Volcker is about activity, not entity

The Volcker Rule applies to a banking entity's activities anywhere in the world, but its substantive prohibitions can be turned on or off by characterising a trade as market-making, hedging, or customer-facilitation. The result is that the rule operates not by preventing trading but by structuring how trading is documented, governed, and metric-monitored. The "RENTD" framework (reasonably expected near-term demand of clients) is the conceptual centre. For an actuary, the relevance is that the Volcker compliance metrics overlap heavily with market-risk metrics and trading-book risk limits, and the same data feeds support both.

Watch out "SIFI" is used in three different senses in US regulatory practice. First, a bank holding company over the EPS threshold (currently $250 billion automatically, $100 billion to $250 billion at Fed discretion). Second, a nonbank financial company designated by FSOC (currently none). Third, a financial market utility designated by FSOC under Title VIII (eight FMUs designated, including the major derivatives clearinghouses and securities depositories). When someone says "the bank is a SIFI", clarify which of these meanings is in play.

OLA's value lies in the threat, not the use

Title II has never been invoked. But the existence of OLA, together with the section 165(d) living-will requirement and the FDIC's published SPOE strategy, has shaped how US G-SIBs are structured. The "clean holdco" structure (the top-tier holding company holds the equity of the operating subsidiaries and issues TLAC-eligible long-term debt but does not itself conduct material operations) is a direct response to the OLA / SPOE strategy. So OLA's effect on system design is significant even though its operational use has been zero.

Tailoring and the 2023 retrospective

The EGRRCPA tailoring framework was defended at the time as a sensible recognition that not every $100 billion bank looks like a US G-SIB. The 2023 SVB failure became the lens through which that defence is now judged. The supervisory response (Barr Review, long-term debt proposal, stress-test scenario broadening) has re-tightened parts of the regime for Category III and IV firms, but the basic four-category architecture remains in place. The political contest over whether tailoring went too far or just far enough will run for some time yet, and is likely to be revisited in any future financial-stress episode.

Dodd-Frank cannot be read in isolation from international standards

The Vice-Chair for Supervision role was created in 2010 precisely so that there was a designated US official sitting at the Basel Committee, the FSB, and the BIS. The G-SIB surcharge, the TLAC standard, the LCR, the NSFR, and the FRTB are all Basel or FSB standards that the Federal Reserve has implemented through Dodd-Frank machinery. To read US prudential rules without knowing the international parent standards is to mistake transposition for invention; conversely, to read the international standards without knowing how they are tailored in US implementation is to misunderstand what the rules actually require in practice.
