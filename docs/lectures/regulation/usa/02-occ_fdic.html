OCC and FDIC: chartering, supervision, and deposit insurance

The United States is the only major economy that runs a dual banking system, in which a commercial bank may hold either a federal charter granted by the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency or a state charter granted by one of fifty state banking departments. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation insures the deposits of every insured bank in either system, supervises the state-chartered banks that are not Federal Reserve members, and resolves any insured depository institution that fails. Between them, the OCC and FDIC sit at the heart of US prudential supervision alongside the Federal Reserve, and their division of labour defines the regulatory map that every American bank navigates.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC), founded in 1863 as part of the wartime National Currency Act, charters and supervises all national banks, federal savings associations, and federal branches and agencies of foreign banks. It oversees roughly 1,100 institutions which together hold about 70 per cent of US commercial banking assets. The OCC is an independent bureau of the US Treasury; the Comptroller is appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate for a five-year term, and sits ex officio on the FDIC Board. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC), founded in 1933 by the Banking Act (Glass-Steagall) after the bank-run wave of 1929 to 1933 had wiped out about 4,000 banks, insures deposits up to $250,000 per depositor per insured bank per ownership category, supervises roughly 3,000 state-chartered banks that are not members of the Federal Reserve System, and is the statutory receiver for every failed insured depository institution.

The interaction of charter type and Fed membership produces the US "supervisor stack": a national-chartered bank is automatically a Federal Reserve member and FDIC-insured, and is supervised by the OCC for safety and soundness and by the Fed at the holding-company level; a state-chartered bank that opts into Fed membership is supervised by its state regulator and the Fed; a state-chartered bank that does not opt in is supervised by its state regulator and the FDIC. Deposit insurance is universal: every commercial bank in either system pays a quarterly assessment to the Deposit Insurance Fund, which targets a reserve ratio of 2 per cent of insured deposits.

Feynman test Imagine you want to open a commercial bank in the United States. The first question your lawyer asks is "federal or state?". If you pick federal, the OCC writes your charter, sends in examiners, and supervises you for life; you also become a Fed member by operation of law. If you pick state, your state banking department writes the charter, and you then choose whether to apply to be a Federal Reserve member; if you say yes, the Fed becomes your federal supervisor, and if you say no, the FDIC becomes your federal supervisor. Whichever path you pick, you join the FDIC's deposit-insurance pool and pay an assessment every quarter. If you fail, the FDIC drives onto your premises on a Friday evening, transfers your insured deposits to another bank by Monday morning, and pays the difference out of the insurance fund. The OCC is the chartering and supervisory agency; the FDIC is the insurer, the resolution authority, and a supervisor of last resort for the banks that picked the state-only path.

Step 2

Why this matters

The US supervisory architecture is the most fragmented of any major economy. Where the UK has the PRA inside the Bank of England, the euro area has the SSM inside the ECB, and South Africa has the Prudential Authority inside the SARB, the United States has the Federal Reserve, the OCC, the FDIC, the CFPB, the NCUA, and fifty state banking departments, with a coordinating body, the FFIEC, that prescribes the common examination forms. Understanding which agency supervises which bank is the entry point to almost every US banking regulatory question; if you cannot place a bank on the OCC, Fed, FDIC, or state grid, you cannot identify the rulebook that binds it.

The deposit-insurance regime is also peculiarly consequential for US bank funding. With $250,000 of insurance per depositor per bank, US retail deposits are de facto risk-free up to that limit, which suppresses retail funding costs and shapes how banks price savings products. The flip side is that uninsured deposits, the funds above $250,000 held by businesses, corporate cash managers, and high-net-worth households, are exposed to bank failure and have proved unstable in the digital-banking era: more than 90 per cent of Silicon Valley Bank's deposits were uninsured when the bank failed in March 2023, and the run that brought it down took two days to wipe out a $200 billion balance sheet. Knowing the insurance line is knowing the run line.

The FDIC's resolution authority is the most consequential of any global bank supervisor. Where most jurisdictions still resolve failed banks through ad-hoc government interventions or through court-supervised insolvency proceedings, the FDIC has run a continuous receivership programme since 1934. Between 2008 and 2014 it resolved more than 500 banks, almost always over a single weekend, using techniques such as purchase-and-assumption transactions, bridge banks, and loss-share arrangements. The IADI Core Principles for Effective Deposit Insurance, the global template that the UK FSCS and the South African CoDI both implement, were written with the FDIC model in mind.

For an actuary inside a US bank, three institutional facts have direct day-to-day consequences. The first is that examiners can show up at any time; the OCC's resident-examiner programme places teams on-site at the largest banks continuously. The second is that the FDIC assessment is a meaningful line item; a community bank may pay several million dollars a year, and a large bank tens or hundreds of millions, with the rate driven by the bank's CAMELS rating, capital position, and risk metrics. The third is that resolution planning ("living wills") under Title I of Dodd-Frank, jointly administered by the FDIC and the Fed, drives material changes to legal-entity structure, intra-group funding, and operational continuity arrangements for any bank above $250 billion.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture a 2-by-2-by-2 cube. The first axis is charter type (national or state). The second axis is Federal Reserve membership (member or non-member). The third axis is FDIC insurance (insured or uninsured; in practice almost all commercial banks are insured, so this dimension is largely a yes/no for whether the entity is a bank at all). Each cell of the cube identifies the supervisor stack that applies, and almost every prudential question in the US system reduces to placing a bank in the right cell.

Three flows of authority matter. First, primary supervisory authority for each bank flows from its placement in the matrix: examination, enforcement, and rule-making for safety and soundness is the OCC's for national banks, the Fed's for state member banks, and the FDIC's for state non-member banks. Second, holding-company supervision flows independently to the Federal Reserve regardless of the bank-level supervisor. Third, deposit insurance and resolution flow to the FDIC for every insured depository institution, putting the FDIC in a back-up examiner role even for banks whose primary supervisor is the OCC or the Fed; the FDIC retains examination rights so it can manage its insurance-fund exposure.

Step 4

The detail

OCC: history

The OCC is the oldest bank supervisor in the United States. It was created by the National Currency Act of 1863, signed by President Lincoln during the Civil War. The Act had two purposes: to create a uniform national paper currency to replace the patchwork of notes issued by some 1,500 state-chartered banks, and to create a market for federal war bonds by requiring national banks to hold them as backing for their note issue. The newly chartered "national banks" issued banknotes printed by the federal government and backed by US Treasury securities. The Act was repealed and re-enacted in 1864 as the National Bank Act, which remains, with extensive amendment, the OCC's organic statute.

The title "Comptroller of the Currency" reflects the original function: controlling the issuance of national bank notes. The note-issuance role disappeared in 1935 with the Federal Reserve's monopoly on US currency, but the title and the agency persisted because the chartering and supervision role had grown into the OCC's primary work. The OCC survived the Great Depression intact, although thousands of the banks it supervised did not.

Three later statutes reshaped the OCC's perimeter. The Federal Financial Institutions Examination Council (FFIEC) was established in 1979 to coordinate examination practice across the federal banking agencies. The Financial Institutions Reform, Recovery, and Enforcement Act (FIRREA) of 1989 reorganised supervision after the savings-and-loan crisis, abolishing the Federal Home Loan Bank Board and creating the Office of Thrift Supervision (OTS) as a separate supervisor for thrifts. The Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 then abolished the OTS and merged its functions into the OCC, with effect from July 2011; this is why the OCC today supervises federal savings associations as well as national banks.

OCC: governance and reach

The OCC is an independent bureau of the US Treasury. The Comptroller of the Currency is appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate to a five-year term, and may be removed only for cause. The Comptroller is supported by senior deputy comptrollers covering large bank supervision, midsize and community bank supervision, international banking, bank supervision policy, public affairs, and the chief counsel. The agency is funded entirely from assessments on the banks it supervises, with no appropriations from Congress.

National banks
Banks chartered by the OCC under the National Bank Act. Required to include "National Association" or "N.A." in the legal name. Automatically Federal Reserve members. Automatically FDIC-insured. About 1,100 in number as of 2024, but disproportionate by assets: includes JPMorgan Chase Bank N.A., Bank of America N.A., Wells Fargo Bank N.A., Citibank N.A., and U.S. Bank N.A., which together hold the bulk of US commercial-banking assets.
Federal savings associations
Thrifts chartered by the OCC under the Home Owners' Loan Act, supervising the federal half of the dual thrift system. Inherited from the abolished Office of Thrift Supervision in 2011. Concentrated in mortgage lending, with statutory portfolio limits (the "qualified thrift lender" test) requiring at least 65 per cent of assets in housing-related categories.
Federal branches and agencies
US branches of foreign banks operating under a federal licence rather than a state licence. Permitted under the International Banking Act of 1978. About 50 in number, including the US branches of Deutsche Bank, BNP Paribas, Mizuho, and HSBC. State-licensed foreign branches and agencies (the more common configuration) are supervised by the state regulator plus the Fed.

For supervisory purposes the OCC divides its portfolio into three programmes. The large bank supervision programme covers the eight largest national banks and applies a continuous, on-site, examiner-in-residence model with dedicated lead and examiner-in-charge teams for each bank, and specialised functional examiners for capital, market risk, credit risk, operational risk, BSA/AML, and IT. The midsize and community bank supervision programme covers everything else, with examination cycles of 12 or 18 months depending on size and condition, and risk-based scoping of each exam. The international bank supervision programme covers federal branches of foreign banks.

OCC: publications

The OCC produces three main families of public output. The Comptroller's Handbook is the canonical examiner manual, organised by topic (credit underwriting, allowance for credit losses, BSA, internal control, IT) and used directly during examinations. Interpretive letters are responses to specific bank questions about whether an activity is legally permissible for a national bank; they have precedential value and form the de facto common law of permissible national-bank activities. OCC Bulletins announce new policies, guidance, and rule actions, and are the primary channel through which the OCC communicates supervisory expectations between formal rule-makings.

OCC: fintech and crypto

Three OCC actions between 2020 and 2021 reshaped the legal map for crypto and fintech in US banking. Interpretive Letter 1170 (July 2020) confirmed that national banks may provide custody services for cryptoassets on behalf of customers. Interpretive Letter 1172 (September 2020) allowed national banks to hold reserve deposits for stablecoin issuers. Interpretive Letter 1174 (January 2021) permitted national banks to participate in independent node-verification networks and to use stablecoins for payment activities. A subsequent Interpretive Letter 1179 (November 2021) clarified that banks must obtain non-objection from their supervisory office before commencing any of these activities. The OCC has also issued conditional national trust charters to fintech firms (Anchorage Digital, Protego, Paxos), although the fintech charter for non-bank lenders proposed in 2018 has been substantially curtailed by court rulings and political pushback.

FDIC: history

The FDIC was created by the Banking Act of 1933, the legislation usually called Glass-Steagall after its co-sponsors Senator Carter Glass and Representative Henry Steagall. The Act was a direct response to the bank-run wave of 1929 to 1933, during which approximately 4,000 commercial banks failed and depositors lost an estimated $1.3 billion (in 1933 dollars). The original deposit-insurance coverage was $2,500 per depositor when the FDIC opened on 1 January 1934; it was lifted to $5,000 within a few months, and has been raised periodically since.

Coverage limit history
$2,500 in 1934; $5,000 later in 1934; $10,000 in 1950; $15,000 in 1966; $20,000 in 1969; $40,000 in 1974; $100,000 in 1980; $250,000 in 2008 (emergency under the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act, originally temporary), made permanent in 2010 by the Dodd-Frank Act. The 1980 jump from $40,000 to $100,000 was a key facilitator of the savings-and-loan crisis, because it allowed brokered deposits to flow into weak thrifts in $100,000 chunks.

Two later statutes reshaped the FDIC. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation Improvement Act (FDICIA) of 1991 introduced prompt-corrective-action requirements, the "least-cost resolution" rule, and the systemic risk exception that allows the least-cost rule to be overridden. The Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 made the $250,000 limit permanent, abolished the separate Savings Association Insurance Fund, raised the statutory minimum reserve ratio for the Deposit Insurance Fund from 1.15 per cent to 1.35 per cent of insured deposits, gave the FDIC Title II Orderly Liquidation Authority for SIFIs, and required joint FDIC/Fed resolution plans for banks above $250 billion.

FDIC: governance

The FDIC is governed by a five-member Board of Directors. The Chair and Vice Chair are appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate for five-year terms, as is one additional internal member. The remaining two seats are filled ex officio by the Comptroller of the Currency and the Director of the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. No more than three of the five may be from the same political party, which makes the FDIC formally bipartisan. The agency, like the OCC, is funded entirely from its own resources: insurance assessments and investment income on the Deposit Insurance Fund.

Deposit Insurance Fund (DIF)
The FDIC's reserve fund, into which insurance assessments are paid and out of which depositor payouts and resolution losses are met. Statutory minimum reserve ratio of 1.35 per cent of insured deposits (raised from 1.15 per cent by Dodd-Frank in 2010). Long-run target reserve ratio of 2 per cent, set by the FDIC Board. Balance peaked at about $129 billion in late 2022. Reserve ratio fell from 1.27 per cent to about 1.11 per cent after the March 2023 failures of SVB and Signature, with the gap recouped through a 2024 special assessment on uninsured deposits.
Risk-based assessments
Each insured bank pays a quarterly assessment whose rate depends on the bank's CAMELS rating, capital ratios, and risk metrics (asset quality, earnings, liquidity, growth). Rates range from a few basis points for the strongest small banks to over 30 basis points for weak banks. Large banks pay an additional scorecard-based assessment that captures complexity and interconnectedness.
Coverage rules
$250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. Eight categories: Single Accounts; Certain Retirement Accounts; Joint Accounts; Revocable Trust Accounts; Corporation, Partnership, and Unincorporated Association Accounts; Irrevocable Trust Accounts; Employee Benefit Plan Accounts; Government Accounts. A depositor can therefore hold well in excess of $250,000 of insured deposits at a single bank by spreading across categories.

FDIC: supervisory role

The FDIC is the primary federal supervisor for state-chartered banks that are not Federal Reserve members. This is by far the largest population of US banks by count: roughly 3,000 state non-member banks, mostly community banks of less than $1 billion in assets, although some, including the now-failed SVB and Signature, were considerably larger. The FDIC also retains "back-up" examination authority over every insured depository institution, including those whose primary supervisor is the OCC or the Fed, because the FDIC bears the insurance-fund loss if any of them fails. In practice the FDIC participates in OCC and Fed examinations of larger banks rather than running its own parallel exams.

FDIC examinations use the FFIEC's Uniform Financial Institutions Rating System, known by its acronym CAMELS (Capital, Asset quality, Management, Earnings, Liquidity, Sensitivity to market risk). Each of the six components is rated 1 (best) to 5 (worst); the composite rating is a synthesised assessment of overall condition. CAMELS ratings are confidential and never published, but they drive deposit-insurance assessment rates, examination frequency, and the bank's ability to expand or to engage in non-banking activities.

FDIC: resolution role

The Federal Deposit Insurance Act gives the FDIC the statutory authority to act as receiver for every insured depository institution that fails. The FDIC has run this programme continuously since 1934 and has developed a distinctive operational model: a failing bank is closed by its chartering authority (the OCC or the state) on a Friday after the close of business, the FDIC is appointed receiver immediately, and the bank reopens under new ownership or under FDIC control on the following Monday morning, with insured depositors paid in full. Between 2008 and 2014 the FDIC resolved 525 banks in this way.

FDICIA introduced the least-cost resolution requirement: the FDIC must choose the resolution method that minimises the loss to the Deposit Insurance Fund, taking into account all costs and recoveries on a present-value basis. The main methods, in rough order of frequency, are:

Purchase and Assumption (P&A)
An acquiring bank purchases the failed bank's assets and assumes its insured deposits (and often some or all uninsured deposits). The FDIC covers any shortfall between the assets transferred and the liabilities assumed. The most common method; preserves continuity of banking services and avoids depositor payouts. Often paired with a loss-share agreement under which the FDIC absorbs a share of credit losses on the transferred loan portfolio for a defined period.
Bridge bank
The FDIC charters a temporary bank (a "bridge depository institution") to hold the failed bank's operations while a permanent buyer is sought. Used when the FDIC cannot find a buyer over the failure weekend. Limited to two years (extendable). Used in 2023 for Silicon Valley Bridge Bank N.A. and Signature Bridge Bank N.A.
Deposit payoff
The FDIC pays insured depositors directly out of the DIF, up to the $250,000 limit; uninsured depositors and other creditors share in the receivership recoveries. Used when no buyer can be found and a bridge bank is not warranted. Less common because P&A typically produces a lower DIF loss.
Open bank assistance
The FDIC provides financial assistance to a failing bank to keep it open. Severely restricted by FDICIA except in narrow circumstances; rarely used since the 1990s because of the moral-hazard objection.

The systemic risk exception

FDICIA's least-cost rule can be overridden only by invoking the systemic risk exception. This requires the concurrence of two-thirds of the FDIC Board, two-thirds of the Federal Reserve Board, and the Treasury Secretary in consultation with the President. When invoked, it allows the FDIC to take actions that would otherwise breach least-cost, most notably extending insurance to deposits above the $250,000 limit. The exception has been used three times: in 2008 for the FDIC's Temporary Liquidity Guarantee Program (extending insurance to non-interest-bearing transaction accounts and guaranteeing certain bank debt during the crisis); in March 2023 to allow full deposit protection at Silicon Valley Bank; and in the same week in March 2023 for Signature Bank.

The 2023 invocations are worth understanding in detail because they show the mechanism at work. SVB failed on Friday 10 March 2023 after a two-day deposit run; more than $40 billion left the bank in a single day. The FDIC was appointed receiver and immediately transferred operations to Silicon Valley Bridge Bank N.A. Over the weekend, the FDIC Board, the Fed Board, and Treasury Secretary Yellen (in consultation with the President) invoked the systemic risk exception, allowing the FDIC to fully protect all depositors at SVB regardless of the $250,000 cap. The same exception was invoked for Signature Bank, which failed on the Sunday. The reasoning was that allowing uninsured depositors to take losses would trigger contagion to other regional banks with high uninsured-deposit ratios. First Republic Bank, which suffered a slower run over the following six weeks, was ultimately resolved on 1 May 2023 through a P&A transaction in which JPMorgan Chase acquired most of the bank without invocation of the systemic risk exception; the JPM bid was sufficiently strong to satisfy least-cost.

Orderly Liquidation Authority (Title II)

Title II of the Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 created a separate, parallel resolution regime for "covered financial companies", defined as bank holding companies and non-bank SIFIs whose failure would pose a systemic risk. The intent was to provide an alternative to the bankruptcy code for large interconnected firms whose failure under ordinary Chapter 11 would risk a Lehman-style contagion. Orderly Liquidation Authority (OLA) designates the FDIC as receiver and gives it powers similar to those it holds for insured depositary institutions: it can transfer assets and liabilities, charter a bridge institution, and impose losses on shareholders and unsecured creditors. The Treasury may extend liquidity to the FDIC-controlled bridge through the Orderly Liquidation Fund, with any net cost recovered through risk-based assessments on the surviving financial industry.

OLA has never been invoked. Title I of the same Act requires SIFIs to maintain "living wills" demonstrating that they could be resolved without recourse to OLA, through ordinary bankruptcy. The expectation is that OLA is the political "break glass" backstop, with living-will-supported bankruptcy as the primary path. The 2023 SVB resolution did not invoke OLA because SVB itself was the insured depository institution; the holding company, SVB Financial Group, filed for ordinary Chapter 11 bankruptcy.

The FFIEC and the federal coordinating architecture

The Federal Financial Institutions Examination Council (FFIEC), established by statute in 1979, is the coordinating body for the federal banking and credit-union supervisors. Its members are the OCC, the Federal Reserve Board, the FDIC, the National Credit Union Administration (NCUA), the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), and a state liaison committee. The FFIEC prescribes uniform principles, standards, and report forms for the federal examination of financial institutions; the most consequential outputs are the CAMELS rating system, the Uniform Bank Performance Report (UBPR), and the Call Report (Form FFIEC 031/041) which every insured bank files quarterly. The FFIEC also operates the Central Data Repository through which Call Reports are submitted, and runs the Home Mortgage Disclosure Act (HMDA) data programme.

State banking departments and the dual banking system

Every US state operates its own banking department which charters and supervises state-chartered banks. State chartering predates federal chartering by half a century and has remained the more common path: of roughly 4,500 US commercial banks, about 70 per cent are state-chartered. State regulators include the New York Department of Financial Services (NYDFS), the California Department of Financial Protection and Innovation (DFPI), the Texas Department of Banking, and the Florida Office of Financial Regulation, each with examination, enforcement, and rule-making authority over its state-chartered banks. The Conference of State Bank Supervisors (CSBS) is the trade association for state regulators and operates the Nationwide Multistate Licensing System (NMLS) that coordinates non-bank financial-services licensing.

The dual banking system is the term for this parallel federal and state chartering structure. The historic argument in favour is regulatory diversity: a bank that finds one regulator unsuitable can convert to the other charter, and competition between regulators is said to keep each honest. The historic argument against is regulatory arbitrage: a bank can shop for the lightest-touch regulator, and competing for charters drives a race to the bottom. Both descriptions contain some truth; charter conversions in either direction happen regularly, often after a dispute with the existing primary supervisor.

Worked example: placing two banks on the supervisor grid

Bank A: JPMorgan Chase Bank, National Association. National charter, granted by the OCC under the National Bank Act. Because national banks are Federal Reserve members by operation of law, JPMorgan Chase Bank N.A. is a Fed member. As an insured depository institution, it is FDIC-insured. The primary federal prudential supervisor is therefore the OCC, with the Federal Reserve supervising JPMorgan Chase & Co., the parent bank holding company, and the FDIC retaining back-up examination authority and resolution authority. CFPB has consumer-protection rule-writing authority because total assets exceed $10 billion. The supervisor stack is OCC + Fed (holdco) + FDIC (back-up + resolution) + CFPB (consumer). State banking departments do not feature.

Bank B: Silicon Valley Bank (pre-failure). California state charter, granted by the California DFPI. SVB opted into Federal Reserve membership at incorporation, so it was a state member bank. Insured depository institution. The primary federal prudential supervisor was therefore the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco, on behalf of the Federal Reserve Board, with the California DFPI as the state supervisor, and the FDIC as insurer and back-up examiner. CFPB had consumer-protection rule-writing authority once assets exceeded $10 billion. The supervisor stack was state DFPI + Fed (bank and holdco) + FDIC (back-up + resolution) + CFPB. After SVB's failure on 10 March 2023, the FDIC was appointed receiver and operationalised through Silicon Valley Bridge Bank N.A., a national-charter bridge bank chartered by the OCC for that purpose.

The contrast illustrates the matrix: SVB was state-chartered and Fed-member, JPMorgan Chase Bank is national-chartered and (automatically) Fed-member. Both are FDIC-insured. Both have Federal Reserve supervision at the holding-company level. The bank-level federal supervisor differs because the bank-level federal supervisor follows the charter.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Regulatory competition or regulatory arbitrage

The dual banking system is the standing controversy of US prudential supervision. The positive view, articulated by generations of state regulators and by the Conference of State Bank Supervisors, is that choice between federal and state charters keeps each regulator honest: a state regulator that examines too lightly will lose banks to the OCC, and an OCC that examines too heavily will lose banks to the states. The negative view, articulated by Fed and FDIC officials at various times, is that banks shop for the lightest-touch regulator and that the competition for assessment revenue drives convergence at the lower end of supervisory rigour. Both descriptions contain truth; what matters in practice is that conversions in either direction are common and are often the proximate event before a bank fails. Several mid-2000s failed thrifts had converted from FDIC supervision to OTS supervision in the years before failure.

The deposit-insurance line is the bank-run line

Until March 2023 the practitioner view was that $250,000 was generous enough to cover almost all retail depositors, who were the historical run risk. The SVB run demonstrated that the relevant run risk in 2023 is uninsured corporate cash held in concentrated commercial banks, that this cash moves at fibre-optic speed when a Twitter cycle starts, and that the $250,000 limit is irrelevant to that segment. The systemic risk exception was the existing mechanism to lift the limit when needed; it worked, but its existence is also moral hazard since uninsured depositors at any large bank now reasonably believe they will be made whole in a true crisis. The forward question for US deposit-insurance policy is whether to formalise some version of expanded coverage for business transaction accounts (the Treasury and FDIC have studied this) or to keep the implicit-bailout system that operated in 2023.

The FDIC's resolution toolkit is the global template

The IADI Core Principles for Effective Deposit Insurance, published by the International Association of Deposit Insurers and endorsed by the FSB, were written with the FDIC operating model in mind. The UK FSCS, the EU Deposit Guarantee Scheme Directive, and the South African Corporation for Deposit Insurance (CoDI) are all variants of the FDIC template, with national modifications. The FDIC's distinctive contribution is operational: the Friday-close, Monday-open resolution choreography is unique to the US, and other jurisdictions have been slow to match it because they lack the FDIC's standing receivership infrastructure.

Watch out The OCC, the FDIC, and the Fed are independent agencies and can and do disagree publicly on rule-making. The 2010 to 2024 cycle has produced several high-profile splits, including over the long-term debt rule for regional banks, the proposed Basel III endgame rule, and the 2024 climate financial-risk guidance. The fact that a rule appears in one agency's bulletin does not mean it is the consensus federal position; check whether the other two agencies have issued matching guidance and what their public statements say. The same applies in reverse for state-level supervisory positions, which can be substantially tougher than federal positions (NYDFS is the clearest example, particularly on cyber, BSA/AML, and consumer protection).

The FFIEC creates the only consistency that exists

For a bank that operates across multiple states with both national and state subsidiaries, the FFIEC's uniform examination forms are the single most useful thing the federal architecture produces. The Call Report is filed by every insured bank in the same format, the CAMELS rating uses the same six-component scheme, and the UBPR shows every bank in the same peer-group format. Without the FFIEC there would be no apples-to-apples comparison across regulators; with it, a bank can benchmark itself against any peer regardless of who supervises it.

The 2023 failures will reshape regional bank rules through this decade

The Fed's own April 2023 review of SVB blamed Federal Reserve supervision for being too slow on identified weaknesses, but it also blamed the tailoring rules adopted under the 2018 Economic Growth, Regulatory Relief, and Consumer Protection Act for relieving banks in the $100 billion to $250 billion range from the strict liquidity and resolution-planning rules that applied above $250 billion. The FDIC's parallel reviews of SVB and Signature reached similar conclusions for resolution planning and uninsured deposit concentration. Expect long-term debt requirements, more demanding resolution plans, and tougher uninsured-deposit liquidity assumptions to roll down into the regional bank tier over the second half of this decade. For credit-risk models and capital-planning teams in any US bank with more than $50 billion in assets, this is a known live front in the rule-making cycle.

Read the OCC's interpretive letters when an activity is novel

The OCC's interpretive letters are not statutes or regulations and have no formal precedential force, but they function as the de facto common law of permissible national-bank activities. If you are at a national bank and considering a new line of business (crypto custody, stablecoin reserves, embedded payments), the right starting point is the OCC's interpretive-letter library, not the National Bank Act itself. The Bank Activities database on the OCC website indexes letters by topic; reading the relevant letters before writing a legal-permissibility memorandum is standard practice in national-bank legal departments.

  • Lecture 29: Dodd-Frank: the post-crisis statute that gave the FDIC Title II resolution authority, required living wills, created the CFPB, and reorganised consolidated supervision of US financial firms.
  • Lecture 30: US GAAP and CECL: the accounting framework under which US banks report, and the Current Expected Credit Loss model that is the US analogue of IFRS 9.
  • Lecture 27: Federal Reserve: the third leg of the US federal banking-supervision stool, with primary supervisory authority for state Fed-member banks and consolidated authority over every bank and savings-and-loan holding company.

Source material

  • OCC, "About the OCC": occ.treas.gov/about. Authoritative agency description, mandate, and history.
  • FDIC Strategic Plan: fdic.gov/about/strategic. FDIC's own statement of mandate, deposit-insurance scope, and resolution authority.
  • National Bank Act, 12 U.S.C. ch. 2 (the OCC's organic statute).
  • Federal Deposit Insurance Act, 12 U.S.C. ch. 16 (the FDIC's organic statute, including resolution and systemic-risk-exception provisions).

Cross-references

  • For the international supervisory framework that the OCC and FDIC implement in US rules, see lecture BIS and BCBS; US rule-making transposes the BCBS standards through joint OCC/Fed/FDIC final rules.
  • For deposit-insurance as a global concept and the IADI Core Principles that the FDIC informs, see the IADI section of BIS and BCBS.
  • For comparison with the UK deposit-insurance scheme administered by the Bank of England, see docs/wiki/regulation/uk/; the FSCS coverage limit of GBP 85,000 is the UK analogue of the FDIC's $250,000.
  • For the South African Corporation for Deposit Insurance (CoDI), launched 2024 and modelled on the FDIC, see docs/wiki/regulation/south_africa/.

Further reading

  • FDIC, Crisis and Response: An FDIC History, 2008-2013, published 2017. The FDIC's own account of the financial-crisis resolutions, including detail on least-cost analysis and the systemic-risk-exception invocations.
  • FDIC, Managing the Crisis: The FDIC and RTC Experience 1980-1994, published 1998. The savings-and-loan-crisis history, including the standard reference on bridge banks and loss-share agreements.
  • Federal Reserve Board, Review of the Federal Reserve's Supervision and Regulation of Silicon Valley Bank, April 2023. The Fed's own post-mortem of SVB; concise and unusually candid.
  • FDIC, FDIC's Supervision of Signature Bank, April 2023. The FDIC's parallel post-mortem.
  • Conference of State Bank Supervisors, The CSBS Reentry Briefing Book. Useful primer on the state-regulator side of the dual banking system.
