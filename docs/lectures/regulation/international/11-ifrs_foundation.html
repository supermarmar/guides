IFRS Foundation: the standard-setter for accounting

The IFRS Foundation is a not-for-profit, public-interest body based in London that governs two of the most consequential standard-setting boards in global finance: the International Accounting Standards Board, which writes the IFRS Accounting Standards used in over 140 jurisdictions, and the International Sustainability Standards Board, which now sets the disclosure framework for climate-related financial risks. For a bank, the Foundation's output determines what counts as a loss, when it must be recognised, and how the resulting numbers reach the balance sheet that the Basel framework then taxes.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The IFRS Foundation is a not-for-profit, public-interest organisation headquartered at Canary Wharf in London. It oversees the International Accounting Standards Board (IASB), which sets IFRS Accounting Standards, and the International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB), which sets IFRS Sustainability Disclosure Standards. The Foundation was created in 2001 from the reform of the older International Accounting Standards Committee (IASC, 1973). The ISSB was added at COP26 in November 2021 in response to investor demand for a global baseline for climate and sustainability disclosure. The Foundation is funded by voluntary contributions from member jurisdictions, by licensing revenue from the Big Four accounting firms, and by sales of its intellectual property; total revenue was around 67.6 million pounds in 2024.

IFRS Accounting Standards are required or permitted in more than 140 jurisdictions, including the EU, the UK, South Africa, Canada, Australia, Brazil, and most of Asia outside Japan, India, and China. The United States does not use IFRS; it uses US GAAP, set by the Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB), which is the subject of a separate lecture. Japan permits IFRS alongside Japanese GAAP, and India operates IND-AS, a converged but not identical variant. The IFRS Foundation has no power to enforce its standards. Adoption depends on each jurisdiction writing the standards into local law, sometimes through an explicit "endorsement" mechanism such as the EU's that can delay or modify what is adopted.

Feynman test Imagine a small London office full of accountants and lawyers writing a giant rulebook on how every company in the world should describe what it owns and what it owes. The rulebook has no legal force whatsoever. But 140 governments have decided, voluntarily and one by one, that their listed companies must follow it. So when Barclays publishes a profit number, when Standard Bank publishes an impairment charge, when Banco Santander publishes a balance sheet, all three are obeying a book written by a body that cannot fine them, cannot sue them, and cannot inspect them. The reason it works is that investors will not buy shares in companies whose accounts they cannot understand, and a single rulebook is the only way to make accounts comparable across borders. The IFRS Foundation is the BCBS of accounting: a writer of recommendations that the world treats as law.
What is beyond the source The source wiki describes the Foundation, its boards, the Monitoring Board, the Trustees, and the funding structure, with a substantial digression on IAS 39 and IFRS 9. This lecture adds: a sharper account of the standard-setting due process (research, discussion paper, exposure draft, deliberation, post-implementation review); the role of the EU "endorsement" mechanism and equivalent UK board; the practical mechanics of the ISSB's first two standards (IFRS S1 and S2) and their 2024 effective date; and the practitioner's view of why ad-hoc relief from IFRS standards is so hard to obtain even during crises like COVID.
Step 2

Why this matters

Accounting is a regulatory choice and the choice has massive consequences. A bank's reported capital depends entirely on the accounting standards it follows because regulatory capital starts from accounting equity and works back. Change the rule on when a loan loss must be recognised and you change CET1 capital, the leverage ratio denominator, the impairment line on the income statement, and ultimately the dividend the bank can pay. IFRS 9, the IASB's most consequential standard for banks, replaced the old incurred-loss model of IAS 39 with an expected-loss model. The change shifted billions of pounds of provisions onto bank balance sheets at adoption in January 2018, and turned every credit-risk model in every bank into an accounting input.

Cross-border comparability is the second reason the Foundation matters. A global investor cannot compare HSBC to JPMorgan if HSBC reports under IFRS and JPMorgan under US GAAP unless the investor can mentally translate between them. IFRS Accounting Standards were designed to make this translation unnecessary for the 140 jurisdictions that have adopted them. The US has not adopted; the two boards (IASB and FASB) ran a formal convergence project from 2002 to about 2016 that closed most but not all of the gaps, and the political appetite to close the rest has weakened. The IFRS-US GAAP divide is now a permanent feature of the global accounting landscape.

The third reason is sustainability. The ISSB was created in November 2021 at COP26 in response to a proliferation of voluntary sustainability frameworks (TCFD, SASB, CDSB, Integrated Reporting) that produced incomparable disclosures. The IFRS Foundation absorbed several of these frameworks and gave them a single standard-setter. IFRS S1 (general sustainability disclosure) and IFRS S2 (climate disclosure) became effective for reporting periods beginning on or after 1 January 2024, with phased adoption in most adopting jurisdictions. For a bank, this matters because climate-related financial risk is now a disclosure obligation, not a voluntary commitment, and the disclosure framework is set by the same body that sets the accounting framework. The two now sit under one roof for a reason.

For an actuary working inside a bank, three institutional facts have direct day-to-day consequences. First, IFRS standards take five to ten years from research project to effective date, so models you build today will be judged against accounting requirements you can already read in draft form. Second, the IASB resists ad-hoc relief during crises (it did not suspend IFRS 9 for COVID, for instance, although it published transition guidance), so a model that fails under stress is unlikely to be helped by an accounting carve-out. Third, the IFRS-Basel relationship is intentional and overlapping: the Basel framework uses IFRS-equivalent definitions for many concepts (default, regulatory expected loss versus accounting ECL, fair value), and a model built only against one of them will fail audit against the other.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture four tiers stacked vertically. At the top, the Monitoring Board: a small group of capital-markets regulators whose job is to oversee the Trustees and link them to public authorities. Below it, the Trustees: 22 individuals appointed for renewable three-year terms who govern the Foundation, set strategy, raise the funding, and appoint board members. Below them, the two standard-setting boards (IASB and ISSB), each independent and each producing its own family of standards. At the operational level, the IFRS Interpretations Committee, which resolves narrow application questions raised by preparers and auditors.

Three flows of authority matter. Authority flows down from the Monitoring Board to the Trustees (Trustee appointments must be approved) and from the Trustees to the boards (board members are appointed by Trustees, who can also decline reappointment). Technical work flows the other way: the boards develop standards through due process, and the Interpretations Committee feeds narrow application questions back into the boards' work. And funding flows in from outside: voluntary contributions from member jurisdictions, licensing fees from the Big Four accounting firms (Deloitte, EY, KPMG, PwC), and earned revenue from the sale and licensing of the standards themselves.

Step 4

The detail

History: from IASC to IFRS Foundation to ISSB

The story begins in 1973 with the International Accounting Standards Committee (IASC), founded in London by accountancy bodies from Australia, Canada, France, Germany, Japan, Mexico, the Netherlands, the United Kingdom and Ireland, and the United States. The IASC produced International Accounting Standards (IAS series) for nearly three decades. By the late 1990s the IASC was widely seen as under-resourced, governed by part-time committees with no fiduciary structure, and producing standards that had been criticised for excessive optionality. A reform initiated by the Strategy Working Party in 1998 to 2000 culminated in the creation of the IFRS Foundation (initially called the International Accounting Standards Committee Foundation) on 1 April 2001, with the IASB as its full-time professional standard-setting board.

The first decade after the reform was transformative. The European Union mandated IFRS for all listed companies from 1 January 2005, which immediately made IFRS the accounting language of around 7,000 European listed companies. Australia, South Africa, and most of the rest of the Commonwealth followed. The IASB renamed the older standards still in force "IAS" and prefixed the new ones "IFRS"; both labels coexist today (IAS 1, IAS 12, and IAS 39 are still live alongside IFRS 9, IFRS 15, and IFRS 17).

The Monitoring Board was added in January 2009 to provide a link to public authorities. The financial crisis had exposed a perceived gap in the Foundation's accountability: the Trustees governed themselves, with no formal supervisory body above them. The Monitoring Board fills that gap. It does not write standards and does not intervene in technical decisions; its remit is to confirm that the Trustees are discharging their duties and to approve Trustee appointments.

The most recent structural change was the formation of the International Sustainability Standards Board on 3 November 2021, announced by the Trustees at COP26 in Glasgow. The ISSB consolidated several voluntary frameworks (the Climate Disclosure Standards Board, the Sustainability Accounting Standards Board, the integrated reporting work of the Value Reporting Foundation, and the World Economic Forum's Stakeholder Capitalism Metrics) under the IFRS Foundation umbrella. Its first two standards, IFRS S1 and IFRS S2, were published in June 2023 and became effective for reporting periods beginning on or after 1 January 2024.

The Monitoring Board

The Monitoring Board provides the formal link between the Trustees and public authorities. Its members are securities regulators and capital-markets supervisors from the major IFRS-adopting jurisdictions. The composition was designed to ensure that the public authorities responsible for enforcing financial-reporting requirements in their jurisdictions can hold the Foundation to account at one remove from the standard-setting boards themselves.

Monitoring Board membership
The Board of IOSCO; the IOSCO Growth and Emerging Markets Committee; the Financial Services Agency of Japan; the European Commission; the US Securities and Exchange Commission; the Comissao de Valores Mobiliarios of Brazil; the Financial Services Commission of Korea; the Ministry of Finance of the People's Republic of China; and the UK Financial Conduct Authority. The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision attends as an observer.
Monitoring Board duties
Confirm the Trustees discharge their duties under the IFRS Foundation Constitution. Approve the appointment and reappointment of Trustees. Meet the Trustees at least once a year. Provide a channel for public authorities to raise concerns with the Foundation.

The Basel Committee's observer status is significant. It is the institutional acknowledgement that bank prudential regulation depends on the accounting framework, and that the BCBS therefore has a direct interest in IFRS standard-setting even though it has no vote on Foundation matters.

The Trustees

The 22 Trustees govern the Foundation. They are appointed by the existing Trustees for renewable three-year terms, subject to Monitoring Board approval, and are drawn from a geographically balanced pool of professional backgrounds: former accounting-firm partners, retired regulators, academics, and senior preparers and users of financial statements. The Constitution requires geographical diversity (broadly Europe, the Americas, Asia and Oceania, and Africa and Middle East) and a mix of professional perspectives.

The Trustees have five core responsibilities: overseeing the Foundation's governance and strategy; overseeing the due process under which standards are made; maintaining the Constitution and the Due Process Handbook; appointing members of the IASB, ISSB, IFRS Interpretations Committee, and advisory bodies; and securing the funding. Crucially, the Trustees are not involved in technical decisions; this is the responsibility of the standard-setting boards. The separation between governance (Trustees) and technical work (boards) is the central design feature of the post-2001 reform and is what gives IFRS standards their professional credibility.

The IASB

The International Accounting Standards Board is a full-time professional board of 14 members appointed by the Trustees for renewable five-year terms (with a maximum of ten years for the chair and vice-chair, eight years for ordinary members). Members are required to have practical experience as preparers, auditors, users, or academic researchers of financial reports. Geographical balance is required by the Constitution.

The IASB owns the development and publication of IFRS Accounting Standards, including the IFRS for SMEs Accounting Standard (a simplified standard for small and medium-sized companies). It also approves Interpretations developed by the IFRS Interpretations Committee. Its decisions are technical; they are not subject to Trustee or Monitoring Board override on the substance of standards.

IFRS 9 Financial Instruments
Effective 1 January 2018. Classifies financial assets into three measurement categories (amortised cost, FVOCI, FVTPL), governs hedge accounting, and introduces the expected credit loss (ECL) model for impairment. Replaced IAS 39's incurred-loss model. The most consequential IFRS standard for banks. Covered in detail in lecture 12.
IFRS 15 Revenue from Contracts with Customers
Effective 1 January 2018. Five-step model for revenue recognition. Replaced IAS 18 and IAS 11. Limited direct impact on banks because most banking income is interest income (governed by IFRS 9) rather than revenue from contracts, but relevant for fee income, custody fees, and investment-banking advisory revenue.
IFRS 16 Leases
Effective 1 January 2019. Requires lessees to recognise nearly all leases on the balance sheet as a right-of-use asset and a lease liability. Replaced IAS 17. Material impact on banks as lessees (branches, office buildings, IT equipment); negligible impact on lessor accounting.
IFRS 17 Insurance Contracts
Effective 1 January 2023. Replaced IFRS 4 (a transitional standard that allowed varied national practice). Standardises measurement of insurance liabilities using a general measurement model, a variable-fee approach for participating contracts, and a premium-allocation approach for short-duration contracts. Hugely consequential for bancassurance groups and any bank with insurance subsidiaries.
IAS 1 Presentation of Financial Statements
Sets out the structure and content of financial statements. Effective in its current form for many years and amended frequently. The "top-of-stack" standard that defines what a complete set of IFRS financial statements looks like. Being replaced in stages by IFRS 18 (Presentation and Disclosure in Financial Statements), effective 1 January 2027.
IAS 39 Financial Instruments: Recognition and Measurement
The predecessor to IFRS 9. Largely superseded but partially retained: entities may elect to continue applying IAS 39's hedge-accounting requirements rather than IFRS 9's, pending completion of the IASB's separate macro-hedge accounting project. The election is a relic of the IASB's struggle to write hedge-accounting rules that European banks could accept.

The ISSB

The International Sustainability Standards Board was established at COP26 in Glasgow in November 2021 in response to investor demand for a single global baseline of sustainability disclosure. The proliferation of voluntary frameworks (TCFD, SASB, CDSB, integrated reporting) had produced incomparable disclosures even among companies that claimed to follow "international best practice". The G7, G20, IOSCO, FSB, and finance ministers from over 40 jurisdictions backed the creation of a single standard-setter. The IFRS Foundation absorbed several of the voluntary bodies into the new board.

The ISSB has 14 members appointed by the Trustees on the same terms as IASB members. Its mandate is to develop IFRS Sustainability Disclosure Standards that provide investors with a global baseline of decision-useful sustainability information. The "investor focus" is important: the ISSB does not write standards for the general public, regulators, or stakeholders other than investors. Its standards are designed to feed the same audience that consumes IFRS Accounting Standards.

IFRS S1 General Requirements for Disclosure of Sustainability-related Financial Information
Effective 1 January 2024. Sets the general framework for sustainability-related financial disclosure: governance, strategy, risk management, metrics and targets. Aligned to the four-pillar TCFD structure. Connected to but separate from financial-statement disclosures, with consistency requirements where relevant.
IFRS S2 Climate-related Disclosures
Effective 1 January 2024. The first topic-specific ISSB standard. Requires disclosure of climate-related risks and opportunities, transition plans, Scope 1, 2, and 3 greenhouse-gas emissions, and climate-related scenario analysis. Replaces TCFD reporting in jurisdictions that have adopted IFRS S2 (the TCFD itself was wound up in October 2023 with monitoring of its recommendations passed to the ISSB).

Both standards include a phased-adoption mechanism. Jurisdictions can permit companies to disclose only climate-related information (under IFRS S2) in the first reporting period and to defer Scope 3 emissions disclosure for an additional year. The phasing was designed to bring the standards in quickly while letting preparers build data systems.

Standard-setting due process

The Foundation's reputation rests on the rigour and transparency of its due process. The Due Process Handbook, maintained by the Trustees and overseen by the Due Process Oversight Committee, sets out the steps a standard must pass through before it can be issued. The process for a major new IFRS Accounting Standard typically takes five to ten years from research project to effective date.

  1. Agenda consultation. Every five years the IASB consults publicly on its work programme. The output is a ranked list of topics to be added to the active agenda.
  2. Research project. A multi-year phase during which staff and board members analyse the issue, consult academics and practitioners, and decide whether a new standard is justified.
  3. Discussion paper. An optional intermediate step setting out the issues and the board's preliminary thinking, with no specific draft text. Public comment period typically 120 days.
  4. Exposure draft. The first concrete proposal for a new standard or major amendment. Public comment period of at least 90 days (often longer for major standards; IFRS 9 had several exposure drafts over six years). Fieldwork and effects analysis are conducted in parallel.
  5. Deliberation of comments. The board reviews every comment letter (often hundreds) in public sessions, voting on each substantive issue. Each meeting is webcast and recorded.
  6. Re-exposure if needed. If the board makes substantial changes to an exposure draft, it must consider whether to re-expose. Re-exposure is common for major standards (IFRS 9 was re-exposed; IFRS 17 was re-exposed several times).
  7. Final standard. Published with a Basis for Conclusions (a non-binding explanation of why the board made each decision) and effective-date provisions, typically two to three years after publication to allow for implementation.
  8. Post-implementation review (PIR). Three to five years after the effective date, the IASB reviews how the standard is working in practice. The PIR can lead to clarifications, narrow-scope amendments, or rarely a full reconsideration.

The ISSB follows the same due process. IFRS S1 and S2 went through a 120-day exposure draft in 2022, with around 1,400 comment letters received, before the standards were finalised in June 2023.

Funding

The Foundation is funded from two sources: contributed revenue and earned revenue. In the year ended 31 December 2024 total revenue was 67.6 million pounds, of which contributed revenue was 61 per cent and earned revenue was 39 per cent.

Contributed revenue consists of voluntary contributions from member jurisdictions, seed funding for the ISSB from Canada, China, Germany, and Japan, philanthropic grants, and contributions from companies. Most jurisdictions have established either a levy on listed companies or a system of publicly supported financing; the funding mechanisms differ widely. The UK, for instance, funds its contribution through the FRC (Financial Reporting Council), which in turn levies listed companies. Deloitte, EY, KPMG, and PwC have separate licensing agreements with the Foundation for which they pay annual fees on top of their voluntary contributions.

Earned revenue is dominated by licensing of intellectual property. Companies and organisations that wish to reproduce, translate, or use the standards commercially (in textbooks, software systems, training materials, or proprietary tools) must hold a licence. The Foundation aggressively protects the copyright of its standards; this is one reason that paraphrases and unofficial summaries circulate while the original text is paywalled.

Jurisdictional adoption: who uses IFRS and who does not

IFRS Accounting Standards are required for domestic listed companies in over 140 jurisdictions. The largest non-adopters are the United States, Japan, India, and China, each for different reasons.

European Union
All listed companies have been required to use IFRS for consolidated accounts since 1 January 2005, under the IAS Regulation. The EU operates an "endorsement" mechanism: the European Commission, advised by EFRAG (European Financial Reporting Advisory Group) and ARC (Accounting Regulatory Committee), formally adopts each standard into EU law. Endorsement can be delayed (the original IFRS 9 macro-hedge accounting carve-out is the most famous example) or in principle refused, although outright refusal has never happened.
United Kingdom
Following Brexit, the UK established its own endorsement mechanism. The UK Endorsement Board, formed in 2021, considers each new or amended IFRS standard and decides whether to adopt it for UK use. UK-adopted IFRS has so far diverged minimally from IFRS as issued by the IASB, but the divergence option exists.
United States
Does not permit IFRS for domestic public companies. The SEC permits foreign private issuers to file using IFRS without reconciliation to US GAAP since 2007. The US uses US GAAP, set by the Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB). IASB and FASB ran a formal convergence project from 2002 to 2016; convergence has effectively stopped since. The biggest current gap is in credit-loss accounting (IFRS 9 expected credit loss versus FASB's CECL standard), covered in the US GAAP lecture.
Japan
Permits, but does not require, IFRS for listed companies. Several hundred Japanese listed companies have voluntarily adopted IFRS, but Japanese GAAP remains the default. The Accounting Standards Board of Japan (ASBJ) maintains a "modified IFRS" called JMIS, which has limited uptake.
India
Uses Ind-AS (Indian Accounting Standards), which are converged with IFRS but contain carve-outs, particularly on financial instruments. Ind-AS adoption began in 2016 for large listed companies and has progressed in stages. Banks adopted Ind-AS later than other companies and the implementation of Ind-AS 109 (the Ind-AS version of IFRS 9) has been repeatedly deferred.
South Africa
Full IFRS for listed companies on the JSE since 2005. South African banks follow IFRS Accounting Standards including IFRS 9 in full. The South African Institute of Chartered Accountants (SAICA) is a member of the IFRS Advisory Council.

The IFRS Interpretations Committee

The Committee (formerly the International Financial Reporting Interpretations Committee, IFRIC; the older "IFRIC interpretations" are still in force under that name) is the operational mechanism for resolving narrow application questions. It has 14 voting members appointed by the Trustees, drawn primarily from large accounting firms, preparers, and users.

When preparers or auditors encounter a question on how to apply an existing IFRS standard, they can submit it to the Committee. The Committee considers whether the question is widespread and important enough to warrant standard-setting attention. If so, it can either propose a narrow-scope amendment to an existing standard, issue an "agenda decision" (a written explanation of how the standard applies, with no formal amendment), or refer the matter to the IASB. Agenda decisions, despite being formally non-authoritative, are treated as effectively binding in most jurisdictions because audit firms apply them when reviewing financial statements.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The IFRS Foundation is small but consequential

The Foundation has perhaps 200 staff in London. The IASB has 14 board members. The ISSB has another 14. This is a tiny organisation by the standards of global financial regulation. It punches above its weight because its output is binary: a country either adopts IFRS or it does not, and adoption is mostly a one-way ratchet. Once the EU adopted IFRS in 2005, removing the requirement would have been politically and operationally impossible. The same is true of every adopting jurisdiction. The Foundation's leverage comes from network effects in adoption, not from staff numbers or budget.

Standards take five to ten years from research to effective date

This is the single most important fact for any model owner. A standard that becomes effective in 2030 has been visible in exposure-draft form since 2025 and in discussion-paper form since 2022. If your model has to comply with the new standard, you have years to prepare; if you are surprised, it is because you stopped reading. The IASB and ISSB publish their work plans publicly and update them several times a year. Subscribe to the IFRS Foundation's monthly update and to your jurisdiction's endorsement board's update, and you will not be caught short.

Banks complain about procyclicality but the IASB resists ad-hoc relief

The expected-credit-loss model in IFRS 9 is procyclical: as the economy deteriorates, ECL increases, eating into capital just when the bank needs it most. Banks lobbied for relief during the early COVID period in 2020, asking the IASB to suspend or modify IFRS 9's forward-looking assessment. The IASB declined to suspend the standard but did publish a public statement reminding preparers that long-term economic forecasts in highly uncertain conditions should be weighted with judgement rather than mechanically. The episode is instructive: the Foundation will provide implementation guidance under stress, but it will not rewrite a standard to soften its effects in a particular cycle. This is a deliberate feature of the design, not a bug.

Watch out "IFRS-compliant" is not the same as "EU-adopted IFRS compliant" or "UK-adopted IFRS compliant". Each jurisdiction with an endorsement mechanism can delay or modify what it adopts, and a few have done so for tactical reasons. The clearest example is the European Commission's IFRS 9 macro-hedging carve-out, which allowed European banks to continue applying part of IAS 39 to portfolio hedges of interest-rate risk even after IFRS 9 became effective. When advising on a bank's reporting framework, always check the local endorsement status, not just the IASB-issued standard.

The ISSB is the new front line

Climate-related financial disclosure is the most active accounting and disclosure topic at the moment. IFRS S2 is being adopted, modified, or actively rejected in different jurisdictions on a timeline that is changing month by month. The EU operates a separate but partially overlapping framework (the European Sustainability Reporting Standards, set by EFRAG under the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive); the UK is consulting on adoption of IFRS S1 and S2 directly; the US has retreated from a federal climate-disclosure rule under SEC and the picture is shifting jurisdiction by jurisdiction. Banks will need to disclose under multiple regimes for several more years before a single global baseline settles. Following the ISSB's published work plan is the cleanest way to track where things are going.

The EU has tried to assert endorsement autonomy multiple times

The EU's endorsement mechanism has produced several public confrontations with the IASB. The 2004 IFRS 9 macro-hedging carve-out (formally a carve-out from IAS 39 that was inherited into IFRS 9) is the longest-running. There was a serious debate in 2018 around whether the EU would endorse IFRS 17 Insurance Contracts on time; the European Commission ultimately did, with annual mandatory transitional arrangements. The pattern is that the Commission rarely refuses endorsement outright but uses the threat to extract changes or delays. UK and EU watchers should treat endorsement as a parallel political track, not an automatic stamp.

The US-GAAP divide is partial but real

The biggest remaining gap between IFRS and US GAAP for banks is in credit-loss accounting. IFRS 9 uses a three-stage expected-credit-loss model with a 12-month ECL for performing exposures and lifetime ECL after a significant increase in credit risk. US GAAP's CECL (Current Expected Credit Loss) requires lifetime ECL from day one for all exposures. The two produce different impairment numbers for the same portfolio, especially for short-dated lending where CECL recognises the full life of expected losses up front. For a bancassurance or cross-listed group, this is a permanent operational headache that no amount of convergence work has resolved.

Standards are written for the preparer, not the user

IFRS standards are written in the language of accountants for the people who prepare financial statements, not for the investors who read them. This is the opposite of the ISSB's design philosophy, which writes for investors directly. The mismatch is a feature: accounting standards have to be auditable and enforceable, which requires precise definitions and operational tests; investor-focused disclosure standards prioritise comparability and decision-usefulness over enforceability. Expect this tension to surface as the ISSB matures and starts publishing topic-specific standards beyond IFRS S2.

Step 6

Test yourself

  1. What is the IFRS Foundation and where is it based? Who oversees the Trustees, and what mechanism gives that body its public-accountability role?
  2. Distinguish between the IASB and the ISSB. When was each created, and what does each produce?
  3. List the Trustees' five core responsibilities. Why are the Trustees deliberately kept out of technical standard-setting decisions?
  4. Describe the typical due-process steps from research project to a finalised IFRS Accounting Standard. Roughly how many years does it take?
  5. Name three IFRS Accounting Standards that are particularly relevant to banks, and explain what each one governs.
  6. What is the difference between IAS 39 and IFRS 9 in their treatment of credit-loss recognition? Why was the change made?
  7. What is the "endorsement" mechanism used by the EU and (post-Brexit) by the UK? Give one example of where endorsement has diverged from IASB-issued IFRS.
  8. Which of the world's largest economies do not use IFRS, and what do they use instead? Why has full convergence not been achieved?
  9. What is IFRS S2 and when did it become effective? Why was the ISSB created at COP26 rather than as an arm of one of the existing voluntary frameworks?
  10. A bank's auditor relies on an "agenda decision" published by the IFRS Interpretations Committee. The agenda decision is formally non-authoritative. Why does the bank still have to follow it?
