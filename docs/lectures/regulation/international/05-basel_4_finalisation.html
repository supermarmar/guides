Basel III finalisation (Basel IV): the output floor and the end of variability

In December 2017 the Basel Committee published BCBS d424, the last piece of the post-crisis reform programme. The 2017 reforms left the architecture of Basel III untouched but rebuilt the way risk-weighted assets are calculated. They recalibrated the standardised approach for credit risk so that it became granular and risk-sensitive enough to serve as a benchmark, they constrained the internal ratings-based approach with parameter floors and removed it altogether for large corporates and financial institutions, they replaced the internal-model approach to credit-valuation adjustment and to operational risk with revised standardised approaches, and they imposed an output floor at 72.5 per cent that caps the capital benefit any bank can derive from internal models. The market calls this Basel IV. The Committee calls it Basel III finalisation. Either way it is the single biggest change to risk-weight calculation since 2004.

Step 1

The 30-second version

In December 2017 the Basel Committee published BCBS d424, titled Basel III: Finalising post-crisis reforms. The 2017 reforms are a single, integrated package. They do six things. First, they replace the flat, ratings-driven standardised approach for credit risk with a more granular and risk-sensitive table: loan-to-value bands for residential and commercial mortgages, a stand-alone treatment for specialised lending, an SME carve-out at 85 per cent (or 75 per cent under the retail definition), and a positive credit conversion factor for unconditionally cancellable commitments. Second, they constrain the IRB approach by removing the advanced IRB option for exposures to large corporates (revenue above 500 million euros), banks, and other financial institutions, and by imposing input floors on probability of default, loss given default, and exposure at default. Third, they replace the internal-model approach for credit-valuation adjustment with a standardised approach (SA-CVA) and a basic approach (BA-CVA), aligned with the revised market-risk framework. Fourth, they replace the standardised, alternative-standardised, and advanced measurement approaches for operational risk with a single income-and-loss-based standardised measurement approach. Fifth, they impose an output floor at 72.5 per cent of the standardised-approach risk-weighted-asset total. Sixth, they add a leverage-ratio buffer for global systemically important banks at 50 per cent of the bank's risk-weighted G-SIB surcharge.

Original implementation date 1 January 2022, with the output floor phased in over five years to 1 January 2027. The COVID-19 pandemic pushed the Committee to defer everything by one year in March 2020, with the output floor now phasing from 1 January 2023 to 1 January 2028. National implementations have slipped further: the EU's CRR3 applies from 1 January 2025 with the floor phasing to 2030, and the UK's Basel 3.1 (PRA PS9/24 and PS17/25) applies from 1 January 2027 with the floor phasing to 2030. The US has not yet finalised its implementation. Whatever the calendar, the 2017 reforms are the rules under which every bank you cover will operate by the end of this decade.

Feynman test Imagine each bank carries a measuring tape and a ruler. Before 2017 the supervisor said "use whichever you prefer". The big banks all bought their own measuring tapes, calibrated them in the basement, and measured their risk as 30 per cent of what their smaller competitors got with the ruler. The supervisor ran a hypothetical-portfolio exercise: same loans, same numbers, same instructions, the answers came back different by a factor of two. The supervisor faced a choice. Throw away the measuring tapes (no, the EU and Swiss would not allow it). Throw away the ruler (no, the US would not allow it). The compromise: keep the tape, but check it against the ruler. From now on, no matter what your tape says, your reported risk has to be at least 72.5 per cent of what the ruler gives. And while we are at it, redesign the ruler so it actually measures risk properly, redesign the tape so its inputs are floored, and replace the bit of tape that measured operational risk and CVA with a fixed metre stick because no one trusts what banks were doing there at all. That is BCBS d424.
What is beyond the source The source wiki summarises the BCBS d424 high-level text. This lecture adds: the supervisory evidence that triggered the reforms (the Committee's 2013 and 2014 hypothetical-portfolio exercises showing risk-weight variability of two to three times for similar portfolios); the politics of the negotiation (the EU and Switzerland defending IRB, the US and UK pushing the floor); the structural and business-model consequences (less low-margin large-corporate lending under IRB, redistribution of capital from advanced-model banks to standardised-approach banks); the EU "parallel-stack" debate and the UK's "single-stack" alternative; the post-COVID delays and the divergence in national timelines (EU 2025, UK 2027, US still unsettled); and why the market calls this Basel IV when the BCBS itself refuses the label.
Step 2

Why this matters

Lecture 4 covered the first phase of Basel III: the redefinition of capital quality, the conservation and countercyclical buffers, the 3 per cent leverage ratio, the LCR, and the NSFR. Those reforms changed the numerator of the capital ratio (what counts as capital) and added a parallel non-risk-based constraint (leverage) and two liquidity constraints. What the first phase did not change was the denominator. The denominator, risk-weighted assets, remained calculated by exactly the same internal-ratings-based and standardised approaches the Basel II framework had introduced in 2004.

By the time the first phase of Basel III was finalised in 2010, the Committee was already aware that the denominator was the problem. A series of supervisory studies between 2011 and 2014, including hypothetical-portfolio exercises for the trading book and the banking book, showed that risk-weighted assets for similar exposures could vary by a factor of two or three between banks using internal models. Some of the variability was justified by genuine portfolio differences. A material portion was not. Investors lost faith in reported risk-weighted ratios; analysts began computing "fully loaded" capital ratios using leverage instead. The Committee faced a credibility problem. The 2017 reforms exist to fix it.

For a credit-risk modeller the practical consequences are immediate. If you run an A-IRB book for large corporates, banks, or other financial institutions, your A-IRB model is going to be retired. You will move those exposures to F-IRB, where loss given default and exposure at default are set by supervisory formula rather than by your model. If you run A-IRB for retail, SMEs, or specialised lending you keep your models, but every parameter has an input floor, and the aggregate output floor caps how much the model can save you relative to the standardised approach. Whether the output floor binds at the portfolio level depends on the mix: a bank with cherry-picked low-risk corporate IRB exposures will be floored; a bank with a balanced retail book may not be. Either way, banks are now required to compute and disclose the parallel standardised-approach RWA, which doubles the calculation workload of capital reporting.

For a supervisor or an exam candidate the importance is broader. BCBS d424 is the most consequential change to capital adequacy since the introduction of internal models in the 1996 Basel I market-risk amendment. It is also the first time the Basel Committee has explicitly retreated from the internal-models programme. The direction of travel has flipped: post-1996 the trend was towards more model permissions, post-2017 the trend is towards floors, constraints, and standardised benchmarks. Understanding why the trend flipped, and where the politics of the next round will land, is essential context for any work on regulatory capital over the next decade.

Step 3

The mental model

The output floor is the single most important mental model in BCBS d424. Picture two parallel calculations of risk-weighted assets for the same balance sheet. One is the bank's own preferred number, computed using the mix of standardised and internal-model approaches the bank has supervisory permission to use. The other is a hypothetical, parallel number computed entirely with the revised standardised approaches. The reported risk-weighted-asset figure must be the higher of the bank's own number and 72.5 per cent of the parallel standardised number.

Three properties of the mental model deserve emphasis. First, the floor is applied at the aggregate level, not exposure by exposure. A single low-risk-weight A-IRB mortgage does not need to clear the floor on its own; what matters is the total IRB-RWA across the bank versus the total SA-RWA. Second, the floor reference uses the revised standardised approach, not the old Basel II approach. The old approach was insufficiently risk-sensitive to serve as a meaningful benchmark; redesigning it was a prerequisite for the floor to work. Third, the floor is parallel to and independent of the leverage ratio. A bank could in principle bind on the risk-weighted minimum, on the output floor, on the leverage ratio, or on the G-SIB leverage-ratio buffer. Capital planning under BCBS d424 means tracking four binding constraints at once.

Step 4

The detail

Why finalisation was needed

The case for finalisation was built between 2010 and 2016 on three pillars. The first was empirical evidence of unjustified variability in risk-weighted assets. In 2013 the Basel Committee published a hypothetical-portfolio exercise in which thirty-two banks were given an identical book of corporate, sovereign, and bank exposures and asked to compute the risk-weighted assets they would assign. The implied capital requirements ranged from below 6 per cent of exposure to above 16 per cent for the same portfolio. The Committee ran similar exercises for the trading book and reached comparable conclusions. The variability had two sources: model differences (defensible) and parameter and modelling-choice differences (less defensible).

The second pillar was loss of market confidence. After 2011 investors and analysts began to discount reported risk-weighted ratios and to look instead at simpler measures such as tangible common equity to tangible assets, or the leverage ratio. The Basel III leverage ratio was itself a partial response to this loss of confidence, but its 3 per cent calibration meant it bound only on the lowest-risk-weighted-asset-density books. For everyone else the risk-weighted ratio remained the headline, and the headline was no longer believed.

The third pillar was political: the EU, Switzerland, and Japan wanted to preserve internal models because their banks had built around them and because their supervisors had invested in approving them; the US wanted floors because US banks had never had broad A-IRB approval and were operating at a structural disadvantage. The compromise that produced BCBS d424 was simple in shape: keep internal models, but constrain them with input floors, scope reductions, and an output floor calibrated to satisfy the US side without rendering A-IRB economically pointless. The negotiations on the exact floor calibration consumed most of 2016 and the first half of 2017. The final number, 72.5 per cent, was lower than the 80 per cent the US originally pushed for and higher than the 70 per cent the EU and Switzerland would have preferred. It is not derived from any theoretical principle. It is a political number.

Revised standardised approach for credit risk

The revised standardised approach is the single largest part of BCBS d424 in pages and in calibration effort. The old Basel II standardised approach assigned a flat 35 per cent risk weight to all residential mortgages, a flat 75 per cent to retail exposures, and used ratings tables for corporates and banks with little granularity. The revised approach is materially more granular in every asset class, and that granularity is the precondition for using it as the floor reference.

For exposures to banks the revised approach offers an external-ratings-based table where ratings are permitted (jurisdictions may opt out of using external ratings under the second amendment to BCBS 462), and a more granular Standardised Credit Risk Assessment (SCRA) for unrated exposures or for jurisdictions that do not permit ratings. The SCRA splits bank exposures into Grade A, Grade B, and Grade C based on the bank's compliance with regulatory capital requirements and the soundness of its capitalisation, with risk weights of 40 per cent, 75 per cent, and 150 per cent respectively. The external-ratings table assigns 20 per cent for AAA to AA-, 30 per cent for A+ to A-, 50 per cent for BBB+ to BBB-, 100 per cent for BB+ to B-, and 150 per cent for below B-.

For exposures to corporates the revised approach adds an SME carve-out at 85 per cent of risk weight for corporate SMEs (revenue not exceeding 50 million euros), and 75 per cent under the regulatory retail bucket where exposure is small enough and granular enough to qualify. It also introduces an explicit treatment for specialised lending (project finance, object finance, commodity finance) with risk weights varying by phase: 130 per cent in the pre-operational phase of a project, 100 per cent in the operational phase, and 80 per cent for high-quality operational-phase exposures.

For residential real estate the revised approach replaces the flat 35 per cent risk weight with a table of risk weights varying by loan-to-value band, under one of two approaches. The whole-loan approach assigns the entire exposure a risk weight that depends on the LTV at origination; the loan-splitting approach splits the loan at 55 per cent LTV, assigning 20 per cent risk weight to the secured portion and the counterparty risk weight to the residual. Under the whole-loan approach the risk weight rises from 20 per cent at LTVs at or below 50 per cent to 70 per cent above 100 per cent LTV, with the counterparty risk weight applied where the prudential criteria for residential real estate (legal enforceability, valuation, documentation, ability-to-pay assessment, and prudent LTV at origination) are not met. The reform aligns risk weight with the actual loss-given-default sensitivity of mortgages, which depends primarily on LTV. A parallel schedule with materially higher weights applies to income-producing residential real estate (IPRRE), where repayment is materially dependent on the cash flows generated by the property rather than the borrower's other income.

Compared with the flat 35 per cent risk weight under Basel II, the revised whole-loan approach is materially more risk-sensitive: a low-LTV owner-occupied mortgage at 45 per cent LTV now attracts a 20 per cent risk weight (a 43 per cent reduction in RWA), while a high-LTV mortgage at 95 per cent LTV attracts a 50 per cent risk weight (a 43 per cent increase). The income-producing schedule applies where servicing the loan depends materially on rental income from the property, capturing the buy-to-let and investor-mortgage segments that performed worst in the 2008 crisis. Under the loan-splitting alternative, the portion of the loan up to 55 per cent of property value receives a 20 per cent weight and the residual the counterparty risk weight, regardless of the bands above.

For commercial real estate the revised approach splits exposures into general CRE and income-producing CRE, with risk weights varying by LTV in each. For retail exposures the old single 75 per cent bucket is split into regulatory retail (non-revolving) at 75 per cent, regulatory retail revolving (transactors at 45 per cent, revolvers at 75 per cent), and other retail at 100 per cent. The transactor / revolver distinction is the most novel piece: a credit-card customer who pays off the balance every month (transactor) is materially less risky than one who carries a balance (revolver), and the revised approach captures the difference.

For off-balance-sheet exposures the revised approach makes the credit conversion factors more risk-sensitive. The single biggest change is the introduction of a positive CCF for unconditionally cancellable commitments. Under Basel II these had a 0 per cent CCF on the theory that the bank could cancel them at will and so was never exposed; supervisory experience showed that banks did not in practice cancel these lines during stress, and they were a major source of unexpected drawdown in the financial crisis. The revised approach assigns a 10 per cent CCF.

External rating versus SCRA
The revised standardised approach offers two methods for risk-weighting unrated bank exposures: the External Credit Risk Assessment (ECRA) for jurisdictions that permit external ratings, and the Standardised Credit Risk Assessment (SCRA) for those that do not. The SCRA is the operational mechanism by which the US implementation can dispense with credit ratings while keeping the standardised approach functional.
Investment-grade corporate
For unrated corporate exposures, the revised approach allows a 65 per cent risk weight (down from 100 per cent) for exposures to "investment-grade" corporates as defined by the bank's own due diligence and listed-securities issuance. The carve-out keeps the standardised-approach burden moderate for high-quality corporate lending.
Regulatory retail
A retail exposure qualifies for the 75 per cent regulatory-retail risk weight if it meets four criteria: it is to an individual or small business, it is one of a sufficient number of similar exposures (granularity), the total aggregate exposure to any single counterparty is below 1 million euros, and the product type is one of the listed retail categories.
Specialised lending
A subset of corporate exposures where repayment depends almost entirely on the income generated by the asset financed, with no other meaningful source of recovery. Project finance, object finance, and commodities finance. The revised approach gives these their own risk-weight schedule rather than treating them as undifferentiated corporates.

Revised IRB approach for credit risk

The IRB framework as a whole is preserved. What changes is its scope, its input parameters, and its supervisory expectations. The three changes interact.

On scope, the advanced IRB approach is removed for exposures to large and mid-sized corporates (consolidated annual revenue above 500 million euros), banks, and other financial institutions. For these asset classes, banks that previously used A-IRB must move to F-IRB, in which the bank estimates probability of default but supervisory-prescribed values are used for loss given default and exposure at default. The removal addresses the asset classes where the Committee judged the lack of default data made internal LGD and EAD estimates unreliable: large-corporate defaults are infrequent, bank failures are rarer still, and the loss distributions are too dispersed for bank-internal modelling to produce stable parameters. The IRB approach is removed altogether for exposures to equities, which are now treated under the revised standardised approach with risk weights from 100 per cent (long-term equity holdings under regulated equity programmes) to 400 per cent (speculative unlisted equity).

On input parameters, the revised IRB approach introduces or recalibrates floors for the three core parameters. The probability of default floor is now 5 basis points for corporate, mortgage, and other retail exposures, with 10 basis points for qualifying revolving retail revolvers and 5 basis points for transactors. The loss given default floors apply only under A-IRB and vary by collateral type: 25 per cent for unsecured corporate, 5 per cent for residential mortgages secured by property, 30 per cent for unsecured other retail, with secured-LGD floors of 0 per cent for financial collateral, 10 per cent for receivables, 10 per cent for commercial or residential real estate collateral, and 15 per cent for other physical collateral. For qualifying revolving retail the LGD floor is 50 per cent both for transactors and for revolvers. The exposure at default floor is the sum of on-balance-sheet exposure plus 50 per cent of the off-balance-sheet exposure measured using the standardised-approach credit conversion factor; in effect EAD cannot be reduced more than 50 per cent below the standardised level.

On supervisory expectations, the revised framework prescribes more detail about how parameters must be estimated. The downturn LGD adjustment is more prescriptive, the long-run-average PD calibration is more explicit, and the requirements for treating defaulted exposures (LGD in-default and best estimate of expected loss) are tightened. The F-IRB supervisory parameters are themselves recalibrated: unsecured corporate LGD is reduced from 45 per cent to 40 per cent for exposures to non-financial corporates, and the collateral haircut schedule for secured exposures is tightened. Finally, the 1.06 scaling factor that Basel II applied to IRB risk-weighted assets is removed, on the basis that the new floors and constraints achieve the same conservatism through more transparent means.

Input floor versus output floor
Two distinct mechanisms. The input floors constrain the parameters (PD, LGD, EAD) that go into the IRB risk-weight formula, so even an internal-model estimate below the floor is replaced by the floor value when the formula is run. The output floor constrains the total IRB-RWA at the bank level relative to the parallel standardised-approach RWA. A bank can be bound by both at once, by either, or by neither.
F-IRB versus A-IRB after 2017
Under F-IRB the bank estimates PD only; LGD and EAD are supervisory-fixed (45 per cent for senior unsecured corporate exposures pre-2017, now 40 per cent for non-financial corporates). Under A-IRB the bank estimates PD, LGD, and EAD. After BCBS d424, A-IRB is no longer available for exposures to large corporates, banks, or other financial institutions; F-IRB remains available for these. A-IRB remains available for retail, SME, and specialised lending, subject to input floors.
PD floor of 5 basis points
Applies to most corporate, mortgage, and other retail exposures, replacing the lower 3 basis point floor in some prior implementations. The floor binds for very high-quality investment-grade borrowers where internal PD estimates can fall below 1 basis point. For credit-card transactor portfolios the same 5-basis-point floor applies, recognising that even transactors carry some default risk.
Removal of the 1.06 scalar
Under Basel II IRB risk-weighted assets were multiplied by 1.06 to capture model risk and to maintain the calibration of the framework against Basel I. Under BCBS d424 the scalar is removed; the new constraints (input floors, output floor, scope restrictions, removal of the AMA for op risk) are deemed sufficient to maintain prudence. The removal is a modest offsetting capital relief for IRB banks.

Revised CVA framework

Credit-valuation adjustment is the mark-to-market accounting reflection of counterparty credit risk on uncleared derivatives. Basel III in 2010 introduced a CVA capital charge after large CVA losses during the financial crisis exceeded outright default losses for many banks. The original Basel III CVA framework offered banks two methods: an internal-model approach (advanced CVA, or IMA-CVA) for banks with VaR model approval, and a standardised approach for others. The advanced approach allowed a bank to model the joint behaviour of counterparty credit spreads and derivative exposures using its own VaR model, with all the modelling challenges that entails.

BCBS d424 removes the internal-model approach and replaces it with two tiered approaches. The Basic Approach (BA-CVA) applies a fixed risk-weight schedule by counterparty rating, in a structure very close to the original Basel II standardised approach but recalibrated. The Standardised Approach (SA-CVA), available subject to supervisory approval, is sensitivities-based and aligned with the revised market-risk framework: the bank computes sensitivities of CVA to counterparty credit spreads and to market-risk factors, and aggregates them under the FRTB sensitivities-based-method machinery. Both approaches now include the exposure component of CVA risk, which the original Basel III CVA framework did not capture properly. There is also a simplified approach for small derivative books: a bank with aggregate notional of uncleared derivatives at or below 100 billion euros may calculate CVA capital as a flat multiplier (100 per cent) of its counterparty-credit-risk capital, avoiding the SA-CVA / BA-CVA calculation altogether.

The structural point is that CVA is now a fully standardised risk in regulatory capital terms: there is no internal-model option at all. This is the first time since Basel II that a risk category has lost its internal-model option entirely (operational risk loses its IMA in the same package, see next subsection). The Committee's view was that CVA is too complex and too sparse in observed data to be modelled robustly at most banks, and that the SA-CVA's alignment with FRTB market risk produces an acceptable trade-off between risk sensitivity and comparability.

Revised operational risk framework

Operational risk under Basel II had three approaches in ascending complexity: the Basic Indicator Approach (BIA), the Standardised Approach (TSA), and the Advanced Measurement Approach (AMA). AMA was an internal-model approach allowing banks to compute capital from loss distributions fitted to their internal loss data and adjusted by scenario analysis and business-environment indicators. AMA was both the most flexible approach and the most heavily criticised: loss-distribution modelling is intrinsically data-poor (tail losses are rare by definition), and post-crisis operational-risk losses including misconduct (LIBOR fines, mis-selling redress, sanctions penalties) exceeded almost every bank's AMA capital by a wide margin.

BCBS d424 replaces all four approaches (BIA, TSA, AMA, and the alternative standardised) with a single Standardised Measurement Approach (SMA). The SMA has two components:

Operational risk capital = BIC × ILM

The Business Indicator Component (BIC) is a marginal-coefficient function of the Business Indicator, which is a three-part income proxy: an interest, leases, and dividends component; a services component; and a financial component. The Business Indicator approximates the bank's gross income across business lines without the volatility distortions of accounting net interest income. The marginal coefficients are 12 per cent for the first 1 billion euros of BI, 15 per cent for the next 29 billion euros (BI between 1 and 30 billion euros), and 18 per cent above 30 billion euros. The bucketed structure means that operational-risk capital rises faster than proportionally with bank size, reflecting empirical evidence that large banks have more frequent and more severe operational losses.

The Internal Loss Multiplier (ILM) adjusts the BIC up or down based on the bank's actual loss history over the preceding ten years. The Loss Component (LC) is 15 times the average annual operational-risk loss over that ten-year window. The ILM formula is:

ILM = ln(exp(1) - 1 + (LC / BIC)^0.8)

The ILM equals 1 when LC equals BIC; it rises above 1 when historical losses exceed the income-based proxy and falls below 1 when historical losses are smaller. The 0.8 exponent and the natural-log functional form mean that the ILM scales with losses at a decreasing rate, so a bank with very large historical losses does not face arbitrarily large capital requirements. At national discretion, supervisors may set ILM equal to 1 for all banks in their jurisdiction, removing the loss-history adjustment altogether; the US has indicated it intends to take this option, while the EU and UK have not. Even where the ILM is set to 1, banks must still disclose their ten-year loss history under Pillar 3 to support market comparability.

Business Indicator (BI)
An income-statement proxy designed to capture a bank's operational-risk exposure without the procyclicality of gross income. Sum of three components: interest, leases and dividends; services (fees and commissions, both received and paid, plus other operating income and expense, all in absolute value); and financial (net P&L on the trading and banking books, in absolute value, averaged over three years).
Loss Component (LC)
15 times the bank's average annual operational-risk losses over the preceding ten years (with a transitional five-year window for banks lacking ten years of data). Includes losses above a 20,000-euro threshold, with a higher 100,000-euro threshold at supervisory discretion. The factor of 15 is calibrated so that the loss component and the business indicator component are of comparable magnitude for a "typical" bank, making the ILM ratio meaningful.
National discretion on ILM
Supervisors may set ILM equal to 1 for all banks in their jurisdiction, effectively switching off the loss-history adjustment. The US has signalled this preference, partly to reduce the punitive effect of misconduct losses on banks that have already paid fines. The EU and UK retain the ILM. The disclosure of historical losses is required in both cases.

The output floor

The output floor is the integrating mechanism that ties the package together. It is articulated in a single sentence: total risk-weighted assets must be the higher of the bank's permitted calculation and 72.5 per cent of the risk-weighted assets calculated using the revised standardised approaches.

The "permitted calculation" for the bank may include any mix of standardised and internal-model approaches the bank has supervisory approval for: standardised credit risk, F-IRB, A-IRB, SA-CCR, internal-model-method counterparty credit risk, BA-CVA, SA-CVA, the standardised approach for market risk, the internal-models approach for market risk under FRTB (see lecture 6), and the SMA for operational risk. The "parallel standardised calculation" replaces every internal-model approach with its standardised counterpart: A-IRB and F-IRB credit risk become standardised credit risk, IMM counterparty credit risk becomes SA-CCR, IMA market risk becomes SA market risk, SA-CVA stays as SA-CVA (it is already standardised; BA-CVA may also be used). The SMA is used in both calculations because there is no internal-model alternative.

The transition is phased over five years, originally beginning at 50 per cent in 2022 and rising by 5 percentage points per year to 72.5 per cent in 2027. The COVID deferral shifted the schedule by one year: 50 per cent in 2023, 55 per cent in 2024, 60 per cent in 2025, 65 per cent in 2026, 70 per cent in 2027, 72.5 per cent in 2028. National implementations have extended the schedule further; in the EU and UK the floor begins phasing in 2025 to 2027 and reaches 72.5 per cent in 2030 (see the political-economy subsection in section 5 below).

The economic effect of the floor depends entirely on the ratio of a bank's IRB-RWA density to its SA-RWA density. A bank with mostly retail mortgages where A-IRB risk weights are 10 to 20 per cent and standardised risk weights are 25 to 40 per cent will likely be floored: 72.5 per cent of the standardised number will exceed the A-IRB number. A bank with a mixed portfolio where the IRB and SA densities are closer in magnitude may be unaffected. The floor binds aggregate-on-aggregate, not exposure-on-exposure, so the mix matters as much as the individual densities.

Leverage-ratio buffer for G-SIBs

The first phase of Basel III introduced a 3 per cent leverage ratio (Tier 1 capital divided by leverage-ratio exposure measure) as a non-risk-based backstop. BCBS d424 adds a supplementary leverage-ratio buffer for global systemically important banks, calibrated at 50 per cent of the bank's risk-weighted G-SIB surcharge. A G-SIB in bucket 1 has a 1 per cent risk-weighted surcharge and so a 0.5 per cent leverage-ratio buffer; a bucket-2 G-SIB has a 1.5 per cent risk-weighted surcharge and a 0.75 per cent leverage-ratio buffer; a bucket-3 G-SIB has a 2 per cent risk-weighted surcharge and a 1 per cent leverage-ratio buffer; and so on. The largest G-SIBs in the highest buckets face leverage-ratio requirements of 4 to 4.5 per cent rather than the baseline 3 per cent.

The buffer takes the form of a capital-distribution constraint, exactly analogous to the risk-weighted capital conservation buffer. A G-SIB that fails its leverage-ratio buffer faces restrictions on dividends, bonuses, and AT1 coupon payments, scaled by how deep the shortfall is. The buffer must be met with Tier 1 capital (the leverage-ratio numerator), not just CET1. Distribution constraints depend jointly on the bank's CET1 risk-weighted ratio and its Tier 1 leverage ratio: a G-SIB that meets one but not the other faces the constraint associated with the failed requirement; a G-SIB that fails both faces the higher of the two constraints.

BCBS d424 also makes various refinements to the leverage-ratio exposure measure: the treatment of derivatives is aligned with SA-CCR rather than the original current-exposure method, the off-balance-sheet treatment is updated for consistency with the revised standardised approach, and jurisdictions may exempt central-bank reserves from the exposure measure during periods of exceptional macroeconomic conditions (provided they recalibrate the minimum ratio commensurately and require disclosure). The reserve exemption was used widely during COVID-19 in the EU, UK, and US.

Worked example: a residential mortgage portfolio under SA, A-IRB, and the floor

Set-up

A bank holds a portfolio of 100 million euros of seasoned UK residential mortgages, all with full title to the property as collateral, an average LTV at origination of 75 per cent, an average current LTV of 60 per cent, an average PD of 50 basis points, and a downturn LGD of 8 per cent. The bank uses A-IRB for this portfolio. There are no off-balance-sheet exposures and no defaulted loans. Maturity is 25 years; correlation parameter R for retail mortgages is fixed at 0.15 under the IRB formula. Risk-weighted-asset density figures are illustrative.

Standardised approach RWA

Under the revised standardised approach with the whole-loan method, an LTV of 60 per cent falls in the 50 to 60 per cent band, with a risk weight of 25 per cent. The standardised RWA on the 100 million-euro portfolio is:

SA-RWA = 100,000,000 × 25% = 25,000,000 euros

A-IRB RWA

Under the revised A-IRB formula for residential mortgages the capital requirement K is:

K = LGD × N(sqrt(1/(1-R)) × G(PD) + sqrt(R/(1-R)) × G(0.999)) - PD × LGD

where N is the standard normal cdf and G is its inverse. With PD = 0.005, LGD = 8 per cent (floored at 5 per cent so unconstrained here, but check: the LGD floor for residential mortgages with full property collateral is 5 per cent, so the LGD of 8 per cent is unfloored), R = 0.15:

G(0.005) ≈ -2.576
G(0.999) ≈ 3.090
sqrt(1/0.85) ≈ 1.085
sqrt(0.15/0.85) ≈ 0.420
K = 0.08 × N(1.085 × (-2.576) + 0.420 × 3.090) - 0.005 × 0.08
K = 0.08 × N(-2.795 + 1.298) - 0.0004
K = 0.08 × N(-1.497) - 0.0004
K = 0.08 × 0.0672 - 0.0004
K = 0.00538 - 0.0004
K ≈ 0.00498 = 0.498 per cent

RWA is K × 12.5 × EAD (the 12.5 converts capital to RWA at the 8 per cent ratio), so:

IRB-RWA = 0.00498 × 12.5 × 100,000,000 = 6,225,000 euros
IRB risk-weight density ≈ 6.2 per cent

Output-floor check

The floor reference at full phase-in (72.5 per cent) is:

Floor reference = 0.725 × SA-RWA = 0.725 × 25,000,000 = 18,125,000 euros

Since IRB-RWA (6.2 million) is below the floor reference (18.1 million), the floor binds. The reported RWA is the higher of the two:

Reported RWA = max(6,225,000, 18,125,000) = 18,125,000 euros
Reported risk-weight density ≈ 18.1 per cent

The bank's A-IRB calculation would have given a 6.2 per cent risk-weight density; the floor lifts that to 18.1 per cent, almost a three-times increase. Capital required at an 8 per cent minimum ratio rises from 498,000 euros to 1,450,000 euros for the same portfolio. This is the kind of mortgage book that is most exposed to the floor: high-quality, low-LTV, low-PD, fully-collateralised mortgages where A-IRB gives a very low risk weight and the SA floor lifts it substantially.

What changes if the LTV is higher

If the average current LTV were 90 per cent instead of 60 per cent, the SA risk weight would be 50 per cent, the SA-RWA would be 50 million euros, the floor reference would be 36.25 million euros, and the A-IRB risk weight would also be higher because LGD on a 90 per cent LTV mortgage would be larger (perhaps 18 per cent). The gap between the two would narrow. At LTV bands above 100 per cent the SA risk weight is 70 per cent and the A-IRB number can approach it; the floor may not bind for the highest-LTV portion of a portfolio at all.

What this tells you about the bank-level floor

This is one portfolio. The bank's aggregate output-floor check is across all portfolios, so a heavily-floored low-LTV mortgage book can be offset within the bank by an unfloored, high-risk-weight book elsewhere. But across most large European retail banks, the residential-mortgage portfolio is the single largest A-IRB exposure and the single biggest source of floor pressure. Industry impact studies have estimated that mortgage books account for roughly half of the aggregate floor impact in the EU.

Implementation timeline

The original BCBS d424 timeline set 1 January 2022 as the implementation date for the credit, CVA, operational, and leverage reforms, with the output floor phasing from 50 per cent in 2022 to 72.5 per cent in 2027. In March 2020 the GHOS deferred all of these by one year in response to the COVID-19 pandemic: 1 January 2023 became the new implementation date, and the output floor phase-in shifted to 50 per cent in 2023 rising to 72.5 per cent in 2028. FRTB market risk was deferred by the same year.

National implementations have diverged further. The EU's CRR3 and CRD VI package, adopted in May 2024, applies the credit-risk reforms from 1 January 2025, with the output floor phasing from 50 per cent in 2025 to 72.5 per cent in 2030. The UK PRA's Basel 3.1 package (PS9/24 published July 2024, with subsequent updates including PS12/25 and PS17/25) applies from 1 January 2027, with the output floor phasing from 50 per cent in 2027 to 72.5 per cent in 2030. The US has not yet finalised its implementation as at 2026; the original "Basel III Endgame" proposal in 2023 was withdrawn after industry pushback in 2024, and the Federal Reserve, OCC, and FDIC are reconsidering the calibration. The end-state is a four-jurisdiction situation in which the same Basel standard is applied with materially different transitional schedules and (in places) different calibrations, undercutting some of the level-playing-field rationale for the reforms in the first place.

Watch out The Committee's d424 publication says "2022, phased to 2027". The actual rule applied to your bank says something different, depending on jurisdiction. EU banks face 2025 to 2030; UK banks face 2027 to 2030; US banks face an open question. Always check the local rule for the transitional schedule before quoting a number for a specific bank, and always check whether the local regulator has departed from the BCBS calibration on any specific item (UK CRE LTV thresholds and EU SME carve-outs are well-known divergences).
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

BCBS d424 is a political compromise, not a technical design

The 72.5 per cent calibration of the output floor, the 500 million-euro revenue threshold for the removal of A-IRB on large corporates, the choice to retain A-IRB for retail mortgages, and the carve-out for high-quality unrated investment-grade corporates were all settled by negotiation rather than by analysis. The US side pushed for an 80 per cent floor and broader IRB removal; the EU and Swiss side pushed for a 70 per cent floor and IRB retention. Almost every quantitative parameter in the package can be traced to a give-and-take between the two camps. Reading the package as a coherent technical design will mislead you; reading it as the durable compromise that ended a decade-long fight will not.

The EU "parallel-stack" debate

The single most contested implementation question in the EU was whether the output floor should apply at the consolidated level only (the "single stack" approach, where the floor is computed once at the bank holding company level) or at multiple levels including individual exposures and asset classes (the "parallel stack" approach). The single-stack approach is the BCBS d424 design. The parallel-stack approach was promoted by some EU industry voices because it would have allowed transitional carve-outs at the sub-portfolio level for SME lending, unrated corporates, and low-risk mortgages, blunting the impact of the floor on European retail and SME credit. The Council and Parliament ultimately adopted the single-stack approach in CRR3, but with several transitional carve-outs (the unrated-corporate 65 per cent risk weight, an SME supporting factor, and infrastructure carve-outs) embedded inside the standardised approach itself. The EU implementation is BCBS d424-compliant in form but materially looser in calibration; the next RCAP review will likely flag the divergence.

The UK Basel 3.1 implementation

The UK's PRA chose a different path. PS9/24 (July 2024) implements BCBS d424 closer to the original calibration, without most of the EU's transitional carve-outs. The PRA also chose to apply the standardised credit-risk reforms with greater fidelity to the BCBS text on residential mortgages, on commercial real estate, and on credit conversion factors. Subsequent PRA statements have softened some calibrations (PS17/25 reduced the residential-mortgage risk-weight floor for prime owner-occupied portfolios), but the UK is broadly the most BCBS-faithful Basel 3.1 implementation in Europe. The trade-off has been UK banks lobbying against the resulting capital impact, particularly on SME and commercial-real-estate books. Lecture 20 covers the UK implementation in detail.

The structural shift in bank business models

BCBS d424 changes the relative attractiveness of different bank business models. Pre-2017, A-IRB-permitted banks could lend to large investment-grade corporates at very low risk-weight densities (5 to 10 per cent), making relationship-banking and corporate lending economically attractive. Post-2017, the same lending will run at F-IRB and be subject to the output floor, raising risk-weight densities to 30 to 50 per cent. Banks have responded by shifting away from large-corporate lending toward retail, SME, and specialised lending where A-IRB is preserved and floors are less binding. The trend toward originate-to-distribute models for large-corporate credit, with banks holding only the relationship and selling the loan to non-bank investors, has accelerated. So has the migration of large-corporate financing toward the bond market, where banks earn fees rather than carry risk.

The CVA exemption that did not survive

The original BCBS d424 CVA framework, like the previous Basel III CVA framework, exempts transactions with non-financial corporates, sovereigns, multilateral development banks, and pension funds from the CVA capital charge. This exemption matters because it makes derivative hedging for the non-financial corporate sector economically viable. The EU has maintained the exemption in CRR3; the UK has narrowed it in Basel 3.1; the US implementation is open. The Committee's view is that the exemption is a politically necessary deviation from the framework, and in successive consultations it has indicated that future revisions may remove it. Modellers working on CVA capital should expect this exemption to narrow over the next decade.

Why "Basel IV" is a market term, not a BCBS term

The Committee is explicit that BCBS d424 is the "finalisation of Basel III", not a new accord. The reasoning is partly political (calling the package Basel IV would have made the negotiation politically harder because the EU and Switzerland would have faced ratification of a new accord rather than an amendment of an existing one) and partly technical (the package preserves the three-pillar architecture, the capital-quality and buffer rules, and the leverage and liquidity ratios from the 2010 framework, so it is genuinely incremental). The industry, the press, and most national regulators call it Basel IV because the scale of change is more accord-shaped than amendment-shaped. The UK PRA splits the difference by calling it "Basel 3.1". You will see all three names. They refer to the same document.

The post-2017 trajectory: less model, more standardised

BCBS d424 marks a decisive shift in the Basel direction of travel. From 1996 (when the first market-risk internal-model approach was permitted) to 2010 (when Basel III was first published) the trend was towards more internal models, on the theory that they captured risk more accurately. From 2017 onward the trend has been towards floors, standardised approaches, and the retreat from internal models. CVA and operational risk lost their internal-model approaches entirely. A-IRB lost large corporates, banks, financial institutions, and equities. The output floor caps what remains. The implicit BCBS judgement is that internal models, useful as they are for internal risk management, are not robust enough to set capital requirements without standardised backstops. The next round of Basel reforms, whenever it comes, is unlikely to reverse this trend.

What an actuary needs to do operationally

If you work on regulatory capital you will be running two parallel calculations for the rest of your career: the bank's preferred number and the floor-reference number. Your data infrastructure must support both. Your model documentation must address both. Your Pillar 3 disclosures must report both. This doubles the calculation workload and roughly doubles the validation workload, but it also gives the bank a cleaner basis for capital optimisation: every model change can now be evaluated for its effect on both numbers, and any A-IRB model improvement that does not move the floor reference is a wasted effort if the bank is currently floor-bound. Capital optimisation under BCBS d424 is a different game from capital optimisation under Basel II.
