Basel I (1988): the original capital accord

In July 1988 the G10 central bank governors signed a five-page table of numbers that became the foundation of modern banking capital regulation. Basel I introduced the 8 per cent ratio of capital to risk-weighted assets, the split between Tier 1 and Tier 2 capital, the five risk-weight buckets, and the credit-conversion factors for off-balance-sheet exposures. Every subsequent capital accord, every internal-ratings model, every output floor in Basel IV traces its lineage to this single document.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Basel I, formally the International Convergence of Capital Measurement and Capital Standards and signed by the G10 central bank governors on 15 July 1988, is the first international banking-capital accord. It requires every internationally active bank to hold total capital equal to at least 8 per cent of its risk-weighted assets, with at least half of that capital (4 per cent of RWA) sitting in Tier 1, defined as paid-up share capital plus disclosed reserves. Assets are sorted into five risk-weight buckets (0, 10, 20, 50, and 100 per cent), and off-balance-sheet exposures are first multiplied by a credit-conversion factor and then weighted by the counterparty's category. Goodwill is deducted from Tier 1 and unconsolidated banking subsidiaries from total capital. Implementation was to be completed by the end of 1992.

The accord is twenty-eight pages long. Most of the substantive content is in four annexes: the definition of capital, the on-balance-sheet risk weights, the off-balance-sheet credit-conversion factors, and the transitional arrangements. It addresses credit risk only. Market risk was added by the January 1996 Amendment, which introduced a value-at-risk-based capital charge and, for the first time in international banking regulation, allowed banks to compute their own capital requirements using internal models subject to supervisory approval. Operational risk and liquidity risk were left for Basel II and Basel III respectively.

Feynman test Imagine you run a bank and your regulator gives you a simple table. On the left, broad categories of loan: cash, government debt, mortgages, corporate loans. On the right, a number between zero and one hundred per cent. You add up your loans in each category, multiply each total by its number, and add the results. That figure is your "risk-weighted assets". The regulator then tells you: hold capital equal to at least 8 per cent of that figure. Half of it must be the highest-quality capital you can find, basically ordinary shares and retained profits. That is Basel I. The whole thing fits on the back of an envelope. The accord exists because, before 1988, banks in different countries did this arithmetic in different ways, and the regulators wanted everyone using the same envelope.
What is beyond the source The wiki note reproduces the full text of the 1988 accord and lists its four shortcomings. This lecture adds: the macro-political context (the Latin-American debt crisis and the rise of Japanese banks with very thin capital), the negotiating history (the 1987 Anglo-American bilateral that set the agenda), the 1996 Market Risk Amendment in proper detail (covered exposures, the VaR-based standard, and the internal-models option that was the direct precursor to Basel II IRB), and the long afterlife of Basel I (its survival as the conceptual core of the Basel IV output floor, and its enduring use as a sanity-check framework even where Advanced IRB has formally replaced it).
Step 2

Why this matters

Basel I is the moment at which capital adequacy stopped being a domestic supervisory matter and became an internationally negotiated standard. Before 1988, every G10 country had its own definition of bank capital, its own minimum ratio, and its own list of permitted instruments. A Japanese bank could count unrealised gains on equity holdings as capital; a US bank could not. A French bank could count titres participatifs as Tier 1; a German bank had no equivalent instrument. Cross-border comparison was impossible and competitive arbitrage was rampant. Basel I gave the world a single arithmetic.

Two specific anxieties drove the negotiation. The first was the Latin-American debt crisis. Starting with Mexico's default in August 1982, sovereign defaults rolled through Latin America and emerging Asia. The major US, UK, and continental banks were heavily exposed: in 1982 the nine largest US money-centre banks held Latin American sovereign claims equivalent to roughly 175 per cent of their combined capital. The Federal Reserve and the Bank of England spent the next five years watching their own banks slowly absorb losses while running on capital ratios that, on any honest measurement, were dangerously thin. Both supervisors became convinced that the trajectory of declining capital ratios across the G10 had to be reversed, and that no single jurisdiction could do it alone without putting its banks at a competitive disadvantage.

The second anxiety was specifically about Japanese banks. Through the 1980s Japanese banks expanded aggressively into international lending markets, partly because their domestic capital requirements were extremely permissive (in particular, 45 per cent of unrealised gains on Japanese equity holdings could be counted as capital, a treatment that became enormously valuable as the Nikkei rose). By 1987 Japanese banks held five of the top ten global ranking spots by assets, with reported capital ratios that looked respectable by Tokyo's rules but very thin by London's or New York's. US and UK supervisors saw the result as competitive distortion: their own banks were losing market share to Japanese banks that were effectively undercapitalised. The Anglo-American bilateral agreement of January 1987, brokered between the Federal Reserve and the Bank of England, set the template that Basel adopted eighteen months later and was specifically designed to force the Japanese into a common framework.

The "level playing field" rationale, repeated in every BCBS Basel I document, is the polite term for this dynamic. The accord was as much a competition-policy instrument as a prudential one. Its political achievement was to get twelve different national supervisory regimes, each with its own banking lobby and its own definition of capital, to agree on a single number (8 per cent) and a single split (half of it in Tier 1). That achievement has not been matched in any other area of international financial regulation since.

For an actuary working inside a modern bank, four legacies of Basel I are directly load-bearing today. First, the concept of risk-weighted assets survives unchanged: every later accord computes a denominator that is conceptually identical to Basel I's. Second, the tier structure of capital survives: Tier 1 and Tier 2 are still the headline categories, even after Basel III split Tier 1 into CET1 and AT1. Third, the 8 per cent minimum survives literally: every subsequent accord left it untouched and added buffers and surcharges on top. Fourth, the standardised approach to credit risk in Basel IV is recognisably the same construct, with finer-grained risk weights but the same logic: classify the exposure, apply a number, sum, multiply by 8 per cent.

Step 3

The mental model

The whole accord reduces to a single inequality. Total capital, computed according to a precise definition, must be at least 8 per cent of risk-weighted assets, computed according to a precise table. The numerator is a sum of capital tiers with limits; the denominator is a sum of exposures multiplied by risk weights. Once you know what counts in each, you can compute the ratio for any bank in twenty minutes.

Three properties of this construction are worth fixing in mind. First, the ratio is a relative measure: a bank with £100 of corporate loans needs £8 of capital, but the same £100 of cash needs none, because risk weights modulate the requirement. Second, the ratio is additive: the RWA of a portfolio is the sum of its component RWAs, with no correlation or diversification benefit. Third, the threshold is uniform: every G10 bank is held to the same 8 per cent, regardless of size, geography, or business model. All three properties were eventually relaxed (correlation entered via the IRB asymptotic single risk-factor model in Basel II; surcharges by bank type entered via G-SIB buffers in Basel III), but the 1988 framework began with this clean simplicity.

Step 4

The detail

Constituents of capital: Tier 1

The accord defines Tier 1, the "core" or "basic equity" tier, as comprising only two elements: paid-up share capital (issued and fully paid ordinary shares or common stock, plus non-cumulative perpetual preference shares) and disclosed reserves. Disclosed reserves are post-tax retained earnings shown explicitly on the published balance sheet, plus share premium, legal reserves, and general reserves. In consolidated accounts, minority interests in the equity of subsidiaries that are less than wholly owned are included. Revaluation reserves and cumulative preference shares are explicitly excluded from Tier 1.

The Committee's reasoning, laid out in paragraph 1 of section I, is that equity capital and disclosed reserves are the only element of capital common to all G10 banking systems, are wholly visible in published accounts, and form the basis on which markets judge capital adequacy. Tier 1 is the test of quality the Committee was prepared to enforce internationally; everything else was a compromise to bring national variants into the tent.

Paid-up share capital
Issued and fully paid ordinary shares (common stock) and non-cumulative perpetual preferred stock. Permanent, fully loss-absorbing, with no fixed maturity and no obligation to pay dividends. Cumulative preference shares are excluded from Tier 1 because the obligation to pay accrued dividends compromises loss absorbency.
Disclosed reserves
Reserves created or increased by appropriation of retained earnings, share premium, retained profit, general reserves, and legal reserves, all visible on the face of the published balance sheet. Their disclosure is what distinguishes them from undisclosed reserves and makes them eligible for the higher tier.
Minority interests
In consolidated accounts, the share of equity in subsidiaries held by external shareholders is admitted to Tier 1 at the consolidated level, on the rationale that it absorbs losses at the subsidiary just as the parent's equity does.

Constituents of capital: Tier 2

Tier 2, "supplementary" capital, comprises five categories that share a weaker claim to loss absorbency than Tier 1. The Committee admitted them because each had a substantial constituency in at least one G10 country, and excluding them would have made the accord unsignable. The categories are undisclosed reserves, asset revaluation reserves, general provisions or general loan-loss reserves, hybrid debt-equity instruments, and subordinated term debt.

Undisclosed reserves
Reserves that have been passed through the profit and loss account and are accepted by the supervisor, but are not shown separately on the published balance sheet. Treated as equity in substance but invisible to outside scrutiny. Admitted to Tier 2 only because Japan and a small number of continental European jurisdictions held large sums in this form.
Asset revaluation reserves
Arise either from explicit revaluation of fixed assets (such as bank premises) at current market value, or as latent unrealised gains on long-term equity holdings carried at historic cost. Latent revaluation reserves on equity holdings are admitted at a 55 per cent discount to the difference between historic cost and market value, to reflect price volatility and the notional tax that would crystallise on sale. This 55 per cent haircut was the key concession to Japanese banks.
General provisions / general loan-loss reserves
Provisions held against future, presently unidentified losses, freely available to absorb whatever losses subsequently materialise. Specific provisions, those ascribed to identified impairment of particular assets, are excluded because they represent a reduction in asset value rather than a buffer of capital. General provisions are capped at 1.25 percentage points of risk-weighted assets, with a temporary exception allowing up to 2.0 points.
Hybrid debt-equity instruments
Instruments combining features of equity and debt: unsecured, subordinated, fully paid up, not redeemable at the holder's initiative, available to absorb losses on a going-concern basis without forcing liquidation, and with service obligations deferrable. Cumulative preference shares qualify. National examples named in the accord include UK perpetual subordinated debt, French titres participatifs and titres subordonnés à durée indéterminée, German Genusscheine, and US mandatory convertible debt.
Subordinated term debt
Conventional unsecured subordinated debt with a minimum original maturity of over five years, plus limited-life redeemable preference shares. In the last five years to maturity, a cumulative discount of 20 per cent per year is applied to reflect the diminishing remaining loss-absorbing life. Capped at 50 per cent of Tier 1, because these instruments do not absorb losses on a going-concern basis and only become loss-absorbing in liquidation.

Limits, restrictions, and deductions

The accord imposes four numerical limits that, taken together, ensure Tier 2 cannot dominate the capital base. Tier 2 in aggregate cannot exceed 100 per cent of Tier 1, so Tier 1 must be at least half of total capital. Subordinated term debt within Tier 2 cannot exceed 50 per cent of Tier 1. General provisions within Tier 2 are capped at 1.25 percentage points of RWA. Latent revaluation reserves on equities are admitted only at a 55 per cent discount.

Two deductions then bite. Goodwill is deducted from Tier 1, on the grounds that it is an accounting artefact with no realisable value in resolution. Investments in unconsolidated banking and financial subsidiaries are deducted from total capital, to prevent the same capital being counted twice (once at the parent and once at the subsidiary). National authorities also retain discretion to deduct holdings of capital issued by other banks, to limit the "double-gearing" risk that systemic shocks transmit through cross-holdings of bank capital.

Watch out The 1988 accord does not deduct investments in non-financial subsidiaries from capital, does not deduct deferred tax assets, does not deduct defined-benefit pension surplus, and applies no threshold-based deductions of the sort introduced in Basel III. The Basel I deduction list is striking by what it omits. Most of the deductions you encounter in a modern capital plan were added later, typically because each one had been used in practice by some bank to overstate its real loss-absorbing capacity.

The five risk-weight buckets

Annex 2 of the accord assigns every on-balance-sheet asset to one of five risk-weight buckets: 0, 10, 20, 50, and 100 per cent. The 10 per cent bucket is rarely used in practice; the working buckets in most banks' balance sheets are 0, 20, 50, and 100. The structure embeds two organising principles that became permanent features of every later accord. First, sovereigns are treated more favourably than banks, and banks more favourably than corporates. Second, the favourable treatment of sovereigns and banks depends on OECD membership, a proxy for credit quality that aged badly.

0 per cent
Cash; claims on central governments and central banks denominated in the national currency and funded in that currency. The "zero-weight sovereign" treatment applies regardless of the sovereign's actual credit rating, on the assumption that a central bank can always print its own currency to repay claims denominated in it.
0, 10, 20, or 50 per cent (national discretion)
Claims on domestic public-sector entities other than central government, and loans guaranteed by such entities. National supervisors choose the weight from this menu, producing wide cross-border variation in how municipal and regional-government exposures are treated.
20 per cent
Claims on multilateral development banks (World Bank, EBRD, ADB, IDB) and claims they guarantee; claims on banks incorporated in the OECD and on banks incorporated outside the OECD with a residual maturity up to one year. The short-residual-maturity rule was a workaround to allow trade-finance and interbank lending to emerging-market banks at favourable weights.
50 per cent
Loans fully secured by mortgage on residential property that is or will be occupied by the borrower or is rented. The 50 per cent weight applies regardless of the LTV ratio of the mortgage, so a 95 per cent LTV loan receives the same treatment as a 50 per cent LTV loan: a glaring example of the lack of differentiation that later motivated Basel II.
100 per cent
All other assets: corporate loans, claims on non-OECD banks with residual maturity over one year, premises and other fixed assets, equity investments in non-banking entities, and any exposure not specifically slotted elsewhere. The 100 per cent bucket is the dustbin where everything goes that does not fit the favourable categories above, and in practice it dominates the RWA total of any commercially active bank.

Off-balance-sheet exposures and credit-conversion factors

Annex 3 covers off-balance-sheet exposures: items that are not loans on the balance sheet today but may give rise to credit exposure in the future. The framework converts each off-balance-sheet item into a credit-equivalent amount by multiplying its notional by a credit-conversion factor (CCF), and then applies the on-balance-sheet risk weight appropriate to the counterparty. Four CCF categories are defined.

100 per cent CCF
Direct credit substitutes: general guarantees of indebtedness, standby letters of credit serving as financial guarantees, acceptances. Economically equivalent to a loan, so converted at face value.
50 per cent CCF
Transaction-related contingent items: performance bonds, bid bonds, warranties, standby letters of credit related to particular transactions. Less likely to crystallise than direct credit substitutes.
20 per cent CCF
Short-term self-liquidating trade-related contingent items, such as documentary credits collateralised by the underlying shipments.
0 per cent CCF
Commitments with an original maturity of up to one year, or which can be unconditionally cancelled at any time. The zero treatment of cancellable commitments was widely exploited and was tightened in later accords.

Derivatives received a separate, more elaborate treatment, because the credit exposure on a swap or forward depends on the current mark-to-market plus an "add-on" for potential future exposure. Annex 3 sets out add-on percentages varying with residual maturity and underlying (interest rate vs. exchange rate vs. equity vs. precious metals vs. other commodities), in what became known as the "current exposure method" for counterparty credit risk. This method survived almost unchanged until SA-CCR replaced it in 2017.

The target standard ratio

Section III of the accord, two paragraphs long, sets the target standard ratio of capital to weighted risk assets at 8 per cent, of which the core (Tier 1) element will be at least 4 per cent. The ratio was to be observed by internationally active banks in member countries by the end of 1992, giving a transitional period of roughly four and a half years from publication. An interim minimum of 7.25 per cent applied from end-1990. The number 8 per cent was the product of negotiation rather than analysis: it sat above what most G10 banks held in 1988 (the US money-centre banks were running closer to 6 per cent) and was deemed politically achievable in four years.

The accord describes 8 per cent as a "minimum" and notes that supervisors may set higher ratios at their discretion. The Bank of England and the Federal Reserve immediately did so. The PRA's modern "Pillar 2A" add-ons and the Federal Reserve's stress-test-derived requirements are the direct descendants of this discretion.

The 1996 Market Risk Amendment

Eight years after Basel I, in January 1996, the Committee published the Amendment to the Capital Accord to Incorporate Market Risks, with effect from end-1997. The amendment was the first significant retrofit of the framework and is the precursor of every subsequent move towards model-based regulatory capital.

The amendment introduced a capital charge for the market-risk exposures arising from a bank's trading-book positions, plus its banking-book exposures to foreign exchange and commodities. Covered exposures comprised four categories: interest-rate risk in the trading book, equity risk in the trading book, foreign-exchange risk (trading and banking book), and commodities risk (trading and banking book). Options across all four categories were addressed by a separate, more elaborate treatment to capture non-linearity.

Two approaches were offered. The standardised approach used a building-block methodology: separate specific-risk and general-market-risk capital charges, computed via prescribed risk weights and offsets, broadly analogous to the credit-risk standardised approach. The internal-models approach permitted banks, subject to supervisory approval, to compute their market-risk capital charge using their own value-at-risk model. The VaR-based charge was the higher of the previous day's VaR and the average of the daily VaR over the preceding sixty business days, multiplied by a supervisory factor (minimum 3) that scaled up the bank's own model output to provide a prudential buffer. Banks had to demonstrate model adequacy through quantitative standards (99 per cent one-tailed confidence interval, ten-day holding period, minimum one year of historical data) and qualitative standards (independent risk control, daily backtesting, regular stress testing).

The internal-models approach is the single most consequential innovation in the post-1988 evolution of bank capital regulation. For the first time, an internationally agreed prudential rule said: under conditions, the regulator will accept the bank's own quantitative model as the basis for capital. That principle was the foundation on which Basel II built the Foundation and Advanced IRB approaches for credit risk and the Advanced Measurement Approach for operational risk, and on which Basel III built the Internal Model Method for counterparty credit risk. It is also the principle that Basel IV has spent the last decade partially rolling back, through input floors, output floors, and the elimination of internal models for operational and CVA risk.

Worked example: a small balance sheet under Basel I

Consider a stylised internationally active bank with four asset classes on its balance sheet. Apply the Basel I risk weights to each, sum the risk-weighted amounts, and multiply by 8 per cent to find the capital requirement. The arithmetic that follows is the complete Basel I compliance check.

Asset class Notional Weight RWA ------------------------------------- ----------- -------- ----------- Cash and OECD-sovereign holdings 2,000 m 0% 0 m Interbank claims on OECD banks 1,500 m 20% 300 m Residential mortgages, owner-occupied 3,000 m 50% 1,500 m Corporate loans 3,500 m 100% 3,500 m ------------------------------------- ----------- -------- ----------- Total on-balance-sheet 10,000 m 5,300 m Off-balance-sheet (illustrative): Standby LC, corporate counterparty 500 m × 100% CCF × 100% = 500 m Undrawn commitment, < 1 yr, cancellable 800 m × 0% CCF × 100% = 0 m ------- Add to RWA 500 m Total RWA 5,800 m Minimum total capital = 8% × 5,800 m = 464 m Minimum Tier 1 capital = 4% × 5,800 m = 232 m

The capital requirement of 464 million on a balance sheet of 10 billion translates to a Basel I leverage equivalent of 4.64 per cent: capital equal to 4.64 per cent of nominal assets, but 8 per cent of risk-weighted assets. The difference comes from the favourable risk weights on cash, sovereigns, OECD bank claims, and mortgages, which together account for 65 per cent of the balance sheet but only 31 per cent of RWA. The arithmetic is mechanical and produces a single number; the policy choices are buried entirely in the choice of risk weights and CCFs.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The four shortcomings, in plain English

Basel I had four well-known weaknesses that the BCBS itself acknowledged from the mid-1990s onwards and that motivated the entire Basel II project. They are worth stating precisely because every subsequent accord can be read as a response to one or more of them.

The first is lack of credit-quality differentiation. All corporate loans received the same 100 per cent risk weight regardless of the borrower's credit quality. A loan to AAA-rated General Electric required exactly the same capital as a loan to a B-rated leveraged-buyout vehicle. By the late 1990s, syndicated-loan and credit-default-swap markets were pricing these exposures at radically different spreads, demonstrating that the market saw the risks as wholly different even though the accord did not. Basel II's Standardised and IRB approaches were both attempts to fix this.

The second is lack of correlation and diversification. RWA is computed additively, with no recognition that a portfolio of uncorrelated 100 per cent-weighted exposures is less risky than the same notional concentrated in a single name. A bank could not benefit from sectoral or geographical diversification, nor was it penalised for concentration. Basel II's IRB approach embedded an asymptotic single risk-factor model that introduced an implicit correlation parameter, though it still does not reward diversification at the portfolio level.

The third is narrow risk coverage. Basel I addressed credit risk, with country-transfer risk treated as a subset. Market risk arrived only in the 1996 Amendment. Operational risk, liquidity risk, interest-rate risk in the banking book, concentration risk, and reputational risk were ignored entirely. The post-1988 history of bank failures (Barings 1995, LTCM 1998, the early-2000s op-risk losses, the 2008 liquidity collapse) is largely a history of risks that Basel I did not cover.

The fourth is static treatment. The 8 per cent ratio applied at all times, with no countercyclical adjustment. A bank that built up exposures in a boom faced no incremental capital requirement; a bank running down its book in a recession got no relief. The Basel III conservation buffer, countercyclical capital buffer, and stress-testing framework all address this static-framework critique.

How banks gamed Basel I, and why the gaming hollowed it out

By the mid-1990s, banks had identified two structural arbitrages in the accord that systematically eroded its prudential intent. The first was regulatory-capital arbitrage through securitisation. A bank could pool 100 per cent-weighted corporate loans, sell them into a securitisation vehicle, retain the equity tranche (which absorbed most of the credit risk), and find that its RWA had fallen by 90 per cent or more even though the bank's economic exposure to the pool was largely unchanged. The retained risk was effectively unweighted under Basel I because the vehicle was off-balance-sheet. Basel II's revised securitisation framework was the response, and the framework had to be fundamentally rewritten again after the 2008 crisis revealed its remaining flaws.

The second was credit-derivative shaping. Banks bought credit-default-swap protection on their highest-quality 100 per cent-weighted loans (AAA corporates) and retained their lower-quality 100 per cent-weighted loans (B-rated corporates), because both received the same capital treatment but the AAA loans paid a smaller spread relative to that capital. This systematically tilted bank portfolios towards the riskier end of any given risk-weight bucket, exactly the opposite of the prudential intent. The phenomenon was sufficiently well documented in the late 1990s that it became one of the headline justifications for Basel II.

Why the OECD/non-OECD split aged badly

Basel I used OECD membership as a proxy for sovereign and bank credit quality. In 1988 this was a defensible shorthand; OECD members were almost all developed economies with investment-grade sovereign ratings. By the late 1990s the proxy had broken down. Mexico joined the OECD in 1994 and defaulted on its short-term external debt within months. South Korea joined in 1996 and became the epicentre of the 1997 Asian crisis. Turkey, an OECD member since 1961, experienced repeated banking crises. Meanwhile non-OECD Singapore had a stronger credit rating than several OECD members. Basel II replaced the OECD-membership criterion with external credit ratings (under the Standardised approach) and internal ratings (under IRB), removing the political distortion but introducing dependence on the credit-rating agencies that has since drawn its own criticism.

Why Basel I still surfaces in practice

Basel I was formally superseded by Basel II in 2007 in most G10 jurisdictions, but the framework has never fully gone away, for three reasons that matter to a working actuary.

First, the standardised approach in Basel II, III, and IV is recognisably Basel I refined. The structure is the same: classify the exposure, apply a risk weight, sum, multiply by 8 per cent. The risk weights are finer and depend on external ratings or, under Basel IV, on prescribed risk drivers, but the architecture is identical. A bank that understands Basel I can read a Basel IV standardised-approach calculation almost without retraining.

Second, the output floor in Basel IV is Basel I's revenge. The output floor caps the benefit of internal models at 27.5 per cent of standardised RWA (equivalently, internal-model RWA cannot fall below 72.5 per cent of standardised RWA). Because the standardised approach is itself an evolved Basel I, the output floor functionally re-anchors capital requirements to a Basel-I-style standardised computation, even for banks otherwise using A-IRB. For many large banks, the binding constraint on regulatory capital from 2026 onwards will be a Basel-I-derived number multiplied by 0.725.

Third, the conceptual furniture of Basel I survives unchanged: the 8 per cent ratio, the tier structure, the credit-equivalent treatment of off-balance-sheet, the CCF concept, the deduction approach to goodwill and unconsolidated subsidiaries. These are the load-bearing components of every modern capital regime. The Basel I document is short, readable, and still the cleanest exposition of the architecture; it remains worth reading in the original.

Watch out Do not describe Basel I as "obsolete". It is superseded as a binding standard in most jurisdictions, but its conceptual core is alive in the Basel IV standardised approach, the output floor, the leverage ratio (which is conceptually a Basel-I-style ratio with all weights set to 100 per cent), and the design of every national rulebook. An exam answer that treats Basel I as a museum exhibit will lose marks; the framework is the lattice on which everything else hangs.

  1. State the Basel I target standard ratio in full. What is the minimum Tier 1 ratio implied by it, and by what date were internationally active G10 banks expected to meet it?
  2. List the five constituents of Tier 2 capital under Basel I. For each, state the principal limit or restriction imposed by the accord.
  3. Why does Basel I impose a 50 per cent cap on subordinated term debt within Tier 2, and a 100 per cent cap on Tier 2 as a whole? Frame the answer in terms of going-concern versus gone-concern loss absorbency.
  4. A bank holds 1,000 of cash, 500 of claims on OECD banks, 2,000 of owner-occupied residential mortgages, and 1,500 of corporate loans. Compute its risk-weighted assets and its minimum total-capital requirement under Basel I.
  5. Describe two ways in which banks systematically arbitraged Basel I in the 1990s. Why did each arbitrage erode the prudential intent of the accord?
  6. What did the January 1996 Market Risk Amendment add to the Basel I framework? Distinguish the standardised approach from the internal-models approach, and state the principal innovation of the internal-models approach.
  7. Explain why the OECD-membership criterion used in the 20 per cent and short-residual-maturity risk weights aged badly after 1994. Give two specific country examples.
  8. The 1988 accord deducts goodwill from Tier 1 and unconsolidated banking subsidiaries from total capital. Why those two specifically, and why was the Basel I deduction list so much shorter than the modern one?
  9. State the four shortcomings of Basel I as identified by the BCBS itself. For each, name the post-1988 development (Basel II, Basel III, or the 1996 Amendment) that was designed to address it.
  10. Explain how the Basel IV output floor at 72.5 per cent of standardised RWA effectively re-anchors capital to Basel I logic. Why does this matter for an A-IRB bank's capital plan from 2026 onwards?
