Supporting BCBS standards: governance (BCBS 328) and IRRBB principles (BCBS 368)

Two of the BCBS's most consequential non-capital standards. The 2015 corporate governance principles for banks (BCBS 328) set out how a bank's board should be composed, what it should do, and how its three lines of defence should fit together. The 2016 interest rate risk in the banking book standard (BCBS 368) sets out how banks should identify, measure, monitor, and control IRRBB, introduces the six standardised shock scenarios, and ties the whole framework to a supervisory outlier test calibrated to Tier 1 capital. Together they answer the two questions a supervisor asks of every bank: who is in charge of the risk, and how much of it is actually there.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Two short documents, two big jobs. BCBS 328, Corporate Governance Principles for Banks, published in July 2015, sets out 13 principles that together describe how a bank should be run from the top: what the board does, how senior management implements board direction, how the risk-management function and internal audit sit alongside the business, how compensation is aligned with prudent risk-taking, and how the whole system is disclosed to the outside world. BCBS 368, Interest Rate Risk in the Banking Book, published in April 2016, sets out 12 principles for banks and 3 for supervisors covering one specific risk: the risk that movements in market interest rates change the economic value or the future earnings of the bank's non-trading book. It introduces a standardised set of six interest-rate shocks and a supervisory outlier test which compares the worst-case Economic Value of Equity (EVE) loss with the bank's Tier 1 capital, at a 15 per cent threshold revised down from the 20 per cent of own funds in the predecessor 2004 standard.

The two standards sit in Pillar 2 of the Basel framework, not Pillar 1. Neither produces a binding capital number. Both are enforced through supervisory review: the supervisor inspects the bank's governance arrangements against BCBS 328 and the bank's IRRBB measurement and management against BCBS 368, and may impose a Pillar 2 capital add-on, a remediation programme, or governance interventions if the bank falls short. The 2023 failure of Silicon Valley Bank, an IRRBB blow-up that materialised inside the banking book of a US regional, is the most visible demonstration of why governance and IRRBB are now treated as inseparable: an inadequate board oversight regime and an unhedged interest-rate position produced both failures together.

Feynman test Imagine a hospital. BCBS 328 is the rulebook for how the hospital is run: who is on the board, what the chief executive does, how the audit committee checks the accounts, how the doctors and nurses are paid, how complaints are handled. It says nothing about how to treat patients. BCBS 368 is the protocol for one specific disease: how to test for it, how to measure how bad it is, what counts as a dangerous level, and what to do if a patient's measurement crosses the line. The hospital must apply both. A well-run hospital with a bad protocol for that disease kills patients. A great protocol applied by a chaotic hospital kills patients too. Banking regulators learned in 2008, and again in 2023, that you cannot have one without the other.
What is beyond the source The wiki source files in bcbs_144.md and bcbs_188.md cover the 2008 sound principles for liquidity risk management and the Basel III liquidity framework; they do not cover governance or IRRBB. This lecture builds out the two missing topics from the BCBS publications directly: BCBS 328 (the July 2015 governance principles, which superseded the 2010 Enhancing Corporate Governance for Banking Organisations) and BCBS 368 (the April 2016 IRRBB principles, which superseded the 2004 Principles for the Management and Supervision of Interest Rate Risk). It adds the six standardised shock scenarios, the supervisory outlier test, the three lines of defence framing (which BCBS 328 codified for banks), and the EBA Guidelines on IRRBB (EBA/GL/2018/02 and the 2022 update under CRD V) that translate BCBS 368 into binding EU law.
Step 2

Why this matters

The 2008 to 2009 crisis was not solely a capital crisis. The Walker Review in the UK, the Senior Supervisors Group reports across the G7, and the BCBS's own 2010 review all concluded that governance failures at board and senior-management level were a primary, in some cases the primary, cause of bank failures. The Royal Bank of Scotland's board approved the 2007 ABN AMRO acquisition with limited due diligence and against the advice of its own treasury function. HBOS's board permitted a corporate lending strategy whose risk profile was not understood by either the board or the regulator. Lehman Brothers' board signed off on accounting treatments (the Repo 105 transactions) that misrepresented the firm's leverage. In each case the formal structures of governance existed but did not function. BCBS 328 is the codified response: the supervisor will now inspect not just whether the board exists but whether it does its job.

IRRBB became materially more important between 2008 and 2024 for a single reason: the rate cycle. Through 2008 to 2021 central bank policy rates in most developed economies stayed at the effective lower bound, sometimes negative. Banks earned compressed margins on deposit franchises whose economic value depended on rates rising again. Many banks responded by extending the duration of their asset book, locking in higher yields, often without fully hedging the resulting interest-rate position. When inflation arrived in 2022 and policy rates rose by 400 to 500 basis points within 18 months, the unrealised losses on those long-dated assets crystallised. Silicon Valley Bank in March 2023 was the most spectacular case: an investment book of high-quality long-dated US Treasuries and agency MBS sat at a fair-value loss that exceeded the bank's tangible common equity, the deposit franchise that was supposed to fund it became unstable when uninsured depositors realised the position, and the bank collapsed inside 48 hours. SVB was the textbook IRRBB realisation that BCBS 368 was designed to detect and prevent.

For an actuary working inside a bank, three operational facts follow. First, the board you report to is the subject of BCBS 328: your work product is consumed by the board's risk committee, and the supervisor will judge that consumption. Second, the IRRBB measurement frame in your bank, the calibration of the six scenarios, the behavioural assumptions for non-maturity deposits, and the supervisory outlier test result, are now standard supervisory questions in any Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP) review. Third, the EU is moving parts of IRRBB from Pillar 2 into Pillar 1 under CRD VI; the lecture covers this in section 5. Your model in 2026 sits in Pillar 2; your model in 2030 may sit in Pillar 1 in the EU.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture two panels side by side. On the left, the governance pyramid: board at the top with its risk and audit committees, senior management in the middle, and three lines of defence below them, each owning a different responsibility for the same risk. On the right, a yield curve being deformed in six different ways: the six standardised shocks of BCBS 368. The governance picture says who decides how much IRRBB is acceptable. The yield-curve picture says how the bank measures whether it has more IRRBB than the chosen level.

Three flows of authority tie the two panels together. Risk appetite flows down: the board approves an IRRBB limit (typically expressed as a maximum EVE sensitivity to the six shocks, plus a maximum NII sensitivity), the CRO operationalises it, and the treasury function manages day-to-day positioning against it. Measurement flows up: treasury, risk, and finance jointly produce the six-scenario EVE and NII numbers, which are reported to the asset-liability committee (ALCO), then to the risk committee, then to the board. Assurance flows sideways: internal audit periodically validates that the IRRBB measurement framework is what the board approved, that the limits are observed, and that the numbers in the board pack are the numbers coming out of the model.

Step 4

The detail

Part A: BCBS 328 governance principles

BCBS 328 was published in July 2015 and replaced the 2010 document Principles for Enhancing Corporate Governance. The 2015 revision was prompted by the post-crisis governance reviews mentioned in section 2 and by the wider work of the FSB on board effectiveness. The standard contains 13 principles. The first 12 are addressed to banks, the 13th to supervisors. Each principle is supported by detailed sub-principles; the summary below captures the operative content of each.

Principle 1: Board's overall responsibilities
The board has overall responsibility for the bank, including approving and overseeing senior management's implementation of the bank's strategic objectives, governance framework, and corporate culture. The board sets the risk appetite, approves the risk-management framework, and is ultimately accountable for the bank's safe and sound operation. Critically, the standard makes clear that delegation does not transfer accountability: the board cannot delegate away its overall responsibility for the bank.
Principle 2: Board qualifications and composition
Board members should be and should remain qualified, individually and collectively, for their positions. They should understand their oversight and corporate governance role and be able to exercise sound, objective judgement. A board should include a sufficient number of independent non-executive directors to bring independent challenge. The board's collective skills mix should cover risk management, financial expertise, legal, technology, and the business areas the bank operates in.
Principle 3: Board's own structure and practices
The board should define appropriate governance structures and practices for its own work, and put in place the means for such practices to be followed and periodically reviewed. This covers the role of the chair (who should be independent or, if executive, balanced by a strong senior independent director), the committee structure (audit, risk, nomination, compensation), the size and composition of committees, and the use of self-evaluation against a published charter.
Principle 4: Senior management
Under the direction and oversight of the board, senior management should carry out and manage the bank's activities in a manner consistent with the business strategy, risk appetite, and policies approved by the board. The CEO, CFO, and CRO are the standard senior-management trio. The principle is explicit that senior management is accountable to the board for the day-to-day running of the bank.
Principle 5: Governance of group structures
In a group, the board of the parent has overall responsibility for the group and for ensuring the establishment and operation of a clear governance framework appropriate to the structure, business, and risks of the group and its entities. The principle addresses the conflict between local subsidiary boards (which have legal duties to the subsidiary) and the group board (which has duties to the consolidated entity). Group-wide risk identification and a consistent risk-management framework across the group are required.
Principle 6: Risk management function
Banks should have an effective independent risk management function, under the direction of a chief risk officer (CRO), with sufficient stature, independence, resources, and access to the board. The CRO should report directly to the board's risk committee (with a dotted line to the CEO for administrative matters), should have the authority to influence decisions that affect the bank's risk exposure, and should not be removed without prior consultation with the board.
Principle 7: Risk identification, monitoring, and controlling
Risks should be identified, monitored, and controlled on an ongoing bank-wide and individual entity basis. The sophistication of the risk-management and internal control infrastructure should keep pace with changes to the risk profile, the external risk landscape, and industry practice. This principle covers the technical machinery: risk measurement methodologies, limit frameworks, stress testing, and model validation.
Principle 8: Risk communication
An effective risk governance framework requires robust communication within the bank about risk, both across the organisation and through reporting to the board and senior management. The principle is shorthand for "the right information, to the right people, at the right time". Risk reporting must be comprehensive, timely, accurate, and actionable, in line with BCBS 239 on risk-data aggregation.
Principle 9: Compliance
The bank's board of directors is responsible for overseeing the management of the bank's compliance risk. The board should establish a compliance function and approve the bank's policies and processes for identifying, assessing, monitoring, reporting, and advising on compliance risk. The compliance function should be independent of management.
Principle 10: Internal audit
The internal audit function should provide independent assurance to the board and should support the board and senior management in promoting an effective governance process and the long-term soundness of the bank. Internal audit reports to the audit committee of the board, not to the CEO. This is the structural guarantee of Line 3 independence.
Principle 11: Compensation
The bank's remuneration structure should support sound corporate governance and risk management. Variable remuneration should align with prudent risk-taking, with deferral, malus, and clawback applied to material risk-takers. Compensation should be adjusted for all types of risk, symmetric with risk outcomes (loss as well as gain), and sensitive to the time horizon of risks. This principle is the codification of the FSB Principles for Sound Compensation Practices.
Principle 12: Disclosure and transparency
The governance of the bank should be adequately transparent to its shareholders, depositors, other relevant stakeholders, and market participants. The disclosure should include governance structures and policies, the bank's risk-management framework, the qualifications of board members, the composition of committees, and material related-party transactions.
Principle 13: The role of supervisors
Supervisors should provide guidance for and supervise corporate governance at banks, including through comprehensive evaluations and regular interaction with boards and senior management. They should require improvement and remedial action as necessary, and should share information on corporate governance with other supervisors. This principle is addressed to the supervisor, not the bank, and explicitly authorises supervisory intervention in governance matters.

The three lines of defence model

BCBS 328 endorses, without literally legislating, the three lines of defence model that has become the standard organisational frame for risk management in banks. The model originated in the internal-audit profession (the IIA published its position paper on three lines in 2013, updated to "Three Lines Model" in 2020) and was adopted by supervisors as a useful way to describe how a bank should be organised internally so that each risk has a clear owner, a clear overseer, and an independent assurer.

Line 1: business and operations
The risk owner. Anyone in a revenue-generating or operations function who originates, manages, or operationally handles a risk. Front-office traders, lending officers, treasury dealers, branch managers, IT operations, and product owners are all Line 1. Their job is to make the day-to-day decisions that create or change the risk position, within the limits and policies set by the board. They are accountable for those decisions and for the controls embedded in their own processes.
Line 2: risk management and compliance
The overseer. Specialist risk and compliance functions independent of Line 1. The CRO function (with its sub-disciplines: credit risk, market risk, operational risk, IRRBB, liquidity risk, model risk) and the compliance function are both Line 2. Their job is to set the risk policy, design the limit framework, run the second-look monitoring on Line 1's risk-taking, perform independent measurement of risk exposures, and challenge Line 1 where appropriate. Independence is structural: Line 2 does not report into a revenue function and is funded outside it.
Line 3: internal audit
The assurer. Internal audit, reporting to the board's audit committee, not to the CEO. Its job is to give the board independent assurance that Lines 1 and 2 are doing their jobs: that the controls Line 1 says it has, exist; that the risk measurement Line 2 produces, is accurate; that the policies the board approved, are being followed. Internal audit does not own or oversee risk in the operational sense; it audits the people who do. The structural feature that makes Line 3 work is the reporting line into the audit committee, which insulates it from CEO and CFO pressure.

The model is not a magic formula. It works when each line is staffed, resourced, and empowered to do its job; it fails when, for example, Line 2 risk is staffed too thinly to challenge Line 1, or when internal audit is treated as a junior career stop rather than a senior-grade function. Supervisory examinations now routinely include an assessment of three-lines-of-defence effectiveness, with particular attention to CRO seniority, internal-audit reporting lines, and the resources and turnover of Line 2.

Part B: BCBS 368 IRRBB principles

BCBS 368 was published in April 2016 and replaced the 2004 Principles for the Management and Supervision of Interest Rate Risk. The standard contains 12 principles for banks (numbered 1 to 12) and 3 for supervisors (numbered 1 to 3). The big change between the 2004 standard and the 2016 standard was the introduction of the six standardised shock scenarios and the supervisory outlier test linked to Tier 1 capital. The standard's effective implementation date was 2018, with EU transposition through the EBA Guidelines (EBA/GL/2018/02 and the 2022 update).

Scope of IRRBB
The risk to a bank's capital and earnings arising from adverse movements in interest rates that affect banking-book positions. Banking book is everything that is not in the trading book: customer loans, customer deposits, treasury holdings of bonds for liquidity, derivatives used to hedge banking-book positions. Trading-book interest-rate risk is covered by FRTB under Pillar 1, not by BCBS 368. The distinction matters because IRRBB is an accrual-accounting risk (gains and losses crystallise over time as positions reprice) while trading-book risk is a fair-value risk (gains and losses crystallise immediately through P&L).
Three IRRBB sub-types
Gap risk is the risk that the timing of repricing on the asset side does not match the timing of repricing on the liability side; for example, fixed-rate mortgages funded by floating-rate deposits. Basis risk is the risk that two floating-rate positions referenced to different benchmarks (for example, SONIA-linked loans funded by Bank Rate-linked deposits) do not move together. Option risk is the risk that customer behavioural options (mortgage prepayment, deposit redemption, embedded caps and floors) change the timing or amount of cash flows in response to interest-rate moves.
EVE: Economic Value of Equity
The present value of all expected banking-book cash flows, calculated on a run-off basis (no new business). EVE sensitivity to a shock is the change in this present value, dEVE = PV(post-shock) − PV(pre-shock). EVE is a long-horizon, balance-sheet-wide measure. It captures the economic effect of a rate move on the bank's franchise. EVE is the metric used in the supervisory outlier test.
NII: Net Interest Income
Projected net interest income over a 12-month horizon (some banks extend to 24 or 36 months), typically calculated on a constant-balance-sheet assumption (new business replaces maturing business at prevailing market rates). NII sensitivity is dNII under each shock. NII is a short-horizon, earnings-based measure. It captures the near-term P&L effect of a rate move. Both EVE and NII are required by BCBS 368.

The six standardised shocks

BCBS 368 specifies six standardised yield-curve shocks that banks must apply when calculating EVE sensitivity for the supervisory outlier test. The shocks are calibrated by currency using a historical-distribution method (a 99th-percentile move over a six-year rolling window) with floors and caps to prevent unrealistic calibrations. The 2016 calibrations for the major currencies are: parallel shock of ±200 basis points for USD, GBP, EUR; ±100 basis points for JPY; ±400 basis points for some emerging-market currencies. Short and long shocks are larger and smaller than the parallel shock respectively, with a tapering shape.

1. Parallel up
All tenors of the yield curve shift up by the calibrated parallel amount. Tests sensitivity to a uniform rate rise. Typically the most painful scenario for banks with long-duration fixed-rate assets funded by short-duration liabilities (the classic banking-book exposure).
2. Parallel down
All tenors shift down by the calibrated parallel amount, subject to a post-shock floor (rates cannot go below the post-shock floor, originally negative for major currencies). Tests sensitivity to a uniform rate fall. Painful for banks that hold large non-maturity deposit franchises whose economic value depends on the deposit rate being below the market rate.
3. Steepener
Short rates fall, long rates rise. The yield curve gets steeper. Typically benign for banks with the classic borrow-short-lend-long profile (their reinvestment yields improve and funding costs fall). Important to test because the bank's hedging may have inadvertently created a flattening exposure.
4. Flattener
Short rates rise, long rates fall. The yield curve flattens or inverts. Painful for banks with the classic profile (funding costs rise faster than asset yields). The exact scenario that drove margin compression at many US regional banks in 2022 to 2023.
5. Short rate up
Short rates rise, long rates relatively unchanged. Tests the bank's sensitivity to a near-term tightening cycle. Effect is concentrated in floating-rate liabilities and short-dated funding.
6. Short rate down
Short rates fall, long rates relatively unchanged. Tests sensitivity to a near-term easing cycle. Effect is concentrated in floating-rate assets and the deposit franchise (where deposit rates that cannot go below zero create a floor on the bank's ability to reduce funding cost).

The supervisory outlier test (SOT)

The supervisory outlier test is the bridge between the IRRBB measurement framework and the supervisor. The mechanic is straightforward. The bank runs all six shocks through its EVE model and reports the maximum loss across the six scenarios. The supervisor compares that loss to the bank's Tier 1 capital. If the maximum loss exceeds 15 per cent of Tier 1 capital, the bank is an "outlier" and the supervisor will investigate further. The 2004 predecessor standard used 20 per cent of own funds (Tier 1 plus Tier 2). The 2016 revision tightened the threshold (15 per cent rather than 20) and narrowed the capital base (Tier 1 only, no Tier 2), so the effective tightening was material.

Crossing the SOT is not an automatic capital requirement. BCBS 368 is a Pillar 2 standard: the result feeds into the supervisor's review of the bank's overall capital adequacy under SREP (in the EU) or its equivalent. The supervisor may then require remediation, additional reporting, a Pillar 2 capital add-on, or restrictions on dividend distribution. The 2022 EBA Guidelines added a second SOT on NII sensitivity (a 5 per cent of Tier 1 threshold on a 1-year NII decline under the parallel-up and parallel-down scenarios), specifically to capture banks whose EVE looked benign but whose near-term earnings were vulnerable.

Worked example: parallel-up shock on a stylised banking book

A small bank has the following banking-book balance sheet, in millions, at a flat yield curve of 3 per cent.

Assets                                             PV
  10-year fixed-rate mortgages, 4.5% coupon       1,000
  Cash and 3-month T-bills                          200
                                                  -----
  Total assets                                    1,200

Liabilities and equity
  Non-maturity deposits, 0.5% rate                  900
  3-month wholesale funding, 3% rate                200
  Tier 1 capital                                    100
                                                  -----
  Total                                           1,200

Now apply the parallel-up shock, a uniform +200 basis-point shift in the yield curve. The bank uses a behavioural model that assigns a 4-year effective duration to the non-maturity deposits (capturing the stickiness of the deposit franchise). Effective durations: mortgages 7 years, T-bills 0.25 years, NMD 4 years, wholesale funding 0.25 years.

Asset PV change:
  Mortgages:     -1,000 * 7   * 0.02 = -140
  T-bills:         -200 * 0.25 * 0.02 =   -1
                                       -----
  Total assets                          -141

Liability PV change (decline in liability PV is a gain to equity):
  NMD:             +900 * 4   * 0.02 =  +72
  Wholesale:       +200 * 0.25 * 0.02 =  +1
                                       -----
  Total liabilities                     +73

dEVE = dAssets - dLiabilities = -141 + 73 = -68

The shock destroys £68 million of EVE. The SOT compares this to Tier 1 capital of £100 million:

SOT ratio = |dEVE| / Tier 1 = 68 / 100 = 68 per cent

The bank is far outside the 15 per cent SOT threshold. The supervisor will require either a material reduction in IRRBB exposure (more hedging, shorter-duration assets, or a longer-duration funding mix) or a Pillar 2 capital add-on, or both. The intuition is that a bank with £100 million of capital should not be running an interest-rate position that destroys £68 million of franchise value on a 200 basis-point shock. This balance-sheet shape, long-duration fixed-rate assets funded by sticky deposits and short-term wholesale, is precisely the SVB pre-failure profile in stylised form.

Behavioural modelling: the hardest part

EVE under the six shocks is only as accurate as the behavioural assumptions on the contractually open-ended balance-sheet items. Three items dominate. Non-maturity deposits (current accounts, instant-access savings) have no contractual maturity, no contractual repricing date, and a rate the bank can change at will. The behavioural model must assign them an effective duration (typically 3 to 7 years for a stable retail franchise; shorter for corporate deposits and digital-bank deposits). Prepayment-sensitive mortgages have a contractual maturity but a behavioural maturity that shortens as rates fall and lengthens as rates rise (the classic negative convexity). Pipeline commitments (mortgage offers issued but not yet drawn) have option-like properties that depend on rate moves between offer and drawdown. BCBS 368 requires that behavioural assumptions be documented, supported by data, validated independently, and tested for sensitivity. The supervisor will challenge each assumption as part of the SREP review.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Pillar 2 is not the soft pillar

A common misconception is that because BCBS 368 sits in Pillar 2, it has no teeth. The post-2023 evidence says the opposite. SREP capital add-ons for IRRBB at material outliers have run into the hundreds of basis points of CET1 ratio in EU and UK banks. The supervisor has additional powers under Pillar 2: restrictions on dividends, restrictions on the variable component of pay, mandated reductions in IRRBB exposure within a fixed window. A bank that fails the SOT and refuses to remediate will see its Pillar 2 requirement climb until remediation becomes cheaper than the capital cost.

SVB was a Pillar 2 supervision failure as much as a bank failure

Silicon Valley Bank's collapse in March 2023 is the case study every IRRBB practitioner should know in detail. SVB held a large held-to-maturity (HTM) portfolio of long-dated US Treasuries and agency MBS, funded by an uninsured deposit base concentrated in venture-capital-backed technology companies. As the Federal Reserve raised rates by 500 basis points across 2022, the fair-value loss on the HTM portfolio grew to roughly $15 billion, against tangible common equity of about $12 billion. Because the portfolio was HTM, the fair-value loss did not flow through P&L or CET1; CET1 looked fine on the regulatory return. But when uninsured depositors realised that mark-to-market the bank was insolvent, they ran. The bank was put into FDIC receivership within 48 hours. SVB is the empirical proof that Pillar 2 supervisory attention to IRRBB is not optional, that HTM accounting can mask economic insolvency, and that the deposit franchise's behavioural assumptions can flip in days.

Governance and IRRBB are linked

BCBS 328 Principle 7 (risk identification and monitoring) and BCBS 368's Principle 1 (the board has overall responsibility for IRRBB) overlap by design. The board cannot satisfy 328 without understanding 368: it must know what IRRBB the bank is running, how that compares to its appetite, what the SOT result is, and what the supervisor thinks about it. Several post-2023 supervisory reviews have explicitly examined whether bank boards understood their IRRBB position; in some cases boards had been receiving the EVE numbers but not the supervisor's interpretation of those numbers against the SOT. The remedial action is more often training and reporting redesign than additional capital.

Watch out The EU is moving parts of IRRBB from Pillar 2 toward Pillar 1 under CRD VI and CRR III. The European Commission and EBA have signalled that an outright Pillar 1 capital charge for IRRBB will not be introduced (the BCBS work to that effect concluded that a single methodology robust enough for Pillar 1 use was not yet available), but the supervisory outlier tests will operate on a more rule-based footing, with binding remediation triggers rather than supervisory discretion. The practical effect is that the SOT thresholds (15 per cent EVE, 5 per cent NII) become harder to negotiate around. Track the EBA technical standards and Q&As, not just the BCBS text, if you are working in the EU.

The 2016 calibration is now out of date

The BCBS 368 standardised shocks were calibrated on a rolling six-year window of historical rate moves up to roughly 2014. Six years of effective-lower-bound rates dragged the calibrated shocks downward. As the post-2022 rate moves enter the calibration window, the shocks will grow. EBA and PRA have flagged that they will update their domestic calibrations periodically and the supervisor will use the latest calibration in supervisory reviews. Models that hard-code the 2016 numbers will need re-versioning.

The 13th principle is the most important

BCBS 328 Principle 13 ("the role of supervisors") is the principle most often skipped in bank summaries of the standard. It is the principle that authorises everything else: comprehensive evaluations, regular interaction with boards, supervisory intervention in governance arrangements, and information-sharing between supervisors. Without Principle 13, BCBS 328 would be advice; with it, the standard is enforceable through SREP and the equivalents elsewhere. Reading the document, treat Principle 13 as the operative one.

Step 6

Test yourself

  1. BCBS 328 contains 13 principles. The first 12 are addressed to banks and the 13th to a different audience. Who is the 13th principle addressed to, and why does that matter for the enforceability of the standard?
  2. State the three lines of defence and the role of each in a single sentence. Identify which line internal audit belongs to and why its reporting line into the audit committee is structurally important.
  3. BCBS 328 Principle 6 covers the risk management function. Name three structural features the standard requires of the CRO role.
  4. BCBS 328 Principle 11 covers compensation. What are the three risk-adjustment features the standard expects of variable remuneration for material risk-takers?
  5. List the six standardised shock scenarios of BCBS 368. For a bank with long-duration fixed-rate mortgages funded by short-dated wholesale funding, which scenario is typically the worst?
  6. Distinguish EVE from NII. Which is used in the supervisory outlier test, what horizon does it cover, and on what business-volume assumption is it calculated?
  7. State the supervisory outlier test threshold under BCBS 368 and the threshold under the 2004 predecessor standard. Identify the two changes between them.
  8. BCBS 368 distinguishes three IRRBB sub-types: gap risk, basis risk, and option risk. Give one concrete bank balance-sheet example of each.
  9. A bank has Tier 1 capital of £500 million. Its EVE sensitivities under the six shocks are: parallel up −£90m; parallel down +£20m; steepener +£10m; flattener −£40m; short rate up −£60m; short rate down +£15m. Does the bank pass the SOT? Show your working.
  10. SVB failed in March 2023 because of an IRRBB realisation. In a sentence, explain why the bank's CET1 ratio was reported as adequate immediately before failure even though the bank was economically insolvent, and what this implies about the limits of Pillar 1 capital ratios as IRRBB measures.
