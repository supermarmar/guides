BIS and the Basel Committee

The Bank for International Settlements is a bank for central banks, a meeting place, and a host. The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision is the meeting that writes the rules. Neither has any formal authority. Both have shaped almost every line of modern banking regulation.

Lecture 1 of 12 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: docs/wiki/regulation/international/bis/bis.md
Step 1

The 30-second version

The Bank for International Settlements (BIS), founded in 1930 in Basel, is the oldest international financial institution. It is owned by 63 central banks accounting for about 95 per cent of world GDP, including the Bank of England, the ECB, and the South African Reserve Bank. It does three things: it provides banking services to central banks (asset management, gold deposits, foreign-exchange operations), it acts as a forum where central bank governors meet bimonthly to compare notes, and it hosts the committees that write international standards. The most consequential of those committees is the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS), set up in 1974 after the Herstatt collapse showed that no one was watching banks' cross-border operations. The BCBS now has 45 member institutions from 28 jurisdictions and writes the standards every internationally active bank in the world implements.

Neither institution has any legal authority. The BIS is a private bank with the trappings of a treaty organisation. The BCBS publishes "standards" that are formally just recommendations. The reason these recommendations matter is that the people in the room are the central bank governors and chief supervisors who, when they go home, can write whatever they agreed to in Basel directly into national rulebooks. The Basel Process turns informal consensus into binding national law through a one-step path: agree here, legislate at home.

Feynman test Imagine a private club in Basel where every central bank governor on earth turns up six times a year for dinner. Over the cheese course they argue about whether banks have enough capital. Eventually they agree on a number. They write the number on a piece of paper and call it a "standard". The paper has no legal force whatsoever. But when each governor flies home, they walk into their parliament or their rulebook committee and say "we agreed to this in Basel, write it down". Within five years every internationally active bank on the planet holds capital to that exact number. That is the entire architecture of international banking regulation. The BIS is the building. The BCBS is the dinner. The standards are the napkin.
What is beyond the source The source wiki covers the BIS and BCBS as institutions and traces the Basel accords. This lecture adds: a sharper account of how the BCBS converted private consensus into globally binding law without ever having any binding authority (the "Basel Process"); the post-crisis governance changes (creation of the Group of Governors and Heads of Supervision in 2009 as the BCBS's oversight body); the practical mechanics of BCBS standard-setting (consultation papers, quantitative impact studies, peer review); and the relationship between the BCBS and the other Basel-resident bodies (FSB, IAIS, CPMI, IADI).
Step 2

Why this matters

Every modern banking rule, from the 8 per cent minimum capital ratio to the way operational-risk losses are categorised, originated as a BCBS standard. The PRA Rulebook, the EU CRR, the Federal Reserve's Reg Q and Reg WW, the SARB Prudential Standards, and the OSFI Capital Adequacy Requirements all implement Basel. If you cannot read a BCBS publication and trace its journey into your local rulebook, you cannot interpret your local rulebook reliably; the local rule is often a tightening or loosening of an underlying Basel rule, and the underlying rule is the one with the rationale attached.

The institutional history also explains the gaps. Basel was set up by the central banks of ten developed economies (the G10), and for thirty years that membership shaped what the standards covered and what they ignored. Operational risk arrived in Basel II only because the G10 supervisors were tired of seeing rogue-trader and IT-failure losses but had no capital charge for them. Liquidity arrived in Basel III only because the G10 supervisors had spent eighteen months pumping liquidity into their own banks during the crisis. The Basel rules are a record of what the world's central bankers worried about in the previous decade, codified.

For an actuary working inside a bank, three institutional facts have direct day-to-day consequences. First, the Basel cycle runs roughly fifteen years from one major accord to the next, so any model you build today will be subject to a redesign before it retires. Second, BCBS standards have multi-year transition windows, so the rules you implement now are usually two consultation papers and three working drafts old. Third, BCBS members are obligated to peer-review each other's implementation through the Regulatory Consistency Assessment Programme (RCAP), so divergence from Basel by a national regulator is publicly named, ranked, and shamed.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture three concentric circles. At the centre, the BIS the bank: a small Basel-based institution providing financial services to central banks. Around it, the BIS the forum: a venue where central bank governors meet bimonthly. Around that, the Basel Process: the set of committees, secretariats, and oversight bodies that turn the forum's conversations into published standards.

Three flows of authority matter. Standards flow down from the BCBS to its member jurisdictions, who then write them into national law. Implementation reports flow up through the BCBS's Regulatory Consistency Assessment Programme, which scores each jurisdiction's faithfulness to the agreed text. And technical expertise flows sideways across the six BIS-hosted committees and the three associations, so that work on, say, payment-system risk done at CPMI feeds into BCBS thinking on settlement-risk capital.

Step 4

The detail

The BIS as an institution

The Bank for International Settlements was created by the Hague Agreement of 1930. Its original purpose was to administer the reparation payments owed by Germany after the First World War; its name derives from this settlement role. By the mid-1930s the reparations function had collapsed and the bank had reinvented itself as a co-operative agency for European central banks. The Bretton Woods Conference of 1944 voted to abolish the BIS, on the theory that the new IMF and World Bank made it redundant. European central bankers ignored the resolution and the abolition was quietly dropped by 1948.

Today the BIS does three jobs. First, it is a bank for central banks: it accepts deposits in foreign currencies and gold, manages reserves, runs collateralised lending and short-term liquidity facilities, and conducts foreign-exchange transactions on behalf of about a third of the world's official reserves. Second, it is a forum for central bank governors: the bimonthly meetings in Basel are where governors compare notes on the world economy, on financial markets, and on supervisory practice. Third, it hosts and provides secretariat support to the standard-setting committees described above. The BIS itself has roughly 700 staff and is based in a circular tower next to Basel station, with regional offices in Hong Kong and Mexico City.

Membership
63 central banks own the BIS. Membership is by invitation of the existing board. The Bank of England, ECB, Federal Reserve, Bank of Japan, People's Bank of China, and SARB are all members. Russia's membership rights were suspended after February 2022 but the central bank remains a shareholder.
Governance
Three levels: Board of Directors (21 directors, six ex-officio from major member central banks plus nine elected); General Meeting of member central banks (one per year, votes on dividends and the annual report); BIS management (General Manager runs day-to-day operations).
Legal status
An international organisation under public international law (Hague Agreement, 1930; Headquarters Agreement with Switzerland). Immune from Swiss taxation and most Swiss regulatory oversight. Its capital is held in shares some of which were privately held until a 2000 buyback; today it is wholly owned by central banks.
Financial Stability Institute
A BIS unit founded in 1999 that runs training and policy work for central bankers and supervisors worldwide, especially in emerging markets. The vehicle by which the Basel standards are taught and supported outside the BCBS membership.

The Basel Process

The Basel Process is the BIS's term for the system of regular meetings, committee work, and informal consultation through which international standards are produced. Six committees and three associated bodies sit under three oversight groupings: the Global Economy Meeting (GEM), the All Governors' Meeting, and the Group of Governors and Heads of Supervision (GHOS).

GEM
Global Economy Meeting. The bimonthly gathering of governors from BIS member central banks. Oversees the Committee on the Global Financial System (CGFS), the Committee on Payments and Market Infrastructures (CPMI), and the Markets Committee. The forum where governors discuss the world economy.
All Governors' Meeting
Includes all 63 BIS member central banks. Oversees the Central Bank Governance Forum (CBGF) and the Irving Fisher Committee on Central Bank Statistics (IFC). Sets broader strategic direction for the BIS.
GHOS
Group of Governors and Heads of Supervision. The oversight body of the BCBS, created in its current form in 2009. Comprises the central bank governors and chief supervisors of BCBS member jurisdictions. Endorses every major BCBS standard before publication. The political body that gives BCBS standards their legitimacy.

The Basel Process produces two visible outputs: committee reports analysing specific topics, and internationally agreed standards. The international agreement is the precondition for globally consistent rules; without it, no individual jurisdiction would risk imposing tougher requirements on its own banks for fear of competitive disadvantage. But agreement in Basel does not substitute for national legislation. Each agreement must be approved and implemented at the national level through the usual rule-making process: in the EU, through CRR amendments adopted by Council and Parliament; in the UK, through PRA rulebook changes after consultation; in the US, through Federal Reserve, OCC, and FDIC final rules after notice-and-comment.

The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision

The BCBS was created in 1974 in response to two cross-border bank failures earlier that year: Bankhaus Herstatt in Germany (which collapsed during a German banking-hours afternoon, leaving counterparties on the other side of the Atlantic with unpaid dollar legs of foreign-exchange settlements) and Franklin National Bank in the United States. Both cases exposed a gap in international supervisory cooperation. The G10 central bank governors set up the Committee on Banking Regulations and Supervisory Practices in late 1974, holding its first meeting in February 1975 under the chairmanship of George Blunden of the Bank of England. The committee was renamed the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision in 1990.

Membership has expanded from the original G10 to 45 institutions from 28 jurisdictions. The current membership includes the EU as a bloc (represented by the ECB and the European Commission), and individual EU members (France, Germany, Italy, Spain, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, Belgium, Sweden). Non-EU additions since 2009 include China, India, Brazil, Russia (rights suspended), South Africa, Saudi Arabia, Indonesia, Mexico, Argentina, and Turkey.

The BCBS Charter and how standards are made

The BCBS Charter, last revised in 2018, states that the Committee's mandate is "to strengthen the regulation, supervision and practices of banks worldwide with the purpose of enhancing financial stability". The Charter is explicit that the BCBS "does not possess any formal supranational authority. Its decisions do not have legal force. Rather, the BCBS relies on its members' commitments". In practice the Committee produces three types of output, in declining order of force:

Standards
The strongest output. Set out minimum requirements that members commit to implement in their national legal frameworks within an agreed transition period. The Basel I, II, and III accords are standards. Members "should seek the greatest possible equivalence of standards and their outcome" when literal transposition is impossible.
Guidelines
Supplement standards in specific areas. Elaborate how the standards should be implemented. Examples: guidelines on stress testing, on operational-risk management, on credit-risk management.
Sound practices
Descriptive: what good supervision and good banking practice look like in the field. Members are encouraged to compare their own practices against the sound-practices benchmark.

The BCBS standard-setting process typically takes three to five years from initial concept to final publication. The stages are:

  1. Issues paper or discussion paper. An early-stage paper signalling that the Committee is thinking about a topic. Open to comment from industry but does not yet propose specific rules.
  2. First consultative document. A draft standard, usually with multiple options for industry to comment on. Issued for public consultation, typically over three to four months.
  3. Quantitative Impact Study (QIS). Member banks submit data so the Committee can calibrate the proposed rule. QIS results inform changes to the draft.
  4. Second consultative document. A revised draft incorporating feedback and QIS results. Often a substantial redesign of the first draft.
  5. Final standard. Endorsed by GHOS and published. Implementation date typically two to five years from publication, with transitional arrangements (phase-ins, parallel running) for major changes.
  6. RCAP (Regulatory Consistency Assessment Programme). After implementation, each member jurisdiction is peer-reviewed for faithfulness to the agreed text. Results published, ranking jurisdictions as "compliant", "largely compliant", "materially non-compliant", or "non-compliant".

The Basel accords: a one-page genealogy

The BCBS's most consequential output is the series of capital accords. Each one is the subject of a later lecture in this series. The genealogy:

Basel Concordat (1975, revised 1983)
The first BCBS publication. Sets out principles for sharing supervisory responsibility for cross-border banking groups between home and host supervisors. Not a capital standard. Foundational for cross-border supervisory cooperation.
Basel I (1988)
The first capital accord. 8 per cent minimum ratio of capital to risk-weighted assets, with capital split into Tier 1 (equity and disclosed reserves) and Tier 2 (supplementary). Risk weights of 0, 10, 20, 50, and 100 per cent applied to broad asset classes. Implementation by end-1992. Amended in 1996 to add a market-risk capital charge with internal-model option.
Basel II (2004, refreshed 2006)
Three-pillar framework: minimum capital requirements (Pillar 1), supervisory review (Pillar 2), and market discipline (Pillar 3). Introduced the Standardised, Foundation IRB, and Advanced IRB approaches for credit risk; the Basic Indicator, Standardised, and Advanced Measurement Approaches for operational risk. Implementation began 2007 (most jurisdictions).
Basel III (2010, amended through 2017)
Post-crisis rebuild. Tighter capital definitions (CET1 introduced, deductions cleaned up), capital conservation and countercyclical buffers, leverage ratio (3 per cent), Liquidity Coverage Ratio (30-day stress), Net Stable Funding Ratio (one-year structural), G-SIB and D-SIB surcharges. Phased in 2013 to 2019.
Basel III finalisation, "Basel IV" or "Basel 3.1" (BCBS d424, 2017)
Revised standardised approaches for credit, operational, and CVA risk; constraints on A-IRB; replacement of internal-model CVA with standardised and basic approaches; replacement of standardised operational risk with a single income-and-loss-based approach; introduction of a 72.5 per cent output floor; FRTB market-risk framework. Implementation now 2026 to 2030 in most jurisdictions.

The other Basel-resident bodies

Three associations have separate legal identity but share the BIS address and Basel community. Knowing the division of labour avoids confusing their outputs with those of the BCBS.

Financial Stability Board (FSB)
Founded 2009 (succeeding the Financial Stability Forum). Coordinates the work of national financial authorities and international standard-setters in pursuit of global financial stability. Owns the G-SIB designation methodology and the TLAC standard. Reports to G20. Covered in lectures 9 and 10.
International Association of Insurance Supervisors (IAIS)
Sets standards for insurance regulation. Produces the Insurance Core Principles and the ComFrame framework for internationally active insurance groups. Not relevant to banking regulation directly, but worth knowing for an actuary.
International Association of Deposit Insurers (IADI)
Sets standards for deposit-insurance systems. Produces the Core Principles for Effective Deposit Insurance. Standards influence design of national schemes such as the UK FSCS, US FDIC, and South African CoDI.

The Regulatory Consistency Assessment Programme (RCAP)

Adopted in 2012, RCAP is the BCBS's tool for monitoring how faithfully member jurisdictions implement the Basel standards. Each jurisdiction is assessed every few years on a standard-by-standard basis, with detailed gap analysis between the local rulebook and the BCBS text. The output is a public report grading the jurisdiction's implementation as compliant, largely compliant, materially non-compliant, or non-compliant.

RCAP has had real consequences. The EU was rated "materially non-compliant" on parts of its initial CRR implementation in 2014 because of carve-outs for SMEs and infrastructure exposures; subsequent CRR amendments narrowed the gap. Switzerland and the United States have been rated largely compliant overall but with specific issues flagged in successive reviews. RCAP is the closest thing the BCBS has to an enforcement mechanism, and it works by reputational pressure rather than by legal force.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Always start with the BCBS text, not the local rule

National rulebooks are derived. They paraphrase, condense, and occasionally elaborate. When a CRR article or a PRA rule is ambiguous, the underlying BCBS standard usually disambiguates it because the standard has the policy rationale attached as a non-binding commentary. Reading the local rule alone gives you the requirement; reading the BCBS standard tells you why the requirement is there and what it is trying to achieve.

Track the consultation papers, not just the final standard

By the time a Basel standard is finalised, the politically interesting decisions are usually settled. The intellectual disagreements are visible in the consultation papers and in the QIS results. If you want to predict where the next round of revisions will land, read the comments submitted by industry to the most recent consultation, not the standard itself. Industry comments are usually thoughtful and predictive of next-cycle revisions.

Watch out The BCBS does not enforce its own standards. When a national regulator's rule is more or less stringent than Basel, the BCBS will note it in an RCAP report but cannot do anything about it. So "Basel-compliant" and "PRA-compliant" are different statements. Saying a bank meets Basel III is not the same as saying it meets the PRA rulebook; the latter is the legally binding test.

The BIS does not publish standards, the BCBS does

A common slip is to say "the BIS requires" something. The BIS itself does not write banking standards. It hosts the committee that does. The distinction matters in exam answers and in regulatory correspondence; "BIS standards" usually means BCBS standards in informal speech, but the institutional attribution is to the BCBS.

The Basel calendar is the bank's strategic clock

Major Basel reforms run on roughly fifteen-year cycles. Basel I was 1988. Basel II was 2004. Basel III was 2010 with finalisation in 2017. The next major redesign will likely arrive in the mid-2030s. Knowing where in the cycle you are tells you how stable the rules will be over the lifetime of a long-dated model. Models built in 2014 ran into the finalised Basel III in 2022 to 2026; models built in 2025 should expect the next round around 2035.

Standards are written for the regulator, not for the regulated

BCBS publications are addressed to supervisors, not to banks. The audience is the central banker and the chief supervisor who will turn the text into national rules. This is why Basel documents read as principles-with-formulae rather than as rulebooks: they are templates for national rule-making, not direct compliance manuals. Industry banks read them because they want to know what is coming; supervisors read them because they have to write the rules that implement them.
