FSB: the post-crisis coordinator

The Financial Stability Board sits one rung above the Basel Committee, the IAIS, and IOSCO, and one rung below the G20 leaders. It does not write banking rules, insurance rules, or securities rules. It identifies vulnerabilities across the whole financial system, coordinates the standard-setters whose rules cover the parts they already supervise, and chases what falls between them. Its remit is global financial stability, not banking stability; that wider lens is what makes it the youngest, most political, and most cross-sectoral of the international regulatory bodies.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The Financial Stability Board (FSB) is the international body that coordinates the work of national financial authorities and the international standard-setters (BCBS, IAIS, IOSCO, IASB, CPMI, CGFS) on global financial stability. It was founded in April 2009 at the G20 London Summit as the successor to the Financial Stability Forum (FSF), which had been set up by the G7 in 1999 after the Asian financial crisis but proved too narrow when 2008 hit. The FSB's Plenary has around 70 members spanning 24 jurisdictions, six international institutions, and six standard-setting bodies. It is hosted at the BIS in Basel under a service agreement, but it has separate legal identity as a Swiss not-for-profit association. It reports to the G20 leaders and finance ministers.

The FSB has no legal authority of its own. Its influence comes from two channels: G20 endorsement of its recommendations, which gives them political weight in national capitals; and peer review of its members' implementation, which exposes laggards to reputational pressure. In the rare cases where it does write its own rules, the most consequential are the G-SIB designation methodology and the Total Loss-Absorbing Capacity (TLAC) standard, both of which are the subject of the next lecture.

Feynman test Imagine a global financial system as a city. The BCBS is the building inspector for the banks. The IAIS is the inspector for the insurance offices. IOSCO is the inspector for the stock exchanges. The IASB is the accountant who writes everyone's bookkeeping rules. Each inspector knows their building inside out, but no one is watching the alleys between the buildings, where shadow-banking lorries cross from one to another at night. After the 2008 fire, the G20 mayors set up the FSB. The FSB is not another building inspector. It is the city planner who looks at the whole map, asks the existing inspectors what they are missing, commissions joint inspections of the alleys, and reports back to the mayors twice a year. The FSB cannot issue a building permit. But when it tells the mayors that the alleys are full of flammable waste, the mayors tell every inspector to clean them up, and the inspectors do.

It also locates the FSB precisely against the BCBS, so the question "who owns the G-SIB list, the BCBS or the FSB" has a clean answer (the FSB, on a methodology jointly developed with the BCBS).
Step 2

Why this matters

Before 2008, international financial regulation had a sectoral architecture inherited from the 1970s and 1980s. The BCBS covered banks. The IAIS, founded in 1994, covered insurers. IOSCO, founded in 1983, covered securities markets and listed firms. The IASB and its predecessor IASC covered accounting. Each was strong in its own sector and largely silent on everything else. The Financial Stability Forum, set up by the G7 in 1999, was meant to thread the sectors together, but its small membership (G7 plus a handful of others) and its limited mandate meant it functioned more as a discussion club than a coordinator with teeth.

The 2008 crisis exposed the architecture's central weakness. The largest losses did not come from the regulated cores of banking, insurance, or securities; they came from the joins. Off-balance-sheet vehicles holding mortgage-backed securities sat in a regulatory grey area between bank and securities supervision. Monoline insurers writing credit-default swaps on those vehicles sat between insurance and derivatives supervision. Money-market funds runnable like banks but unregulated as banks sat between asset management and prudential supervision. AIG's Financial Products division, which wrote the credit-default swaps that bankrupted the parent, was overseen by the US Office of Thrift Supervision because AIG happened to own a small thrift, a regulator with no expertise in derivatives. The pre-crisis architecture had no body whose remit covered these cracks.

The FSB was the G20's institutional answer. Its existence acknowledges that financial stability is not the sum of bank stability, insurance stability, and securities-market stability; it is a property of the whole system, including the parts no individual standard-setter owns. For an actuary working inside a bank, the FSB matters for four practical reasons. First, the G-SIB designation that triggers a higher capital requirement and TLAC eligibility comes from the FSB, not the BCBS. Second, the FSB's NBFI work programme is reshaping how supervisors think about the perimeter of bank regulation, which directly affects how internal-models teams scope counterparty exposures to funds and other non-banks. Third, the FSB's climate-risk work (originally TCFD, now ISSB and supervisory expectations) feeds the climate-risk capital and disclosure expectations that PRA, ECB, and other national supervisors now build into Pillar 2. Fourth, the FSB's resolution-regime standards (Key Attributes of Effective Resolution Regimes) are the foundation on which the EU BRRD, the UK resolution framework, and the US Title II live; the bail-in mechanics every credit risk team must now understand for distressed-debt analysis trace back to a single FSB document.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture three layers stacked vertically. At the top, the G20 leaders and finance ministers, who give the FSB its political mandate and endorse its outputs. In the middle, the FSB itself, with a Plenary, Steering Committee, Standing Committees, and a small Secretariat. At the bottom, the FSB's members and partners: the national authorities and the standard-setting bodies whose rules together cover the regulated financial system. The FSB does not sit above the standard-setters in a legal sense; it sits next to them in a coordinating role, with the G20 acting as the political layer that gives the coordination weight.

Three flows of authority matter. Mandate flows down from the G20 to the FSB, which sets the work programme accordingly. Coordination flows sideways from the FSB to the standard-setters and national authorities, with the FSB acting as convener rather than rule-maker. Implementation evidence flows back up through the FSB's peer-review programmes to the G20, which then uses the findings to apply political pressure on laggards.

Step 4

The detail

History: from FSF to FSB

The story of the FSB begins with a German central banker and an Asian currency crisis. In 1998, in the aftermath of the 1997 Asian financial crisis and the collapse of the Long-Term Capital Management hedge fund, the G7 finance ministers asked Hans Tietmeyer, then president of the Deutsche Bundesbank, to recommend new structures for cooperation among national and international supervisory bodies. Tietmeyer reported in February 1999 with a proposal for a Financial Stability Forum. The G7 endorsed the proposal at its February 1999 meeting and the FSF held its first meeting in April 1999 in Washington, chaired by Andrew Crockett, then general manager of the BIS.

The FSF was a discussion club. Its membership was the G7 plus a small number of other significant financial centres (Australia, Hong Kong, Netherlands, Singapore, Switzerland), plus the IMF, World Bank, BIS, OECD, and the international standard-setters. It met twice a year, produced reports, and developed the original Compendium of 12 Key Standards. It did not have a permanent secretariat, did not produce its own rules, and did not have a peer-review programme of any consequence. By the standards of the time, this was thought sufficient.

2008 made clear that it was not. The FSF had warned about leverage in the financial system, about deficiencies in mortgage-securitisation underwriting, and about the limited transparency of the credit-default-swap market. The warnings did not translate into action because the FSF had no political principal with the authority to override domestic resistance in any one jurisdiction. The G7 was the wrong forum: by 2008, the G7 represented a shrinking share of global finance, and the largest emerging markets (China, India, Brazil) had no seat. When the G20 leaders met in Washington in November 2008 and London in April 2009, they made two decisions about the FSF. First, they re-founded it as the Financial Stability Board, with an expanded mandate. Second, they expanded membership to all G20 jurisdictions plus the existing FSF participants. The FSB was formally established on 2 April 2009 at the G20 London Summit and endorsed its original Charter in September 2009 at the G20 Pittsburgh Summit. The Charter was revised in 2012 to clarify the FSB's not-for-profit association status under Swiss law.

The institutional contrast between the FSF and the FSB tells you what changed in 2009. The FSF was a forum hosted within the BIS structure with no separate legal identity, no permanent staff to speak of, and no formal accountability to any political body. The FSB is a Swiss not-for-profit association with its own legal personality, a Secretariat of around 30 staff, a formal Charter, and direct accountability to the G20. The change is from think-tank to coordinator.

Membership

The FSB Plenary has roughly 70 members organised into three groups.

Member jurisdictions (24)
All 19 G20 country members plus the European Union as a bloc, plus Hong Kong, Netherlands, Singapore, Spain, and Switzerland. Each jurisdiction is typically represented by three institutions: the central bank, the lead financial supervisor, and the finance ministry. Major jurisdictions get up to three Plenary seats; smaller ones get one or two.
International financial institutions (6)
International Monetary Fund (IMF), World Bank, Bank for International Settlements (BIS), Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), European Central Bank (ECB), European Commission. Each holds Plenary seats.
International standard-setting and other bodies (6)
Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS), International Association of Insurance Supervisors (IAIS), International Organization of Securities Commissions (IOSCO), International Accounting Standards Board (IASB, through the IFRS Foundation), Committee on Payments and Market Infrastructures (CPMI), Committee on the Global Financial System (CGFS). These are the bodies whose work the FSB coordinates and which together cover the regulated financial system.

The membership design is deliberate. By seating national authorities, international institutions, and standard-setters at the same table, the FSB makes coordination a structural feature rather than a discretionary act. A finance minister cannot ignore what the central bank governor next to her has agreed; a BCBS chair sitting opposite an IAIS chair cannot publish a banking standard that contradicts an insurance one without it being noticed. The friction of getting an agreement is high, but once an agreement is reached, the institutional commitment behind it is broad.

Governance: Plenary, Steering Committee, Standing Committees, Regional Consultative Groups

The FSB has a five-layer governance structure: the Chair, the Plenary, the Steering Committee, the four Standing Committees, and the Regional Consultative Groups. A small Secretariat supports all five.

Chair
A senior official from a member authority, appointed by the Plenary for a three-year term, renewable once. The Chair sets the agenda, represents the FSB to the G20, and chairs both the Plenary and the Steering Committee. Past chairs include Mark Carney (Bank of Canada and Bank of England), Randal Quarles (Federal Reserve), and Klaas Knot (De Nederlandsche Bank).
Plenary
The decision-making body. Meets in person at least twice a year, typically in Basel. Roughly 70 members. Approves the work programme, endorses major outputs, and elects the Chair and Steering Committee members. Decisions are taken by consensus; in practice this means no major output is published over the explicit objection of any G20 jurisdiction.
Steering Committee
A smaller executive body of around 25 members drawn from the Plenary. Provides operational direction between Plenary meetings, manages crisis response, and oversees the Standing Committees. Meets four to six times a year.
Standing Committee on Assessment of Vulnerabilities (SCAV)
The horizon scanner. Identifies and assesses risks to global financial stability across all sectors. Produces the FSB's regular vulnerabilities reports to the G20, the annual Global Monitoring Report on Non-Bank Financial Intermediation, and the periodic Crypto-Asset Markets reports. Chaired by a senior central banker.
Standing Committee on Supervisory and Regulatory Cooperation (SRC)
The cross-border supervisory coordinator. Hosts the Crisis Management Groups for each G-SIB, manages cross-border resolution issues, and addresses gaps in supervisory cooperation. The forum where home and host supervisors of internationally active banks meet outside their BCBS work.
Standing Committee on Standards Implementation (SCSI)
The peer-review engine. Designs and oversees the FSB's two peer-review programmes: thematic peer reviews (on a specific topic across all members) and country peer reviews (a full review of one member jurisdiction). Publishes the results, which expose laggards to reputational pressure.
Standing Committee on Budget and Resources (SCBR)
Internal administration. Oversees the FSB's budget, staff resourcing, and the BIS funding agreement. Not policy-facing but essential for the FSB's operation as a Swiss not-for-profit with permanent staff.
Regional Consultative Groups (6)
Americas, Asia, Commonwealth of Independent States, Europe, Middle East and North Africa, and Sub-Saharan Africa. Each Group includes FSB members from the region plus non-member jurisdictions, giving the wider community a voice in FSB work. Meet at least twice a year. The vehicle by which FSB recommendations are explained to and influenced by jurisdictions outside the 24-member Plenary.

Mandate

The FSB Charter sets out four mandate elements. First, to assess vulnerabilities affecting the global financial system and identify and review the regulatory, supervisory, and related actions needed to address them. Second, to promote coordination and information exchange among authorities responsible for financial stability. Third, to monitor and advise on market developments and their implications for regulatory policy. Fourth, to advise on and monitor best practice in meeting regulatory standards, including through standards implementation peer reviews.

Note what is not in the mandate. The FSB has no mandate to write banking, insurance, or securities standards; those remain with the BCBS, IAIS, and IOSCO. It has no enforcement powers. It has no funds to disburse to member jurisdictions. Its mandate is purely diagnostic, coordinative, and advisory. The teeth are entirely on loan from the G20 and from member commitments.

Powers, or the absence thereof

The FSB has no formal powers. Like the BCBS, its outputs are formally recommendations. Like the BCBS, its influence rests on three institutional mechanisms.

The first is G20 endorsement. When the FSB submits a recommendation that is then endorsed in a G20 communiqué, the political weight behind the recommendation shifts. Member jurisdictions whose finance ministers and central bank governors agreed to the G20 statement are publicly committed to implementing it. The G-SIB and TLAC standards both followed this path: FSB development, G20 endorsement, then implementation through the relevant national authority (the BCBS for the capital surcharge component, individual resolution authorities for the TLAC component).

The second is member commitment. FSB members undertake, on joining, to maintain open and transparent financial systems, to implement international financial standards, and to submit to peer review. These commitments are not legally binding but are institutionally costly to break.

The third is peer-review reputational pressure. FSB peer reviews are public documents. Their findings are detailed and specific. A jurisdiction rated as having significant gaps in its implementation of, say, the Key Attributes of Effective Resolution Regimes faces direct pressure from the IMF (in Article IV surveillance), from market participants pricing the sovereign and its banks, and from the G20 itself in subsequent communiqués.

Watch out "FSB recommendations" and "FSB standards" are not interchangeable terms. The vast majority of FSB outputs are recommendations: policy guidance, principles, or frameworks that other standard-setters and national authorities are expected to translate into binding rules. The FSB has produced very few standards in the BCBS sense of the word, and the two well-known examples (G-SIB methodology, TLAC standard) are unusual cases where the FSB did the original drafting because the cross-sectoral or cross-border nature of the issue meant no single existing standard-setter was the natural home. Assume an FSB output is a recommendation unless the document itself uses the word "standard".

Publications: what the FSB actually produces

The FSB's published output falls into seven categories.

G-SIB methodology (2011, updated 2013, 2018, 2023)
The framework for identifying globally systemically important banks. Five categories (size, interconnectedness, substitutability, complexity, cross-jurisdictional activity), each with multiple indicators, combined into a single score. Above a threshold a bank is designated a G-SIB and assigned to a bucket triggering an additional capital requirement. Jointly developed with the BCBS; the FSB publishes the annual list. Covered in detail in the next lecture.
TLAC standard (November 2015)
"Total Loss-Absorbing Capacity" requirement for G-SIBs. A minimum amount of loss-absorbing instruments that a G-SIB must hold at the resolution-entity level to ensure orderly resolution without taxpayer support. Two pillars: a TLAC ratio (16 per cent of RWA from 2019, 18 per cent from 2022) and a leverage-ratio component. Implemented in the EU through MREL and in the UK through PRA rules. Covered in the next lecture.
Key Attributes of Effective Resolution Regimes (October 2011, revised October 2014)
The foundational FSB standard on bank resolution. Twelve "Key Attributes" covering resolution authority, resolution powers (including bail-in), funding of resolution, cross-border cooperation, and resolution planning. The blueprint on which the EU BRRD, UK Banking Act resolution provisions, US Dodd-Frank Title II, and equivalent regimes elsewhere are built.
Compendium of Standards (inherited from FSF, maintained by FSB)
The list of "12 Key Standards for Sound Financial Systems", covering macroeconomic policy and data transparency (3 standards), institutional and market infrastructure (5 standards), and financial regulation and supervision (4 standards: BCBS Core Principles, IAIS Insurance Core Principles, IOSCO Objectives and Principles, IADI Core Principles). The Compendium does not write the standards; it lists them and the body responsible for each.
Climate-related financial disclosure framework (TCFD, June 2017)
The recommendations of the Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures, set up by the FSB in 2015 under Mark Carney's chairmanship. Four pillars (governance, strategy, risk management, metrics and targets) and 11 detailed disclosure recommendations. Became the template for almost every subsequent climate-disclosure regime worldwide. In 2023 the TCFD work was absorbed into the IFRS Foundation's new ISSB, signalling a normal lifecycle in which the FSB incubates a new agenda and then transfers it to an established standard-setter.
Crypto-asset regulation framework (July 2023)
The FSB's first complete cross-sectoral framework for crypto-assets, covering both unbacked crypto-assets and "global stablecoins". Builds on earlier high-level recommendations (2020 on global stablecoins, 2022 on the wider crypto-asset market). The framework is policy guidance to national authorities; specific rule-making is left to the IOSCO (markets), the BCBS (bank exposure capital), and individual jurisdictions.
Non-bank financial intermediation (NBFI) work programme
An ongoing FSB priority. Annual Global Monitoring Report on NBFI tracks the size and risks of the shadow-banking system (now formally renamed NBFI). Specific workstreams on money-market funds (recommendations October 2021), open-ended funds, leverage in NBFI, and margin practices. The work programme illustrates the FSB's natural role: identifying a cross-sectoral risk that no individual standard-setter would have prioritised, then driving the relevant standard-setters and national authorities to action.

The FSB also publishes annual implementation monitoring reports on the post-crisis reforms; periodic vulnerabilities assessments to the G20; peer review reports (thematic and country); and progress reports on specific work programmes (resolution, OTC derivatives reform, financial benchmarks reform).

Peer reviews

Peer review is the FSB's nearest substitute for an enforcement mechanism. Two types run in parallel.

Thematic peer reviews focus on a single topic across all FSB members. Recent examples include peer reviews on financial-resource and governance arrangements for central counterparty resolution, on supervisory and regulatory approaches to climate-related risks, and on money-laundering and terrorist-financing risks in the international supervisory community. Each thematic review produces a public report with findings, country-by-country implementation status, and recommendations to individual jurisdictions.

Country peer reviews are full reviews of one FSB member's financial-stability framework. Each FSB member is reviewed approximately every five to seven years. Country reviews cover the full architecture: macroprudential framework, resolution regime, supervision of banks and non-banks, market infrastructure oversight, and implementation of agreed international reforms. The reports are detailed and named: a country rated as having "important shortcomings" in a specific area cannot easily ignore the finding because it is on the public record.

FSB peer reviews coexist with two related international monitoring exercises: the IMF's Financial Sector Assessment Programme (FSAP), which assesses the stability of a country's whole financial system, and the BCBS's RCAP, which assesses faithfulness to Basel standards specifically. The three programmes are coordinated; an FSAP for a major financial centre and an FSB country review usually run on overlapping cycles to avoid duplicative on-site visits.

Funding and hosting

The FSB is hosted at the BIS in Basel under a service agreement signed in January 2013 and renewed in 2021 for the period to January 2028. The BIS provides the FSB with office space, IT, meeting facilities, and other administrative support free of charge, and additionally contributes to staff compensation and direct operating costs. In practice the FSB Secretariat sits in the BIS tower in Basel and is staffed largely by secondees from member central banks and supervisors. The arrangement keeps the FSB small (roughly 30 to 40 staff) and embedded in the central-banking community without making it part of the BIS itself; legally the FSB is a Swiss not-for-profit association with its own balance sheet.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The FSB is a coordinator, not a standard-setter

The first instinct on encountering the FSB is to treat it as a super-regulator above the BCBS, IAIS, and IOSCO. This is wrong. The FSB does not have the technical depth of any of these bodies and does not aspire to. Its outputs are typically high-level frameworks, principles, or policy recommendations. The technical rules that implement them are written by the relevant sectoral standard-setter. When you see a long technical rule attributed to the FSB, check the authorship: it will almost always have been drafted jointly with one of the standard-setters, with the FSB providing the cross-sectoral lens and the standard-setter providing the technical detail.

The G-SIB list and TLAC are the exceptions, not the rule

The two most consequential pieces of FSB original rule-making (G-SIB methodology, TLAC standard) are visible precisely because they are unusual. Both were issues that no individual standard-setter naturally owned: G-SIB designation requires cross-sectoral data and a coordinated cross-border view that the BCBS alone could not have produced; TLAC required an integrated package of resolution and capital requirements that cut across BCBS (capital) and the resolution authorities (the practical implementation). For these issues the FSB was the natural home. Most other FSB recommendations end up implemented through BCBS, IAIS, IOSCO, or national rules; the FSB's name on the cover page is the political endorsement, not the technical authorship.

The FSB has a natural lifecycle: identify, incubate, hand off

The TCFD-to-ISSB transition is the cleanest example. The FSB identified climate-related financial disclosure as a cross-sectoral gap in 2015. It set up an industry-led task force (TCFD) to develop a framework. The framework was published in 2017, became the global default by around 2020, and was then absorbed into the IFRS Foundation's new International Sustainability Standards Board in 2022 and 2023 (with the TCFD itself formally disbanded in 2023). This is the FSB working as designed: spot the gap, develop the framework, hand it to a permanent home, move on. Expect the same lifecycle to play out for the FSB's current cross-sectoral work on crypto-assets and on NBFI.

Watch out Declining FSB attention to a topic is not the same as a topic becoming less important. When the FSB hands a work programme off to a sectoral standard-setter, the issue often grows in importance for the regulated firms; the standards become more detailed, more binding, and more local. Climate-related disclosure is a richer obligation for a UK or EU bank in 2026 than it was in 2017, even though the FSB itself does less direct work on it now. Tracking only the FSB's current priorities will miss the issues that have just become a national supervisor's priorities.

For an actuary, the FSB matters through four channels

First, the G-SIB designation, which feeds into the bank's capital surcharge and its TLAC requirement. Second, the TLAC and resolution framework, which determines the seniority and bail-inability of the bank's funding stack, with direct read-across to credit risk modelling for senior unsecured bonds. Third, the NBFI work programme, which expands the supervisory perimeter of bank counterparty exposures and increasingly affects how internal-models teams scope exposures to funds, securitisation vehicles, and crypto-asset issuers. Fourth, climate-related risk, where the FSB's TCFD-derived framework, now via ISSB, is the basis for the supervisory expectations that PRA, ECB, and other supervisors apply in Pillar 2.

Read FSB reports for the diagnosis, not the prescription

FSB reports are best read for their diagnostic content: what is the FSB worried about, why, and what data is it citing. The prescriptive content (the recommendations themselves) is usually high-level and will be translated into specific rules by the standard-setters or national authorities. A practitioner who wants to understand where supervisory attention is heading should read the FSB's annual NBFI Global Monitoring Report and its periodic vulnerabilities assessments; both give an early signal of the issues that will land as national supervisory expectations 18 to 36 months later.

Step 6

Test yourself

  1. What body did the FSB succeed, when was the predecessor body founded, and why did it need to be re-founded as the FSB in 2009?
  2. Distinguish the FSB's mandate from that of the BCBS. Where does each one stop and the other begin?
  3. Who is the FSB's political principal? Why does the FSB report to that body rather than to the UN or the IMF?
  4. Name the four FSB Standing Committees and explain what each does in one sentence.
  5. How many member jurisdictions does the FSB have, and what other categories of member sit alongside them? Roughly how many Plenary members in total?
  6. The FSB has produced very few "standards" in the BCBS sense. Name two and explain why each became an FSB original rather than being left to a sectoral standard-setter.
  7. Describe the FSB's peer-review programme. What two types of peer review run in parallel, and how do they relate to the IMF's FSAP and the BCBS's RCAP?
  8. What is the TCFD, when was it set up, and what happened to it in 2022 to 2023?
  9. An FSB report on non-bank financial intermediation flags rising leverage in open-ended funds. Trace the likely path from that finding to a binding rule on a bank's counterparty exposure to such a fund.
  10. The FSB is hosted at the BIS in Basel. Are the FSB and the BIS the same legal entity? What is the practical relationship between the two?
