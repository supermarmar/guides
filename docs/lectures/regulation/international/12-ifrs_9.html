IFRS 9: financial instruments, classification, and expected credit losses

IFRS 9 is a three-phase accounting standard that replaced IAS 39 from 1 January 2018. It rewrote how financial assets are classified and measured (amortised cost, fair value through other comprehensive income, fair value through profit or loss), it replaced the incurred-loss impairment model with a forward-looking expected credit loss model built around three stages, and it rebuilt hedge accounting on a more principles-based foundation. For banks, the impairment chapter is the most consequential single accounting reform of the post-crisis era.

Step 1

The 30-second version

IFRS 9 Financial Instruments replaced IAS 39 with effect from 1 January 2018 and is the IASB's response to the 2008 financial crisis. The standard runs in three phases. Phase one is classification and measurement: every financial asset is sorted into one of three categories (amortised cost, fair value through other comprehensive income, or fair value through profit or loss) by passing two tests, the business model test and the SPPI test on contractual cash flows. Phase two is impairment: banks recognise a forward-looking expected credit loss (ECL) allowance from origination, in three stages (12-month ECL while credit risk has not materially changed, lifetime ECL after a significant increase in credit risk, lifetime ECL with interest on amortised cost once an asset is credit-impaired). Phase three is hedge accounting: the IAS 39 80-to-125 per cent bright-line effectiveness test is replaced by a principles-based qualifying criterion that aligns hedge accounting more closely with a bank's actual risk management.

The change that mattered most for banks was phase two. IAS 39's incurred-loss model only allowed a provision once a loss event had occurred, which everyone knew arrived too late. IFRS 9 forces banks to provide for losses from day one of a loan's life and to escalate to a lifetime view as soon as credit risk deteriorates significantly. On transition in January 2018, most large banks saw provisions rise by roughly 30 to 50 per cent. Every bank now has a PD, LGD, and EAD model for accounting purposes, often sitting alongside the Basel IRB model and tuned differently (point-in-time and forward-looking rather than through-the-cycle and downturn).

Feynman test Imagine a bank as a person who runs a small lending business out of a shoebox. Under the old standard (IAS 39), the rule was: only write a loss into the books once a borrower has actually missed a payment or shown other obvious distress. The result was that on the day before a recession the shoebox looked healthy, and on the day a recession began the shoebox suddenly emptied. IFRS 9 says: even at the moment you lend, set aside a little money for the chance the borrower defaults in the next twelve months. If the borrower's credit risk deteriorates significantly, set aside enough to cover all the losses you would ever expect on the whole loan. If the borrower actually defaults, switch the interest accrual to net of allowances. The numbers in the shoebox now move smoothly with the economic outlook, instead of jumping all at once when bad news arrives.

This lecture adds: the institutional history of why IFRS 9 was issued in four tranches between 2009 and 2014; the economic mechanics of why ECL was designed as PD multiplied by LGD multiplied by EAD discounted by the original effective interest rate; the practical implementation choices banks make on staging (absolute versus relative PD thresholds, watch-list overlays, the 30-day rebuttable presumption); the close-but-distinct relationship with US GAAP CECL; the COVID-19 IASB statement of March 2020 and its consequences for overlay accounting; and a sharper account of where Basel IRB and IFRS 9 models can share architecture and where they must diverge (TTC versus PIT, downturn versus best-estimate, regulatory versus accounting LGD).
Step 2

Why this matters

The 2008 financial crisis exposed an awkward fact about the incurred-loss model in IAS 39. Through 2006 and 2007 banks across the developed world were originating residential mortgages, corporate loans, and structured exposures that were already, in hindsight, mispriced for risk. Their accounting balance sheets showed almost no impairment because the contractual cash flows had not yet missed and no observable loss event had occurred. When the loss events did arrive in 2008 and 2009, allowances spiked vertically over a few quarters. The G20 and the Financial Stability Board explicitly asked the IASB to fix the timing problem. The IASB's answer, after a five-year project and three rounds of consultation, is the expected credit loss model in IFRS 9.

For an actuary or modeller inside a bank, IFRS 9 has four direct day-to-day consequences. First, accounting impairment is now a model-driven number rather than a case-by-case judgement, which means the credit risk modelling team owns a quarterly closing process that the Chief Financial Officer signs off in front of auditors. Second, the model has to be forward-looking, which forces a macroeconomic scenario framework (typically three to five scenarios, each weighted by a probability) into the credit risk machinery. Third, staging decisions are judgmental and material, because moving an exposure from stage 1 to stage 2 typically multiplies the allowance several times over. Fourth, the choice of significant-increase-in-credit-risk (SICR) triggers has the largest single influence on stage 2 balances and therefore on group P&L; this is the area where supervisory benchmarking and audit scrutiny are highest.

The standard also matters for capital. The Basel framework treats IFRS 9 allowances as a deduction from regulatory capital where they exceed regulatory expected loss, and includes transitional arrangements that allow phased recognition of day-one ECL impact on CET1. The narrative banks tell investors in stress tests, capital planning, and Pillar 3 disclosures now runs through IFRS 9 ECL, not the older specific-plus-collective provision split. The standard is also close to, but not the same as, the US GAAP CECL standard, which requires lifetime ECL for every asset from day one with no staging. Banks operating in both jurisdictions run two impairment engines on the same portfolios.

Step 3

The mental model

IFRS 9 has two pictures that any actuary should be able to draw from memory. The first is the classification decision tree for a financial asset: ask the business-model question, ask the SPPI question, and a category falls out. The second is the three-stage ECL machine: every exposure starts in stage 1, can migrate to stage 2 if credit risk deteriorates significantly, and lands in stage 3 once credit-impaired, with a possibility of curing back to a lower stage.

Three flows of accounting consequence matter. Classification determines the measurement basis, which determines whether fair value changes hit profit or loss or other comprehensive income. The ECL allowance reduces the net carrying amount on the balance sheet for amortised-cost assets, and is presented through OCI for FVOCI assets without changing the FVOCI carrying amount. The stage of an exposure determines both the size of the allowance and whether interest revenue is calculated on the gross or the net carrying amount.

Step 4

The detail

History: IAS 39 to IFRS 9

The story begins with IAS 39 Financial Instruments: Recognition and Measurement, issued by the International Accounting Standards Committee in March 1999 and adopted by the IASB in April 2001. IAS 39 was widely criticised as the most complex IFRS standard ever written. It had four measurement categories (held-to-maturity, loans and receivables, available-for-sale, fair value through profit or loss), an opaque embedded-derivative test, a rule-driven hedge accounting framework with a quantitative 80-to-125 per cent effectiveness bright line, and an impairment model that allowed loss recognition only after the occurrence of an observable loss event. The IASB had always intended to replace IAS 39 in its entirety, but after the G20 leaders' London summit in April 2009 the project was split into three phases so that the most pressing problems could be solved first.

The four published tranches of IFRS 9 are: classification and measurement of financial assets (November 2009), classification and measurement of financial liabilities (October 2010), hedge accounting (November 2013), and impairment together with a limited refinement of classification (July 2014). The mandatory effective date was set as 1 January 2018, with early application permitted. Insurance entities and entities applying the IFRS for SMEs Standard had different transition arrangements. The hedge accounting chapter in IFRS 9 is optional: an entity may continue to apply IAS 39 hedge accounting until the IASB completes its separate macro hedging project.

Scope

IFRS 9 applies to all financial assets and most financial liabilities. Insurance contracts are within the scope of IFRS 17. Leases are within the scope of IFRS 16, except that lessee net investments in leases are subject to the IFRS 9 impairment requirements. Some loan commitments and financial guarantee contracts fall within IFRS 9 even though they are not, on their face, financial liabilities; the practical consequence is that undrawn lending commitments carry an ECL allowance.

Chapter 4: classification of financial assets

Classification is the gateway to measurement. Every financial asset is classified at initial recognition by reference to two tests. The first is the entity's business model for managing the asset, which can be hold-to-collect (HTC), hold-to-collect-and-sell (HTCS), or other (typically trading). The second is the SPPI test: whether the contractual cash flows of the asset are solely payments of principal and interest on the principal amount outstanding.

Business model test
Assessed at the level at which the entity actually manages the assets, which is usually a portfolio rather than an individual instrument. Hold-to-collect means the objective is to collect contractual cash flows over the life of the assets; occasional sales are permitted but should be infrequent and not significant in value. Hold-to-collect-and-sell means both collecting cash flows and selling are integral to the objective. The "other" business model captures trading books and assets actively managed on a fair-value basis.
SPPI test
Solely Payments of Principal and Interest. Principal is the fair value of the asset at initial recognition. Interest consists of consideration for the time value of money, for credit risk, for other basic lending risks and costs, and a profit margin. A vanilla fixed-rate loan passes. A loan whose interest is linked to the borrower's equity performance fails. A loan with a leverage feature on the interest rate fails. Failing SPPI sends the asset to FVTPL.
Amortised cost
HTC business model plus SPPI. The default category for the retail and corporate loan book of a commercial bank. Carrying amount is initial fair value plus transaction costs, accreted at the effective interest rate, minus principal repayments, minus the ECL allowance. Interest revenue is recognised by the effective interest method on the gross carrying amount (stages 1 and 2) or on the amortised cost (stage 3).
Fair value through other comprehensive income (FVOCI)
HTCS business model plus SPPI. Carrying amount on the balance sheet is fair value; fair-value changes are presented in OCI; the ECL allowance is also presented in OCI without reducing the balance-sheet carrying amount. On derecognition (typically sale), cumulative gains and losses are recycled from OCI to P&L. Typical use case: high-grade liquid bonds held for liquidity management.
Fair value through profit or loss (FVTPL)
Everything else: trading-book assets, derivatives, non-SPPI loans, and instruments designated at fair value to eliminate an accounting mismatch. Carrying amount is fair value; all fair-value changes go to P&L; no ECL allowance because the asset is already remeasured to fair value at each reporting date.
Equity instruments
Default category is FVTPL. At initial recognition, an entity may make an irrevocable election to present subsequent fair-value changes of a non-trading equity holding in OCI, with no recycling on disposal and no impairment testing. Dividends still hit P&L. This election is made instrument by instrument.

Chapter 5 part 1: financial liabilities

The classification and measurement of financial liabilities was largely carried forward from IAS 39. The default category is amortised cost. Trading liabilities and liabilities designated at FVTPL under the fair value option are measured at fair value through profit or loss. The one substantive change concerns own-credit risk: under IAS 39, a liability designated at FVTPL was remeasured to fair value with the full change including the own-credit component recognised in P&L, so a deterioration in the entity's own credit produced an accounting gain. IFRS 9 corrects this by requiring the own-credit component of the fair-value change to be presented in OCI rather than in P&L.

Chapter 5 part 2: the impairment model (ECL)

The expected credit loss model is the most consequential part of IFRS 9 for banks. The general approach (applicable to amortised-cost assets, FVOCI debt assets, lease receivables, loan commitments and financial guarantee contracts in scope) is organised around three stages. There is a simplified approach for trade receivables and contract assets, and a separate set of rules for purchased or originated credit-impaired (POCI) assets.

Stage 1
Financial instruments not credit-impaired at the reporting date and without significant increase in credit risk since initial recognition. The loss allowance equals 12-month expected credit losses, defined as the portion of lifetime ECL representing losses from default events possible within twelve months of the reporting date. Interest revenue is calculated by applying the effective interest rate to the gross carrying amount.
Stage 2
Credit risk has increased significantly since initial recognition but the asset is not yet credit-impaired. The loss allowance equals lifetime expected credit losses, defined as the present value of all credit losses that result from any default event over the remaining expected life of the instrument. Interest revenue is still calculated on the gross carrying amount.
Stage 3
Credit-impaired: one or more events have occurred that have a detrimental impact on the estimated future cash flows of the asset. The loss allowance equals lifetime ECL. Interest revenue is now calculated on the amortised cost (gross carrying amount net of the loss allowance), which is why a stage 3 transition typically produces an immediate decrease in net interest income as well as an increase in impairment expense.
Significant increase in credit risk (SICR)
An assessment made at each reporting date by comparing the risk of default occurring on the instrument as at the reporting date with the risk of default occurring at initial recognition. The comparison is made on the change in the risk of default, not on the change in expected loss amount. The standard offers a rebuttable presumption that SICR has occurred when contractual payments are more than 30 days past due, and an optional simplification that an instrument with low credit risk at the reporting date can be deemed not to have suffered SICR.
Definition of default
The standard allows an entity to use the definition of default consistent with internal credit risk management practice, subject to a rebuttable presumption that default does not occur later than 90 days past due. In practice most banks align the IFRS 9 default definition with the Basel definition under CRR Article 178 (90 days past due plus unlikeliness-to-pay indicators).
12-month ECL versus lifetime ECL
12-month ECL is the portion of lifetime ECL representing losses from default events possible in the twelve months after the reporting date. It is not the cash shortfalls predicted over the next twelve months; it is the lifetime cash shortfalls that will result if a default occurs in the next twelve months, weighted by the probability that the default occurs in that window. Lifetime ECL captures the expected losses from default events over the entire remaining contractual life.
Forward-looking information (FLI)
The estimate of ECL must reflect reasonable and supportable information about past events, current conditions, and forecasts of future economic conditions. In practice this means a multi-scenario macroeconomic framework: typically three to five named scenarios (base, upside, downside, severe downside) each with a probability weight, and PD and LGD curves conditioned on the macroeconomic variables in each scenario.
Simplified approach
Mandatory for trade receivables and contract assets without a significant financing component; available by election for lease receivables and for trade receivables and contract assets with a significant financing component. The allowance equals lifetime ECL from day one with no staging. Typical implementation is a provision matrix combining ageing buckets with historical and forward-looking loss rates.
POCI assets
Purchased or originated credit-impaired assets are credit-impaired at initial recognition. The effective interest rate is the credit-adjusted EIR, which already incorporates expected credit losses. The carrying amount on the balance sheet is the present value of expected cash flows at the credit-adjusted EIR. Subsequent changes in lifetime ECL go through P&L as impairment gain or loss. POCI assets never enter the stage 1/2/3 cycle.

The ECL formula

IFRS 9 does not prescribe a formula. It prescribes properties: the estimate must be an unbiased, probability-weighted amount; it must reflect the time value of money; and it must incorporate reasonable and supportable information including forecasts. The industry standard implementation, by direct analogy with Basel IRB, is to decompose ECL into a probability of default (PD), loss given default (LGD), and exposure at default (EAD), discounted to the reporting date using the original effective interest rate.

For a single exposure over its remaining expected life, the ECL allowance is:

$$ECL = \sum_{t=1}^{T} PD_{t} \cdot LGD_{t} \cdot EAD_{t} \cdot DF_{t}$$

where the sum runs over time buckets covering either the next twelve months (for stage 1) or the remaining expected life (for stage 2 and stage 3); $PD_t$ is the marginal probability of default in bucket $t$ conditional on survival to the start of $t$; $LGD_t$ is the loss given default applicable to a default in bucket $t$; $EAD_t$ is the expected exposure at default at the start of bucket $t$; and $DF_t$ is the discount factor from the midpoint of bucket $t$ to the reporting date using the original effective interest rate (or the credit-adjusted EIR for POCI assets).

For a probability-weighted multi-scenario calculation the formula becomes:

$$ECL = \sum_{s} w_{s} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^{T} PD_{t,s} \cdot LGD_{t,s} \cdot EAD_{t,s} \cdot DF_{t} \right]$$

where $w_s$ is the probability weight on scenario $s$ and the inner term is the scenario-conditional ECL. The discount factor is usually held constant across scenarios because the original effective interest rate is fixed at initial recognition, but PD, LGD and EAD are conditioned on the macroeconomic path in each scenario.

Chapter 6: hedge accounting

IFRS 9 rebuilt hedge accounting on a more principles-based foundation than IAS 39, with the explicit objective of aligning the accounting outcome more closely with the entity's actual risk management. The standard recognises three types of hedging relationship: fair value hedges (of changes in fair value of a recognised asset, liability or firm commitment attributable to a particular risk), cash flow hedges (of variability in cash flows attributable to a particular risk), and hedges of a net investment in a foreign operation.

The two principal changes from IAS 39 are the removal of the quantitative 80-to-125 per cent effectiveness bright line in favour of a principles-based qualifying criterion (the existence of an economic relationship, the dominance of credit risk over the fair-value change, and the appropriate designation of the hedge ratio), and broader eligibility for hedged items including risk components of non-financial items. Macro hedging is explicitly out of scope of the IFRS 9 chapter and remains under IAS 39 pending the IASB's separate Dynamic Risk Management project. Entities may make an accounting policy choice between applying IFRS 9 hedge accounting and continuing to apply IAS 39 hedge accounting in full.

Worked example: a corporate loan migrating through the stages

Setup. On 1 January 2024 a bank originates a five-year amortised-cost corporate loan of £1,000,000 to a single corporate borrower. The contractual coupon is 6 per cent annual, paid in arrears. There are no transaction costs and no fees, so the effective interest rate equals the contractual rate of 6 per cent. The original 12-month PD is 0.5 per cent, the lifetime PD over the remaining life is 2.5 per cent, the LGD is 40 per cent best-estimate, and the EAD at the reporting date equals the gross carrying amount.

Stage 1 at origination (1 January 2024). The 12-month ECL is computed by applying the PD multiplied by LGD multiplied by EAD product, discounted to the reporting date. For simplicity the discount over a single 12-month horizon at 6 per cent is approximated as a factor of $1/1.06 \approx 0.9434$:

$$ECL_{\text{12m}} = 0.005 \cdot 0.40 \cdot 1{,}000{,}000 \cdot 0.9434 \approx 1{,}887$$

The bank books an opening loss allowance of approximately £1,887 against the loan and a corresponding impairment expense in P&L. Interest revenue is recognised at 6 per cent on the gross carrying amount of £1,000,000.

Stage 2 transition (31 December 2025). By the end of year 2, the borrower's external rating has been downgraded from BBB to BB-, internal monitoring has placed the exposure on the watch list, and the bank's PD model conditional on the latest forward-looking macroeconomic scenarios produces a remaining-life lifetime PD of 9 per cent (up from an origination-equivalent remaining-life PD of 2 per cent at the reporting date). The relative deterioration triggers the bank's SICR criterion. The exposure moves to stage 2 and the allowance must now reflect lifetime ECL. Approximating the lifetime ECL as PD multiplied by LGD multiplied by EAD with a single average discount factor of 0.84:

$$ECL_{\text{lifetime}} = 0.09 \cdot 0.40 \cdot 1{,}000{,}000 \cdot 0.84 \approx 30{,}240$$

The bank increases the allowance from roughly £1,887 to roughly £30,240, recognising an impairment expense of approximately £28,353. Interest revenue is still recognised at 6 per cent on the gross carrying amount.

Stage 3 transition (30 June 2026). Six months later the borrower misses two consecutive interest payments and is more than 90 days past due. The bank's default definition is triggered. The exposure moves to stage 3 and is individually assessed; the workout team estimates an expected recovery of 50 per cent of the outstanding balance through collateral enforcement and counter-claims, implying an LGD on individual assessment of 50 per cent. The lifetime ECL on individual assessment becomes approximately:

$$ECL_{\text{stage 3}} = 1.00 \cdot 0.50 \cdot 1{,}000{,}000 \cdot 0.93 \approx 465{,}000$$

(PD is set to 1 because default has occurred.) The allowance is increased to approximately £465,000, with the further impairment expense of approximately £435,000 hitting P&L. From this point interest revenue is calculated by applying the effective interest rate of 6 per cent not to the gross carrying amount of £1,000,000 but to the amortised cost of approximately £535,000 (gross carrying amount net of the allowance), which immediately compresses the bank's net interest income on this loan.

The cumulative impairment expense recognised in P&L across the three stages is approximately £465,000, against an outstanding balance of £1,000,000 and an ultimate workout recovery of around £500,000 (assuming the bank's recovery estimate is realised). Compare this with the IAS 39 incurred-loss path under which the bank would have booked essentially nothing until June 2026 and then a single specific provision of approximately £500,000 in one quarter.

Modifications and derecognition

When the contractual cash flows of a financial asset are renegotiated and the modification is not substantial enough to require derecognition (the standard's substantial-modification threshold is judgmental but is usually approached via a 10 per cent present-value test borrowed from financial-liability practice), the entity recalculates the gross carrying amount as the present value of the modified cash flows discounted at the original effective interest rate. The difference between the old and new gross carrying amounts is a modification gain or loss recognised immediately in profit or loss. Fees incurred adjust the carrying amount and are amortised over the remaining life. When the modification is substantial, the original asset is derecognised, a new asset is recognised, and the new asset is classified afresh under IFRS 9 (often re-entering stage 1 if the new credit risk at recognition no longer meets the SICR criterion relative to the new origination point).

The treatment of forborne exposures is a sensitive area where IFRS 9 staging and the EBA forborne-exposure framework intersect. A forbearance measure typically counts as SICR evidence, but does not automatically result in stage 3 unless the entity's credit-impaired definition is triggered.

Interaction with Basel IRB

IFRS 9 and the Basel IRB framework both decompose credit risk into PD, LGD and EAD, but they answer different questions and therefore calibrate the parameters differently. Basel IRB capital is intended to cover unexpected loss over a one-year horizon to a 99.9 per cent confidence level; the parameters are through-the-cycle (PD), downturn (LGD) and downturn-stressed (EAD). IFRS 9 ECL is intended to estimate the probability-weighted expected loss; the parameters are point-in-time, forward-looking and best-estimate. The differences are summarised below.

PD: through-the-cycle versus point-in-time and forward-looking
Basel IRB requires a through-the-cycle PD that averages across the credit cycle and is intentionally stable. IFRS 9 requires a point-in-time PD that reflects current economic conditions and forward-looking macroeconomic scenarios. Many banks build a single rating model and apply either a TTC anchor (for Basel) or a PIT and scenario-conditional transformation (for IFRS 9) to derive the appropriate parameter.
LGD: downturn versus best-estimate
Basel IRB LGD is calibrated to a downturn scenario; IFRS 9 LGD is a best-estimate (probability-weighted across scenarios). The Basel downturn LGD is therefore typically higher than the IFRS 9 LGD for the same exposure, particularly for collateralised lending.
EAD: regulatory CCF versus expected exposure profile
Basel IRB uses a credit conversion factor (CCF) applied to undrawn commitments. IFRS 9 requires an expected exposure profile over the relevant horizon, which may diverge from the regulatory CCF for revolving products such as credit cards and overdrafts.
Horizon: one-year versus 12-month or lifetime
Basel IRB capital uses a fixed one-year horizon. IFRS 9 uses a 12-month horizon in stage 1 and a lifetime horizon (potentially many years) in stage 2 and stage 3. For a long-dated mortgage portfolio, the stage 2 ECL can be twenty to fifty times the stage 1 ECL on the same exposure.
Definition of default
The Basel definition under CRR Article 178 (90 days past due plus unlikeliness-to-pay) is normally adopted as the IFRS 9 default definition to ensure consistency. A divergence here is permitted but invites supervisory and audit attention.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The transition was the largest accounting event of the post-crisis decade

On 1 January 2018, most large banks saw provisions rise by 30 to 50 per cent on transition to IFRS 9. The day-one CET1 impact was sufficient that the Basel framework was amended to allow a five-year transitional add-back of a declining proportion of the day-one ECL increase, and most jurisdictions made use of the transitional. The transition was managed quietly, but in retrospect it was the single largest accounting change in banking since IAS 39 was introduced.

SICR is where the standard meets reality and reality wins

The standard is deliberately silent on the precise SICR threshold. In practice every bank operates a combination of three things: an absolute backstop (the 30-day rebuttable presumption), a relative trigger expressed as a multiple or absolute increase in lifetime PD relative to the origination PD (calibrated portfolio by portfolio), and qualitative overlays (watch list, forbearance, credit-risk management actions). The relative-PD trigger is the area of largest model risk and the area where supervisory benchmarking exercises (EBA, ECB) consistently identify the widest inter-bank variation. If you are designing or validating an IFRS 9 model, expect to spend at least as much time on the SICR design as on the PD, LGD and EAD models combined.

Watch out The 30-day rebuttable presumption is rarely rebutted in practice. The standard technically allows rebuttal where the entity has reasonable and supportable information demonstrating that credit risk has not significantly increased even though contractual payments are 30 days past due; supervisors and auditors almost never accept this in practice. Treat the 30-day backstop as a hard absolute trigger and design your relative-PD threshold to bite earlier.

Forward-looking information was tested hard by COVID-19

In March 2020 the IASB issued a short statement clarifying that entities should not mechanically apply their existing ECL methodology in the light of the COVID-19 pandemic. In particular, the extension of payment holidays to whole classes of borrowers should not automatically be treated as SICR. The statement explicitly endorsed the use of post-model overlays where the underlying ECL models could not credibly incorporate the specific effects of the pandemic and the unprecedented government support measures. The episode established overlays as a permanent feature of IFRS 9 implementations rather than a transitional patch. Most large banks now run a documented overlay framework with governance, sensitivity analysis and disclosure equal in rigour to the underlying models.

IFRS 9 is now the foundation of the credit-risk capital-planning narrative

The forward-looking, multi-scenario, probability-weighted nature of IFRS 9 ECL makes it a natural anchor for credit-risk stress testing and ICAAP. The same scenarios that drive ECL are typically used in regulatory and reverse stress tests. The same PD and LGD curves, with parameter substitutions, can be repurposed for severe-scenario projections. The result is a tighter integration of accounting impairment, regulatory capital and internal capital adequacy than has ever existed before, but also a single set of judgments (scenarios, weights, SICR thresholds, overlays) that propagates through every aspect of credit-risk reporting.

The relationship with US GAAP CECL is close but not identical

The FASB issued ASU 2016-13, the Current Expected Credit Loss (CECL) standard, on a parallel timeline to IFRS 9. CECL requires lifetime ECL for every in-scope financial asset from day one, with no staging. The conceptual rationale is similar to IFRS 9 (forward-looking provisioning) but the operational mechanics differ materially: there is no SICR concept, no 12-month allowance, and consequently no stage transition gain or loss. For globally active banks reporting under both standards (notably the major US, Canadian and Japanese banks), the result is two parallel impairment engines on the same portfolios, with reconciliations explained in detail in the financial statement disclosures. CECL is the subject of lecture 30 in the wider series.

Always read the standard alongside the application guidance

The body of IFRS 9 is short and principles-based. The application guidance in Appendix B is where the operational detail lives: SICR triggers, default definition, expected-life conventions for revolving products, treatment of collateral, treatment of modifications, discount rate conventions. Banks that misread an Appendix B paragraph make the kind of mistakes that surface as restatements two years later. Always read the standard text and the corresponding Appendix B paragraph in parallel.

Further reading

  • IFRS Foundation, IFRS 9 Project Summary (July 2014). The IASB's own short summary of the completed standard, including the rationale for the three-stage model.

  • EBA, Guidelines on credit institutions' credit risk management practices and accounting for expected credit losses, May 2017. The EU supervisory companion to the BCBS guidance.
  • EY, Deloitte and KPMG IFRS 9 impairment handbooks. The Big Four handbooks are the most useful practical reference for implementation questions; updated annually.
