Basel III: the post-crisis rebuild

After the 2008 crisis the Basel Committee redesigned bank prudential regulation from scratch. Basel III did not replace Basel II, it patched its three biggest holes: capital that turned out to be too thin and too low-quality, leverage that grew unconstrained behind risk-weighted ratios, and liquidity that the framework had ignored entirely. The result is a stack of CET1, AT1 and Tier 2 capital sitting beneath layered buffers, a leverage-ratio backstop running alongside the risk-based ratios, and two new liquidity standards (the LCR over thirty days and the NSFR over one year) governing how a bank funds itself.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Basel III is the post-crisis rebuild of Basel II. Published in December 2010 (BCBS 189 for capital, BCBS 188 for liquidity) and phased in from 2013 to 2019, it leaves the Basel II risk-weight architecture broadly intact but rewrites three things. First, the definition of capital: common equity becomes a separate tier called Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1), with hard minimum of 4.5 per cent of risk-weighted assets; total Tier 1 must be at least 6 per cent; total capital remains at 8 per cent. Second, a set of buffers sits on top of the minimums: a capital conservation buffer of 2.5 per cent of CET1; a countercyclical buffer of 0 to 2.5 per cent set by national macroprudential authorities; and a G-SIB or D-SIB surcharge of 1 to 3.5 per cent for systemically important banks. Third, two non-risk-based standards run in parallel: a leverage ratio of 3 per cent of Tier 1 to total exposure (4 per cent in South Africa), a Liquidity Coverage Ratio of 100 per cent over a thirty-day stress, and a Net Stable Funding Ratio of 100 per cent over one year.

The minimum is unchanged at 8 per cent of risk-weighted assets, but a bank operating at the minimum after the phase-in is a different creature. It must hold at least 4.5 per cent CET1, plus 2.5 per cent CET1 conservation buffer, plus whatever countercyclical and systemic surcharges apply in its jurisdictions. A medium-sized internationally active bank, with say 1 per cent CCyB and 1 per cent D-SIB, is required to hold 9 per cent CET1 before it can pay a dividend, on top of meeting the 6 per cent Tier 1, the 8 per cent total ratio, the 3 per cent leverage ratio, the 100 per cent LCR and the 100 per cent NSFR. Capital quality, capital quantity, leverage, short-term liquidity and structural funding all bind simultaneously. The bank that minimises one minimum will usually find another binding.

Feynman test Imagine you ran a pub. Before 2008 the rule was "keep enough money in the till to cover one in twelve of your drinks if they spill". You could meet this by counting cheques the brewery had not yet honoured, IOUs from regulars, and a painting on the wall, all as "money". After 2008 the rule changes. Now you must keep one in twenty in actual cash, on top of which you must hold an extra one in forty as a "rainy-day jar" you cannot touch without telling the council, plus an extra one in forty if the council thinks there is too much drinking happening generally, plus an extra one in forty if you are the only pub in town. On top of all that you must hold enough cash to pay for a month of beer deliveries even if no customers turn up, and enough longer-dated funding to cover a year of being unfashionable. That is Basel III. Same headline ratio, completely different pub.

Step 2

Why this matters

The crisis Basel II did not see coming

The 2007 to 2009 financial crisis was the proximate cause. The first wave struck in August 2007 when BNP Paribas froze redemptions on three of its asset-backed money-market funds. The second wave hit in March 2008 when Bear Stearns failed and was sold to JPMorgan over a weekend with Federal Reserve support. The third wave, in September 2008, took down Lehman Brothers (which filed for Chapter 11), Merrill Lynch (which was sold to Bank of America), AIG (which was rescued with an 85 billion dollar emergency loan from the Federal Reserve), Washington Mutual, and Wachovia. In the UK, Northern Rock had already suffered a public run in September 2007 and was nationalised in February 2008; the Royal Bank of Scotland and HBOS required emergency capital injections from the UK government in October 2008. In continental Europe, Fortis, Dexia, Hypo Real Estate and the Icelandic banks all required public support. By early 2009 most major developed-country banking systems were either being recapitalised by their state, were operating under emergency liquidity facilities from their central bank, or both.

The post-mortem identified three failures of the Basel II framework. The first was capital quality. Basel II's capital definition allowed instruments with weak loss-absorption properties to count as Tier 1: deferred tax assets, mortgage servicing rights, hybrid securities with step-up coupons that incentivised redemption, minority interests in subsidiaries that could not be moved across the group, and goodwill from acquisitions. When losses came, much of the Tier 1 capital on the balance sheet could not absorb them. The market knew this and discounted reported Tier 1 ratios in favour of "tangible common equity" metrics that stripped out the soft components. The second was leverage. Several of the failed firms had reported Tier 1 ratios in the high single digits or low double digits in 2007, while running balance-sheet leverage of thirty to fifty times equity. The risk-weighted ratios said they were safe; the leverage said they were not. The third was liquidity. Basel II had no liquidity standard at all. Banks funded long-dated illiquid assets with overnight wholesale paper, and when the wholesale market closed in 2008 they could not roll. Central banks had to fund the entire wholesale market.

The political timetable

The political response came through the G20. At the Pittsburgh summit in September 2009, leaders endorsed a programme of financial-sector reform to be developed by the BCBS and the newly upgraded Financial Stability Board. At the Seoul summit in November 2010, leaders endorsed the capital and liquidity reforms agreed by the BCBS earlier that year. The BCBS published the agreed text in December 2010 as two documents: BCBS 189 (capital) and BCBS 188 (liquidity). The package was deliberately staged over six years: minimum CET1 ratios began phasing in on 1 January 2013, the capital conservation buffer began phasing in from 1 January 2016, the countercyclical buffer and G-SIB surcharge from the same date, the leverage ratio became a Pillar 1 requirement on 1 January 2018, the LCR became fully binding on 1 January 2019, and the NSFR on 1 January 2018 in most jurisdictions (with delays in some).

The staging reflects the political reality that banks could not be told to triple their capital and write a new funding plan overnight in the middle of a recovery. A bank can raise capital, retain earnings, or shrink risk-weighted assets to improve its ratio; the staging gave them six to nine years to do all three.

Why Basel III did not become Basel III the moment it was published

Even before BCBS 189 was finalised in December 2010, the Committee knew the framework would need further work. The 2010 text left several big items unresolved: the standardised approach for counterparty credit risk (eventually published as SA-CCR in 2014); the FRTB market-risk overhaul (consultations 2012 onwards, finalised 2019); the constraints on internal models for credit risk and the introduction of an output floor (the 2017 finalisation, BCBS d424). The 2010 to 2013 reforms in this lecture are the "original" Basel III. The 2014 SA-CCR and CVA additions are usually treated as part of Basel III. The 2017 finalisation is variously called "Basel III finalisation", "Basel IV" or, in the UK, "Basel 3.1". Lecture 5 covers the 2017 finalisation; lecture 6 covers the FRTB; this lecture stops at the 2010 to 2013 package and references the rest only where it bears on the architecture.

Step 3

The mental model

The Basel III capital stack is a layered cake. The minimum at the bottom is the same shape as Basel II (8 per cent of RWA) but the recipe is stricter. The buffers above it are new. The leverage ratio runs alongside the stack as a backstop, calibrated so that the risk-weighted requirements remain the binding constraint for most banks most of the time. The two liquidity standards run on a separate balance sheet entirely.

Three things to take from the picture. First, the bottom 8 per cent of RWA is unchanged in headline terms from Basel II. The redesign is in the recipe (CET1 is now a separate, harder tier) and in everything that sits above the 8 per cent line. Second, the buffers are stacked: they apply on top of the minimums and are filled with CET1 only. A breach of the buffers does not violate the minimum, but it does restrict the bank's ability to pay dividends, buy back shares, or pay discretionary bonuses. Third, the leverage ratio, LCR, and NSFR are parallel constraints, not parts of the same stack. A bank can hit all the capital ratios and fail the LCR. A bank can hit the LCR and fail the leverage ratio. The framework binds the joint.

Step 4

The detail

Capital quality: the three tiers redefined

Basel III rewrote the definition of capital. Total regulatory capital remains the sum of Tier 1 (going-concern) and Tier 2 (gone-concern), but Tier 1 is split into Common Equity Tier 1 and Additional Tier 1, and the eligibility criteria for each are tightened. The "going-concern" versus "gone-concern" distinction matters: CET1 and AT1 must absorb losses while the bank is still operating; Tier 2 only needs to absorb losses in a wind-down or resolution.

Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1)
The highest-quality, fully loss-absorbing form of capital. Consists of: common shares issued by the bank that meet the strict eligibility criteria; share premium (stock surplus) on those common shares; retained earnings; accumulated other comprehensive income and other disclosed reserves; eligible minority interests in consolidated subsidiaries; minus a long list of regulatory adjustments (goodwill, other intangibles, deferred tax assets that depend on future profitability, mortgage-servicing rights above thresholds, defined-benefit pension surplus, significant investments in financial entities, the IRB shortfall of provisions to expected losses, and others). After all deductions, CET1 must be at least 4.5 per cent of risk-weighted assets at all times.
Additional Tier 1 (AT1)
Going-concern capital that is not common equity. Must be perpetual (no maturity date, no step-ups or incentives to redeem), subordinated to depositors, general creditors and subordinated debt, with fully discretionary non-cumulative coupons (the bank can cancel them at any time without triggering default), and must absorb losses either through conversion into common equity or through a principal write-down when a pre-specified trigger is breached (typically CET1 falling below 5.125 per cent). The instruments are commonly called "AT1 contingent convertibles" or "CoCos". Combined with CET1, total Tier 1 must be at least 6 per cent of RWA.
Tier 2
Gone-concern capital. Subordinated to depositors and general creditors; minimum original maturity of five years; amortised on a straight-line basis over the last five years before maturity (so a six-year bond counts in full this year, 80 per cent next year, and so on); no incentives to redeem; loss absorption only at the point of resolution. General provisions held under the standardised approach for credit risk also qualify, capped at 1.25 per cent of credit RWA (0.6 per cent under the IRB approach where total expected loss is less than eligible provisions). Combined with Tier 1, total capital must be at least 8 per cent of RWA.

The arithmetic is rigid. Total capital must be at least 8 per cent of RWA. CET1 must be at least 4.5 per cent. Total Tier 1 (CET1 plus AT1) must be at least 6 per cent. The implication is that of the 8 per cent total, at most 3.5 per cent can be AT1 plus Tier 2 combined, and of that 3.5 per cent at most 2 per cent can be Tier 2. In practice the breakdown is 4.5 per cent CET1, 1.5 per cent AT1, 2 per cent Tier 2.

The regulatory adjustments to CET1

The single biggest change from Basel II was the cleanup of deductions. Under Basel II, many adjustments were taken 50 per cent from Tier 1 and 50 per cent from Tier 2. Under Basel III, almost all deductions are taken in full from CET1. The principal deductions are:

  • Goodwill and other intangible assets, deducted in full. This removes the soft acquisition premium that drove much of the pre-crisis Tier 1 inflation.
  • Deferred tax assets that rely on the future profitability of the bank to be realised, deducted in full from CET1; DTAs may be netted with DTLs only if both relate to the same taxation authority.
  • Defined-benefit pension fund surplus, deducted from CET1 net of associated DTLs and unless the bank has unrestricted access to the asset.
  • Investments in own shares (treasury stock), reciprocal cross-holdings in the capital of other banks, and significant investments in unconsolidated financial institutions above a 10 per cent threshold.
  • Mortgage-servicing rights, threshold deductions above specified percentages of CET1.
  • The IRB shortfall: where expected loss under the IRB approach exceeds eligible provisions, the difference is deducted from CET1. This deduction is what makes the IRB approach internally consistent with the accounting; without it banks could carry IRB EL on the RWA side without holding capital for it.
  • Cash-flow hedge reserve related to the hedging of items not on the balance sheet at fair value, removed (in either direction) so that volatility from accounting timing does not feed into CET1.

The minimum ratios

The three minimum ratios are stated as percentages of risk-weighted assets, where RWA is calculated using the same standardised or IRB approaches as under Basel II (with revisions for counterparty credit risk and securitisation, and with the FRTB market-risk rules from 2023). Formally:

CET1 ratio = CET1 capital / risk-weighted assets >= 4.5 per cent
Tier 1 ratio = (CET1 + AT1) / risk-weighted assets >= 6.0 per cent
Total capital ratio = (CET1 + AT1 + Tier 2) / risk-weighted assets >= 8.0 per cent

All three must be met at all times, not just at quarter-end. The 8 per cent headline is identical to Basel I and Basel II; the change is the recomposition.

The capital conservation buffer

Above the 4.5 per cent CET1 minimum sits a fixed buffer of 2.5 per cent of CET1, called the capital conservation buffer (CCoB). This is the first of three buffers, all required to be filled with CET1. The CCoB is not a minimum: a bank operating with CET1 between 4.5 per cent and 7 per cent has breached the buffer but has not breached the minimum. The consequence of breach is not enforcement action but a constraint on distributions: dividends, share buybacks, and discretionary bonus payments must be retained inside the bank until the buffer is rebuilt.

The mechanism is the capital conservation table. The buffer is divided into four quartiles, and the maximum distributable amount falls as the bank moves down the quartiles:

CET1 ratio                Minimum conservation ratio
                          (per cent of earnings retained)
4.5 per cent to 5.125 per cent     100 per cent (no distributions)
>5.125 per cent to 5.75 per cent    80 per cent
>5.75 per cent to 6.375 per cent    60 per cent
>6.375 per cent to 7.0 per cent     40 per cent
>7.0 per cent                        0 per cent (no constraint)

A bank with CET1 of 5.5 per cent (inside the second quartile of the buffer) must retain at least 80 per cent of net income for the year, meaning it can distribute at most 20 per cent in dividends, buybacks and discretionary bonuses combined. The retained 80 per cent rebuilds CET1. If the bank wants to distribute more than the constraint, it must raise capital privately by an offsetting amount and discuss the plan with its supervisor.

The countercyclical buffer

On top of the CCoB sits the countercyclical buffer (CCyB), ranging from 0 to 2.5 per cent of CET1, set by national macroprudential authorities. The buffer is symmetric: it is raised when the authority judges that credit growth in the jurisdiction has become excessive and is contributing to the build-up of system-wide risk; it is lowered (or released entirely) when the cycle turns. The intent is to lean against the credit cycle: build capital in good times so that it can be released in bad times without forcing banks to deleverage at the worst moment.

The principal indicator the BCBS recommends is the credit-to-GDP gap: the deviation of the ratio of private-sector credit to GDP from its long-run trend. A widening positive gap signals that credit is growing faster than the underlying economy. National authorities are free to use additional indicators and to exercise judgement; the credit-to-GDP gap is a guide, not a formula. Decisions to raise the CCyB must be pre-announced up to twelve months in advance to give banks time to adjust; decisions to lower it take effect immediately.

The buffer is jurisdiction-specific. A bank exposed to credit in multiple jurisdictions faces a bank-specific CCyB equal to the weighted average of the buffers in those jurisdictions, weighted by the bank's credit exposures. The macroprudential authority that sets the buffer differs by country: in the UK it is the Financial Policy Committee at the Bank of England; in the eurozone, the European Systemic Risk Board coordinates national authorities; in South Africa, the Financial Stability Committee of the SARB; in the US, the Federal Reserve Board (which has so far never set the buffer above zero).

The G-SIB and D-SIB surcharges

On top of the CCoB and CCyB sits the systemic-importance surcharge. There are two variants. Global systemically important banks (G-SIBs) are designated by the Financial Stability Board using a methodology agreed with the BCBS in 2011 (and refined since), measuring five dimensions: size, interconnectedness, substitutability (how hard it would be to replace the bank's services), complexity, and cross-jurisdictional activity. The annual list, published in November, places each G-SIB in one of five buckets with associated CET1 surcharges from 1 per cent (Bucket 1) to 3.5 per cent (Bucket 5, currently empty as a deterrent against ever larger banks).

Domestic systemically important banks (D-SIBs) are designated by national authorities under a framework set out by the BCBS in October 2012. The methodology mirrors the G-SIB methodology but with adaptations for the national context: typical criteria are size, interconnectedness, substitutability, and complexity, and the surcharge is set by national discretion, typically 0 to 2.5 per cent of CET1. South Africa, for example, designates Absa, FirstRand, Investec, Nedbank and Standard Bank as D-SIBs and applies a higher loss-absorbency requirement of 1 to 2.5 per cent depending on the bank.

A bank may be both a G-SIB and a D-SIB; in that case the higher of the two surcharges applies, not the sum. South Africa has no G-SIBs but five D-SIBs. The UK has multiple G-SIBs (HSBC, Barclays, Standard Chartered) which are also D-SIBs by definition; their effective surcharge is the G-SIB number.

The leverage ratio

Running alongside the risk-weighted ratios is the leverage ratio, a simple non-risk-based backstop. The numerator is Tier 1 capital (the same figure that feeds the risk-weighted Tier 1 ratio). The denominator is the bank's total exposure measure: on-balance-sheet assets net of specific provisions and valuation adjustments, plus the credit equivalent of off-balance-sheet exposures and derivatives, with no netting allowed for loans and deposits and no recognition of physical or financial collateral or guarantees.

Leverage ratio = Tier 1 capital / total exposure measure >= 3 per cent

The 3 per cent calibration was the subject of years of negotiation. The US Federal Reserve and FDIC pushed for higher (5 per cent and 6 per cent for the largest US bank holding companies, applied unilaterally as the "Enhanced Supplementary Leverage Ratio"); European banks pushed for lower or for the inclusion of central-bank reserves to be exempted. The BCBS settled on 3 per cent in 2014 and made it a Pillar 1 minimum from 2018. G-SIBs face a leverage-ratio buffer equal to 50 per cent of their risk-weighted G-SIB CET1 surcharge: a Bucket 1 G-SIB with a 1 per cent CET1 surcharge has a leverage ratio of 3.5 per cent.

South Africa applies a 4 per cent minimum leverage ratio, one per cent higher than the Basel calibration. The SARB also uses a slightly different exposure measure, focusing on non-risk-sensitive exposures including off-balance-sheet items.

The leverage ratio is designed not to bite for most banks most of the time. The deliberate calibration places it well below the typical Tier 1 ratio that would result from meeting the risk-weighted requirements. It bites when a bank has a large balance sheet of low-risk-weight assets (a derivatives clearing book, a repo book, a portfolio of sovereign bonds): the risk-weighted ratios say capital is comfortable, but the unweighted leverage ratio reveals the underlying balance sheet is large. This is what was missed in the run-up to 2008.

The Liquidity Coverage Ratio

The first of the two new liquidity standards is the Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR). The principle is simple: a bank must hold enough high-quality liquid assets (HQLA) to survive a thirty-day stress scenario specified by the Committee. The arithmetic is:

LCR = stock of HQLA / total net cash outflows over 30 calendar days >= 100 per cent

The stress scenario is calibrated to a combined idiosyncratic and market-wide shock, similar to the conditions of late 2008. Net cash outflows are computed by applying prescribed run-off rates to liability categories and inflow rates to asset categories, summed over the thirty days.

The numerator is the stock of HQLA. The Committee defines three tiers, with caps:

Level 1 HQLA
The most liquid assets, included at full value with no cap. Coins and banknotes; central-bank reserves that can be drawn down in stress; marketable securities of sovereigns, central banks, public-sector entities, the BIS, the IMF, and multilateral development banks, where they qualify for a 0 per cent risk weight under the standardised approach; sovereign or central-bank debt issued in the bank's domestic currency. Typically high-grade government bonds.
Level 2A HQLA
Sovereign and corporate debt of high quality (rated AA- or better) and covered bonds. Included at 85 per cent of market value (15 per cent haircut). Combined with Level 2B, cannot exceed 40 per cent of total HQLA.
Level 2B HQLA
Lower-quality but still liquid assets: corporate debt rated between A+ and BBB-, eligible RMBS, and listed common equity meeting strict criteria. Included with haircuts of 25 to 50 per cent. Cannot exceed 15 per cent of total HQLA.

The denominator is total net cash outflows over thirty days, capped so that inflows cannot exceed 75 per cent of outflows (the bank must hold some standalone HQLA, not rely entirely on offsetting inflows). Run-off rates by liability category include:

Stable retail deposits (insured, established relationship)        3 to 5 per cent
Less-stable retail deposits                                      10 per cent and above
Unsecured wholesale funding from small businesses                 5 to 10 per cent
Operational deposits (clearing, custody, cash management)        25 per cent
Non-operational deposits from non-financial corporates           40 per cent
Unsecured wholesale funding from financial institutions         100 per cent
Repo and reverse-repo against Level 1 HQLA                        0 per cent
Repo and reverse-repo against non-HQLA                          100 per cent
Undrawn committed credit facilities to retail                    5 per cent
Undrawn committed credit facilities to financial institutions   40 per cent

The run-off rates are stylised and conservative. The high run-off on wholesale funding (100 per cent on financial institutions, 40 per cent on non-financial corporates) reflects what happened in 2008 to 2009: wholesale funding evaporated within days, retail deposits were sticky.

The Net Stable Funding Ratio

The second liquidity standard, the Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR), addresses structural maturity mismatch. Where the LCR asks "do you have enough liquid assets to survive thirty days?", the NSFR asks "is your funding structure sustainable over a year?". The ratio is:

NSFR = available stable funding (ASF) / required stable funding (RSF) >= 100 per cent

Available stable funding is liabilities and equity weighted by their assumed stability over a one-year horizon. Capital and long-term debt count at 100 per cent. Stable retail deposits count at 95 per cent. Less-stable retail deposits at 90 per cent. Non-financial corporate deposits at 50 per cent. Short-term wholesale funding from financial institutions at 0 per cent. Required stable funding is assets weighted by their assumed liquidity: cash and Level 1 HQLA at 0 to 5 per cent; performing residential mortgages at 65 per cent; other retail loans at 50 to 85 per cent; corporate loans at 50 to 85 per cent; long-term illiquid assets at 100 per cent.

The economic effect is to penalise banks that fund long-dated illiquid assets with short-dated wholesale paper. A bank making twenty-year mortgages funded by overnight repo will have a high RSF (the mortgage gets a 65 per cent weight) and a low ASF (overnight repo from a financial institution gets 0 per cent). The NSFR pushes the bank to fund the mortgages with stickier liabilities (term deposits, term debt, equity) or to shorten the asset side.

The ILAAP and the liquidity SREP

Alongside the quantitative standards, Basel III added a Pillar-2-style framework for liquidity management called the Internal Liquidity Adequacy Assessment Process (ILAAP). The ILAAP is the liquidity equivalent of the ICAAP: a comprehensive, annual, point-in-time self-assessment by the bank of its liquidity position, funding plan, contingency plan, and stress-testing programme. The ILAAP is reviewed by the supervisor through a liquidity SREP, which gives the supervisor a basis to require additional liquidity buffers (Pillar 2 add-ons to the LCR or NSFR) or to challenge the bank's liquidity-risk management. The ILAAP must cover at minimum: franchise-viability risk, retail funding risk, wholesale funding risk, off-balance-sheet exposure risk, marketable and non-marketable liquidity risk, cross-currency liquidity risk, intra-day liquidity risk, inter-entity liquidity risk and funding concentration risk.

Counterparty credit risk and CVA: a Basel III addition

Although not in the original December 2010 publication, two further additions to Basel III in 2011 to 2014 are usually included in the package. First, a CVA capital charge was introduced to capture the risk of mark-to-market losses on counterparty credit risk in OTC derivatives. During the 2008 crisis, losses from credit-spread widening on counterparties (CVA losses) were roughly twice as large as losses from outright counterparty defaults; the Basel II framework had charged for the default piece but not the spread piece. The 2010 BCBS 189 added a CVA charge under both standardised and advanced approaches. Second, in 2014, the BCBS replaced the old Current Exposure Method (CEM) and Standardised Method for measuring counterparty exposures with the Standardised Approach for Counterparty Credit Risk (SA-CCR), a more risk-sensitive non-internal-model approach. The 2017 Basel III finalisation later replaced the internally modelled CVA approach with two new approaches (SA-CVA and BA-CVA); that revision is covered in lecture 5.

Worked example: assembling the CET1 minimum stack

Consider Bank A, a mid-sized internationally active bank operating in two jurisdictions. The jurisdictions' macroprudential authorities have set countercyclical buffers of 1.0 per cent and 0.5 per cent respectively. Bank A's exposures are 60 per cent in jurisdiction 1 and 40 per cent in jurisdiction 2. Bank A has been designated a D-SIB by its home supervisor with a 1.5 per cent CET1 surcharge. Bank A is not a G-SIB.

The bank-specific CCyB is the exposure-weighted average:

Bank-specific CCyB = 0.6 * 1.0% + 0.4 * 0.5% = 0.6% + 0.2% = 0.8%

The CET1 minimum stack is then:

CET1 minimum               4.5%
Capital conservation buffer 2.5%
Bank-specific CCyB          0.8%
D-SIB surcharge             1.5%
Total CET1 required         9.3%

Suppose at year-end Bank A reports CET1 of 11 per cent, AT1 of 1.5 per cent, and Tier 2 of 2 per cent on RWA of 100 billion.

CET1 ratio        = 11 / 100         = 11.0%
Tier 1 ratio      = (11 + 1.5) / 100 = 12.5%
Total capital ratio = (11 + 1.5 + 2) / 100 = 14.5%

Compare to the binding minimums after phase-in: CET1 of 9.3 per cent (4.5 + 2.5 + 0.8 + 1.5), Tier 1 of 10.8 per cent (6.0 + 2.5 + 0.8 + 1.5; buffers always filled with CET1 on top of the Tier 1 minimum), total capital of 12.8 per cent (8.0 + 2.5 + 0.8 + 1.5). The bank exceeds all three, with the smallest "headroom" on CET1: 11.0 per cent against 9.3 per cent required is 1.7 percentage points, or 1.7 billion of CET1. That headroom is the cushion against future losses and risk-weight migration before distributions are constrained.

Now suppose Bank A's CET1 falls during the year to 9.6 per cent. The bank is still above all the minimums (9.6 per cent > 4.5 per cent CET1 minimum, the Tier 1 and total ratios are also still met). But it has entered the buffer: the CET1 of 9.6 per cent is within the fourth quartile of the buffer (between 9.3 - 0.825 = 8.475 per cent and 9.3 per cent of the buffer's bottom and top, after working through the quartile boundaries). The bank is now constrained on distributions. It cannot pay its planned dividend at full value, cannot complete its buyback programme, and discretionary staff bonuses must be reviewed. The bank must produce a capital plan for the supervisor showing how the buffer will be rebuilt. None of this involves enforcement action; the buffer is doing what it was designed to do.

Watch out The Basel III framework as published in 2010 to 2013 left two major risk areas unfinished. Market risk was not redesigned (FRTB consultations began in 2012 and a final text was published in 2019). The standardised and IRB approaches for credit risk were not revised; an output floor was not introduced. These were left for the 2017 finalisation. So when someone says "Basel III" in a 2014 context they usually mean the 2010 to 2013 package; in a 2024 context they probably mean the package plus the 2017 finalisation. Always ask which.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

CET1 is the only ratio that really matters in equity-analyst conversations

The CET1 ratio is the headline number every bank publishes, every analyst tracks, and every supervisor watches. The total capital ratio and the Tier 1 ratio are arithmetic consequences of CET1 plus instruments that are increasingly hard to issue. The market focuses on CET1 because it is the loss-absorbing piece during a going-concern run on the bank: AT1 will only convert or write down at low triggers; Tier 2 only absorbs losses in resolution. The reason CET1 became dominant is exactly the reason it was created: in 2008 the market did not believe the Tier 1 numbers because the soft components were not loss-absorbing.

The political fight over the leverage ratio

The 3 per cent leverage ratio was one of the most contentious decisions in the entire Basel III package. US regulators argued for higher numbers based on the observation that almost all the failed firms in 2008 had been within their risk-weighted capital limits but heavily levered. European banks, especially large derivative-clearing books and Dutch and German banks with large low-risk-weight mortgage books, argued that a 5 per cent leverage ratio would be punitive and would force them to restructure away from low-risk activity. The 3 per cent number is the compromise: meaningful enough to bite for the most leveraged G-SIBs (HSBC, BNP, Deutsche Bank), too low to bite for retail-heavy banks (Lloyds, ING). The US then applied a higher SLR of 5 per cent for its largest bank holding companies in a regulatory layer on top, which is the source of much "level playing field" complaint from European banks.

LCR's known weakness: HQLA hoarding

The LCR requires banks to hold high-quality liquid assets against potential thirty-day outflows. The natural response of risk-averse treasurers has been to over-hold HQLA: keep cash at the central bank rather than lend it, keep more sovereign bonds rather than fund corporate loans, hoard liquidity in subsidiaries rather than move it around the group. The aggregate effect, particularly after Covid liquidity injections in 2020 to 2022, was that banking systems became awash with central-bank reserves, which counted as Level 1 HQLA and made compliance easy but reduced the supply of bank credit to the real economy. The 2023 Silicon Valley Bank failure further showed that LCR compliance does not protect against a deposit run that empties out faster than the thirty-day model assumes; the real run on SVB was over a single afternoon, with social media and same-day wire transfers compressing the run-off rate far beyond the LCR's stylised calibration.

Banks rarely operate at the regulatory minimum, and that is deliberate

The buffer architecture means a bank operating exactly at 4.5 per cent CET1 has breached the conservation buffer entirely and cannot pay a dividend. To pay a dividend, the bank needs to be above the top of the buffer stack, which for most banks is somewhere in the 7 to 10 per cent CET1 range. In practice banks operate with a "management buffer" on top of this (often another 100 to 200 basis points) to insure against quarter-on-quarter volatility in RWA, profit, and AOCI. So the binding constraint for most banks is not the 4.5 per cent minimum but the level of CET1 at which the bank is comfortable paying its target dividend. This is a feature, not a bug: the framework is designed so that the regulatory minimum is the floor of the floor of the floor, with multiple layers of cushion absorbing shocks before the minimum is approached.

The Basel III package reshaped bank business models

Look at large international banks in 2008 against the same banks in 2023. They are less trading-heavy (FRTB, the leverage ratio, and CVA capital have all increased the cost of derivatives and trading inventory). They are less levered (the leverage ratio has been a hard constraint on balance-sheet growth in low-margin businesses). They are more retail-funded (stable retail deposits get favourable NSFR weights; wholesale funding gets penalised). They are more capital-heavy in absolute terms: CET1 ratios that were 6 to 8 per cent in 2008 are routinely 13 to 16 per cent now. They have moved more activity into off-balance-sheet vehicles where capital does not bite (asset management, custody, payments). The framework worked, in the sense that banks are demonstrably more resilient. Whether it worked at the right price is a separate question; the BCBS itself has done several studies suggesting the cost of credit increased by 5 to 15 basis points, while the probability of a systemic crisis fell by enough to justify the cost, on the Committee's own analysis.

Read the BCBS 189 December 2010 text, not the marketing

The original BCBS 189 (December 2010) is 77 pages and is by historical standards exceptionally well written. It explains the rationale for each component before stating the rules. The companion BCBS 188 (December 2010, liquidity) is similarly readable. Most secondary commentary on Basel III recycles a handful of stylised stories about the calibration of the buffers and the run-off rates; the primary texts are clearer than the commentary. Read the primary text, especially the rationale paragraphs that precede each rule.
