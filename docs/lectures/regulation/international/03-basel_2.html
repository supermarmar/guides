Basel II (2004): three pillars and risk-sensitive capital

Basel II replaced the one-size-fits-all 1988 ratio with a framework that lets a bank's own internal models drive its capital charge. It introduced operational risk as a dedicated category, codified the supervisory review process, and built market discipline into a third pillar of disclosure. For the first time, regulatory capital was meant to track the underlying economics of risk, not just the broad asset class.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Basel II is the second international capital accord, published by the BCBS in June 2004 and republished in a comprehensive version in June 2006 that consolidated the 1988 Accord, the 1996 Market Risk Amendment, and the new 2004 text into a single 347-page rulebook. Its architecture is the three pillars: Pillar 1, minimum capital requirements covering credit, market, and operational risk; Pillar 2, the supervisory review of capital adequacy and risk management; Pillar 3, market discipline through public disclosure. The 8 per cent minimum capital ratio of Basel I survived, but how the denominator (risk-weighted assets) is computed was rebuilt from the ground up. Banks could now choose, subject to supervisory approval, between a Standardised Approach using external ratings and an Internal Ratings-Based (IRB) approach using their own probability of default (PD), loss given default (LGD), and exposure at default (EAD) estimates fed into a single supervisor-prescribed risk-weight formula based on the Vasicek single-factor model.

Operational risk arrived as a Pillar 1 category for the first time. Three approaches were offered: the Basic Indicator Approach (15 per cent of three-year average gross income), the Standardised Approach (different beta factors per business line), and the Advanced Measurement Approach (banks' own 99.9 per cent one-year value-at-risk models). Market risk inherited the 1996 Amendment unchanged. Pillar 2 required every bank to run its own Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP) and required every supervisor to run a parallel Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process (SREP), with the power to require capital above the Pillar 1 minimum for risks the Pillar 1 formula missed (interest rate risk in the banking book, concentration, business risk). Pillar 3 imposed semi-annual public disclosure of capital, risk exposures, and the assumptions feeding the internal models. Most member jurisdictions implemented Basel II in 2007 or 2008.

Feynman test Basel I asked a bank one question: how much have you lent, and to which broad asset class? Basel II asked four: how much have you lent, to whom (with what credit quality), with what recovery prospects on default, and over what horizon? The bank does most of the arithmetic itself, the supervisor checks the homework, and the public reads the report card. Think of Basel I as a flat tax on lending and Basel II as a graduated tax where the bank computes its own bracket, subject to audit. The graduated tax is fairer in principle, more accurate where the bank's model is good, more dangerous where the bank's model is wrong, and infinitely harder to police. Every advantage and every problem of the post-2007 banking system flows from that one change.

Step 2

Why this matters

By the late 1990s, Basel I was visibly broken. The 8 per cent ratio applied a 100 per cent risk weight to almost every corporate loan regardless of credit quality, and the same 50 per cent weight to every residential mortgage regardless of loan-to-value. Banks responded by holding their highest-quality exposures (which carried far less economic risk than 8 per cent of the loan value) on balance sheet, where they were over-capitalised, and selling or securitising their lower-quality exposures off balance sheet, where the capital charge was crushing. The growing securitisation market, the rise of credit derivatives, and the explosion of internal economic-capital models inside major banks all signalled the same problem: regulatory capital and economic capital had decoupled. Banks were optimising against the regulation rather than against the risk.

Basel II's answer was to align the two. If a bank could persuade its supervisor that its internal model produced a defensible PD, LGD, and EAD, the bank could use those parameters in a supervisor-prescribed formula and pay capital proportional to the economic risk of each exposure. Capital on a AAA-rated sovereign would fall close to zero. Capital on a sub-investment-grade unsecured corporate loan would rise. Capital on a well-collateralised mortgage would fall further. The hope was that capital would migrate to the exposures that actually deserved it, the incentive to game would disappear, and the regulatory and economic perspectives would finally agree.

Operational risk became a Pillar 1 category for a different reason. The decade preceding Basel II had been disfigured by a series of large operational losses that had nothing to do with credit or market risk. Barings Bank collapsed in 1995 after Nick Leeson's unauthorised derivatives trading hidden in error account 88888. Allied Irish Banks lost 691 million dollars on rogue currency trading by John Rusnak in 2002. Daiwa lost over a billion dollars on unauthorised bond trading by Toshihide Iguchi over eleven years. Beyond rogue trading, IT failures, processing errors, fraud, and legal losses were all visibly material risks for which the 1988 Accord had no charge. (Société Générale's 4.9 billion euro Kerviel loss came in 2008, after Basel II's publication but reinforcing every reason for including the category.) Codifying operational risk gave supervisors a basis to ask about risk management of these exposures and to require capital against them.

Pillar 2 codified what every good supervisor was already trying to do informally: read the bank's own view of its capital adequacy, compare it to the regulatory minimum and to the supervisor's own view, and require more capital where the gap mattered. The novelty was the formal structure. ICAAP became a regulated process inside every bank; SREP became a regulated process inside every supervisor; the conversation between the two became annual and documented. Pillar 3 made the same conversation visible to the market, on the theory that disclosure plus competition would produce better behaviour than rules alone.

For an actuary working in a bank today, Basel II matters because almost every modelling concept in current use originated in it. PD, LGD, EAD as a triple. The Vasicek formula. The supervisory LGD floors of F-IRB. The 250-day downturn LGD requirement. The definition of default at 90 days past due or unlikely to pay. The qualifying revolving retail (QRRE) asset class. The 99.9 per cent confidence level and the one-year horizon. All of it was crystallised in Basel II and persists in modified form in the IFRS 9 framework and in Basel III. Reading the original 2004 text remains the cleanest way to understand why current rules look the way they do.

Step 3

The mental model

Basel II is a three-storey building. The ground floor is Pillar 1, where formulas convert exposures into capital charges. The first floor is Pillar 2, where supervisors look at the whole building and ask whether the ground-floor numbers really cover all the risks. The top floor is Pillar 3, where the building's accounts are open to the public. Every internationally active bank inhabits all three floors simultaneously, and a failure on any one floor invalidates the whole structure.

Three flows of information run through the building. Numbers flow up from Pillar 1: the bank's exposures, its rating systems, its loss data feed into formulas that produce a minimum capital charge. Judgement flows across at Pillar 2: the supervisor compares the bank's ICAAP against the SREP view and may require additional capital for risks Pillar 1 misses. And disclosure flows out at Pillar 3: the market reads what the bank held, what it lost, and how it modelled its risks, and prices the bank's equity and debt accordingly. The architecture only works when all three flows are operating. A bank with a perfect Pillar 1 calculation but a weak ICAAP, or strong internal numbers but opaque disclosure, fails the framework.

Step 4

The detail

The capital ratio that survived

Basel II kept the headline test of Basel I unchanged. Total regulatory capital must be at least 8 per cent of risk-weighted assets, with Tier 1 capital at least 4 per cent of RWA, and Tier 2 capital limited to 100 per cent of Tier 1. Tier 3 short-term subordinated debt could be used at national discretion to support market-risk capital up to 250 per cent of the Tier 1 capital allocated to market risk. The numerator (eligible capital) was largely the 1988 definition. The denominator (RWA) was the bit that was rebuilt.

For market and operational risk the RWA is a notional construct: the capital requirement is computed directly and multiplied by 12.5 (the reciprocal of 8 per cent) to produce an equivalent RWA, which is then added to the credit-risk RWA. So total RWA equals credit-risk RWA plus 12.5 times market-risk capital plus 12.5 times operational-risk capital. A scaling factor of 1.06 was applied to the credit-risk RWA computed under the IRB approach. This was an explicit calibration choice. The first round of quantitative impact studies (QIS) showed that IRB capital across the system was likely to fall significantly below Basel I levels, and the Committee wanted to preserve the aggregate amount of capital in the system while granting risk-sensitivity to individual banks. The 1.06 factor was the lever that delivered both.

Pillar 1, credit risk: the Standardised Approach

The standardised approach is the descendant of Basel I, refined by external credit ratings. Instead of fixed risk weights by broad asset class, the weight depends on the rating issued by an "External Credit Assessment Institution" (an ECAI: in practice S&P, Moody's, or Fitch). Sovereign exposures are weighted 0 per cent for AAA to AA-, rising through 20, 50, 100, 150 per cent to a 100 per cent weight for unrated. Banks fall under one of two national-discretion options: option 1 ties the weight to the sovereign's rating; option 2 ties it to the bank's own rating with a 50 per cent floor for unrated banks. Corporate exposures attract 20 per cent at AAA to AA-, 50 per cent at A+ to A-, 100 per cent at BBB+ to BB-, and 150 per cent below BB-, with unrated at 100 per cent.

Two important asset classes were carved out for preferential treatment. Regulatory retail exposures, defined by orientation to individuals or small businesses, by product (revolving credit, term loan, small-business facility), by granularity (no single counterparty more than 0.2 per cent of the pool), and by exposure size (1 million euros or less to any one counterparty), receive a 75 per cent risk weight regardless of rating. Residential mortgages fully secured on owner-occupied or rented property receive a 35 per cent risk weight. Past-due loans more than 90 days unpaid jump to 100 or 150 per cent depending on provisioning coverage. Commercial real estate stays at 100 per cent; the Committee took the view, vindicated repeatedly since, that commercial property had been "a recurring cause of troubled assets in the banking industry over the past few decades".

Off-balance-sheet items pass through credit conversion factors (CCFs) before risk weighting: 0 per cent for unconditionally cancellable commitments, 20 per cent for short-dated commitments, 50 per cent for longer-dated commitments, 100 per cent for direct credit substitutes and asset sales with recourse.

Credit risk mitigation through financial collateral is recognised under two distinct methods. A bank must apply one method consistently across a portfolio, though national supervisors may permit different methods for the banking book and the trading book.

The Simple Approach

Under the simple approach, the risk weight of eligible financial collateral replaces the risk weight of the counterparty on the collateralised portion of the exposure. The uncollateralised residual retains the counterparty's full risk weight. A 20 per cent floor applies to all substitutions: even if the collateral carries a 0 per cent risk weight (qualifying government bonds, for instance), the collateralised portion cannot be assigned a risk weight below 20 per cent. The one exception is cash collateral denominated in the same currency as the exposure, which may be assigned a 0 per cent risk weight on the collateralised portion.

Eligible collateral is restricted to financial collateral: cash and cash equivalents; gold; rated debt securities issued by sovereigns, public sector entities, banks, or corporates that meet defined minimum rating thresholds (generally investment grade at origination); equities included in a main stock-market index; and UCITS or mutual fund units whose prices are publicly quoted daily. To qualify, collateral must be pledged for the full remaining life of the exposure and revalued at least semi-annually. The simple approach is operationally straightforward but less capital-efficient than the comprehensive approach when collateral quality is high, because the 20 per cent floor binds regardless of collateral rating beyond that threshold.

The Comprehensive Approach

The comprehensive approach adjusts both the gross exposure value and the collateral value for potential market-price movements before computing a net exposure, rather than substituting risk weights. The volatility-adjusted net exposure E* is then risk-weighted at the full counterparty risk weight. The formula is:

$$E^* = \max\!\left(0,\; E\,(1 + H_e) - C\,(1 - H_c - H_{fx})\right)$$

where E is the current mark-to-market exposure, H_e is a supervisory haircut on the exposure that allows for potential increases in its value over the holding period, C is the current mark-to-market value of the collateral, H_c is a supervisory haircut on the collateral that allows for potential falls in its value, and H_fx is an additional 8 per cent currency mismatch haircut applied when the exposure and the collateral are denominated in different currencies. Exposures under a netting agreement that satisfies the Basel II conditions may use a net position for E and an aggregate supervisory-haircut figure before applying the formula.

Basel II prescribes a table of supervisory haircuts calibrated to collateral type, credit rating, and residual maturity, assuming a ten-business-day holding period with daily mark-to-market. Cash denominated in the same currency carries a zero haircut. AAA to AA-rated sovereign bonds with residual maturity under one year carry 0.5 per cent, rising to 4 per cent for maturities over five years. BBB-rated corporate bonds carry 6 per cent at short maturities and 12 per cent beyond five years. Equities in a main index carry 15 per cent. When remargining or revaluation occurs less frequently than daily, the ten-day haircuts are scaled up: the adjusted haircut equals the ten-day figure multiplied by the square root of the actual holding period in business days divided by ten.

Banks that meet supervisory minimum standards for risk management and data infrastructure may substitute internally estimated haircuts for the prescribed table. Own estimates must be produced using a 99 per cent one-tailed confidence interval over a ten-business-day horizon, validated by backtesting against at least one year of daily data, and applied consistently. Supervisory approval for own-estimate haircuts is a discrete permission that does not require full IRB approval for the bank's credit exposures. The comprehensive approach is generally more capital-efficient than the simple approach for high-quality collateral, because it avoids the 20 per cent floor and recognises the full economic offset after haircuts rather than capping the risk-weight substitution.

Worked example: collateralised exposure under the standardised approach

A bank lends 800 million rand to a B- rated corporate and takes 700 million rand of BBB-rated corporate bonds as collateral. The counterparty risk weight is 150 per cent (below BB-). The collateral risk weight is 100 per cent (BBB+ to BB-).

Under the simple approach: RWA = (100% × 700) + (150% × max(800 - 700, 0)) = 700 + 150 = 850 million rand. Capital at 8 per cent = 68 million rand. Without collateral, RWA = 150% × 800 = 1,200 million rand and capital = 96 million rand. The collateral saves 28 million rand of capital.

Under the comprehensive approach, assume a +10 per cent exposure haircut (for potential exposure growth) and a -15 per cent collateral haircut (for potential collateral fall). Adjusted exposure = (1.10 × 800) - (0.85 × 700) = 880 - 595 = 285 million rand. RWA = 150% × 285 = 427.5 million rand. Capital = 34.2 million rand. The comprehensive approach recognises more of the economic value of the collateral but only because the haircuts in this example are mild; with more conservative haircuts the answer could go the other way.

Pillar 1, credit risk: the IRB approach

The Internal Ratings-Based approach is the heart of Basel II. A bank that has supervisory approval to use IRB classifies its banking-book exposures into five asset classes (corporate, sovereign, bank, retail, equity), estimates the underlying risk parameters, and feeds them into a fixed formula to produce capital. The formula is the same for everyone; the parameters are the bank's own. There are two flavours. Under Foundation IRB (F-IRB), the bank estimates only the probability of default (PD), and uses supervisory values for LGD, EAD, and effective maturity (M). Under Advanced IRB (A-IRB), the bank estimates all four. Retail exposures have only one flavour: banks must always provide their own PD, LGD, and EAD, and there is no explicit maturity input.

The risk parameters have specific regulatory definitions that are now ubiquitous in credit risk modelling. PD is the one-year probability of default of an obligor, where default is defined as a payment past due by more than 90 days, or as a determination that the obligor is unlikely to pay without recourse to collateral. PD is measured through-the-cycle in F-IRB and A-IRB, with a 0.03 per cent floor for corporate, sovereign, and bank exposures. LGD is the economic loss on default expressed as a percentage of EAD, measured in a downturn scenario; supervisory LGDs under F-IRB are 45 per cent for senior unsecured corporate, sovereign, and bank exposures, and 75 per cent for subordinated. EAD is the exposure expected at the time of default, including any drawn balances plus expected drawdowns under committed facilities; supervisory CCFs under F-IRB follow the standardised approach. M is the effective maturity, with a one-year floor and a five-year cap, computed on a cash-flow-weighted basis under A-IRB.

The Vasicek risk-weight formula

Once PD, LGD, EAD, and M are in hand, the IRB capital charge K is produced by a single formula derived from the Vasicek (1987) single-factor asymptotic-single-risk-factor (ASRF) model and underpinned by Gordy's (2003) portfolio-invariance theorem. The intuition is that under a single-factor model, with infinitely granular portfolios, the contribution of any one loan to portfolio unexpected loss depends only on the loan's own characteristics, not on the rest of the portfolio. This is the property that lets a regulator write one formula that gives the right answer for every bank.

The corporate, sovereign, and bank capital requirement is:

$$K = \left[ LGD \cdot N\left( \sqrt{\frac{1}{1-R}} \cdot N^{-1}(PD) + \sqrt{\frac{R}{1-R}} \cdot N^{-1}(0.999) \right) - PD \cdot LGD \right] \cdot \frac{1 + (M - 2.5) \cdot b(PD)}{1 - 1.5 \cdot b(PD)}$$

where N is the cumulative standard normal distribution, N inverse is its quantile function, R is the asset correlation, and b(PD) is the maturity adjustment. The asset correlation for corporate, sovereign, and bank exposures interpolates between 24 per cent for very low PDs and 12 per cent for very high PDs:

$$R = 0.12 \cdot \frac{1 - e^{-50 \cdot PD}}{1 - e^{-50}} + 0.24 \cdot \left(1 - \frac{1 - e^{-50 \cdot PD}}{1 - e^{-50}}\right)$$

The maturity adjustment is:

$$b(PD) = \left[ 0.11852 - 0.05478 \cdot \ln(PD) \right]^2$$

and the risk-weighted asset amount is:

$$RWA = 12.5 \cdot K \cdot EAD$$

The expression in square brackets has two parts. The first term, LGD · N(...), is the loss in a stressed scenario at the 99.9 per cent confidence level: it is the conditional loss given that the systemic factor takes its 0.999 quantile. The second term, PD · LGD, is the expected loss that is meant to be covered by provisions, not capital. The difference is the unexpected loss (UL), and capital under Basel II covers UL only. Expected loss is handled separately: if a bank's provisions exceed expected loss, the excess can be recognised in Tier 2 up to 0.6 per cent of credit-risk RWA; if provisions fall short, the shortfall is deducted 50/50 from Tier 1 and Tier 2.

The maturity adjustment scales capital up for longer-dated exposures. At M = 2.5 years the adjustment is exactly one and has no effect; at M = 5 years (the cap) it increases capital by roughly 30 to 60 per cent depending on PD; at M = 1 year (the floor) it reduces capital. The adjustment is steeper for low-PD obligors, where the risk of credit migration during the life of the exposure is proportionally large compared to the risk of outright default.

Retail exposures use a simpler formula with no maturity adjustment and with fixed asset correlations: 15 per cent for residential mortgages; a sliding scale between 3 and 16 per cent for qualifying revolving retail (which is set lower than other retail to reflect the diversifying effect of large pools of small exposures); and a sliding scale (using the same shape as the corporate formula but with different constants) between 3 and 16 per cent for other retail. The retail formula is:

$$K = LGD \cdot N\left( \sqrt{\frac{1}{1-R}} \cdot N^{-1}(PD) + \sqrt{\frac{R}{1-R}} \cdot N^{-1}(0.999) \right) - PD \cdot LGD$$

with R = 0.15 for residential mortgages, R = 0.04 for qualifying revolving retail, and R given by an exposure-weighted formula for other retail.

Watch out The 0.999 in the formula is not the confidence level on the bank's own loss distribution; it is the confidence level on the systemic risk factor. Under the ASRF model, conditional on a 0.999-quantile draw of the systemic factor, the expected loss on any given loan is the term inside the brackets. The link from "0.999 quantile of the systemic factor" to "0.999 quantile of the portfolio loss distribution" is what Gordy's portfolio-invariance result delivers, and it depends on the asymptotic assumption of infinitely granular, single-factor exposures. Real portfolios are neither infinitely granular nor truly single-factor, which is exactly the gap Pillar 2 (concentration risk) and the later Basel III countercyclical buffer try to fill.

Pillar 1, operational risk

Operational risk is defined as the risk of loss resulting from inadequate or failed internal processes, people, and systems, or from external events. The definition includes legal risk but excludes strategic and reputational risk. Three calculation approaches were offered, in ascending order of risk-sensitivity and rigour.

Basic Indicator Approach (BIA)
Operational risk capital equals 15 per cent (the alpha factor) of the average positive annual gross income over the previous three years, with negative or zero years excluded from both numerator and denominator. Gross income is defined as net interest income plus net non-interest income, gross of provisions and operating expenses. No qualifying criteria; available to any bank.
$$K_{\text{BIA}} = \alpha \cdot \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3} \max(GI_i,\; 0)}{n}$$

where \(\alpha = 0.15\), \(GI_i\) is gross income in year \(i\), and \(n\) is the number of years in the three-year window where gross income was positive.

The Standardised Approach (TSA)
Gross income is allocated to eight business lines (corporate finance, trading and sales, retail banking, commercial banking, payment and settlement, agency services, asset management, retail brokerage), each with its own beta factor between 12 and 18 per cent. Capital is the three-year average of the sum across business lines of beta times gross income, with negative single-year totals allowed to offset positives. Requires sound operational risk management and a documented loss-data collection process.
$$K_{\text{TSA}} = \frac{1}{3} \sum_{t=1}^{3} \max\!\left( \sum_{j=1}^{8} \beta_j \cdot GI_{j,t},\; 0 \right)$$

where \(\beta_j\) is the fixed beta factor for business line \(j\), \(GI_{j,t}\) is gross income for that line in year \(t\), and the outer \(\max(\cdot,0)\) floors each annual sum at zero so that a loss-making year cannot reduce the three-year average below zero.

Advanced Measurement Approach (AMA)
Banks use their own operational-risk models to compute the 99.9 per cent value-at-risk over a one-year horizon, with capital equal to that VaR. Models must combine internal loss data, external loss data, scenario analysis, and business environment and internal control factors (BEICFs). Insurance can offset up to 20 per cent of the AMA charge subject to conditions. Requires significant qualitative governance and validation infrastructure. Approval is granted only to a minority of large banks.

The AMA was always controversial. Its data inputs are scarce, its tail estimation is unstable, and its capital outputs varied widely across banks with apparently similar operational-risk profiles. Basel III finalisation (BCBS d424, 2017) abolished the AMA in favour of a single Standardised Measurement Approach (SMA) based on a business indicator and a loss multiplier. The BIA and TSA remain in many jurisdictions during the transition, and the legacy of AMA modelling lives on in the loss-data taxonomies and the seven Basel event-type categories that survive in the SMA.

Pillar 1, market risk

Market risk was not redesigned for Basel II. The 1996 Market Risk Amendment to Basel I, which had introduced a value-at-risk framework with internal-model and standardised options, was carried over almost unchanged. The internal-model approach used a 99 per cent one-tailed VaR over a ten-day horizon, multiplied by a regulatory factor (minimum three, plus a back-testing adjustment up to one). The standardised approach used a building-block breakdown by risk factor (interest rate, equity, foreign exchange, commodities, options). A subsequent revision in 2009 (Basel 2.5) added a Stressed VaR component and the Incremental Risk Charge, in response to trading-book losses during the financial crisis. The full overhaul of market risk came only with the Fundamental Review of the Trading Book (FRTB) in 2016 and 2019, which moved capital from VaR to expected shortfall and is the subject of a later lecture in the credit-risk sequence rather than this one.

Pillar 2: the supervisory review process

Pillar 2 is the formalisation of supervisory judgement. Its objective, in the Committee's words, is "not only to ensure that banks have adequate capital to support all the risks in their business, but also to encourage banks to develop and use better risk management techniques in monitoring and managing their risks". Capital is one tool among several, and the supervisor is empowered to require it where the Pillar 1 formulas fall short. The framework rests on four key principles.

Principle 1
Banks should have a process for assessing their overall capital adequacy in relation to their risk profile and a strategy for maintaining their capital levels. This is the ICAAP. Its five required features are board and senior management oversight, sound capital assessment, comprehensive assessment of risks (covering credit, market, operational, IRRBB, liquidity, and other), monitoring and reporting, and internal control review.
Principle 2
Supervisors should review and evaluate banks' internal capital adequacy assessments and strategies, and their ability to monitor and ensure compliance with regulatory capital ratios. This is the SREP. Supervisors should take action if they are not satisfied with the result.
Principle 3
Supervisors should expect banks to operate above the minimum regulatory capital ratios and should have the ability to require banks to hold capital in excess of the minimum. This is the source of Pillar 2A capital add-ons for risks not fully covered by Pillar 1.
Principle 4
Supervisors should seek to intervene at an early stage to prevent capital from falling below the levels needed to support the bank's risk characteristics, and should require rapid remedial action if capital is not maintained or restored. The basis for prompt corrective action regimes such as the US PCA framework.

The Pillar 2 process is also where specific risks the Pillar 1 formulas do not capture are addressed. Interest rate risk in the banking book (IRRBB) is explicitly placed in Pillar 2, with national supervisors permitted to set mandatory minimum capital requirements where bank populations are homogeneous enough to justify it (the EU implementation imposes outlier tests rather than a formal charge). Credit concentration risk, both single-name and sectoral, is a Pillar 2 issue because the Pillar 1 formula assumes an infinitely granular portfolio. Residual risk from credit risk mitigation, where collateral or guarantees fail to perform as expected, is also captured here. Securitisation risk requires supervisors to look beyond the strict capital treatment to the implicit support and reputational risk a bank may bear. Operational risk capital is reviewed for proportionality, because gross income is a proxy that can mislead. And stress testing under the IRB and internal-model market-risk approaches is required to produce results that, where deficient, force additional capital.

Sitting alongside the four principles is the BCBS's separate publication Core Principles for Effective Banking Supervision. The October 2006 revision codified 25 principles covering supervisory powers, prudential requirements, and corrective and enforcement powers. The September 2012 revision expanded the list to 29, in two groups: principles 1 to 13 on supervisory powers, responsibilities, and functions; principles 14 to 29 on prudential requirements and the supervisory expectations of banks. The Core Principles describe what a competent national supervisor looks like and are the benchmark for the IMF and World Bank's Financial Sector Assessment Programme (FSAP) reviews.

Pillar 3: market discipline

Pillar 3 imposes public disclosure obligations on banks to enable market participants to assess capital adequacy, risk exposures, risk assessment processes, and the consistency of internal models. Disclosures are organised in a series of tables covering scope of application, capital structure, capital adequacy, credit risk (general plus IRB-specific), credit risk mitigation, securitisation, market risk, operational risk, equity exposures in the banking book, and interest rate risk in the banking book.

Frequency is semi-annual for quantitative disclosures and annual for qualitative descriptions of risk management objectives and policies. Large internationally active banks must disclose Tier 1 and total capital adequacy ratios on a quarterly basis, and any risk exposure subject to rapid change must also be reported quarterly. Banks may rely on accounting or listing disclosures to satisfy Pillar 3 where the content overlaps, but must explain any material differences between accounting and supervisory bases. A materiality concept allows banks to omit immaterial information, and a proprietary-information exception allows banks to omit specific confidential information provided general qualitative information is disclosed instead.

Scope of application

Basel II applies on a fully consolidated basis at the level of the internationally active banking group. The 1988 Accord had operated only at the level of the bank entity; the new framework explicitly extended consolidation to all banking and other relevant financial subsidiaries, including holding-company structures where the holding company is the parent of a banking group. Significant minority investments in commercial entities above materiality thresholds (15 per cent of bank capital individually or 60 per cent in aggregate) are deducted from capital, with the deduction split 50/50 between Tier 1 and Tier 2. Investments in unconsolidated banking and financial subsidiaries are deducted in full.

A worked numerical comparison: standardised versus F-IRB versus A-IRB

Worked example: capital on a corporate exposure across the three approaches

Consider a 100 million euro senior unsecured term loan to a BBB-rated corporate with a remaining maturity of three years. The bank's internal estimates are PD = 0.50 per cent, LGD = 35 per cent, EAD = 100 million euros, M = 3 years.

Standardised approach. A BBB-rated corporate attracts a 100 per cent risk weight. RWA = 100 per cent × 100 = 100 million euros. Capital at 8 per cent = 8 million euros.

Foundation IRB. Use the bank's PD of 0.50 per cent. Use supervisory values for the other parameters: LGD = 45 per cent (senior unsecured), M = 2.5 years (the F-IRB default for corporate). Asset correlation:

$$R = 0.12 \cdot \frac{1 - e^{-0.25}}{1 - e^{-50}} + 0.24 \cdot \left(1 - \frac{1 - e^{-0.25}}{1 - e^{-50}}\right) \approx 0.2134$$

Maturity adjustment at M = 2.5 yields a multiplier of exactly 1. Conditional default probability:

$$N\left( \sqrt{\frac{1}{1-0.2134}} \cdot N^{-1}(0.005) + \sqrt{\frac{0.2134}{1-0.2134}} \cdot N^{-1}(0.999) \right) \approx 0.0651$$

K = 0.45 × (0.0651 - 0.005) = 0.0270, RWA = 12.5 × 0.0270 × 100 = 33.8 million euros. Capital at 8 per cent = 2.7 million euros. Applying the 1.06 IRB scaling factor brings capital to roughly 2.9 million euros.

Advanced IRB. Use the bank's own LGD of 35 per cent and M of 3 years. Asset correlation and conditional PD are unchanged. Maturity adjustment at M = 3:

$$b = (0.11852 - 0.05478 \cdot \ln(0.005))^2 \approx 0.1727, \quad \frac{1 + 0.5 \cdot 0.1727}{1 - 1.5 \cdot 0.1727} \approx 1.453$$

K = 0.35 × (0.0651 - 0.005) × 1.453 ≈ 0.0305, RWA = 12.5 × 0.0305 × 100 = 38.1 million euros, capital at 8 per cent times 1.06 = 3.2 million euros.

The standardised approach gives 8 million euros of capital. F-IRB gives 2.9 million, A-IRB gives 3.2 million (the higher number reflects the longer effective maturity in A-IRB, which the F-IRB default of 2.5 years masks). Both IRB approaches more than halve the capital charge against the standardised approach. This is the order of magnitude of the relief that drove every large bank to invest in IRB approval. It is also the source of the regulatory concern that internal models were used to game capital down, which led to the 72.5 per cent output floor introduced in Basel III finalisation.

Implementation, transition, and the parallel run

Most BCBS members implemented Basel II in 2007 or 2008. The United States adopted only the advanced approaches, and only for its largest banks, with full implementation delayed until well after the financial crisis. The framework included transitional floors during the first three years of IRB use: a bank's capital under Basel II could not fall below 95 per cent of its capital under Basel I in 2007, 90 per cent in 2008, and 80 per cent in 2009. A parallel run was required for at least one year before going live, during which the bank had to compute capital under both regimes and report the comparison to the supervisor. The parallel-run requirement and the transitional floor were the practical price of allowing internal models to displace the 1988 risk weights, and both survived into Basel III's IRB regime.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Read the formula as physics, not as arithmetic

The IRB risk-weight formula is not a regulatory invention; it is a one-line summary of a particular structural model of credit risk. Each piece earns its place from the Vasicek single-factor framework. The N inverse of PD locates the obligor's default threshold on the standard-normal scale. The systemic stress is a 0.999 quantile draw of the common factor. The square-root-of-R weighting tells you how strongly the obligor's asset value moves with the common factor. The conditional default probability is the chance that the obligor's idiosyncratic shock is bad enough, on top of the stressed common factor, to push the asset value below the default threshold. Once you read the formula in those structural terms you stop memorising it and start critiquing it: the single-factor assumption hides concentration risk, the asymptotic granularity assumption hides idiosyncratic risk in small portfolios, and the 0.999 confidence level on the factor is not the same as a 0.999 confidence level on the portfolio.

The 1.06 scaling factor is calibration, not science

One of the underappreciated features of Basel II is how much of the headline capital level comes from a single scaling factor. The Committee wanted IRB capital to broadly preserve the aggregate Basel I level. The QIS showed it would not. So the IRB RWA was multiplied by 1.06, a number that has no theoretical justification at all and that survived from QIS to QIS without much further calibration. Knowing this protects you from over-interpreting the formula. The pieces inside the brackets are derived from a model. The 1.06 outside is a policy choice. The 0.999 confidence level is also a policy choice. Pillar 1 capital is part formula and part settlement.

Internal models do not solve the gaming problem; they relocate it

Basel I was gamed by moving exposures across asset-class buckets that did not reflect economic risk. Basel II was gamed by tuning the PD and LGD inputs down to the edge of supervisory tolerance. A bank that wanted lower capital could lengthen the PD master scale, smooth the PD calibration, choose a more favourable downturn LGD reference period, exclude certain defaults from the loss data, or run the regulatory model in parallel to a kinder economic-capital model and quietly migrate ratings between the two. The supervisor's tools (model validation, benchmarking, on-site review) were the new check on the new gaming, and they were never sufficient on their own. The 72.5 per cent output floor in Basel III finalisation is the eventual admission that this could not be contained.

Pillar 2 is where the real money lives in many jurisdictions

For internationally active banks in Europe, Pillar 2A capital add-ons (for risks not adequately captured by Pillar 1) and Pillar 2B buffers (for forward-looking stress) typically add several percentage points of CET1 to the headline Pillar 1 requirement. The bank's own ICAAP and the supervisor's SREP letter together determine these numbers. Reading the SREP letter, not the published Pillar 1 ratio, gives the realistic capital floor. Knowing the SREP cycle (annual in the EU, with a one-year SREP letter trailing the most recent ICAAP) lets you anticipate when the next constraint will bind.

Pro-cyclicality became visible the moment Basel II went live

Basel II was implemented in most jurisdictions in 2007 and 2008. By 2009, PDs across portfolios were two to four times their long-run averages, LGDs in downturn-affected portfolios (mortgages, commercial real estate, leveraged loans) had widened materially, and IRB risk weights had risen sharply. Banks were forced to add capital at the worst possible moment, just as the market was retreating from issuance. The unintended pro-cyclicality of the framework was the single largest reason for the Basel III countercyclical capital buffer, for the conservation buffer, and for the work on through-the-cycle versus point-in-time PDs that has occupied modellers for fifteen years. The lesson is structural, not numerical: any system that ties capital to current observed risk will demand more capital exactly when capital is hardest to raise. Designing around this is the central unresolved problem of risk-based regulation.

Watch out Basel II is the foundation but it is no longer the live capital standard anywhere. Every internationally active bank now operates under Basel III as amended by the 2017 finalisation. The 4 per cent Tier 1 minimum became a 6 per cent Tier 1 minimum (with a 4.5 per cent CET1 sub-minimum), the conservation and countercyclical buffers were added, leverage and liquidity ratios were imposed, F-IRB was restricted, A-IRB was restricted further for low-default portfolios, the AMA was abolished, and an output floor was imposed. Knowing Basel II is essential for understanding why current rules look the way they do, but it is not the test against which any bank is now measured.

The architecture is permanent, the parameters are not

Whatever happens to the IRB formula, the Basel III leverage ratio, the FRTB market-risk capital, or the future treatment of crypto-asset exposures, the three pillars survive. Every subsequent revision has been a change to what fills the pillars, not a change to the architecture. Pillar 1 has gained the leverage ratio and the liquidity ratios as parallel constraints. Pillar 2 has gained countercyclical, systemic, and macroprudential buffers as supervisor-set add-ons. Pillar 3 has expanded its disclosure templates. But the three-pillar division of labour, between bank arithmetic, supervisor judgement, and market discipline, is the conceptual backbone of every banking-regulation regime in the world. It is Basel II's most durable contribution.
