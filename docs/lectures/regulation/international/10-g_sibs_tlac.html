G-SIBs and TLAC: the rules for the systemically important

After the 2008 crisis, governments discovered they could not let the largest banks fail without bringing down the financial system, and could not save them without putting taxpayers on the hook. The Financial Stability Board and the Basel Committee designed two complementary answers: a scoring methodology that flags the banks too big to fail and forces them to hold extra capital, and a separate standard that forces those same banks to pre-fund their own resolution by issuing bail-inable debt. Together these are the rules for the systemically important.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The G-SIB framework is a Basel Committee scoring methodology, owned and published annually by the FSB, that designates roughly thirty banks each year as globally systemically important. A G-SIB is allocated to one of five buckets based on its score across five indicator categories (size, interconnectedness, substitutability, complexity, cross-jurisdictional activity), and is required to hold an additional CET1 surcharge of between 1 and 3.5 per cent of risk-weighted assets above the Basel III minimum. The Total Loss-Absorbing Capacity (TLAC) standard, published by the FSB in November 2015, sits on top of that: it requires every G-SIB to hold at least 18 per cent of RWA and 6.75 per cent of leverage exposure in instruments that can be bailed in during resolution. Together the two standards form the international answer to "too big to fail": designate the banks where failure would be a disaster, make them carry a thicker going-concern capital cushion, and make sure they also carry enough gone-concern loss absorbency to be resolved without public money.

Neither standard is novel in concept. Banks have always been pressured to hold extra capital when they look risky, and senior unsecured debt has always been a buffer between depositors and equity. What the G-SIB and TLAC standards do is formalise both ideas, calibrate them to a specific list of banks, and harmonise the calibration across jurisdictions so that no national supervisor can compete by being soft on its champions. The G-SIB list is updated each November using end-of-prior-year regulatory data. The TLAC requirement has been fully phased in since 1 January 2022. The two standards are operationally intertwined: a bank that fails to meet TLAC ratios automatically fails its overall capital requirement because TLAC sits above the Basel III buffers, and the G-SIB surcharge is the first slice of those buffers.

Feynman test Imagine a city where most buildings are small wooden houses and a handful are forty-storey towers. If a wooden house catches fire, the fire brigade puts it out and the rest of the street is fine. If a tower catches fire, half the district goes with it. The fire chief has two responses. First, force the tower owners to install thicker fire walls and sprinklers than anyone else: that is the G-SIB surcharge, extra capital on top of what every bank carries. Second, force the tower owners to pre-pay a demolition fund, held in instruments the city can convert into rubble-clearance capacity the moment the tower starts to lean: that is TLAC, bail-inable debt sitting ready to absorb losses and recapitalise the bank without anyone calling the taxpayer. The G-SIB rules say "you are dangerous, hold more". The TLAC rules say "if you fail anyway, you fund your own funeral".
What is beyond the source The source wiki gives the bare bones: the list of G-SIBs and the headline TLAC ratios. This lecture adds: the mechanics of the BCBS scoring methodology (12 indicators across five categories, basis-point thresholds, denominator construction); the FSB and BCBS division of labour (BCBS owns the methodology in standard d445, FSB owns the annual list and the TLAC standard); the eligibility tests for external TLAC instruments and the structural answers banks have used to issue them (senior non-preferred in France, Italy, Spain; HoldCo senior in the UK, US, Switzerland); internal TLAC and how it pre-positions resources at material sub-groups; the interaction with Basel III buffers and the MDA stack; the D-SIB framework as the national analogue; and the political-economy of "too big to fail" and how the 2023 episode at Credit Suisse and SVB stress-tested the regime.
Step 2

Why this matters

In the autumn of 2008, the British government injected GBP 37 billion into RBS, Lloyds, and HBOS; the United States Treasury committed USD 700 billion through TARP and another USD 182 billion specifically to AIG; the Netherlands, Belgium, and Luxembourg jointly rescued Fortis; the German government took over Hypo Real Estate; Ireland guaranteed the entire liability stack of its banking system. The fiscal cost of these interventions ran to multiple percentage points of GDP in several jurisdictions, and the political cost was higher still: every voter in every rescuing country now knew that banks could privatise gains and socialise losses. Worse, the implicit guarantee was now explicit. Every large bank around the world could fund itself more cheaply than its size and risk would otherwise allow, because creditors assumed the government would step in if things went wrong. This is the moral hazard of "too big to fail".

The international policy response was developed in parallel along three tracks. The first track, led by the Basel Committee, raised the going-concern capital requirements for everyone: tighter capital definitions, capital conservation and countercyclical buffers, and the leverage ratio (covered in lecture 4). The second track, led by the FSB and the BCBS jointly, picked out the banks where failure would be most damaging and added a further surcharge: this is the G-SIB framework, published as BCBS d255 in 2013 and revised as BCBS d445 in 2018. The third track, led by the FSB and tied to the work of national resolution authorities, designed the legal and operational tools for resolving a large bank without taxpayer support: living wills, single point of entry resolution strategies, cross-border crisis management groups, and, crucially, the TLAC standard published in November 2015.

For an actuary working inside a G-SIB, four things follow directly. First, the G-SIB surcharge enters the bank's overall capital requirement as part of the combined buffer requirement, so it bites at the maximum distributable amount (MDA) trigger: a one-bucket promotion can move the MDA threshold by 50 basis points and constrain dividend and bonus capacity for years. Second, the TLAC ratio is monitored alongside the capital ratio and reported quarterly; falling below it is a regulatory breach with the same severity as failing the CET1 ratio. Third, TLAC has reshaped funding strategy: large banks now issue substantial volumes of senior non-preferred or HoldCo senior debt specifically to meet TLAC, and the eligible-instrument cost of funding is materially higher than ordinary senior unsecured. Fourth, the G-SIB methodology creates direct strategic incentives to shrink or simplify cross-border activity, because cross-jurisdictional exposures and complexity are two of the five indicator categories.

Step 3

The mental model

Two pictures together do most of the work. The first is the G-SIB scoring methodology: five indicator categories of equal weight, each broken into one or more sub-indicators, summed to a score in basis points which determines bucket assignment. The second is the loss-absorbing stack at a G-SIB: the Basel III capital layers on the bottom, the G-SIB surcharge and other buffers stacked on top, and TLAC sitting above and around the capital stack as a separate ratio that must be met by the same instruments plus additional bail-inable debt.

Two relationships are worth fixing now. First, the G-SIB surcharge is a CET1 requirement and counts towards the combined buffer requirement, so failing to meet it triggers the maximum distributable amount mechanism that constrains dividends, AT1 coupons, and discretionary bonuses. Second, TLAC is a separate ratio measured against the same RWA denominator, but the instruments that meet it are not all CET1: Tier 1 capital and Tier 2 capital count, and so does a new class of subordinated, bail-inable senior debt. The same CET1 used to meet the minimum capital ratio counts towards TLAC; the same CET1 used to meet a buffer does not, because the BCBS does not want banks double-counting their buffer cushion as gone-concern loss absorbency.

Step 4

The detail

The G-SIB scoring methodology

The methodology lives in BCBS standard d445 (Global systemically important banks: revised assessment methodology and the higher loss absorbency requirement, July 2018), which replaced the 2013 standard d255. The FSB owns the annual list and reports it to the G20 each November, but the BCBS owns the formula. The mechanics are deliberately simple. About seventy-five of the world's largest banks (those with leverage-ratio exposures above EUR 200 billion or that have been designated previously) submit data on twelve indicators in five categories. For each indicator, the bank's value is divided by the sum of that indicator across all reporting banks. The result is a share, expressed in basis points. The bank's overall score is the equal-weighted average of the five category scores; within each category, the sub-indicators are equally weighted. Scores typically range from about 100 basis points (the smallest banks that fall within the assessment universe) to about 600 basis points (Citigroup, HSBC, JPMorgan).

Indicator denominators
Each indicator is normalised by dividing the bank's absolute value by the sum of that indicator across the assessment sample. This means that if every bank in the world shrank by 10 per cent, no bank's G-SIB score would change. The methodology is relative, not absolute. A bank can have its score rise even as it shrinks, if its peers shrink faster.
Substitutability cap
The substitutability category is capped at 500 basis points after the original 2013 methodology produced anomalous results for custody banks (Bank of New York Mellon, State Street) where extreme concentration in payment and custody businesses dominated their scores. The cap reflects the policy judgement that beyond a certain point, more custody business does not make a bank meaningfully more systemic.
Trading and AFS securities adjustment
From the 2018 revision (d445), trading-book and available-for-sale securities now exclude high-quality liquid assets (HQLA) that are eligible for the Liquidity Coverage Ratio. The argument: holdings of government bonds and other HQLA do not add to complexity in the way private securities do.
Supervisory judgement
The methodology allows national supervisors to recommend that a bank be moved up or down a bucket relative to its quantitative score where the indicators do not capture the full picture of systemic importance. In practice the override has been used sparingly, and movements have been mostly downward (a national supervisor arguing that its bank should be in a lower bucket than the score implies).

Bucketing and the higher loss absorbency requirement

The score is mapped to a bucket using fixed thresholds. The thresholds were set in 2013 to allocate the existing twenty-eight or so G-SIBs roughly evenly across the lower buckets, with the top buckets left empty as a deterrent. The thresholds, expressed in basis points of the overall score:

Bucket 1: 130 to 229 basis points, 1.0 per cent CET1 surcharge
The largest single bucket, typically housing fifteen to twenty banks. Includes most national-champion banks of mid-sized economies and large universal banks that are not in the global top tier. Recent occupants: ING, Santander, Standard Chartered, Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, Royal Bank of Canada, Sumitomo Mitsui.
Bucket 2: 230 to 329 basis points, 1.5 per cent CET1 surcharge
Six to ten banks. Major universal banks with substantial cross-border activity. Recent occupants: Bank of America, Barclays, BNP Paribas, Deutsche Bank, Mitsubishi UFJ FG, Industrial and Commercial Bank of China.
Bucket 3: 330 to 429 basis points, 2.0 per cent CET1 surcharge
Two or three banks. The very largest universal banks with the biggest cross-border and trading-book footprints. Recent occupants: Citigroup, HSBC.
Bucket 4: 430 to 529 basis points, 2.5 per cent CET1 surcharge
Currently holds JPMorgan Chase. The bank with the highest sustained score across the past decade. The 2.5 per cent surcharge plus the conservation buffer alone takes JPMorgan's CET1 minimum to 9.5 per cent before any countercyclical buffer or Pillar 2.
Bucket 5: above 530 basis points, 3.5 per cent CET1 surcharge
Deliberately empty. Reserved as a deterrent: any bank that grows into bucket 5 faces a 3.5 per cent CET1 surcharge and intense supervisory pressure to break itself up. The BCBS has stated that if bucket 5 is ever occupied, a new bucket 6 with a higher surcharge would be added so that bucket 5 again becomes the empty deterrent.

The list is updated annually in November based on end-prior-year data. Movements between buckets are common; movements onto or off the list itself are rare and usually reflect mergers, divestments, or a deliberate strategic reshaping. A bank that is promoted into a higher bucket gets twelve months to phase in the increased surcharge. A bank that drops a bucket gets the relief immediately.

The D-SIB framework as the national analogue

The G-SIB framework only covers banks of global significance. National supervisors apply a parallel methodology to identify domestically systemically important banks (D-SIBs) under the principles set out in BCBS standard d233 (October 2012). The principles are less prescriptive than the G-SIB methodology: each jurisdiction is free to design its own scoring, choose its own indicators, and set its own surcharges, subject to the BCBS principle that the framework should reflect the impact of a D-SIB's failure on the domestic economy. Typical D-SIB surcharges in major jurisdictions run from 1 per cent (most EU countries, applied as the systemic risk buffer or O-SII buffer) to 2.5 per cent (Sweden, Switzerland for the second tier of large banks). In the UK, the PRA applies the O-SII buffer through the systemic risk buffer regime, with rates of between 1 and 3 per cent for the major UK ring-fenced banks. In South Africa, the SARB applies a D-SIB surcharge of between 0.5 and 1.5 per cent to the major banks (Standard Bank, FirstRand, Absa, Nedbank).

D-SIB overlap with G-SIB
A bank that is both a G-SIB and a D-SIB in its home country is subject to the higher of the two surcharges, not the sum. Most G-SIBs are also D-SIBs domestically, but the home regulator typically calibrates the D-SIB surcharge at or below the G-SIB level, so the G-SIB requirement binds.

The TLAC standard

The FSB Principles on Loss-absorbing and Recapitalisation Capacity of G-SIBs in Resolution, published in November 2015 alongside the accompanying Term Sheet, set the international TLAC standard. The objective in plain English: ensure that a G-SIB has enough loss-absorbing and recapitalisation capacity, available at the point of failure, to be put through resolution without recourse to public funds and without disrupting the critical functions the bank performs. The term sheet sets specific minimum requirements, eligibility criteria, and interactions with existing capital requirements.

Minimum external TLAC
Each G-SIB resolution group must hold external TLAC equal to at least 18 per cent of risk-weighted assets and at least 6.75 per cent of the Basel III leverage-ratio denominator. The transition was 16 per cent of RWA and 6 per cent of leverage exposure from 1 January 2019, rising to the full requirement on 1 January 2022. National authorities may set higher requirements; the UK and EU have done so for some banks through the MREL framework (see lecture 21).
Resolution group
The legal entity or set of entities that would be put into resolution as a single unit under the bank's preferred resolution strategy. For a bank using single point of entry (SPE), this is normally the holding company and all its subsidiaries. For a bank using multiple point of entry (MPE), each resolution group has its own TLAC requirement. The resolution strategy is agreed between home and host supervisors through the Crisis Management Group.
Eligible external TLAC instruments
CET1, AT1, and Tier 2 capital all count, subject to the rule that CET1 used for buffer requirements cannot also count. In addition, non-capital eligible TLAC debt must be (1) unsecured, (2) contractually subordinated to operating liabilities (or structurally subordinated through issuance from a holding company), (3) have a remaining maturity of at least one year, (4) have no acceleration rights other than for non-payment, (5) have no offset or netting rights that would frustrate bail-in, and (6) be governed by law that supports the bail-in (or include a contractual recognition clause).
Subordination requirement
At least 33 per cent of the minimum TLAC requirement must be met by debt instruments (rather than capital). The intent is to keep a layer of loss absorbency that is gone-concern in nature, separate from the going-concern capital cushion.
Excluded liabilities
Insured deposits, demand or short-term (less than one year) deposits, derivative liabilities, structured-note liabilities, tax and preferred-creditor liabilities, and any liability that under national resolution law would not be bailed in. The exclusion list ensures that operating liabilities sit ahead of TLAC in the creditor hierarchy.

Internal TLAC

A G-SIB with a single point of entry (SPE) strategy concentrates loss absorbency at the parent holding company or top-tier operating company. To make sure the parent can absorb losses arising in its operating subsidiaries without those subsidiaries themselves entering local resolution, the FSB term sheet requires "internal TLAC" to be pre-positioned at each material sub-group. Internal TLAC is issued by the material sub-group to the parent and counts as a liability of the subsidiary that can be written down or converted to equity at the discretion of the home resolution authority, triggered before any local resolution proceeding starts.

Material sub-group
Any subsidiary that holds more than 5 per cent of the consolidated group's RWA, more than 5 per cent of operating income, or more than 5 per cent of leverage exposure, or is otherwise designated by the host supervisor as critical to the host jurisdiction. Most large G-SIBs have between three and seven material sub-groups, typically a US subsidiary, a UK or European subsidiary, and one or more Asian subsidiaries.
Internal TLAC calibration
Set between 75 and 90 per cent of what the material sub-group's external TLAC requirement would be if it were itself a G-SIB resolution group. The negotiation between home and host supervisors in the Crisis Management Group settles the exact percentage. Higher percentages give host supervisors more comfort that losses can be absorbed locally; lower percentages give the parent more flexibility to manage group capital fungibly.

How banks issue eligible TLAC debt: three structural answers

Subordination at issuance is not a free choice. Each jurisdiction's bankruptcy and securities law constrains how a senior unsecured bond can be made subordinate to operating liabilities. Three structural answers have emerged, each tied to the legal architecture of the issuing bank.

Senior non-preferred (France, Italy, Spain, Belgium)
In 2016 France amended its banking law to create a new statutory class of senior debt ranking below ordinary senior unsecured but above subordinated (Tier 2) debt. Italy, Spain, Belgium, and several other EU member states followed with similar legislation. Banks issue "senior non-preferred" notes from the operating company; these are contractually identified as ranking junior to operating liabilities in resolution. BNP Paribas, Santander, and Intesa Sanpaolo have multi-tens-of-billions of euros outstanding in this class.
Holding company senior (UK, US, Switzerland)
In the UK, US, and Switzerland, large banks restructured to put a non-operating holding company above the main operating bank. The HoldCo issues "HoldCo senior" debt; because the HoldCo has no operating creditors of its own (apart from a handful of administrative items), its senior debt is structurally subordinated to the operating company's liabilities. Holders of HoldCo senior bonds are subordinated by the corporate structure rather than by a clause in the bond. Barclays, HSBC, Standard Chartered, the US bank holding companies (JPMorgan, BofA, Citi, Wells, Goldman, Morgan Stanley) and the Swiss G-SIBs all use this structure.
SPV-based subordination (Germany, Netherlands, Japan)
A small number of issuers have used special-purpose subordinated vehicles to issue TLAC-eligible debt where neither HoldCo senior nor senior non-preferred is available under local law. These structures are operationally more complex and have been losing share to the other two formats.

The pricing premium for eligible TLAC debt over ordinary senior unsecured is typically 30 to 60 basis points in stable markets, widening sharply in stress. Most G-SIBs now run rolling TLAC issuance programmes: the eligible-debt stock is amortising (the one-year residual-maturity requirement means a bond drops out of TLAC during its final year), so issuers must continually replace expiring instruments.

TLAC and the relationship to MREL

The European Union and the United Kingdom implement TLAC for their G-SIBs as the lower bound of a broader Minimum Requirement for own funds and Eligible Liabilities (MREL) framework. MREL applies to all banks subject to resolution planning, not just G-SIBs. For G-SIBs, MREL must be at least as stringent as TLAC; for non-G-SIB banks that the resolution authority intends to put through bail-in resolution, MREL is calibrated according to the bank's resolution strategy, typically at around twice the bank's capital requirement. The UK MREL framework, run by the Bank of England as resolution authority and binding under the Bank Recovery and Resolution Directive (BRRD) as on-shored in UK law, is the subject of lecture 21. The EU MREL framework, under BRRD II and the Single Resolution Mechanism Regulation, is the equivalent for the euro area and the rest of the EU.

Worked example: required capital and TLAC for a bucket-2 G-SIB

A G-SIB sits in bucket 2 (1.5 per cent CET1 surcharge). The home jurisdiction has set the countercyclical capital buffer at 1.0 per cent. There is no Pillar 2 add-on for the purposes of this illustration. The bank has GBP 600 billion of RWA and GBP 1,200 billion of leverage exposure.

Building the going-concern capital requirement from the bottom up:

CET1 minimum (Pillar 1)             4.50% RWA
Additional Tier 1 minimum            1.50% RWA
Tier 2 minimum                       2.00% RWA
------------------------------------------------
Total minimum capital                8.00% RWA

Capital conservation buffer (CET1)   2.50% RWA
G-SIB surcharge (CET1, bucket 2)     1.50% RWA
Countercyclical buffer (CET1)        1.00% RWA
------------------------------------------------
Combined buffer requirement          5.00% RWA  (CET1 only)

Total Basel III requirement         13.00% RWA
of which CET1 minimum + CBR         9.50% RWA

Now the TLAC requirement. TLAC is the binding ratio, not in addition to capital but on top of capital with overlaps allowed:

TLAC minimum                        18.00% RWA
TLAC leverage minimum                6.75% leverage exposure

Tier 1 + Tier 2 used to meet TLAC:
  CET1 (non-buffer portion only)     4.50% RWA
  AT1                                 1.50% RWA
  Tier 2                              2.00% RWA
                                      ------
                                      8.00% RWA from capital

Eligible TLAC debt required         10.00% RWA  (to reach 18%)
  Of which subordinated (33% min)    5.94% RWA  (33% of 18%)

GBP amounts:
  Combined buffer requirement        5.00% × 600bn  = 30.0bn  CET1
  Total Basel III capital           13.00% × 600bn  = 78.0bn
  TLAC                              18.00% × 600bn  = 108.0bn
  Eligible TLAC debt issued         10.00% × 600bn  = 60.0bn

The bank must therefore have GBP 78 billion of Basel III capital and a further GBP 30 billion of bail-inable senior debt sitting above it (note the bail-inable debt is GBP 60 billion in total, but GBP 30 billion already counts towards Tier 2 limits or sits within the capital stack; the additional gone-concern layer is roughly GBP 30 billion of HoldCo senior or senior non-preferred debt). On the leverage exposure ratio, the bank needs 6.75 per cent × GBP 1,200 billion = GBP 81 billion of TLAC, which is comfortably met by the same GBP 108 billion stack provided the leverage exposure is not so much larger than RWA as to make the leverage ratio the binding constraint.

The bank's overall management imperative is then clear. Maintain CET1 above 9.5 per cent of RWA at all times (or face MDA restrictions on dividends). Maintain Tier 1 above 7 per cent and total capital above 8 per cent. Maintain TLAC above 18 per cent of RWA and 6.75 per cent of leverage exposure. And ensure that at least 33 per cent of TLAC (about 5.94 per cent of RWA) is met by debt rather than by capital, so that the gone-concern loss absorbency layer is preserved.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The G-SIB methodology creates real incentives to shrink

HSBC sold its US retail business to Citizens Financial in 2021 and its Canadian operations to Royal Bank of Canada in 2024. Each transaction reduced cross-jurisdictional claims and liabilities (one of the five indicator categories) and trimmed total exposures, which together drive HSBC's score. BNP Paribas exited its US retail subsidiary BancWest in 2021 for similar reasons. Citigroup announced in 2021 that it would exit consumer banking in fourteen markets, a programme explicitly linked by management to the goal of reducing complexity and cross-jurisdictional indicators. The G-SIB surcharge is not the only driver of these decisions, but it is one of the few regulatory mechanisms in the international rulebook that directly punishes scale, and management teams now factor in the bucket implications of any large acquisition or major geographic expansion.

TLAC reshaped the senior unsecured market

Before the TLAC standard, large banks issued mostly two kinds of senior debt: senior unsecured operating-company bonds (the cheapest funding) and Tier 2 subordinated debt (a specialist capital instrument). After 2015, a third class emerged at scale: TLAC-eligible debt, either senior non-preferred or HoldCo senior. Globally, the stock of outstanding eligible TLAC debt now exceeds USD 2.5 trillion, with annual gross issuance of roughly USD 400 to 500 billion. The bid for this paper comes substantially from institutional investors who would previously have bought ordinary senior unsecured: pension funds, insurance company general accounts, and central bank reserve managers. Pricing has settled at a 30 to 60 basis point premium over ordinary senior in normal markets, widening to over 100 basis points in periods of stress (most recently March 2023 around the Credit Suisse resolution).

TLAC and MREL are operationally similar but legally distinct

TLAC is an FSB international standard binding on G-SIBs only. MREL is an EU and UK legal requirement under BRRD binding on all banks subject to resolution planning. For G-SIBs domiciled in the EU or UK, the TLAC ratio and the MREL ratio are usually the same in practice (because MREL is calibrated to be at least as stringent as TLAC), but they are reported separately and have different eligibility criteria around contractual recognition clauses for foreign-law debt. For non-G-SIB banks in the EU and UK that have been determined to require resolution rather than insolvency liquidation, MREL applies but TLAC does not. Lecture 21 covers MREL in detail.

Watch out The 2023 collapse of Credit Suisse, which was resolved by emergency merger into UBS rather than by an open resolution under the Swiss authorities' planned single-point-of-entry strategy, was widely interpreted as a failure of the TLAC regime. The reality is more nuanced. The Swiss authorities did write down all of Credit Suisse's AT1 instruments (CHF 16 billion of bail-in absorbing losses for AT1 holders, who took losses ahead of equity holders contrary to most observers' reading of the creditor hierarchy). What did not happen was a full bail-in resolution under the planned framework; instead, FINMA used emergency powers under Swiss law to force the merger. The episode raised real questions about the political feasibility of open bank resolution under acute stress, but the loss-absorbing instruments worked: TLAC bondholders were not made whole by taxpayers. The lesson for 2023 was about resolution mechanics and market confidence under stress, not about whether TLAC instruments can absorb losses.

The G-SIB list is a strategic signalling device

A bank's bucket assignment is read by markets as the most authoritative external assessment of its systemic importance. Credit rating agencies treat G-SIB status as a factor that elevates the rating of operating-company debt (because resolution makes operating creditors more likely to be paid in full while losses are imposed on HoldCo senior). The same status reduces the implicit subsidy that historically made large-bank funding cheap: by making the absorption mechanism explicit, the G-SIB and TLAC standards have, on most empirical estimates, removed between 30 and 80 basis points of the "too big to fail" pricing benefit. This is the regime working as designed.

Climate, cyber, and the next iteration

The BCBS reviews the G-SIB methodology every three years and is currently consulting on the next round. Two areas under active discussion are the treatment of insurance and asset-management activities within bank groups (currently captured imperfectly through complexity and substitutability indicators) and whether climate-related exposures should enter the methodology as a new category or as a complexity sub-indicator. Expect the 2026-2027 cycle of consultation to produce a revised methodology effective from 2029 or 2030. The TLAC standard is more stable: the FSB has signalled satisfaction with the 18 per cent / 6.75 per cent calibration and does not currently intend to revise the standard, although the eligibility criteria are reviewed periodically for foreign-law contractual recognition.

  • FSB, 2023 list of global systemically important banks: fsb.org/2023/11/2023-list-of-global-systemically-important-banks-g-sibs. Annual updates are published each November.
  • BCBS d233, A framework for dealing with domestic systemically important banks, October 2012: bis.org/publ/bcbs233.htm. The principles for D-SIB designation.

Worth reading for the original methodology and for the discussion of why the 2018 revision narrowed certain indicators.
  • FSB, Evaluation of the effects of too-big-to-fail reforms, final report, March 2021: fsb.org/2021/03/evaluation-of-the-effects-of-too-big-to-fail-reforms-final-report. The FSB's own assessment of whether the G-SIB and TLAC regime has worked.
