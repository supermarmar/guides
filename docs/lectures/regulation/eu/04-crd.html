CRD: the Capital Requirements Directive and Pillar 2

If the CRR is the arithmetic of EU banking regulation, the CRD is the architecture of supervision around it. The directive sets out how banks are authorised, how supervisors review them under Pillar 2, what macroprudential buffers sit on top of the Pillar 1 minima, how boards and remuneration must be structured, and which third-country banks may serve EU clients. It is the legal home of SREP, of the bonus cap, of the combined buffer requirement, and of the supervisory powers that make the CRR enforceable.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The Capital Requirements Directive (CRD), currently Directive 2013/36/EU as amended, is the EU legal instrument that does everything the CRR does not. The CRR is a regulation: directly applicable, identical in every member state, and prescriptive about Pillar 1 capital arithmetic. The CRD is a directive: it must be transposed into national law in each member state, it preserves space for national discretion, and it covers authorisation of credit institutions, governance, the supervisory review process (Pillar 2 SREP), the macroprudential buffer architecture (capital conservation, countercyclical, G-SII, O-SII, and systemic risk buffers), remuneration rules including the bonus cap, and the supervisory powers competent authorities need to enforce the CRR. CRD IV (2013) was amended by CRD V (Directive (EU) 2019/878) alongside CRR2, and again by CRD VI (Directive (EU) 2024/1619) alongside CRR3 to implement the Basel 3.1 governance and supervisory aspects.

The two instruments are inseparable in practice. A bank's binding capital requirement is the Pillar 1 minimum from the CRR, plus the Pillar 2 add-on (P2R) set by the supervisor under the CRD, plus the combined buffer requirement (CBR) constructed from the CRD's buffer toolkit, plus any Pillar 2 capital guidance (P2G) the supervisor expects to be met. The CRR tells you how to calculate the minimum; the CRD tells you how supervisors top it up, how the firm must be governed, and what happens to dividends, AT1 coupons, and bonuses when capital falls into the buffer stack.

Feynman test Imagine you are setting up a board game called "running a bank in the EU". The CRR is the rulebook you read on the back of the box: how many points you score, how risk weights map to capital, what counts as Tier 1. Every player in every member state plays by the same printed rules; you cannot change a comma. The CRD is the referee's handbook. It says who is allowed to play (authorisation), how often the referee inspects your hand (SREP), how much extra you have to put in the pot if the referee thinks you are playing recklessly (P2R), and what extra stake everyone has to put in when the game is going dangerously well (countercyclical buffer). The CRD also says how big a bonus the players are allowed to pay themselves out of winnings (1:1 of salary), and it tells third-country players that to sit at the EU table at all, they need to set up an EU subsidiary or a properly authorised branch (CRD VI). The CRR is the maths. The CRD is the politics, the supervision, and the manners.
What is beyond the source There is no dedicated CRD wiki file in this repository; the CRR file references the CRD only in passing. This lecture therefore covers the directive from the EUR-Lex text and from supervisory practice as it has emerged through the SSM. It adds: the legal mechanics of the regulation-plus-directive package (why some things were carved out of the CRR into the CRD, and what national discretion actually buys); the SREP process as a year-on-year supervisory cycle (SREP letter, P2R, P2G); the interaction of the buffer stack with the maximum distributable amount (MDA); a worked example of how the combined buffer requirement and MDA quartiles bite; the political history of the bonus cap and its post-Brexit fate; and a structural summary of CRD VI's third-country branch regime, which is the single most consequential cross-border change for City of London firms since Brexit.
Step 2

Why this matters

The CRR has the prescriptive arithmetic; the CRD has the supervisory architecture. Pillar 2, the combined buffer requirement, governance and fit-and-proper, the bonus cap, and the third-country branch regime all live in the CRD. For a supervisor, the CRD is the source of operational leverage: it is what allows the ECB or a national competent authority to require more capital than the CRR minimum, to demand changes to a bank's risk management, to refuse authorisation, to remove a director, to constrain dividends. For a bank, the CRD is what determines whether its strategy is permitted (authorisation), how its board is structured (governance), what it can pay its identified staff (remuneration), and what it has to do when its CET1 ratio touches the buffer stack (MDA constraints, capital conservation plan).

For a model developer the CRD is less visible than the CRR but no less important. Pillar 2 is where supervisors challenge model deficiencies that the CRR's Pillar 1 templates do not capture: model risk, IRRBB until CRD VI moved it partly into Pillar 1, concentration risk, step-in risk, ESG. The CRD's governance requirements are what put the model risk committee on the org chart, what put the chief risk officer at board level, and what require validation independent of model development. When the SSM challenges an A-IRB model and ends up adding P2R, that capital impact arrives via the CRD, not the CRR. A bank's effective capital requirement is shaped at least as much by SREP outcomes as by the CRR formulae.

Three institutional facts matter day-to-day. First, the CRD is transposed into national law, so the actual binding text is not "Directive 2013/36/EU" but the national act that implements it (in Germany, KWG; in France, Code monetaire et financier; in Ireland, the European Union (Capital Requirements) Regulations 2014). The directive sets the minimum; member states can be more conservative. Second, CRD amendments have a transposition deadline (usually eighteen months from EU publication), so until member states transpose, the old national rules still apply, creating a window of temporary divergence. Third, the SSM applies the CRD's Pillar 2 toolkit to significant institutions in a harmonised way through the SREP methodology, but national supervisors apply it to less significant institutions with national variation. The same Basel rule can therefore land differently on a French SI and a Portuguese LSI.

Step 3

The mental model

The cleanest way to hold the CRR/CRD package in your head is as two complementary halves of a single prudential framework. The CRR sits on the left, deals in prescriptive arithmetic, applies directly, and produces the Pillar 1 minimum capital requirement. The CRD sits on the right, deals in supervisory architecture, must be transposed, and produces the Pillar 2 add-on, the combined buffer requirement, and the governance frame.

Three flows of authority matter. Standards flow from Basel into both the CRR and the CRD, with the political choice in Brussels being whether a given Basel rule belongs in directly-applicable arithmetic or in a transposed supervisory frame. Supervisory action flows from competent authorities (the ECB for SIs, national supervisors for LSIs) under CRD powers, with the SREP letter as the primary instrument. And national discretion flows downward only within the CRD: the CRR does not permit member states to vary it. This is why the bonus cap was politically contested in the UK and Germany; it sits in the CRD, which is the part of the package with the most political texture.

Step 4

The detail

Legislative form: directive versus regulation

The CRD is a directive in the technical EU-law sense. A directive sets out an objective and a set of minimum requirements that every member state must achieve, but it leaves member states to choose how to write them into national law. A regulation, by contrast, is directly applicable: the EU text is itself the binding rule in every member state without any national act in between. Directives are slower to bite (transposition takes time, and member states miss deadlines), they permit national discretion (a member state can go beyond the directive's minimum), and they create the possibility of divergent text. Regulations are immediate, uniform, and arithmetic.

The CRR/CRD package made a deliberate choice in 2013 to split the prudential framework so that the prescriptive arithmetic sits in the CRR (a regulation, harmonised) and the supervisory architecture sits in the CRD (a directive, allowing national choice). The rationale was that capital ratios need to be calculated identically across the EU to support cross-border banking and the single market, while supervisory practice, authorisation, and remuneration are politically and culturally national matters where a one-size-fits-all rule is neither desirable nor achievable.

Structure of CRD IV

CRD IV (Directive 2013/36/EU) is organised into eleven Titles. The Titles that carry the most weight for examiners and practitioners are Title II (authorisation), Title V (prudential supervision), and Title VII (supervisory review, buffers, remuneration).

Title I: subject matter, scope, definitions
Defines "credit institution" by reference to the CRR. Sets out scope and key terms used throughout the directive.
Title II: access to the activity of credit institutions
Authorisation regime. Any institution carrying out the business of a credit institution within the EU must be authorised by the competent authority of the member state in which it is established. Authorisation requires a programme of operations, initial capital of at least EUR 5 million, two effective directors of good repute and adequate experience, and disclosure of significant shareholders.
Title III: financial holding companies and mixed financial holding companies
Brings the top of a banking group inside the supervisory perimeter. Following CRD V, financial holding companies need their own approval and are subject to supervisory powers in their own right.
Title IV: initial capital of investment firms
Originally applied to investment firms; largely carved out into the IFD/IFR regime by CRD V for smaller investment firms.
Title V: provisions concerning the freedom of establishment and the freedom to provide services
The "passporting" regime. An institution authorised in one member state can provide services and establish branches in any other member state without separate authorisation, subject to home-host coordination.
Title VI: relations with third countries
Originally light-touch. CRD VI substantially extends this Title with a harmonised third-country branch regime (TCB regime), discussed below.
Title VII: prudential supervision
The heart of the directive. Chapter 1 covers principles of prudential supervision, including the role of home and host authorities. Chapter 2 covers the supervisory review and evaluation process (SREP) and Pillar 2. Chapter 3 covers reporting and disclosure. Chapter 4 covers capital buffers (CCB, CCyB, G-SII, O-SII, SyRB) and the maximum distributable amount.
Title VIII: disclosure by competent authorities
Requires national supervisors to publish certain information about how they apply CRD/CRR, ensuring transparency in supervisory practice across the EU.
Title IX, X, XI: delegated acts, amendments, transitional and final provisions
Standard EU directive plumbing: empowerments for the Commission to adopt delegated and implementing acts, consequential amendments to other directives, and transitional rules.

Authorisation (Title II)

Any undertaking taking deposits or other repayable funds from the public and granting credits for its own account must be authorised as a credit institution before commencing business. The competent authority (the ECB for SIs in the SSM, the national supervisor for LSIs) cannot grant authorisation unless the applicant meets a battery of requirements: a programme of operations, robust governance and risk management arrangements, two effective directors of good repute and adequate knowledge and experience, initial capital of at least EUR 5 million, and disclosure of all qualifying shareholdings of 10 per cent or more.

Authorisation can be withdrawn if the institution fails to commence business within twelve months, ceases to carry on business for more than six months, no longer meets the conditions of authorisation, or commits serious infringements. The competent authority has wide discretion in withdrawal, and withdrawal is one of the gravest supervisory tools available.

Fit-and-proper rules, set out in Articles 91 and 92, apply to members of the management body and to key function holders. Members must commit sufficient time, have adequate collective knowledge, skills and experience, act with honesty and integrity, and respect limits on the number of directorships they hold. The fit-and-proper regime is the legal basis for the ECB's "fit-and-proper" assessments under the SSM, which are typically completed within four months of a notification by the institution.

Pillar 2 SREP (Articles 97 to 104)

The supervisory review and evaluation process (SREP) is the CRD's tool for ensuring that supervisors look at each institution holistically, not just through the Pillar 1 lens. Article 97 requires competent authorities to review the arrangements, strategies, processes, and mechanisms implemented by institutions to comply with the CRR/CRD and to evaluate the risks to which they are or might be exposed and the risks they pose to the financial system. The review must cover all material risks, including those not captured or not adequately captured by Pillar 1 (model risk, concentration risk, IRRBB, step-in risk, ESG, conduct, and so on).

The output of the SREP is a SREP decision communicated by letter. The decision quantifies the supervisor's assessment in two pieces of additional own funds:

P2R (Pillar 2 Requirement)
A binding additional own funds requirement on top of the Pillar 1 minimum. P2R is institution-specific, set under Article 104, and must be met at all times. P2R covers risks not adequately captured by Pillar 1: IRRBB (pre-CRD VI), credit concentration, business-model risk, and supervisory concerns about model quality. P2R has its own composition requirement: under the SSM since 2021, at least 56.25 per cent must be met with CET1 and at least 75 per cent with Tier 1.
P2G (Pillar 2 Guidance)
A non-binding capital expectation set under Article 104b. P2G is informed by supervisory stress testing and reflects the supervisor's view of how much additional CET1 the institution should hold to withstand a severe but plausible stress without breaching the combined buffer requirement. Failure to meet P2G does not trigger MDA constraints, but it triggers supervisory dialogue and can lead to a P2R increase at the next SREP cycle.

The SREP cycle runs annually for SIs under the SSM and on a multi-year cycle for LSIs depending on national practice. The result is captured in the institution-specific Overall Capital Requirement (OCR), which is the binding stack of Pillar 1 plus P2R plus the combined buffer requirement, and the Overall Capital Expectation (OCE), which adds P2G on top.

Capital buffers (Title VII Chapter 4)

The CRD's buffer framework is the macroprudential complement to the CRR's microprudential Pillar 1. The buffers are layered on top of Pillar 1 and P2R, and together they form the combined buffer requirement (CBR). All buffers must be met with CET1.

Capital conservation buffer (CCB), Article 129
A fixed 2.5 per cent of risk-weighted assets, applicable to all credit institutions. Phased in from 2016 to 2019. The buffer exists to ensure that institutions enter a downturn with a surplus over the Pillar 1 minimum, so that they can absorb losses without breaching it.
Countercyclical capital buffer (CCyB), Article 130
Set by the national designated authority of the member state in which an exposure is located, from 0 to 2.5 per cent of RWA (with the option to go higher). The institution-specific CCyB rate is a weighted average of the rates in the jurisdictions where its exposures sit. The primary indicator the BCBS recommends is the credit-to-GDP gap, but national authorities have wide discretion. Activated in advance of credit booms, released in downturns.
G-SII buffer, Article 131
1.0 to 3.5 per cent of RWA for institutions identified as global systemically important by the EBA following the FSB G-SIB methodology. EU terminology uses "G-SII" (global systemically important institution) where the FSB uses "G-SIB"; the methodology is the same five-category scoring (size, interconnectedness, substitutability, complexity, cross-jurisdictional activity). Buckets are 1, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, and 3.5 per cent.
O-SII buffer, Article 131
0 to 3 per cent of RWA for "other systemically important institutions", meaning institutions that are systemically important at the national or EU level but not globally so. Identified by national authorities using EBA guidelines based on size, importance for the economy, complexity, and interconnectedness. The 3 per cent ceiling can be exceeded with Commission approval.
Systemic risk buffer (SyRB), Article 133
A discretionary buffer set by member states to address long-term non-cyclical systemic or macroprudential risks not covered by the CRR or the other buffers. Can be set at any level, on the whole RWA or on a defined sectoral subset (for example Belgium's sectoral SyRB on residential mortgages). Above 3 per cent it needs Commission and ESRB consultation; above 5 per cent it needs Commission authorisation.

The combined buffer requirement is the sum of the CCB, the institution-specific CCyB, and (subject to specific stacking rules) the higher of the G-SII, O-SII, and SyRB buffers, with sectoral SyRBs additive on top. The stacking rules are intricate; the simplified mental model is "CCB + CCyB + max of the structural buffers + sectoral SyRB".

The maximum distributable amount (MDA)

The MDA mechanism (Article 141) gives the buffer framework its bite. When an institution's CET1 ratio falls into the combined buffer requirement (that is, falls below Pillar 1 plus P2R plus CBR but above Pillar 1 plus P2R alone), it does not breach a binding requirement, but it loses discretion over distributions. The institution must calculate its maximum distributable amount, which is the maximum total amount it can pay out in dividends, AT1 coupons, and variable remuneration to identified staff. The MDA is calculated by multiplying interim profits since the most recent decision on distributions by a factor that depends on how far into the CBR the CET1 ratio has fallen, divided into quartiles.

Worked example: combined buffer requirement and MDA quartiles

Suppose an O-SII subsidiary of a euro-area G-SII has the following stack. Pillar 1 minimum: 8 per cent (of which 4.5 per cent CET1). P2R: 2 per cent (of which 1.125 per cent CET1). CCB: 2.5 per cent. CCyB (weighted by exposure location): 1 per cent. G-SII at parent level applied to subsidiary: not applicable (subsidiary-level only). O-SII at subsidiary level: 1.5 per cent. SyRB: nil.

CRR Pillar 1 CET1 minimum            =  4.5%
CRD P2R CET1 component               =  1.125%
                                       ------
Total binding CET1 minimum (no buffer) =  5.625%

CCB                                   =  2.5%
CCyB                                  =  1.0%
O-SII                                 =  1.5%
                                       ------
Combined buffer requirement (CBR)     =  5.0%

Overall CET1 requirement (with CBR)   = 10.625%

The institution's CET1 ratio is 9.0 per cent. It is above the binding 5.625 per cent minimum but below the 10.625 per cent total. It is therefore in the CBR. The distance from the binding minimum into the CBR is 9.0% - 5.625% = 3.375%, against a CBR of 5.0%. The institution is 3.375/5.0 = 67.5 per cent of the way through the CBR.

The MDA quartiles map this into a distribution constraint. The bottom quartile of the CBR (0 to 25 per cent of the way through) caps distributions at 0 per cent of distributable profits. The second quartile (25 to 50 per cent) caps them at 20 per cent. The third quartile (50 to 75 per cent) caps them at 40 per cent. The top quartile (75 to 100 per cent) caps them at 60 per cent. At 67.5 per cent of the way through, the institution sits in the third quartile and can pay out no more than 40 per cent of distributable profits as dividends, AT1 coupons, and variable remuneration combined.

The MDA bite is therefore not a cliff edge but a sliding constraint, designed to encourage banks to rebuild capital from earnings before resuming full distributions. The implication for AT1 holders is direct: an institution can be solvent on every binding measure and still skip an AT1 coupon because of the MDA quartile rule.

Remuneration (Title VII Chapter 2, Section II)

The CRD's remuneration rules apply to identified staff: members of the management body, senior management, risk-takers, staff in control functions, and any employee whose total remuneration takes them into the same bracket. The headline rule is the bonus cap (Article 94(1)(g)): variable remuneration cannot exceed 100 per cent of fixed remuneration for any identified staff member, or 200 per cent with explicit shareholder approval under specific procedural conditions. The cap was introduced by CRD IV in 2013 and was politically contested at the time, with the UK challenging it (unsuccessfully) before the Court of Justice. The UK has since repealed the cap post-Brexit (effective late 2023 in the PRA Rulebook), while the EU framework retains it.

Beyond the cap, the directive imposes structural rules on how variable remuneration is paid. At least 40 per cent (60 per cent for very high variable amounts) must be deferred over three to five years. At least 50 per cent of any variable component must be paid in shares or share-linked instruments, AT1 instruments, or other instruments that align incentives with the bank's risk profile. Deferred amounts vest pro rata, with claw-back applicable for at least seven years in cases of misconduct, material risk-management failure, or material restatement. A retention period applies to vested share-based remuneration. Pension benefits paid early or as discretionary additions are subject to a five-year holding period in instruments.

Governance (Title VII Chapter 2)

The CRD lays down a governance frame that has shaped board structure in EU banks. The management body must devote sufficient time, have adequate collective knowledge, and be of sufficiently good repute. Significant institutions must establish a separate risk committee composed of non-executive directors, with members possessing appropriate knowledge of risk management. The risk committee advises the management body on the institution's overall current and future risk appetite and strategy, and oversees the implementation of that strategy by senior management. A chief risk officer (CRO) is required, with direct access to the management body. Conflict of interest policies must be documented and enforced. Internal controls including independent risk control, compliance, and internal audit functions are mandatory and must be independent of the business lines they review.

CRD V (Directive (EU) 2019/878)

CRD V amended CRD IV alongside CRR2 to address gaps revealed by ten years of operation. The principal changes were: bringing financial holding companies and mixed financial holding companies within the direct supervisory perimeter, with their own approval regime; introducing the intermediate parent undertaking (IPU) requirement for non-EU groups with significant EU operations, meaning that groups with two or more EU institutions and total EU assets above EUR 40 billion must consolidate their EU operations under a single EU-incorporated IPU; carving out smaller investment firms into the new Investment Firms Directive (IFD) and Investment Firms Regulation (IFR); updating SREP and Pillar 2 to clarify the distinction between P2R and P2G and to specify the composition requirements for P2R; introducing the formal requirement for institutions to consider ESG risks in their governance and risk management; and updating the buffer framework, including the SyRB stacking rules and the option for sectoral SyRBs.

CRD VI (Directive (EU) 2024/1619)

CRD VI was adopted in May 2024 as part of the EU's 2021 banking package, alongside CRR3, to implement the Basel 3.1 governance and supervisory aspects. The transposition deadline is 11 January 2026 for most provisions, with the third-country branch regime applying from that date. The principal innovations:

Harmonised third-country branch regime (TCB regime)
The single most consequential cross-border change. Pre-CRD VI, third-country banks (including UK banks post-Brexit) could establish branches in EU member states under national rules that varied considerably. CRD VI introduces a harmonised regime: third-country branches must be authorised by the host competent authority, must hold endowment capital, must comply with governance and reporting requirements similar to (though lighter than) those for EU institutions, and are classified as Class 1 (significant) or Class 2 (non-significant) with proportionate requirements. The regime ends London's previous easy access to EU clients through national branch routes such as Germany's "freistellung" and aligns third-country bank treatment across the EU.
ESG risk integration
Mandates the integration of ESG risks (environmental, social, governance) into institutions' governance, risk management, and supervisory review. Requires specific transition planning, scenario analysis, and disclosures for climate and broader environmental risks.
IRRBB partial migration to Pillar 1
Interest rate risk in the banking book, previously a Pillar 2 risk under the BCBS 368 framework and EBA guidelines, partly moves into Pillar 1 under CRD VI. The "supervisory outlier test" (SOT) on economic value of equity and net interest income becomes a harder constraint, with breaches triggering supervisory measures more automatically.
Enhanced fit-and-proper
CRD VI strengthens the ex-ante assessment of management body members. Significant institutions must obtain prior approval for proposed members of the management body (rather than the current notification-and-objection model in some jurisdictions). The collective suitability and time-commitment requirements are tightened.
Updated Pillar 2 framework
CRD VI updates the Pillar 2 articles to integrate the new Pillar 1 changes (FRTB, SMA, output floor) and to confirm the role of P2R and P2G alongside the harmonised CRR3 framework. The output floor, set in CRR3, interacts with P2R: the supervisor can no longer use P2R to "neutralise" the output floor's impact.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

SREP letters are where supervisory action lives

The CRR specifies what a bank's Pillar 1 capital ratio is. The SREP letter, issued under CRD Article 104, specifies what the supervisor expects on top: P2R, P2G, qualitative measures, and any specific concerns about model quality, governance, IT, or business model. Reading historical SREP letters (or their summary in the institution's Pillar 3 disclosure of OCR and OCE) is the single best way to understand where a supervisor is leaning. SREP letters are not public, but the resulting P2R and P2G are disclosed in many cases, and the SSM publishes aggregate SREP outcomes that show where capital pressure is going across the euro area.

The buffer toolkit creates EU-internal divergence

Because the CRD permits national discretion on buffers (particularly CCyB, O-SII, and SyRB), member states have used the toolkit differently. Belgium and the Netherlands have historically been activist, applying sectoral SyRBs on residential mortgages, raising CCyB ahead of credit booms, and identifying a broader set of O-SIIs. Germany has been more cautious in CCyB activation. France raised CCyB to 1 per cent in 2019, lowered it in 2020 during COVID, and has since varied it. The EU is therefore a single Pillar 1 framework with a textured macroprudential overlay; comparing "the EU buffer rate" misses the variation, and the variation is the policy point.

Watch out The MDA is calculated on the basis of interim profits, not retained earnings. A bank in the CBR cannot decide to "use last year's retained earnings" to pay an AT1 coupon if its interim profit is too small to support the coupon under the MDA quartile. AT1 instruments therefore carry a coupon-skip risk that is not the same as default risk, and the trigger is the CBR threshold, not insolvency. This is the structural feature that made AT1 a contested asset class during the 2023 Credit Suisse resolution, when the Swiss FINMA wrote AT1 to zero ahead of equity holders (under Swiss law, not under the CRD).

The bonus cap was the most politically contested CRD measure

The 1:1 bonus cap (or 2:1 with shareholder approval) was inserted into CRD IV at the European Parliament's insistence over UK and Commission objections in 2013. The UK challenged the cap before the Court of Justice in 2014, arguing it exceeded the EU's competence; the Court rejected the challenge. The cap remained in EU law and the UK transposed it through the PRA Rulebook. Post-Brexit, the PRA removed the cap from the Rulebook in October 2023, with effect from 31 October 2023. The EU continues to apply the cap. This is the clearest case where post-Brexit UK supervisory policy has diverged from the EU's CRD on a politically visible issue, and the divergence has become a material factor in UK-EU competition for senior banking talent.

CRD VI's third-country branch regime ends London's easy EU access

Before CRD VI, a UK bank could provide investment services to EU clients through national third-country branch regimes that varied by member state. Some regimes (Germany's section 53b KWG; the Netherlands' freistellungen route) permitted relatively light-touch access to wholesale clients. CRD VI, applying from 11 January 2026, harmonises the regime: a third-country bank must be authorised by the host competent authority as a Class 1 or Class 2 TCB, must hold endowment capital, must report into the host supervisor on a standardised basis, and faces a regime materially more burdensome than the patchwork it replaces. Many UK banks have responded by converting to EU subsidiaries (in Frankfurt, Paris, Dublin, Luxembourg) for EU client servicing; the City of London's wholesale EU client business will continue to migrate.

Pillar 2 is where models meet supervision

For a model developer, the CRR's Pillar 1 templates capture credit risk, market risk, and operational risk by formula. Everything the formula misses (model risk, IRRBB pre-CRD VI, concentration risk, business-model risk, ESG, conduct, step-in risk) lands in Pillar 2 under the CRD. SREP findings on model quality therefore arrive as P2R add-ons, not as direct CRR enforcement. When an A-IRB model is challenged by the SSM through the TRIM exercise or in a regular SREP, the capital impact arrives through P2R. The CRD is in this sense the supervisor's main lever on model outputs, and a model developer who ignores it has not understood where the supervisory pressure actually arrives.

Test yourself

  1. What is the legal distinction between a regulation and a directive in EU law, and why was the CRR/CRD package split between the two forms in 2013?
  2. Name the five capital buffers in the CRD and state the broad permitted range of each. Which buffer is set by the EBA, and which by national designated authorities?
  3. Distinguish P2R from P2G. Which is binding, which constrains distributions when breached, and what is the SSM minimum CET1 composition for P2R?
  4. A bank has Pillar 1 CET1 of 4.5 per cent, P2R CET1 of 1.5 per cent, CCB of 2.5 per cent, CCyB of 1 per cent, O-SII of 1 per cent, and an actual CET1 ratio of 9 per cent. What is the combined buffer requirement, the overall CET1 requirement, and which MDA quartile is the bank in?
  5. Why does the CRD's bonus cap apply only to "identified staff" rather than to all bank employees, and what are the procedural conditions for raising the cap to 2:1?
  6. Explain the difference between G-SII and O-SII buffers in terms of identification methodology, permitted range, and the authority that designates them.
  7. What was the principal change CRD V made to the supervisory perimeter of large EU banking groups, and which non-EU groups did it most directly affect?
  8. Describe the CRD VI third-country branch regime in three sentences. Why does it matter for the City of London?
  9. Why is IRRBB an interesting case study in the CRR/CRD division of labour, and what changes under CRD VI?
  10. A bank's CET1 ratio falls below P1 + P2R but the bank remains solvent on every other measure. What automatic and what discretionary supervisory consequences follow?
