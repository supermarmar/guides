ECB and the Single Supervisory Mechanism

The European Central Bank runs two jobs that, in most other jurisdictions, live in two separate buildings. It sets monetary policy for the euro area through the Eurosystem, and it directly supervises the largest euro-area banks through the Single Supervisory Mechanism. The two roles are deliberately walled off from one another inside the same institution, with separate governing bodies, separate staff, and a legal prohibition on cross-contamination of objectives. Understanding the ECB means understanding which hat it is wearing at any given moment.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The European Central Bank (ECB), founded in 1998 and operational from 1999, has two distinct mandates. The first, set out in TFEU Article 127(1), is monetary policy for the euro area: maintaining price stability through the Eurosystem (the ECB plus the national central banks of the 20 euro-area member states). The second, added by Council Regulation (EU) 1024/2013 and operational from 4 November 2014, is the prudential supervision of euro-area banks through the Single Supervisory Mechanism (SSM). The SSM directly supervises roughly 110 Significant Institutions (SIs) accounting for about 85 per cent of euro-area banking assets; the remaining several thousand Less Significant Institutions (LSIs) are supervised by national competent authorities under ECB oversight. The annual rhythm of supervision is the Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process (SREP), which produces an institution-specific Pillar 2 Requirement (P2R, binding) and Pillar 2 Guidance (P2G, non-binding) on top of the Pillar 1 minima and combined buffer requirement.

The two mandates are separated inside the ECB by a Chinese wall. Monetary policy is decided by the Governing Council; supervision is decided by the Supervisory Board, with the Governing Council holding only a non-objection role over supervisory decisions. Staff, IT systems, and information flows are formally segregated. The architecture exists because the European treaties prohibit the central bank from using its monetary policy tools to support specific banks, and equally prohibit it from softening supervision to protect monetary-policy counterparties.

Feynman test Imagine two very different jobs in the same office tower in Frankfurt. On the top floor, a committee of central bank governors decides how much money to print and what the deposit rate should be. On a different floor, accessed through a different lift, a separate committee of supervisors decides whether a Spanish savings bank has enough capital and whether to fire its chief risk officer. The two committees do not share staff, do not share documents, and are barred by law from helping each other out. If the supervisory committee wants the monetary committee to bail out one of its banks, it has to apply through the same procedure that any commercial bank would. If the monetary committee wants the supervisory committee to go easy on a bank that owes the ECB money, it cannot ask. That is the SSM. One building, two institutions, one wall.

Step 2

Why this matters

Before November 2014, every euro-area bank was supervised by its own national authority. A Spanish bank answered to the Banco de España, an Irish bank to the Central Bank of Ireland, a Cypriot bank to the Central Bank of Cyprus. The national supervisors applied the same Basel rules in name, but the rules were implemented in 19 different rulebooks, enforced with 19 different supervisory cultures, and policed with 19 different levels of willingness to confront politically connected banks. The 2008 to 2012 crises exposed what this looked like in practice: the Irish banking sector grew to several times Irish GDP without supervisory pushback; the Spanish cajas masked loss-making real-estate lending for years; the Cypriot banks took on Greek sovereign exposures that wiped out their capital. Each national crisis became a sovereign crisis because the national authorities had to recapitalise their banks, and each sovereign crisis spread back into the banks via their sovereign-bond holdings. The euro-area called this the sovereign-bank doom loop.

The political answer, announced at the June 2012 euro-area summit, was the Banking Union: take bank supervision out of national hands and put it in a single euro-area institution; create a single euro-area resolution authority to wind banks up when they fail; and eventually create a single euro-area deposit-insurance scheme. The first two pillars exist and function: the SSM for supervision and the SRM (Single Resolution Mechanism) for resolution. The third pillar, the European Deposit Insurance Scheme (EDIS), remains stalled at the political level. The SSM is therefore the most visible institutional consequence of the euro-area crisis, and the body that any banking actuary working on a euro-area institution will spend the most time interacting with.

For a credit-risk modeller or capital-management practitioner, three institutional facts have direct day-to-day consequences. First, the bank's directly-responsible supervisor is the ECB JST, not the national authority, if the bank is significant; the JST decides whether an A-IRB model can go live, what the model's MoC add-on should be, and what the bank's P2R is. Second, the SREP letter that arrives each December sets the bank's binding CET1 stack for the following year; it is the single most important regulatory document the bank receives annually. Third, when a euro-area bank fails or is "failing or likely to fail" (FOLTF), the ECB makes that determination and the SRB executes the resolution; the bank's home state has no operational role.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture the ECB as a single legal entity with two parallel internal structures. On one side, the Eurosystem: the Governing Council sets monetary policy, the Executive Board implements it, and the national central banks execute open-market operations in their own jurisdictions. On the other side, the SSM: the Supervisory Board prepares supervisory decisions, the Governing Council formally adopts them under a non-objection procedure, and the JSTs run day-to-day supervision of SIs with the national competent authorities supervising LSIs underneath them. A wall separates the two, and the Supervisory Board has its own Chair and Vice-Chair who are not Executive Board members.

Three flows of authority matter. Supervisory decisions flow from the JSTs upward through ECB Banking Supervision to the Supervisory Board, are formally adopted by the Governing Council under non-objection, and become binding on the SI. Information flows in both directions across the Chinese wall under tightly controlled protocols, so that, for instance, a bank's monetary-policy counterparty status cannot be used as leverage in a supervisory dispute. And the resolution baton passes from the ECB to the SRB at a single legal moment: the ECB's FOLTF determination, after which the SSM's job is over and the SRM's begins.

Step 4

The detail

History: from Maastricht to the doom loop to the SSM

The ECB was created by the Maastricht Treaty of 1992 and became operational on 1 June 1998 in Frankfurt. The euro was launched as an accounting currency on 1 January 1999 and as a physical currency on 1 January 2002. The treaty mandate was unambiguous: a single monetary policy for the euro area, with price stability as the primary objective. Banking supervision was deliberately left at national level, on the German argument that the central bank's monetary credibility would be eroded if it also had to manage the failure of individual banks.

The 2008 to 2012 crisis falsified that argument. National supervision combined with monetary union produced the sovereign-bank doom loop: weakly supervised national banks failed, were rescued by national governments, the rescue costs strained national sovereigns, and the strained sovereigns then dragged down the banks that held their bonds. Ireland in 2010, Spain in 2012, and Cyprus in 2013 were the three clearest examples. At the June 2012 euro-area summit, leaders agreed to pursue a Banking Union with the ECB as single supervisor. The political deal was struck in 2013: Germany insisted that ECB supervision cover only the largest banks (to keep its Sparkassen and Landesbanken under domestic control); France pushed for all banks; the compromise was the SI/LSI distinction. The SSM Regulation (Council Regulation (EU) 1024/2013) was adopted on 15 October 2013 and entered into force on 4 November 2013, with a one-year preparatory period before operational start.

During that preparatory year the ECB conducted a Comprehensive Assessment of the 130 banks slated to become SIs: an Asset Quality Review of loan classifications and provisioning, plus a stress test run jointly with the EBA. The AQR generated CET1 shortfalls of about €25 billion across 25 banks, which had to be filled before 4 November 2014. The SSM became operational on that date with a clean opening balance sheet and an unprecedented mandate.

Legal basis

Two layers of EU primary and secondary law underpin the ECB's twin roles. The primary law is the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union, as amended by the Lisbon Treaty in 2009. The secondary law is the SSM Regulation, the SRM Regulation, and the broader CRR/CRD framework.

TFEU Article 127(1) and (2)
Sets the ECB's primary objective as maintaining price stability and lists its basic tasks: define and implement monetary policy, conduct foreign-exchange operations, hold and manage official foreign reserves, promote the smooth operation of payment systems.
TFEU Article 127(5) and (6)
Allows the ECB to contribute to "the smooth conduct of policies pursued by the competent authorities relating to the prudential supervision of credit institutions" (127(5)) and provides the legal hook (127(6)) for the Council to confer on the ECB "specific tasks concerning policies relating to the prudential supervision of credit institutions". 127(6) is the article on which the SSM was built.
TFEU Article 282
Defines the ESCB (European System of Central Banks) as the ECB plus all 27 national central banks, and the Eurosystem as the ECB plus the 20 euro-area NCBs. Confirms ECB independence: the ECB and NCBs shall not seek or take instructions from EU institutions, member governments, or any other body.
SSM Regulation (Council Regulation (EU) 1024/2013)
The implementing act under Article 127(6). Defines the perimeter of ECB supervision, the SI/LSI distinction, the Supervisory Board, the JST model, the cooperation with national competent authorities, the supervisory powers, and the separation from monetary policy. Adopted under a special legislative procedure: Council unanimity plus European Parliament consent.
SSM Framework Regulation (ECB Regulation 468/2014)
The ECB's own implementing rules under the SSM Regulation. Sets out the detailed procedures: how JSTs operate, how the SI determination is made, how on-site inspections are conducted, how administrative penalties are imposed, how decisions are appealed.

Governance: who decides what

The ECB has four governing bodies, each with a defined remit. Understanding which body acts on which question is the single most useful piece of institutional knowledge.

Governing Council
The ECB's supreme decision-making body for monetary policy. Comprises the six Executive Board members plus the governors of the 20 euro-area national central banks (26 members in total). Meets twice a month; one meeting is dedicated to monetary policy decisions. Also exercises the formal non-objection role over supervisory decisions prepared by the Supervisory Board.
Executive Board
Six members appointed by the European Council for non-renewable eight-year terms: the President, the Vice-President, and four others. Implements monetary policy as decided by the Governing Council. Runs the ECB as an institution. Members are not on the Supervisory Board (the Chair and Vice-Chair of the Supervisory Board are separate appointments).
Supervisory Board
The body that prepares supervisory decisions. Comprises a Chair (five-year non-renewable term, appointed by Council on Parliament approval), a Vice-Chair (who must be an Executive Board member), four ECB representatives, and one representative per participating member state (currently 21: the 20 euro-area states plus Bulgaria, which opted in via close cooperation in 2020). Meets every three weeks. Its draft decisions go to the Governing Council under the non-objection procedure.
General Council
Advisory body comprising the ECB President, Vice-President, and the governors of all 27 EU national central banks. Will be dissolved when all EU member states adopt the euro. Has no executive role; mostly handles transitional issues for non-euro EU states.
Mediation Panel
Established by Decision ECB/2014/16 to resolve differences of view between the Governing Council and the Supervisory Board when the Council objects to a draft supervisory decision. One member per participating member state, chosen from the Governing Council and Supervisory Board representatives. Reaches its opinion by simple majority; the opinion is not binding but is the formal channel for cross-board disagreement.

The non-objection procedure works as follows. The Supervisory Board prepares a draft supervisory decision. It is transmitted to the Governing Council, which has ten working days (extendable in exceptional circumstances) to object. If the Council does not object, the decision is deemed adopted. If the Council objects, it must explain why in writing, and the matter goes to the Mediation Panel. In practice, Governing Council objections are very rare and almost always concern monetary-policy spillovers rather than the substance of the supervisory judgement.

The SSM perimeter: who is in

The SSM covers credit institutions established in participating member states. Participation is automatic for the 20 euro-area member states. Non-euro EU member states can opt in through close cooperation, a formal arrangement under SSM Regulation Article 7. Bulgaria opted in on 1 October 2020 alongside its entry into the Exchange Rate Mechanism II (the pre-euro exchange-rate corridor). Croatia opted in on the same date and subsequently adopted the euro on 1 January 2023. Denmark and Sweden have not opted in; post-Brexit, the UK is outside the EU entirely. Hungary, Poland, the Czech Republic, and Romania have not opted in.

Significant Institutions and Less Significant Institutions

The Significance assessment is made annually by the ECB on the basis of five criteria set out in SSM Regulation Article 6(4). A bank is significant if it meets any of the following:

Size
Total assets exceed €30 billion. The single most common trigger. Calculated at the highest level of consolidation in a participating member state.
Economic importance
Total assets exceed €5 billion and total assets exceed 20 per cent of the GDP of the member state of establishment. The "small country, big bank" rule that catches Cypriot, Maltese, and Luxembourgish banks.
Cross-border activities
Total assets exceed €5 billion and the ratio of cross-border assets in more than one other participating member state to total assets, or the ratio of cross-border liabilities in more than one other participating member state to total liabilities, exceeds 20 per cent.
Public financial assistance
The institution has requested or received funding from the European Stability Mechanism (ESM) or the European Financial Stability Facility (EFSF). Catches the Spanish, Cypriot, and Greek crisis banks.
Top three rule
The three largest credit institutions in each participating member state are always classified as SIs, regardless of their size. Ensures every member state has at least three banks under direct ECB supervision and prevents a "supervisory desert" where the ECB has no presence in a country.

The ECB can also designate an institution as significant on its own initiative if it considers the institution systemically relevant. The classification is reviewed annually. In 2025 there are about 110 SIs covering roughly 85 per cent of euro-area banking assets. Examples include BNP Paribas (France), Deutsche Bank (Germany), Santander (Spain), ING (Netherlands), and UniCredit (Italy). The remaining several thousand LSIs are mostly the German Sparkassen and cooperative banks, the Spanish cajas, the Italian banche popolari, and the Austrian Volksbanken.

LSIs are directly supervised by national competent authorities (BaFin in Germany, ACPR in France, Banco de España in Spain, and so on) under ECB oversight. The ECB sets supervisory standards, monitors NCA performance, and can take over direct supervision of any LSI it judges necessary. The ECB's general directorate for LSI oversight is DG MS III.

Joint Supervisory Teams

Each Significant Institution is supervised by a Joint Supervisory Team (JST): a mixed team of ECB and national supervisor staff that runs the day-to-day supervisory relationship. The JST is led by an ECB JST coordinator, supported by sub-coordinators from each national competent authority that has supervisory interest in the group (typically the home-state NCA plus host-state NCAs for major foreign subsidiaries). Team size scales with the SI's size and complexity: from around 10 staff for the smallest SIs to over 60 for the largest banking groups such as BNP Paribas or Deutsche Bank.

The JST owns the full supervisory cycle for its bank: business-model analysis, governance assessment, capital and liquidity reviews, model approvals, on-site inspection scoping, and the annual SREP decision. It is the JST coordinator who signs off on internal-model approvals, sets the bank's P2R, and convenes the supervisory dialogue. A bank's CRO and CFO interact with the JST coordinator at least quarterly; the head of internal audit, the chair of the board risk committee, and (for material model changes) the head of model risk all have direct lines into the JST.

Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process (SREP)

The SREP is the annual supervisory cycle that produces an institution-specific supervisory decision for each SI. The methodology is set out in EBA Guidelines on common procedures and methodologies for SREP and operationalised in the ECB's SSM SREP Methodology Booklet (published annually). The assessment has four blocks:

Block 1: Business-model analysis
Examines the viability and sustainability of the bank's business model over a three-year horizon. Covers profitability, strategic plans, market position, peer comparison, and the credibility of the bank's own strategic projections. The output is a score on a 1-to-4 scale (1 = low risk, 4 = high risk).
Block 2: Governance and risk management
Reviews the bank's internal governance, risk culture, risk appetite framework, three lines of defence, and the quality of the risk management and internal audit functions. The block on which JSTs are most likely to recommend changes to board composition, senior personnel, or internal-control processes.
Block 3: Capital adequacy
Assesses risks to capital (credit, market, operational, IRRBB, sovereign concentration, other Pillar 2 risks) and the bank's own ICAAP. Output: the SREP capital requirement, comprising the Pillar 1 minimum, the Pillar 2 Requirement (P2R), the combined buffer requirement (capital conservation buffer, countercyclical buffer, systemic buffers), and Pillar 2 Guidance (P2G).
Block 4: Liquidity adequacy
Assesses liquidity and funding risk and the bank's own ILAAP. Output: an LCR/NSFR sufficiency assessment and any bank-specific liquidity requirements above Pillar 1. Less commonly produces a binding add-on than Block 3.

Each block produces a score; the four scores are aggregated into an overall SREP score on the 1-to-4 scale. The output document, the SREP decision letter, lands at the bank in late autumn and is binding from 1 January of the following year. It is the most consequential annual regulatory document for an SI.

Pillar 2 Requirement vs Pillar 2 Guidance

The Pillar 2 capital outcome of SREP has two distinct components, and the distinction is the single most frequently misunderstood feature of the EU capital stack.

Pillar 2 Requirement (P2R)
A binding capital requirement set institution-by-institution to cover risks not, or not adequately, covered by Pillar 1 (model risk, concentration risk, interest-rate risk in the banking book, sovereign concentration, business-model risks). Sits above the Pillar 1 minimum and below the combined buffer requirement in the MDA (Maximum Distributable Amount) stack. Since 2021, at least 56.25 per cent of P2R must be met with CET1 (the same composition split as Pillar 1: 56.25/18.75/25 between CET1, AT1, T2).
Pillar 2 Guidance (P2G)
A non-binding capital expectation set institution-by-institution to cover the bank's resilience to stress, calibrated from the EBA stress test (or the supervisor's own stress test for non-EBA-participating SIs). Sits above the combined buffer requirement. Does not feed into the MDA calculation: breaching P2G does not trigger automatic distribution restrictions, but it does trigger an intensified supervisory dialogue and a recovery plan. Must be met with CET1.

The MDA stack, top to bottom, runs: P2G (CET1 only, non-binding), combined buffer requirement (CET1 only), P2R (CET1/AT1/T2 split), Pillar 1 minimum (CET1/AT1/T2 split). When CET1 falls into the buffer band, automatic distribution restrictions kick in proportionally (the deeper the breach, the larger the share of earnings that must be retained). When CET1 falls below the top of P2R, the bank fails its capital requirement and the ECB takes formal supervisory action.

Supervisory powers

The ECB's supervisory powers over SIs are set out in SSM Regulation Articles 9 to 18 and supplemented by the powers of national competent authorities under CRD Article 104. The most important are:

Authorisation and withdrawal
The ECB is the sole authority for granting and withdrawing banking licences in participating member states, for both SIs and LSIs. National authorities prepare the application but the ECB takes the decision. Withdrawal is the most severe supervisory action and is used for failed or non-compliant banks.
Significant ownership decisions
Approval of qualifying holdings (acquisitions of 10 per cent or more of a bank's shares) is an ECB decision for all banks, SIs and LSIs alike.
Capital and liquidity add-ons
Through SREP, the ECB sets the P2R and P2G for each SI, and can impose additional own-funds requirements, liquidity requirements, or restrictions on the use of internal models.
On-site inspections
The ECB conducts about 200 on-site inspections per year across SIs, covering credit risk (the largest share), internal models, ICT and operational risk, and governance. Inspections typically run for six to twelve weeks on site and produce a report with mandatory follow-up actions tracked through the JST.
Internal model investigations (IMI)
A separate ECB workstream that assesses A-IRB, IMM, IRC, and FRTB internal models against the CRR and the ECB's guide on internal models. IMIs produce findings of varying severity that must be remediated before the model can be used, or with an interim MoC add-on while remediation is in progress.
Sanctions
The ECB can impose administrative penalties up to twice the profit gained or loss avoided as a result of the breach, or up to 10 per cent of total annual turnover. Sanctions are imposed by the Supervisory Board on the recommendation of an independent investigating unit and are published.

Stress testing and thematic reviews

The SSM participates in the biennial EU-wide stress test coordinated by the EBA. The EBA designs the methodology and the macroeconomic scenario; the ECB applies them to SSM banks and produces the bank-by-bank results. The 2014, 2016, 2018, 2021, and 2023 stress tests have all followed this model. Stress-test results feed directly into the P2G calibration: a bank that depletes more capital in the adverse scenario than its peers receives a higher P2G.

Outside the EBA cycle, the ECB runs its own thematic reviews and targeted reviews. The most consequential of the past few years are the TRIM (Targeted Review of Internal Models) programme of 2017 to 2021, which reviewed almost every A-IRB model at every SI and led to widespread MoC add-ons; the 2022 climate stress test, which assessed banks' exposure to transition and physical climate risks and led to the climate-risk supervisory expectations now embedded in SREP; and ongoing reviews of leveraged finance, commercial real estate, and counterparty credit risk.

Resolution interface: the SRB and SRM

The SSM is the supervisor; the Single Resolution Board (SRB), based in Brussels and established by the SRM Regulation (EU) 806/2014, is the resolution authority. The two bodies have a clean institutional split: the ECB supervises the bank while it is a going concern; the SRB takes over when the bank is no longer viable.

The transition trigger is the failing or likely to fail (FOLTF) determination, which is made by the ECB as supervisor (in close cooperation with the SRB). Once an SI is declared FOLTF, the SRB has 24 hours to decide whether to resolve the bank or let it enter normal insolvency. If resolution is chosen, the SRB uses the resolution tools set out in the Bank Recovery and Resolution Directive (BRRD): bail-in of shareholders and unsecured creditors, sale of business, bridge institution, asset separation. The Single Resolution Fund, financed by ex-ante contributions from all participating banks, provides backstop funding.

The first major test of the architecture was the resolution of Banco Popular Español in June 2017: the ECB declared FOLTF on 6 June; the SRB resolved the bank overnight, selling it to Santander for €1 with bail-in of shareholders and AT1/T2 holders. The architecture worked: no depositor lost a euro, no taxpayer contributed, and the resolution was complete within hours. Subsequent cases (the 2017 winding-up of Banca Popolare di Vicenza and Veneto Banca, the 2021 resolution of Sberbank Europe, the 2023 resolution of Banca Carige's run-off) have confirmed the model.

The third pillar that does not yet exist: EDIS

The Banking Union was designed as a three-legged stool: single supervisor (SSM, done), single resolution (SRM, done), and a European Deposit Insurance Scheme (EDIS). The European Commission tabled an EDIS proposal in 2015 and a revised proposal in 2017, but the file remains blocked at Council level. The political dispute is the familiar north-south one: Germany and the Netherlands argue that legacy non-performing loans on Italian, Spanish, and Greek bank balance sheets must be reduced before deposit risk can be mutualised; Italy and France argue that mutualisation is the precondition for further risk reduction. EDIS has been "imminent" for ten years and remains absent. National deposit guarantee schemes, harmonised by the Deposit Guarantee Schemes Directive, continue to bear the loss in any insured-depositor payout.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The SI threshold is the most consequential line in EU banking

A bank just under €30 billion in assets is supervised by its national authority. A bank just over is supervised by a multi-national JST in Frankfurt with a different supervisory culture, a different examination style, a different documentation expectation, and a different appetite for confrontation. Banks managing growth often deliberately stay just under the threshold; banks just over often complain that their compliance costs jumped by an order of magnitude on the crossing. The classification is not just administrative; it changes how the bank is run.

SSM supervision is more formal and more process-driven than national supervision was

National supervisors before 2014 ran on personal relationships and informal channels. The SSM has codified everything: SREP methodology, JST mandates, on-site inspection manuals, model-investigation procedures. The upside is consistency and predictability. The downside is that supervisory dialogue is slower, more documentary, and less responsive to firm-specific nuance. Practitioners who came up in the national supervisory culture often complain that the SSM has lost something valuable; practitioners who came up in the SSM era treat the formalism as a feature.

Watch out P2R and P2G are not interchangeable, and the difference matters for the MDA. P2R is binding and sits below the combined buffer requirement; breaching P2G does not trigger automatic distribution restrictions, but breaching the combined buffer requirement (which sits between P2R and P2G in the stack) does. When briefing a board on capital headroom, always show the four layers in the correct order and label which trigger the MDA and which do not. The most common reporting error is to lump P2R and P2G into a single "Pillar 2" number; that number is meaningless for distribution-restriction analysis.

The Chinese wall is real, but the political pressure is constant

The ECB is barred by law from intervening in monetary policy for supervisory reasons or in supervision for monetary-policy reasons. In practice the wall holds for individual decisions but cannot hold for the aggregate stance: when monetary policy tightens sharply, banks' net interest income, sovereign-bond holdings, and asset-quality position all change together, and the supervisor has to react to the consequences. The 2022 to 2024 ECB rate-hiking cycle and the parallel rise in SREP attention to IRRBB and unrealised AFS losses is the clearest example. The boundary is contested in academic and political commentary; in operational practice, the wall does what it is designed to do.

The SREP letter is the bank's regulatory year in one document

Everything the bank cares about regulatory-wise lands in the December SREP letter: the P2R for next year, the P2G, the combined buffer requirement, the SREP score, any required management actions (improve governance, replace this director, fix this risk model), any on-site inspection findings to be remediated, any restrictions on distributions or model use. The annual rhythm of an EU bank's regulatory calendar is built around this letter: ICAAP submission in April, JST review meetings through the summer, SREP draft in October, final decision in December, board ratification and embedding in January.

Non-euro EU banks are a different supervisory species

Banks in Sweden, Denmark, Poland, the Czech Republic, Hungary, and Romania face a single rulebook (CRR, CRD) but local supervisors (Finansinspektionen, Finanstilsynet, KNF, ČNB, MNB, BNR). The supervisory tone, the model-approval pace, and the SREP intensity vary substantially across these jurisdictions. Comparing a Polish bank's P2R with a German bank's P2R is not a like-for-like comparison; the underlying methodologies are the same on paper but the supervisory practice is different. Post-Brexit, the UK is outside this entirely; the PRA and BoE run a parallel system covered in lectures 17 to 19.
