CRR: the Capital Requirements Regulation

The Capital Requirements Regulation is the European Union's binding, directly applicable text that turns the Basel accords into law. It is the first half of the CRR/CRD package, the legally enforceable Pillar 1 spine of EU banking prudential rules, and the Single Rulebook's most important component. Where Basel gives the world a recommendation, the CRR gives an EU bank a court-enforceable obligation.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The Capital Requirements Regulation (CRR) is Regulation (EU) No 575/2013, adopted on 26 June 2013, in force since 17 July 2013, and applied to credit institutions and investment firms from 1 January 2014. It is the EU's direct-effect implementation of Basel III. Together with the Capital Requirements Directive (CRD IV, Directive 2013/36/EU, the subject of lecture 16), it forms the CRR/CRD package, the legal foundation of the EU's Single Rulebook for banks. The CRR contains the Pillar 1 minimum capital requirements (credit, market, operational, settlement, and counterparty risk), the leverage ratio, the liquidity standards (LCR and NSFR), the large-exposures regime, and the Pillar 3 disclosure rules. It was substantially amended by CRR2 (Regulation (EU) 2019/876), which implemented FRTB partially, TLAC, the binding leverage ratio, and the NSFR; and again by CRR3 (Regulation (EU) 2024/1623), which implements the Basel 3.1 finalisation package and applies from 1 January 2025 in most respects.

The CRR applies to all EU credit institutions and, with carve-outs, to investment firms (most of which moved to the separate Investment Firm Regulation, IFR, in 2021). It is a Regulation in the formal EU sense: a legal act adopted by the European Parliament and the Council that is binding in its entirety and directly applicable in every member state, without any need for national transposition. This is the crucial institutional difference from its predecessor, the Banking Consolidation Directive of 2006: under a directive, each member state writes its own implementing rules and inserts national choices; under a regulation, the text is the same across all 27 jurisdictions. The Single Rulebook was the explicit policy goal, and the CRR is its core.

Feynman test Basel III is a recipe written by central bankers at a dinner in Switzerland. The CRR is what happens when 27 governments agree to print the recipe in their own legally binding cookbook, but rather than each printing their own slightly different translation, they agree to use one single text in one single book that every kitchen in the union must follow word for word. The CRR is that single text. CRR2 was the first big revision in 2019 to add the FRTB starter, the TLAC main course, and the NSFR side dish. CRR3 is the 2024 revision that finally bakes in everything Basel finalised in 2017, including the famous 72.5 per cent output floor that stops a bank's own internal models from giving it too much of a discount. A Spanish bank, a German bank, and a Dutch bank now read the same article number, with the same footnotes, with the same EBA technical standards attached underneath.

It illustrates the level of detail in the CRR but does not lay out the regulation's structure, history, or amendment cycle. This lecture adds: the institutional difference between a regulation and a directive and why it matters for the Single Rulebook; the architecture of the CRR's 11 Parts; the CRR vs CRD division of labour; the amendment history through CRR2 and CRR3; the political carve-outs (SME supporting factor, infrastructure factor, the green and social supporting factors under CRR3); the relationship to EBA's regulatory technical standards (RTS) and implementing technical standards (ITS); the position of the output floor and its phase-in; and the post-Brexit divergence with the UK CRR. The lecture is the regulatory-architecture lens; the source wiki is one slice of the IRB detail underneath.
Step 2

Why this matters

Before the CRR, EU banking law lived in a series of directives, of which the most recent was the recast Banking Consolidation Directive (Directive 2006/48/EC) and the Capital Adequacy Directive (Directive 2006/49/EC). Directives are binding as to the result to be achieved but leave the form and method to the member states; each state must transpose the directive into its own law, and the resulting national rulebook can differ in detail. The 2006 directives contained more than 150 national discretions: items where a member state could choose between options, exempt certain firms, or set its own calibration. The result was a single market for banking that was, in practice, 27 different rulebooks, with predictable consequences for competitive arbitrage and supervisory consistency.

The 2009 De Larosière report, commissioned by the European Commission after the financial crisis, recommended a Single Rulebook for the EU. The political logic was straightforward. If the EU was to have a banking union with a single supervisor (the ECB's Single Supervisory Mechanism, lecture 14), it needed a single body of law for that supervisor to enforce. The 2013 CRR/CRD package was the legislative answer. The mandatory and harmonised parts of Basel III, where uniform application matters most, went into the CRR. The parts requiring supervisory judgement or national macroprudential discretion went into the CRD. The leverage-ratio formula is in the CRR because everyone must compute it the same way; the countercyclical capital buffer is in the CRD because each national authority sets its own rate.

For a bank operating in the EU, the practical consequence is that the CRR is the text the supervisor will cite when challenging a model, a capital deduction, or a large exposure. EBA-published Q&As, technical standards, and guidelines elaborate the CRR but cannot contradict it. National rulebooks (the German KWG, the French Code monétaire et financier, the Spanish Ley de Ordenación de Crédito) implement the CRD but cannot deviate from the CRR. For a UK-trained risk professional moving to a continental bank, the CRR is the legal text to read; for a continental risk professional comparing notes with a UK counterpart, the post-Brexit divergence between the UK's CRR (now retained as part of the PRA Rulebook, with substantial near-final modifications) and the EU's CRR3 is the reference point.

For an actuary in credit risk modelling, three observations follow. First, the CRR is exhaustively prescriptive on IRB methodology: estimation requirements, observation periods, margins of conservatism, and downturn calibration are all set out in articles, not in guidance. Second, the EBA's regulatory technical standards (RTS), made under specific CRR mandates, are themselves legally binding once adopted by the Commission as Delegated Regulations. Third, the CRR3 introduction of the output floor permanently caps the benefit a bank can obtain from internal models, fundamentally changing the economics of A-IRB investment for many portfolios.

Step 3

The mental model

The CRR is best pictured as a building with 11 floors, each numbered as a Part. Capital walks in at the bottom (Part 2, Own Funds), gets measured against requirements on each floor above it (credit, market, operational, large exposures, liquidity, leverage), and is finally projected outward through the disclosure floor at the top (Part 8). Underneath, in the basement, sit the EBA technical standards that fill in the implementation detail of every mandate the legislators delegated.

Three flows of authority cross this structure. The CRR itself flows top-down from the EU legislators (Parliament and Council, on a Commission proposal) to all credit institutions in the union, without national filter. The EBA technical standards flow bottom-up: drafted by the EBA, submitted to the Commission, adopted as Delegated or Implementing Regulations. And the CRD flows sideways from the EU legislators to the member states, who transpose it into national law, where it is then enforced by the national competent authority or, for significant institutions, the ECB.

Step 4

The detail

Legislative process and adoption

The CRR was proposed by the European Commission in July 2011 as part of the CRD IV package, the EU's response to the 2010 Basel III standards. The proposal was subject to the ordinary legislative procedure, also known as co-decision: the European Parliament and the Council of the EU each had to agree on a text, and final agreement was reached through trilogue negotiations between Parliament, Council, and Commission. The package was adopted on 26 June 2013 and published in the Official Journal on 27 June 2013, entering into force twenty days later on 17 July 2013. The substantive obligations applied from 1 January 2014, with a number of phase-in arrangements for capital deductions, the leverage ratio reporting, and the LCR.

The choice of a Regulation over a Directive for the Pillar 1 rules was politically contested at the time. France and Germany pushed for a Regulation because they wanted maximum harmonisation; the UK was more sceptical because it preferred the flexibility to set tougher national requirements (the so-called "gold-plating" debate). The compromise was that anything strictly quantitative (capital definitions, RWA formulae, LCR calculation) went into the CRR with very few national choices; anything macroprudential or supervisory (buffer rates, Pillar 2, governance) went into the CRD with explicit national discretion.

Structure of the CRR: 11 Parts

The CRR is organised into 11 Parts, themselves divided into Titles, Chapters, Sections, and Articles. The numbering convention runs Part 1 Article 1 through to Part 11 Article 521 in the original text, expanded by CRR2 and CRR3 to over 540 articles plus numerous "bis" insertions (Article 92a, 92b, and so on).

Part 1 General provisions (Articles 1 to 24)
Subject matter, scope, definitions, level of application (solo, sub-consolidated, consolidated), and waivers. Establishes which entities are subject to the CRR: credit institutions and, since 2021, only those investment firms above the IFR thresholds. The definitions article (Article 4) runs to over 140 defined terms and is the most-cited single article in the regulation.
Part 2 Own funds (Articles 25 to 91)
The definitive legal definition of bank capital. CET1 instruments and criteria, AT1 instruments (perpetual, principal-loss-absorbing, fully discretionary coupons), T2 instruments (subordinated debt with minimum five-year maturity), regulatory adjustments and deductions, the treatment of minority interests, and the thresholds for deferred-tax-asset and significant-investment deductions. Almost every line of Part 2 traces directly to the Basel III definition of capital.
Part 3 Capital requirements (Articles 92 to 386)
The Pillar 1 capital requirements. Title I sets the minimum ratios (4.5 per cent CET1, 6 per cent T1, 8 per cent total). Title II is credit risk, including the standardised approach, the IRB approach (F-IRB and A-IRB), credit risk mitigation, and securitisation. Title III is operational risk. Title IV is market risk. Title V is settlement risk. Title VI is credit valuation adjustment (CVA) risk. By far the longest Part of the CRR.
Part 4 Large exposures (Articles 387 to 403)
Limits a bank's exposure to a single counterparty or group of connected counterparties to 25 per cent of Tier 1 capital. Reduced to 15 per cent for G-SIB exposures to other G-SIBs (introduced in CRR2). Defines exposure measurement, exemptions, and reporting. The large-exposures regime is the EU's translation of BCBS d283 (2014) on the supervisory framework for measuring and controlling large exposures.
Part 5 Securitisation transferred risk (Articles 404 to 410, repealed)
Originally on retention requirements for securitisations. Largely repealed by the 2017 EU Securitisation Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2017/2402) and the related CRR amendment, which moved retention rules into the standalone Securitisation Regulation and the calibration rules into Part 3 Title II Chapter 5.
Part 6 Liquidity (Articles 411 to 428, then 428a onwards for NSFR)
Originally the liquidity Part with high-level LCR provisions. The detailed LCR specification is in Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2015/61, adopted October 2014, applied from 1 October 2015. CRR2 inserted Title IV (Articles 428a to 428az) for the NSFR, with a binding 100 per cent ratio applied from 28 June 2021.
Part 7 Leverage (Articles 429 to 430)
The leverage ratio: Tier 1 capital divided by total exposure measure, with a 3 per cent minimum. Reporting and disclosure obligations existed from 2014, but the requirement became a binding Pillar 1 minimum from 28 June 2021 under CRR2. CRR3 adds the G-SIB leverage buffer (50 per cent of the risk-weighted G-SIB buffer) as a separate Part 7 sub-requirement.
Part 7A Reporting (Articles 430 to 430c, inserted by CRR2)
Supervisory reporting obligations. Detailed templates are in EBA implementing technical standards adopted as Commission Implementing Regulations: COREP (capital reporting), FINREP (financial reporting), LCR reporting, NSFR reporting, large-exposures reporting. The reporting framework is the operational interface between every EU bank and its supervisor.
Part 8 Disclosure (Articles 431 to 455)
The Pillar 3 disclosure regime. Standardised qualitative and quantitative templates published semi-annually by large institutions and annually by smaller ones. Detailed templates are in EBA ITS. CRR3 added new templates for ESG risks, the output floor, and the SA-CR mortgage breakdown.
Part 9 Delegated acts (Articles 456 to 464)
The legal basis for the Commission to adopt Delegated Regulations and Implementing Regulations elaborating the CRR, on the basis of draft technical standards prepared by the EBA. Every "the EBA shall develop draft regulatory technical standards" sentence in the body of the CRR cross-references back to Part 9.
Part 10 Transitional provisions (Articles 465 to 519)
Phase-in arrangements. The original 2013 phase-ins (CET1 deductions, capital conservation buffer, leverage ratio reporting) are now largely spent, but CRR3 has reset the meter with new transitionals: a six-year phase-in of the output floor (50 per cent in 2025 rising to 72.5 per cent by 2030), transitional risk weights for unrated corporates, equity exposures, and certain residential mortgage exposures.
Part 11 Final provisions (Articles 520 to 521)
Review clauses (the Commission is obliged to report periodically to the legislators on certain aspects of the regulation), repeals (the old 2006 Banking Consolidation Directive and Capital Adequacy Directive were repealed by the CRD IV package, not by the CRR itself), entry into force, and dates of application.

CRR versus CRD: the division of labour

The CRR and CRD IV (Directive 2013/36/EU) together make up the "CRR/CRD package". A useful rule of thumb is that anything that needs to be exactly the same across all member states is in the CRR, and anything that requires national judgement or transposition into a national supervisory regime is in the CRD. Concretely:

In the CRR (directly applicable)
Definition of own funds; risk-weighted asset calculation; large-exposures limits; LCR and NSFR; leverage ratio formula; the Pillar 3 disclosure templates. The numerical, formulaic, harmonised core.
In the CRD (national transposition)
Authorisation of credit institutions; passporting; the Pillar 2 supervisory review process (SREP); the macroprudential capital buffers (capital conservation, countercyclical, G-SII, O-SII, systemic risk); governance and remuneration rules; sanctions and enforcement powers. The discretionary, judgement-based, supervisory part.

The leverage ratio is in the CRR because the formula is the same everywhere. The countercyclical capital buffer is in the CRD because each national designated authority sets its own rate based on its national credit cycle. The Pillar 1 minimum is in the CRR because it is a one-size-fits-all floor; the Pillar 2 add-on is in the CRD because the supervisor sets it bank by bank.

Own funds (Part 2): the legal definition of capital

The Part 2 architecture follows Basel III. Capital is split into three tiers in descending quality. Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) consists of ordinary shares, share premium, retained earnings, other comprehensive income reserves, and qualifying minority interests, subject to a long list of regulatory adjustments and deductions (goodwill, intangibles, deferred tax assets relying on future profitability, significant investments in financial sector entities, and so on). Additional Tier 1 (AT1) consists of perpetual instruments that absorb losses on a going-concern basis through a trigger-based principal write-down or conversion to CET1, with fully discretionary coupons that the issuer can cancel without triggering default. Tier 2 (T2) consists of subordinated debt instruments with a minimum original maturity of five years, amortised straight-line in the final five years before maturity.

Each tier has detailed eligibility criteria, set out in Articles 28 to 31 (CET1), 52 (AT1), and 63 (T2). The criteria are restrictive: a feature that would survive an audit under IFRS as "equity" can still fail CRR's CET1 criteria, leading to the unusual situation of an instrument that is equity for accounting but not for regulatory capital. Article 26(3) requires the competent authority's prior permission before any new CET1 instrument is recognised, and the EBA maintains a public list of approved CET1 instruments per jurisdiction.

The deductions architecture is dense. Most deductions come straight off CET1 (full deduction approach). A few items (significant investments in unconsolidated financial sector entities, mortgage servicing rights, deferred tax assets from temporary differences) are subject to a 17.65 per cent threshold under Article 48: the aggregate amount of these three items in excess of 17.65 per cent of CET1 is deducted from CET1, while the amount below the threshold is risk-weighted at 250 per cent. This threshold mechanism is one of the most-cited Basel III calibrations in EU supervisory dialogue.

Credit risk (Part 3, Title II): the bulk of Pillar 1

Title II of Part 3 (Articles 107 to 311) sets out the credit-risk capital framework. Banks can choose between the Standardised Approach (SA-CR), the Foundation IRB Approach (F-IRB), and the Advanced IRB Approach (A-IRB), subject to supervisory permission. The IRB chapters contain the prescriptive estimation requirements for PD, LGD, and the credit conversion factor (CCF) for EAD; Article 179 sets the overall requirements for estimation, including the use of historical data, the requirement for empirical evidence rather than pure judgement, and the margin-of-conservatism principle ("the less data an institution has, the more conservative it shall be"). Article 182 contains the requirements for own-conversion-factor estimates, including the five-year minimum observation period for corporates and central governments (rising to seven years) and the downturn-CCF requirement.

Article 179 paragraph 1(f) is the legal basis for the margin of conservatism, an exam favourite: "an institution shall add to its estimates a margin of conservatism that is related to the expected range of estimation errors. Where methods and data are considered to be less satisfactory, the expected range of errors is larger, the margin of conservatism shall be larger." The EBA has elaborated this in its 2016 IRB guidelines (EBA/GL/2017/16) and in the RTS on assessment methodology for the IRB approach (Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2022/439), which is itself excerpted in the source wiki for this lecture.

CRR3 makes substantial changes to Title II. The Standardised Approach for credit risk is rewritten with LTV-sensitive risk weights for residential and commercial real estate (replacing the flat 35 per cent and 50 per cent), more granular treatment of corporate exposures with separate buckets for unrated and investment-grade corporates, and a new "transactor" treatment for revolving retail. The A-IRB approach is constrained: it is no longer available for exposures to large corporates (turnover above 500 million euro) and to financial institutions, which must use F-IRB; equity exposures, previously allowed under IRB, must now be under the standardised approach. PD and LGD input floors are tightened.

Market risk (Part 3, Title IV): the FRTB transition

Title IV (Articles 325 to 377 in the consolidated text) covers market risk capital. The original CRR replicated the 2006 directives' standardised approach and the internal-models approach (IMA) for general and specific risk on positions held in the trading book. CRR2 introduced an FRTB-aligned reporting requirement (the "alternative standardised approach" and "alternative internal model approach", Articles 325a to 325az) from 2021, but only for reporting; the binding capital charge remained under the legacy regime. CRR3 makes FRTB binding: from 1 January 2025 (with limited transitional relief), the alternative standardised approach (SBM, sensitivities-based method) and the alternative IMA are the binding capital framework for market risk, with the legacy standardised and IMA approaches retired. Lecture 6 covers FRTB in detail.

Operational risk (Part 3, Title III): from three approaches to one

Title III (Articles 312 to 324) covers operational risk capital. The original CRR allowed three approaches: the Basic Indicator Approach (BIA, a flat 15 per cent of average gross income), the Standardised Approach (SA, gross income split by business line and weighted), and the Advanced Measurement Approach (AMA, internal loss-distribution models). CRR3 replaces all three with the single Standardised Measurement Approach (SMA), the BCBS-finalised single methodology that combines a Business Indicator (a refined measure of bank size) with an Internal Loss Multiplier reflecting the bank's own operational loss history. The SMA applies from 1 January 2025. Banks with assets below 1 billion euro can opt out of the loss multiplier, applying SMA with an ILM of 1.

CVA risk (Part 3, Title VI)

The CVA capital charge, separate from the counterparty-credit-risk charge inside the credit-risk title, covers the risk of mark-to-market losses on OTC derivative counterparties from deterioration in their credit spreads. The original CRR allowed a standardised and an advanced (internal-models) method. CRR3 replaces these with BA-CVA (basic approach) and SA-CVA (standardised approach), aligned with BCBS d507 (2020). The advanced internal-models option is removed.

Large exposures (Part 4)

The headline 25 per cent of Tier 1 limit on a single counterparty (or group of connected counterparties) is in Article 395. The 15 per cent limit on G-SIB exposures to other G-SIBs was introduced by CRR2 to give effect to BCBS d283 (2014). The framework includes detailed rules on connection (Article 4(1)(39) defines "group of connected clients"), exposure measurement, exemptions (sovereign exposures, certain covered bonds, intragroup exposures with prior permission), and reporting.

Liquidity and the LCR Delegated Regulation

The original CRR (Article 412) set the principle that institutions must hold liquid assets sufficient to cover net liquidity outflows over a 30-day stress period, with details to be specified in a Commission Delegated Regulation. That Delegated Regulation is Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2015/61, adopted 10 October 2014, applied from 1 October 2015 with a 60 per cent minimum rising 10 percentage points each year to 100 per cent by 1 January 2018. The LCR Delegated Act is the practical specification: definitions of high-quality liquid assets (HQLA), run-off rates by deposit category, inflow caps, and the consolidated-group treatment.

CRR2 added the NSFR (Articles 428a to 428az) with a binding 100 per cent ratio from 28 June 2021. The NSFR requires that available stable funding (ASF) exceeds required stable funding (RSF) over a one-year horizon, with ASF and RSF factors prescribed in the regulation by counterparty type, instrument type, and residual maturity.

Leverage ratio (Part 7)

The leverage ratio (Article 429) is Tier 1 capital divided by the total exposure measure, which is broadly the accounting balance sheet plus derivative exposures, securities financing transactions, and off-balance-sheet items (subject to CCFs). The 3 per cent minimum became binding from 28 June 2021 under CRR2. CRR3 adds, for G-SIIs, a leverage ratio buffer equal to 50 per cent of the bank's risk-weighted G-SII buffer rate, applied from 1 January 2023 already under CRR2 transitional and codified under CRR3.

Pillar 3 disclosure (Part 8)

Part 8 sets the framework; the detailed templates are in EBA implementing technical standards. The disclosure regime is proportional: large institutions disclose semi-annually with full template detail, while small and non-complex institutions disclose annually with reduced templates. CRR3 adds ESG-risk disclosure templates (Pillar 3 ESG), output-floor templates showing the bank's RWA before and after the floor, and granular SA-CR breakdowns. The EBA maintains an interactive Single Rulebook portal cross-referencing CRR articles, RTS, ITS, and Q&As.

CRR2 (Regulation (EU) 2019/876): the first major amendment

CRR2 was adopted on 20 May 2019, entered into force on 27 June 2019, and applied from 28 June 2021 in most parts. It implements the post-Basel III package elements agreed between 2013 and 2017 (and not yet covered by the original CRR). Key changes:

FRTB reporting
Introduction of the alternative standardised approach (SBM) and alternative IMA in Articles 325a to 325az, on a reporting-only basis from 2021; binding application deferred and eventually moved to CRR3.
Binding NSFR
A 100 per cent NSFR minimum from 28 June 2021, with reduced ASF/RSF factors for certain low-risk activities (covered bond cover pools, short-term repos backed by HQLA) reflecting EU specificities.
Binding leverage ratio
The 3 per cent leverage ratio became a Pillar 1 minimum from 28 June 2021. The G-SII leverage buffer was added with phased application.
TLAC integration
EU implementation of the FSB TLAC standard for G-SIIs through Article 92a (own funds plus eligible liabilities ratio of 18 per cent RWA from 2022) and the wider MREL framework in the parallel BRRD revision (BRRD2, Directive (EU) 2019/879).
SME and infrastructure supporting factors
CRR2 retained and expanded the SME supporting factor (a 0.7619 multiplier on RWAs for SME exposures up to 2.5 million euro, with a 0.85 factor for the portion above) and introduced an infrastructure supporting factor (a 0.75 multiplier for qualifying infrastructure project finance exposures).
Proportionality reforms
A category of "small and non-complex institutions" (SNCIs) with reduced disclosure, reporting, and remuneration requirements. Threshold: balance sheet below 5 billion euro and meeting further criteria.

CRR3 (Regulation (EU) 2024/1623): the Basel 3.1 finalisation package

CRR3 was adopted on 31 May 2024 and published in the Official Journal on 19 June 2024. It applies from 1 January 2025 in most parts, with key transitional arrangements running to 2032. CRR3 implements the BCBS d424 (2017) finalised Basel III standards, with a number of EU-specific transitionals and carve-outs. The headline elements:

Output floor
The bank's total RWAs (under IRB or internal-model approaches for market and CVA risk) cannot fall below 72.5 per cent of the RWAs that would have been produced under the standardised approaches. Phased in: 50 per cent in 2025, 55 per cent in 2026, 60 per cent in 2027, 65 per cent in 2028, 70 per cent in 2029, 72.5 per cent from 2030. The EU output floor is calculated at the consolidated level, with a transitional option for the calculation at solo level for certain subsidiaries.
Revised SA-CR
LTV-sensitive mortgages (residential and commercial), an "investment grade" sub-category for unrated corporates, a "transactor" treatment for revolving retail, separate buckets for project finance, object finance, and commodities finance, and tightened SME treatment. Specialised lending sub-classes (PF, OF, CF) get their own slotting and IRB approaches.
Constrained A-IRB
A-IRB is no longer available for large corporates (turnover above 500 million euro) or financial institution exposures. Equity exposures move entirely to SA. PD input floors raised; LGD input floors of 5 per cent for unsecured retail and 25 per cent for secured retail; specific LGD floors for collateral types.
SMA for operational risk
Single Standardised Measurement Approach replaces BIA, SA, and AMA. Business Indicator times marginal coefficient, modified by Internal Loss Multiplier (ILM) for banks with assets above 1 billion euro. AMA fully retired.
CVA framework
BA-CVA and SA-CVA replace the older standardised and advanced methods. Internal-models option for CVA is removed. The new framework aligns with FRTB sensitivities methodology on the SA-CVA side.
Full FRTB
The alternative standardised approach (SBM) and alternative IMA introduced by CRR2 for reporting now become the binding market-risk capital framework. Standardised default risk charge, residual risk add-on, and curvature and default risk in IMA.
EU-specific carve-outs
SME supporting factor retained alongside the revised SA-CR. Infrastructure supporting factor retained. New "green and social supporting factor" debate ongoing (not yet adopted at CRR3 level but the Commission is mandated to report). Transitional risk weights for unrated corporates with credit assessment by an "internal credit assessment" (Article 465 transitional). Transitional 1.4 multiplier (instead of 1.6) for low-risk corporate exposures under SA-CR.
ESG and crypto-asset rules
CRR3 inserts a definition framework for environmental, social, and governance risks and requires their integration into ICAAP and Pillar 2; capital requirements remain risk-based but disclosure is enhanced. A separate, transitional crypto-asset prudential treatment is included pending finalisation of a permanent regime.

EBA technical standards and the Single Rulebook

The CRR contains over 100 mandates for the EBA to develop draft technical standards. Once adopted by the Commission as Delegated or Implementing Regulations, these are themselves directly applicable. The flagship example for credit risk modelling is Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2022/439, the RTS on assessment methodology for the IRB approach (cited in the source wiki for this lecture). It specifies how competent authorities assess a bank's compliance with the IRB minimum requirements: data quality, observation periods, margin of conservatism, downturn calibration, and model change processes.

The EBA Single Rulebook portal (interactive online) is the practical entry point. It cross-references each CRR article with the relevant RTS, ITS, Guidelines, and Q&As. A bank's first-line model documentation typically structures its compliance argument by walking the Single Rulebook hierarchy: CRR article, then RTS, then EBA Guidelines (under "comply or explain"), then EBA Q&A.

Worked example: own-funds calculation for a small EU bank

Consider a small EU credit institution at 31 December 2024 with the following position:

  • Ordinary share capital: 300 million euro.
  • Share premium: 50 million euro.
  • Retained earnings (audited): 220 million euro.
  • Other comprehensive income reserves: 30 million euro.
  • Goodwill on a 2018 acquisition: 40 million euro.
  • Other intangible assets (software capitalised): 25 million euro.
  • Deferred tax asset from temporary differences: 35 million euro.
  • AT1 perpetual notes outstanding: 80 million euro.
  • T2 subordinated bond (residual maturity 6 years): 120 million euro.
  • Total RWAs (credit, market, operational): 4,800 million euro.

The CET1 calculation (Articles 26, 36 to 48):

CET1 instruments (Article 26)
  Share capital                          300
  Share premium                           50
  Retained earnings                      220
  OCI reserves                            30
                                       -----
  Sub-total CET1 instruments             600

Deductions (Articles 36 to 48)
  Goodwill (Article 36(1)(b))            (40)
  Other intangibles (Article 36(1)(b))   (25)
  DTA from temp differences:
    35 vs 17.65% of 600 - 65 = 109 -> below threshold,
    no deduction, instead risk-weighted at 250%
                                       -----
  CET1 capital                           535

AT1 capital is 80 million euro (assuming the notes meet all Article 52 criteria including a permanent write-down or conversion trigger at 5.125 per cent CET1 and fully discretionary coupons). Tier 1 is therefore 615 million euro. T2 capital is 120 million euro (no amortisation since residual maturity exceeds five years). Total own funds is 735 million euro.

The CRR Article 92 ratios:

CET1 ratio    = 535 / 4,800 = 11.15%  (minimum 4.5%, comfortably above)
T1 ratio      = 615 / 4,800 = 12.81%  (minimum 6.0%)
Total ratio   = 735 / 4,800 = 15.31%  (minimum 8.0%)

The bank satisfies all Pillar 1 minimum ratios under the CRR. Pillar 2 add-ons (set by the competent authority under CRD Article 104) and the combined buffer requirement (capital conservation buffer, countercyclical buffer, any O-SII buffer, all under the CRD) would be tested separately and require CET1 capital to absorb. Under CRR3, the same bank would in addition need to verify that its total RWAs (if IRB) do not fall below 72.5 per cent of the SA-equivalent RWAs (output floor, phased in to 50 per cent in 2025).

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Read the CRR article number, not the textbook chapter

Every prudential argument inside an EU bank ultimately comes back to a specific CRR article. Risk teams and supervisors converse in article numbers: "Article 178 default definition", "Article 179(1)(f) MoC", "Article 395 large-exposures limit", "Article 92 own-funds ratios", "Article 429 leverage". Internalise the numbering of the most-cited articles, because that is the language regulators and second-line write in. The EBA Single Rulebook portal is the right reference tool; a textbook is the wrong one.

The Single Rulebook is hierarchical

Above any EBA RTS sits the CRR itself; an RTS cannot contradict the level-1 text. Above any EBA Guideline sits the relevant RTS and the CRR; a Guideline cannot impose a requirement not already in the level-1 or level-2 framework. Above any EBA Q&A sits the Guideline. When two interpretations clash, the higher level always wins. This matters because supervisory dialogue often involves a bank citing a permissive Q&A and a supervisor citing a stricter Guideline; the resolution is the relevant level-1 article and the legislative intent behind it.

The output floor changes A-IRB economics permanently

For portfolios where a bank's A-IRB RWAs are below 72.5 per cent of the standardised equivalent (low-default portfolios like sovereigns, financial institutions, and high-quality residential mortgages), CRR3's output floor means that incremental A-IRB modelling effort has no capital benefit once the floor binds. This is the most significant strategic consequence of CRR3 for credit-risk modelling teams. Banks are now triaging which IRB models to maintain, which to retire to SA, and where to focus modelling investment on portfolios where the floor will not bind (corporates and SMEs, where the SA risk weights are typically high enough that IRB still delivers below the floor).

Watch out The CRR is the law. Basel III is not. A model that meets every BCBS standard but fails Article 179(1)(f) on margin of conservatism (because the EBA Guidelines and RTS on IRB elaborate MoC more prescriptively than the BCBS text does) is non-compliant in the EU. Conversely, a model that meets every CRR article but takes a stricter approach than Basel on, say, downturn LGD is fully compliant, because the CRR is the binding text. Never argue "we follow Basel" in an EU supervisory dialogue; always argue "we follow the CRR".

The CRR's prescription is intentional

Critics call the CRR overly prescriptive, with over 1,000 pages of level-1 text and several thousand more in level-2 and level-3 measures. The political answer is that the prescription is the point: the Single Rulebook only works if there is a single text. Less prescription would mean more national variation, which is what the CRR was created to eliminate. The cost is that the CRR is difficult to read end-to-end; it is designed to be read article by article.

UK divergence is now a strategic question

Since Brexit, the UK's CRR (retained in UK law as the "UK CRR" and substantially modified by the PRA's near-final Basel 3.1 rules under SS3-24 and SS4-24) is no longer identical to the EU CRR. The principal divergences in the near-final UK rules concern the treatment of unrated corporates, the SME supporting factor (largely retained in the EU, removed in the UK), the output floor calibration mechanics, and the treatment of certain securitisations. For a UK or Irish bank operating in both jurisdictions, the compliance burden is now genuinely two regimes, not one. This divergence is the subject of lecture 20.

Investment firms are mostly outside the CRR now

Until 2021, the CRR applied uniformly to credit institutions and investment firms. The 2019 Investment Firm Regulation (IFR, Regulation (EU) 2019/2033) and Investment Firm Directive (IFD, Directive (EU) 2019/2034) created a separate, lighter prudential regime for most investment firms based on activity-based K-factors. Only the largest "Class 1" investment firms (those above 30 billion euro of consolidated assets and conducting bank-like activities) remain under the CRR; they are now re-classified as credit institutions. The CRR's scope today is, in practice, "credit institutions plus the very largest investment firms".
