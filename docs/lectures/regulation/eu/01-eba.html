EBA: the European Banking Authority

The European Banking Authority is the EU agency that turns the Capital Requirements Regulation and Directive from a body of policy into an operational rulebook. It does not supervise banks. It writes the technical standards that supervisors and banks have to follow, maintains the Single Rulebook and its Q&A tool, and runs the biennial EU-wide stress test. For an actuary at any EU or EU-subsidiary bank, the EBA Single Rulebook is the daily reference document, sitting one layer below the CRR and one layer above local supervisory expectations.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The European Banking Authority (EBA) is the EU agency responsible for prudential rule-making and supervisory convergence in banking. It was created on 1 January 2011 by Regulation (EU) No 1093/2010 as one of three European Supervisory Authorities (ESAs), the others being ESMA for securities markets and EIOPA for insurance and occupational pensions. Together with the European Systemic Risk Board (ESRB), national competent authorities, and (from 2014) the Single Supervisory Mechanism, the EBA forms part of the European System of Financial Supervision (ESFS). The EBA is based in Paris, having relocated from London on 3 June 2019 as a consequence of the United Kingdom's withdrawal from the EU.

The EBA's central job is to draft the technical detail that fills in the Capital Requirements Regulation (CRR) and Capital Requirements Directive (CRD), plus the Bank Recovery and Resolution Directive (BRRD) and several other Level 1 acts. It issues three categories of binding or quasi-binding output: Regulatory Technical Standards (RTS), which the European Commission adopts as Delegated Regulations and which have direct legal force across the Union; Implementing Technical Standards (ITS), adopted by the Commission as Implementing Regulations and similarly binding; and Guidelines and Recommendations, which operate on a comply-or-explain basis. The EBA also publishes Opinions, technical advice to the Commission, and the Single Rulebook Q&A, which clarifies the application of the rules in practice. It runs the biennial EU-wide stress test, publishes the quarterly Risk Dashboard, and runs the annual Transparency Exercise. It does not supervise individual banks; that work is shared between the SSM for euro-area significant institutions and national competent authorities for everyone else.

Feynman test The EU Parliament and Council passed a regulation called the CRR that says, in effect, "banks must calculate capital using PD, LGD, and EAD". That instruction is a one-paragraph policy. It is not yet usable by a bank or a supervisor. Somebody has to write the formulas, the data definitions, the validation thresholds, the disclosure templates, and the reporting tables. That somebody is the EBA. The Commission could not do this work; the Council certainly could not; the European Parliament has no technical capacity to do it. So the EU outsourced the rule-detail factory to a Paris-based agency of about 250 staff. The EBA writes the technical standards, the Commission rubber-stamps them as Delegated or Implementing Regulations, and those regulations apply directly in every member state without further national transposition. The EBA is, in short, the engineering department of EU banking regulation.
What is beyond the source The source wiki entry is a stub. This lecture builds the picture from the founding regulation 1093/2010, the EBA website, and recent EBA work programmes. It adds: the architecture of the ESFS and the EBA's place in it; the legal mechanics by which an EBA draft becomes a directly applicable EU regulation; the difference between RTS, ITS, Guidelines, and Q&A in terms of legal force; the methodology of the EU-wide stress test and how it interacts with the SREP; the political tension between EBA technocracy and Commission policy preferences, visible most clearly in the SME supporting factor and the output-floor calibration; and the practical consequence of the move from London to Paris.
Step 2

Why this matters

The CRR and CRD set policy at a deliberately high level. The CRR says, for instance, that an institution using the Internal Ratings Based approach must estimate PD, LGD, and EAD for each exposure class; it does not say how. The EBA fills in that "how" through an RTS on the IRB assessment methodology, an RTS on the specification of the materiality threshold for the definition of default, Guidelines on PD estimation, Guidelines on LGD estimation, Guidelines on the treatment of defaulted exposures under IRB, and so on. For a credit risk modeller, the policy framework is the CRR; the working document is the relevant EBA RTS or Guideline.

The same pattern repeats across the whole prudential framework. Market risk is governed at policy level by Part Three, Title IV of the CRR; the calibration of the alternative standardised approach under FRTB is set by EBA RTS. Operational risk is governed by the CRR; the calculation of the business indicator and historical losses is set by EBA RTS. Liquidity is governed by the CRR; the LCR formula is set out in Commission Delegated Regulation 2015/61, which itself implements an EBA technical standard. Recovery and resolution planning is governed by the BRRD; the methodology for the minimum requirement for own funds and eligible liabilities (MREL) is set by EBA RTS. The EBA is the rule-detail factory for the entire EU prudential corpus.

Three institutional facts matter for day-to-day work. First, EBA RTS and ITS, once adopted by the Commission, are directly applicable across all 27 member states with no national transposition, which means a French-bank and a Dutch-bank credit risk modeller are working from identical legal text. Second, EBA Guidelines are formally non-binding but national supervisors must publicly state whether they comply, and in practice they almost always do, so for an operational modeller a Guideline is effectively binding even though it is technically optional. Third, the EBA Single Rulebook Q&A is the dispute-resolution mechanism for almost every ambiguity in EU banking law; when a phrase in the CRR is unclear, the Q&A is where the authoritative interpretation appears, and a national supervisor will normally follow it even though Q&A answers are not legally binding either.

Step 3

The mental model

The EU's financial supervisory architecture has two axes. The vertical axis separates macroprudential oversight (the European Systemic Risk Board, ESRB, which monitors system-wide risk) from microprudential supervision (which looks at individual institutions). The horizontal axis separates banking (EBA, with SSM as the euro-area supervisor), securities markets (ESMA), and insurance and pensions (EIOPA). Underneath all of it sit the 27 national competent authorities, each of whom retains direct supervisory power over institutions not delegated to the SSM, and over conduct, anti-money-laundering, and consumer-protection matters even for the largest banks. The EBA's specific contribution is rule-making and convergence; supervision is somebody else's job.

Three flows of authority run through the diagram. Rules flow down from the EBA to the supervisors who apply them, by way of Commission adoption for RTS and ITS and by way of national supervisor declarations for Guidelines. Risk information flows up from supervisors and banks to the EBA, which aggregates it in the Risk Dashboard and the Transparency Exercise and feeds it into the stress test. And convergence pressure flows sideways from the EBA to national supervisors through peer reviews, common methodologies, and the breach-of-Union-law procedure, which the EBA can invoke when a national supervisor is alleged to be applying EU law incorrectly.

Step 4

The detail

History: from CEBS to EBA

The EBA's predecessor was the Committee of European Banking Supervisors (CEBS), established by Commission Decision in 2004 as one of three "Level 3" committees under the Lamfalussy process. CEBS was advisory only. It had no rule-making power, no binding instruments, and no mechanism for resolving disagreements between national supervisors. Its outputs were guidelines that member states applied at their own discretion. In the run-up to the 2008 crisis CEBS produced useful technical work on, among other things, the supervisory review process and stress testing, but it had no authority to enforce convergence and the resulting national divergences in supervisory practice were one of the issues identified by the post-crisis review.

The de Larosière Report of February 2009, commissioned by the European Commission, recommended a comprehensive overhaul of EU financial supervision. Its central proposal was the creation of three new European Supervisory Authorities to replace the Level 3 committees, with binding technical-standard-making powers, dispute-resolution mechanisms between national supervisors, and a coordinating Joint Committee. The report also proposed the ESRB to fill the macroprudential gap. The European Commission adopted the recommendations and proposed the founding regulations in 2009; they were agreed by Council and Parliament in 2010 and entered into force on 1 January 2011. The EBA inherited CEBS's staff and premises in London and immediately began producing the technical standards that the post-crisis Basel III implementation would require.

The EBA's most visible relocation came after the UK's 2016 referendum on EU membership. With Brexit confirmed, the EBA could no longer be based in a non-member state. Several member states bid to host the agency; the EU27 Council voted in November 2017 to relocate to Paris, in the same building complex (the Tour Europlaza in La Défense, later moving to the Tour Cristal). The move was completed on 3 June 2019. About half of the London-based staff transferred; the remainder were replaced locally, with consequent loss of institutional memory in the transition period. ESMA, separately, is also Paris-based; EIOPA is in Frankfurt.

Governance

Board of Supervisors
The EBA's main decision-making body. Composed of the heads of the 27 national competent authorities for banking, plus a representative of the ECB, the Commission, the ESRB, ESMA, and EIOPA. Chaired by the EBA Chairperson. Decides on RTS, ITS, Guidelines, and Opinions by qualified majority or simple majority depending on the matter. Meets at least four times a year.
Management Board
Six members of the Board of Supervisors plus the Chairperson, elected by the Board of Supervisors. Responsible for ensuring the EBA carries out its mission. Prepares the work programme and budget. Meets more frequently than the full Board.
Chairperson
Full-time five-year appointment, renewable once. Represents the EBA externally and chairs the Board of Supervisors and Management Board. The current Chairperson is José Manuel Campa (since 2019). Previous chairs were Andrea Enria (2011 to 2018, subsequently Chair of the SSM Supervisory Board) and Adam Farkas (interim).
Executive Director
Full-time five-year appointment. Manages the EBA's day-to-day operations and its staff of about 250. Reports to the Chairperson and the Management Board. Implements the work programme.
Board of Appeal
A joint body of all three ESAs. Hears appeals against EBA decisions (and ESMA and EIOPA decisions) by natural or legal persons directly affected. Six members and six alternates, drawn from outside the EU institutions. The Board of Appeal's decisions can themselves be appealed to the Court of Justice of the EU.

The voting architecture in the Board of Supervisors is politically significant. Most technical-standard decisions are taken by qualified majority of the NCAs, with non-voting representation from the Commission and the ECB. For decisions specifically affecting the Banking Union the voting rules were adjusted in 2013 to include a double-majority requirement, protecting non-euro-area member states from being outvoted by the larger euro-area bloc. This rule remained in place after the UK's departure but applies in practice only to Sweden, Denmark, and the non-euro central and eastern European member states.

Mandate

Regulation 1093/2010 sets out the EBA's mandate in five strands. The first is contribution to the creation of a Single Rulebook, the single set of harmonised prudential rules applying across the EU. The second is promoting supervisory convergence, so that the rules are applied consistently in different member states. The third is assessing risks and vulnerabilities, both through the biennial stress test and through ongoing market monitoring. The fourth is consumer protection in banking, with specific powers over financial products, including the ability to temporarily ban harmful products under certain conditions. The fifth is international cooperation, including coordination with the BCBS and other third-country authorities. Anti-money-laundering powers were added by Regulation (EU) 2019/2175, giving the EBA a lead coordinating role over AML supervision across the Union until those powers are transferred to the new EU AML Authority (AMLA), established in 2024 and based in Frankfurt.

Powers and instruments

The EBA's output is organised into several legal categories with different degrees of binding force. Knowing which category a given document falls into is essential when interpreting what it says.

Regulatory Technical Standards (RTS)
Drafted by the EBA under a specific empowerment in a Level 1 act (typically the CRR, CRD, or BRRD). Submitted to the European Commission, which adopts them as a Delegated Regulation under Article 290 TFEU. Once adopted and published in the Official Journal, they have direct legal effect in all 27 member states. The Commission may amend the draft only in defined circumstances and must explain any amendment publicly. RTS are the EBA's strongest output, legally indistinguishable from any other piece of EU regulation once adopted.
Implementing Technical Standards (ITS)
Also drafted by the EBA under empowerment in a Level 1 act, but adopted by the Commission as an Implementing Regulation under Article 291 TFEU. Used where the technical detail concerns uniform conditions for implementing existing Level 1 obligations, such as reporting templates, disclosure formats, and notification procedures. Legally binding in the same way as RTS, but the Commission's discretion to amend the draft is even narrower.
Guidelines and Recommendations
Issued by the EBA directly, without Commission adoption. Operate on a comply-or-explain basis: within two months of publication, each NCA must notify the EBA whether it complies, intends to comply, or does not comply, and if not, why not. Financial institutions to whom Guidelines are directly addressed (such as banks for Guidelines on internal governance) have the same obligation. In practice almost all NCAs declare compliance with almost all Guidelines, making them effectively binding even though they are not legally so. Examples include the Guidelines on PD estimation (EBA/GL/2017/16), Guidelines on outsourcing arrangements (EBA/GL/2019/02), and Guidelines on the management of IRRBB and CSRBB (EBA/GL/2022/14).
Opinions
Technical advice from the EBA on a specific question, typically addressed to the Commission ahead of a legislative proposal or to a national supervisor on a supervisory question. Not legally binding but politically influential. The EBA's Opinion on the implementation of the Basel III reforms in the EU (2019) was the main technical input into the Commission's CRR3 proposal of 2021.
Single Rulebook Q&A
A searchable online database in which questions about the interpretation of EU banking law are answered by the EBA in consultation with the Commission and other ESAs. Answers are formally non-binding but are followed in practice by NCAs and by the SSM. The Q&A is the main mechanism by which the operational meaning of the CRR is clarified over time. As of 2024 it contains several thousand answers and is the busiest single page on the EBA website.
Breach of Union law investigation
A power under Article 17 of Regulation 1093/2010 by which the EBA can investigate alleged failures by an NCA to apply Union law correctly. The EBA issues a Recommendation; if not followed, the matter can escalate to the Commission, which can issue a Formal Opinion; ultimately the EBA can address a Decision to the individual financial institution if the NCA still fails to act and the breach is direct and unconditional. Rarely used but a real instrument; invoked, for example, in the Danske Bank AML investigation.

The EU-wide stress test

The EBA runs an EU-wide stress test on a biennial basis (since 2021; previously annual or irregular). The exercise is run jointly with the ECB (for SSM banks), the ESRB (which designs the macroeconomic scenarios), and the national competent authorities. The 2023 exercise covered 70 banks representing about 75 per cent of EU banking-sector assets; the 2025 exercise is expected to be similar.

The methodology has three core features. The first is a constrained bottom-up approach: banks run their own models against a common scenario, but under detailed methodological constraints set by the EBA covering credit-risk parameter projections, market-risk shocks, net-interest-income modelling, and operational-risk losses. Banks cannot, for instance, project a PD lower than its current value over the horizon for most portfolios. The second is a three-year horizon, with the scenario typically starting at the end of the previous year. The third is a static-balance-sheet assumption: banks must not assume management actions to mitigate the impact, and the balance sheet is held constant over the horizon. The output is a projected CET1 ratio under baseline and adverse scenarios at the end of each of the three projected years.

The results are published bank-by-bank, with detailed templates showing the drivers of the projected CET1 trajectory. There is no pass-fail threshold in the exercise itself; instead, the projected adverse-scenario CET1 ratio feeds into the SSM's SREP for euro-area significant institutions, where it informs Pillar 2 Guidance (P2G), the non-binding capital expectation set by the supervisor on top of the binding Pillar 2 Requirement. The EBA stress test methodology has been influential globally and is closely studied by the Bank of England, the Federal Reserve, and others when designing their own stress tests.

Risk Dashboard and Transparency Exercise

Alongside the biennial stress test, the EBA publishes two regular risk-monitoring outputs. The Risk Dashboard is a quarterly publication of aggregated risk indicators for the EU banking sector, including capital ratios, asset quality, profitability, funding, and liquidity metrics. It is the most-cited source of EU-wide banking statistics in policy debate. The Transparency Exercise is an annual bank-by-bank disclosure exercise that publishes detailed balance-sheet, capital, and risk-exposure data for around 130 institutions, with results released on the EBA website. The Transparency Exercise was originally designed as a complement to the stress test in years when no stress test was run, and has since become an annual fixture.

The Single Rulebook

The Single Rulebook is the term used for the integrated body of harmonised prudential law that applies across the EU. Its core components are the Capital Requirements Regulation (CRR) and Capital Requirements Directive (CRD) for prudential capital, the Bank Recovery and Resolution Directive (BRRD) for recovery and resolution, the Deposit Guarantee Schemes Directive (DGSD), the Markets in Financial Instruments Directive and Regulation (MiFID II / MiFIR) for investment services, and the Anti-Money-Laundering Directive (AMLD, soon AMLR). Around these Level 1 acts sit the EBA's RTS, ITS, and Guidelines, which together fill in the operational detail. The EBA hosts an interactive Single Rulebook website at eba.europa.eu/single-rule-book through which the consolidated text of each provision can be looked up alongside the relevant Q&A and any related technical standards.

Major recent work

The current work programme is dominated by implementation of the Basel III finalisation through CRR3 and CRD6. The EBA published its roadmap for CRR3 implementation in December 2023, listing more than 140 mandates for RTS, ITS, Guidelines, and reports that need to be delivered between 2024 and 2028. The most consequential mandates include the RTS on the standardised approach for credit risk, the RTS on the IRB approach including the output floor's interaction with internal models, the RTS on the alternative standardised approach and internal model approach for market risk under FRTB, and the RTS on operational risk loss data and the business indicator.

ESG risk is a second major workstream. The EBA published a Report on the role of environmental and social risks in the prudential framework in October 2023 and is working on Guidelines on ESG risk management, expected to come into force in 2026. The Guidelines on IRRBB and CSRBB (EBA/GL/2022/14) introduced credit-spread risk in the banking book as a specific Pillar 2 risk category. Operational resilience is being addressed through the Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) framework, with the EBA jointly responsible alongside ESMA and EIOPA. AML supervision continues at the EBA until the transfer of operational AML supervision to AMLA, expected from 2025.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Read the EBA text, not the CRR alone

The CRR is the legislative envelope. The substantive rule, in almost every area, is in the EBA RTS or Guideline that fills in the technical detail. If you are setting up a PD model, the CRR tells you that you need one and that the parameter must reflect a long-run average default rate; the EBA Guidelines on PD estimation tell you what data window, what definition of default, what calibration procedure, and what validation tests are expected. Reading only the CRR will leave you with the policy intent and none of the operational specification.

Watch the consultation papers

The EBA's draft RTS and Guidelines are published for consultation typically three to six months before adoption. The consultation paper contains the EBA's preferred option, the alternatives considered, the rationale, and (in most cases) the results of any cost-benefit analysis. Industry comments are public and are useful as a window into where the standard might still move before finalisation. The final RTS, once adopted by the Commission, almost never changes; the consultation window is the only point at which technical input can realistically shift the text.

Watch out The EBA does not supervise banks. When a bank has a supervisory question about how a rule applies to it, the answer comes from its NCA (or the SSM for euro-area significant institutions), not from the EBA. The EBA Q&A can clarify the general interpretation of a rule, but it cannot answer "may I do this in my model?" The Q&A process is explicitly for generic interpretation; institution-specific questions are bounced back to the supervisor. Confusing the two leads to wasted submissions.

Guidelines are not optional in practice

The legal status of an EBA Guideline is comply-or-explain. The practical status is that almost every NCA complies. The SSM, in particular, complies with every Guideline that addresses topics within its remit. A bank that decides to deviate from a Guideline because it is technically non-binding will find the deviation challenged in the SREP process, in any model approval, and in any internal-audit review. The right way to use a Guideline is as a binding requirement, treating the comply-or-explain mechanism as a feature of the supervisor's flexibility, not the bank's.

The EBA is technocratic; the Commission is political

The EBA writes the technical detail; the Commission decides what gets adopted. The two have visible disagreements in some areas. The SME supporting factor in the CRR (a discount on capital requirements for SME exposures) was a Commission addition for which the EBA produced sceptical technical analysis. The output floor in CRR3 was calibrated more leniently than the EBA's technical recommendation, with carve-outs the EBA had argued against. The Basel-faithfulness of EU prudential law is generally less than the EBA would prefer and more than the Council and Parliament would prefer; the Commission sits between them. When reading an EBA Opinion that disagrees with a final Commission text, the gap usually reflects this political negotiation.

The UK still uses EBA work

The UK has not been an EU member since 31 January 2020 and is no longer represented at the EBA. The PRA has formally taken over the technical-standard-setting role that the EBA used to play for UK banks. In practice, however, the PRA continues to consume EBA work: EBA Q&A answers on the pre-Brexit CRR are still relied on as interpretive guidance for the UK version of the CRR (the "UK CRR"), and the PRA's near-final CRR rules (CP16/22 and the resulting SS-series statements) track EBA RTS closely in most areas, with deliberate divergences only where UK supervisors have policy reasons to depart. The actuarial implication is that even for a UK-based modeller, the EBA Single Rulebook remains a useful reference for legacy interpretation.

The stress test is a methodology document, not just an exercise

The biennial stress test produces bank-by-bank results, but the more important output for many practitioners is the methodological note that accompanies each exercise. The note specifies how every parameter should be projected: which PD floors apply, how net interest income should be projected under the static balance-sheet assumption, what shocks should be applied to trading-book positions. This methodology is then frequently incorporated into supervisor expectations for banks' own internal stress tests, including ICAAP. A bank running ICAAP scenarios that ignore the EBA methodology will find its assumptions challenged.
