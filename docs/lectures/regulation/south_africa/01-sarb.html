SARB: the South African Reserve Bank

The South African Reserve Bank is the country's central bank, its macroprudential authority, its prudential supervisor (working through the Prudential Authority that sits inside it), and, since 2021, its bank resolution authority. It is constitutionally entrenched as an independent institution, owned by private shareholders by historical accident, and is the single most important regulator that a South African bank deals with on any given day.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The South African Reserve Bank (SARB) is South Africa's central bank, established in 1921 under the Currency and Banking Act of 1920. It is one of the oldest central banks outside Europe and North America, and one of the very few central banks in the world that is still owned by private shareholders rather than by the state. Its constitutional status is unusually strong: sections 223 to 225 of the 1996 Constitution of the Republic of South Africa establish the SARB, fix its primary object, and entrench its independence. The current statute is the South African Reserve Bank Act 90 of 1989, which consolidated the earlier legislation and remains the operating charter of the institution.

The SARB has a single primary object expressed in identical words in the Constitution and the SARB Act: to protect the value of the currency in the interest of balanced and sustainable economic growth. It pursues this object through four roles. First, it sets monetary policy through the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), which targets headline CPI inflation in a 3 to 6 per cent band, with the MPC's preferred point in the middle of that range. Second, it safeguards financial stability through the Financial Stability Committee (FSC), which sets the countercyclical capital buffer, designates D-SIBs, and publishes the semi-annual Financial Stability Review. Third, since the 2017 Financial Sector Regulation Act inaugurated South Africa's Twin Peaks model, it houses the Prudential Authority (PA), which is the operational prudential supervisor of banks, insurers, and market infrastructures (the PA is the subject of lecture 23). Fourth, since the Financial Sector Laws Amendment Act of 2021 (FSLAA) brought the deposit insurance and resolution framework into force, the SARB is the designated resolution authority for South African banks, operating through the Corporation for Deposit Insurance (CoDI), which became operational on 1 April 2024.

Feynman test Imagine that one building in Pretoria does everything that, in the United Kingdom, the Bank of England, the PRA, the Resolution Directorate, and the FSCS do between them. That building is the SARB. The Governor chairs the meeting that sets the repo rate. Down one corridor, a separate committee chaired by the same Governor sets the macroprudential buffer for every bank in the country. Down a second corridor, a legally separate but organisationally embedded authority writes the prudential rules and runs supervision. Down a third, a wholly-owned subsidiary stands ready to resolve a failing bank. And in the basement, the bank runs the wholesale settlement system that every other bank settles through. It is one institution doing the work of five.

Step 2

Why this matters

For any actuary working inside a South African bank, the SARB is the day-to-day prudential touch point. The PA, which is the legal supervisor, sits inside the SARB and reports administratively to the Governor. The prudential rulebook the bank applies, the Regulations Relating to Banks and the SARB Prudential Standards, is issued from inside the SARB. The macroprudential setting that determines the bank's countercyclical buffer is decided by the FSC, whose secretariat is at the SARB. The repo rate that prices every floating-rate loan on the bank's book is set by the MPC at the SARB. The settlement system through which the bank closes its end-of-day position is run by the SARB. If the bank fails, the resolution authority that takes it into resolution is the SARB. There is no significant prudential or systemic question for a South African bank that does not eventually run back to one of the committees, departments, or subsidiaries of the SARB.

The institutional history also explains some of the local idiosyncrasies that confuse practitioners coming from other jurisdictions. South Africa runs Twin Peaks, which means that the prudential supervisor (the PA) and the conduct supervisor (the FSCA) are separate institutions. But unlike the Australian model, on which Twin Peaks was loosely based, South Africa's prudential supervisor is administratively embedded inside the central bank rather than being a standalone authority. The PRA is statutorily independent from the SARB in policy and supervisory decisions; it is organisationally and operationally part of the SARB in budget, premises, and reporting lines. This dual position is constantly tested in practice and matters for any question about who actually owns a given prudential decision.

For the ASSA F107 candidate, the SARB structure is examinable in detail. The exam regularly asks candidates to name the SARB's statutory committees, describe the constitutional mandate, identify which body sets the CCyB, and explain the resolution architecture. Answers that confuse SARB and PA, or that omit the FSLAA 2021 resolution framework, lose marks. The lecture below gives you the exam-grade detail for each.

Step 3

The mental model

The SARB sits under two layers of authority, the Constitution and the SARB Act, and itself houses or owns five major operating arms: the Monetary Policy Committee, the Financial Stability Committee, the Prudential Authority (with its own Prudential Committee), the Corporation for Deposit Insurance, and the operational departments (financial markets, payments, currency, research, financial surveillance). Three flows of authority matter. Decisions flow down from the statutory committees to the regulated population: the MPC's repo-rate decision flows to every loan and deposit priced off the prime rate, the FSC's CCyB flows to every bank's capital requirement, the PA's standards flow to every bank's compliance manual. Reporting flows up from the regulated population to the supervisory departments and then to the committees: prudential returns, payment-system data, financial-surveillance reports. And cooperation flows sideways across the SARB's committees and with the other Twin Peaks regulator (the FSCA) through the formal Financial System Council of Regulators and the SARB-FSCA Memorandum of Understanding.

Step 4

The detail

History: 1920 to today

The South African Reserve Bank was created by the Currency and Banking Act 31 of 1920, passed in the wake of the post-First-World-War sterling crisis. South Africa was at the time still a sterling-area dominion, and the Act sought to restore convertibility and to create a domestic note-issuing authority. The SARB opened for business on 30 June 1921, making it the oldest central bank in Africa and one of the earliest of the inter-war central-bank foundations. Its design borrowed heavily from the Bank of England and from the post-war recommendations of Sir Henry Strakosch, who advised on the legislation.

Three statutory landmarks then shaped the institution over the next century. The Currency and Exchanges Act of 1933 confirmed the SARB's sole right to issue South African banknotes and gave it operational responsibility for exchange control, which it still discharges through the Financial Surveillance Department. The South African Reserve Bank Act 90 of 1989, the current charter, consolidated and modernised the earlier legislation and remains the operating statute of the bank today (it has been amended several times, most consequentially by the FSR Act 2017 and the FSLAA 2021). The Constitution of the Republic of South Africa, 1996, then entrenched the bank's existence and its mandate at constitutional level, making the SARB one of only a handful of central banks in the world whose independence is protected against ordinary legislative change.

Two features of the SARB that often surprise practitioners are byproducts of the original 1920 design. The bank was set up with private share capital, partly to provide the initial paid-up capital without burdening the fiscus and partly to insulate the institution from political interference. The shares were never bought back, and nationalisation has been discussed at intervals but never enacted, so the SARB remains owned by roughly 660 private shareholders. The dividend is capped at 10 cents per share per year, voting rights are capped at 10,000 shares per holder, and shareholders have no influence over monetary policy or supervisory decisions, so the ownership structure is now largely symbolic. The political controversy around it, however, is real: the ruling African National Congress and the Economic Freedom Fighters have both repeatedly called for nationalisation, and amendments to the SARB Act giving the state full ownership have been tabled in parliament at various points without passing.

Constitutional status: sections 223 to 225

The 1996 Constitution does three things that matter for any reading of the SARB. Section 223 establishes the SARB as "the central bank of the Republic" and provides that it "is regulated in terms of an Act of Parliament", that Act being the SARB Act 90 of 1989. Section 224 sets out the primary object: "the primary object of the South African Reserve Bank is to protect the value of the currency in the interest of balanced and sustainable economic growth in the Republic". Section 224(2) then provides the operative independence clause:

"The South African Reserve Bank, in pursuit of its primary object, must perform its functions independently and without fear, favour or prejudice, but there must be regular consultation between the Bank and the Cabinet member responsible for national financial matters."

Section 225 then confirms the regulatory framework: "The powers and functions of the South African Reserve Bank are those customarily exercised and performed by central banks, which powers and functions must be determined by an Act of Parliament and must be exercised or performed subject to the conditions prescribed in terms of that Act". This three-section package is doctrinally important. It anchors the SARB's mandate ("protect the value of the currency"), its purpose ("in the interest of balanced and sustainable economic growth"), and its independence ("without fear, favour or prejudice") at constitutional level. Any statutory amendment that conflicts with these provisions would be open to constitutional challenge, which makes the SARB's independence harder to dismantle than that of most central banks worldwide.

Governance: the Governor, Deputies, Board, and the three statutory committees

The SARB Act 90 of 1989 sets up the governance architecture. The bank is run day to day by a Governor and three Deputy Governors, all appointed by the President of the Republic after consultation with the Minister of Finance and the SARB Board, for renewable five-year terms. The Governor is the public face of the institution and chairs the MPC, the FSC, and the Board. The three Deputy Governors hold portfolio responsibilities that are not fixed in statute but are typically allocated across monetary policy and economic research, financial stability and the Prudential Authority oversight, and operations including payments, currency, and reserves.

The Board of Directors consists of fifteen members: the Governor, the three Deputy Governors, four directors appointed by the President (typically representing commerce, finance, agriculture, and labour), and seven directors elected by the shareholders. The Board oversees corporate governance and administration, including the bank's budget and remuneration, but explicitly does not direct monetary policy or supervisory decisions, which are vested in the relevant statutory committees.

Three statutory committees do the public-policy work of the SARB. The exam-grade summary of each follows.

Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)
Eight members: the Governor (chair), the three Deputy Governors, and four other senior SARB officials appointed by the Governor. Sets the repurchase rate (the "repo rate"), which is the policy rate at which the SARB lends to banks against eligible collateral, and which anchors the prime lending rate and most floating-rate retail and wholesale loans. Meets six times per year, with each meeting concluded by a public statement and a press conference. Operates under an inflation-targeting framework, with a 3 to 6 per cent target band on headline CPI agreed with the National Treasury; since 2017 the MPC has informally aimed for the 4.5 per cent midpoint, and a formal review of the framework was launched in 2025.
Financial Stability Committee (FSC)
Chaired by the Governor, with the Deputy Governor responsible for financial stability, the CEO of the Prudential Authority, the Commissioner of the FSCA, the CEO of the National Credit Regulator, and a senior National Treasury official as members. The FSC is South Africa's macroprudential authority. It identifies and monitors systemic risks, sets the countercyclical capital buffer (CCyB, currently set at 0 per cent), designates domestic systemically important banks (D-SIBs), and publishes the Financial Stability Review (FSR) twice a year. The FSC was given statutory standing by the FSR Act 2017; the SARB had run a Financial Stability Department and published the FSR since 2004, but the FSC formalised the macroprudential decision-making process.
Prudential Committee (PC)
The statutory decision-making body of the Prudential Authority, made up of the CEO of the PA (chair), the Governor, and the three Deputy Governors. The PC approves the prudential standards, designates supervised institutions, takes formal supervisory actions, and signs off on supervisory plans. The PC's existence is the legal mechanism by which the PA is run as a separate decision-making authority while being administratively embedded inside the SARB.

Three other internal committees deserve mention but are not statutory in the same sense. The Risk Management Committee oversees enterprise risk inside the SARB. The Audit Committee oversees the SARB's own financial reporting. The Reserves Management Committee oversees the management of the country's gold and foreign-exchange reserves, which currently stand at roughly USD 60 billion and constitute the SARB's single largest balance-sheet item.

The primary mandate and the monetary policy framework

The SARB's primary object, fixed in section 224 of the Constitution and section 3 of the SARB Act, is to protect the value of the currency in the interest of balanced and sustainable economic growth. In operational terms, the SARB pursues price stability through an inflation-targeting framework, the original target having been set in February 2000 in agreement with the National Treasury. The target is expressed in terms of headline CPI inflation in a 3 to 6 per cent band, which has been in place continuously since 2000 (the band was briefly narrower from 2002 to 2003 but reverted). Since around 2017 the MPC has anchored expectations on the 4.5 per cent midpoint, treating the upper and lower bounds as outer rather than central. A formal monetary policy framework review was announced in 2025, with options on the table including a single-point target (the MPC has indicated a preference for a 3 per cent point target) and a narrower band.

The main policy instrument is the repo rate, set at MPC meetings. The transmission mechanism runs through the prime lending rate (commercial bank prime, anchored at repo plus 350 basis points by long-standing convention), through bond yields, and through the exchange rate. The SARB does not operate a fixed exchange-rate regime; the rand is floating and the SARB intervenes only rarely and primarily to smooth disorderly market conditions. Open market operations and the management of the daily liquidity shortage are the technical mechanisms through which the policy rate is enforced. The SARB also runs a standing facility (the marginal lending facility) and a deposit facility, the spread between which forms a corridor around the repo rate.

Financial stability and macroprudential policy

The SARB's financial stability mandate was given statutory standing by the FSR Act 2017, which added financial stability to the SARB's responsibilities and created the FSC as the macroprudential decision-making forum. Operationally, the Financial Stability Department of the SARB conducts the analytical work: systemic-risk monitoring, macroeconomic and financial stress scenarios, network analysis of interbank exposures, and the production of the semi-annual Financial Stability Review. The 2022 climate-risk stress test conducted jointly with the Prudential Authority was the first formal climate-stress exercise in South Africa and is a useful exam-relevant example of macroprudential action.

The macroprudential toolkit includes the CCyB, the systemic risk buffer, the D-SIB designation and surcharge, and a power (less often used) to issue directives to address sectoral risks such as housing-market overheating. The CCyB is set at 0 per cent at the time of writing, with the FSC having signalled that it expects to activate the buffer above zero during periods of excessive credit growth. D-SIB designation is reviewed annually and results in a Higher Loss Absorbency requirement of between 0.5 and 2 per cent of risk-weighted assets, depending on the systemic importance score, which is added to the bank's minimum CET1 requirement.

The Prudential Authority: Twin Peaks and the 2017 to 2018 transition

Before 2018, banking supervision in South Africa was conducted by the Bank Supervision Department (BSD) of the SARB, which had operated under the Banks Act 94 of 1990 since 1991. Insurance supervision was conducted separately by the Financial Services Board (FSB), which also covered conduct supervision of insurers, pension funds, collective investment schemes, and capital markets. Conduct supervision of banks was largely absent, a structural gap exposed by repeated mis-selling cases.

The Twin Peaks model, formally introduced by the Financial Sector Regulation Act 9 of 2017, restructured this architecture. The Bank Supervision Department of the SARB and the prudential parts of the FSB were combined into a new Prudential Authority, which became operational on 1 April 2018. The conduct parts of the FSB were renamed and reorganised as the Financial Sector Conduct Authority (FSCA), which is a separate juristic person reporting to the Minister of Finance. The PA sits inside the SARB as an administratively embedded body that is statutorily independent in its decisions, with the Prudential Committee as the formal decision-making body and the CEO of the PA reporting administratively to the Governor. The PA is the subject of lecture 23.

The practical consequence of this design is a deliberate blurring. The SARB and the PA share offices, IT, and most corporate services. Senior PA staff sit on SARB-wide committees. Many senior staff have rotated between the SARB and the PA. But formally, prudential decisions are taken by the Prudential Committee under the Banks Act and the FSR Act, not by the SARB Board or the Governor acting alone. For exam purposes, attribute prudential rulings to the PA, attribute monetary and macroprudential rulings to the SARB.

SARB Prudential Standards: the local Basel implementation

South Africa is a member of the BCBS and applies Basel III. The legal vehicle for that implementation is a two-tier rulebook. The Regulations Relating to Banks, issued under section 90 of the Banks Act 94 of 1990, set out the bulk of the Basel framework in South African law: capital, leverage, liquidity, large exposures, and the supervisory return forms (the well-known BA returns: BA 100 for the prudential balance sheet, BA 200 for credit risk, BA 300 for liquidity, BA 700 for capital, and so on). The Regulations were comprehensively revised in 2013 to implement Basel III and have been amended several times since to track BCBS updates, most recently to implement the Basel III finalisation (Basel 3.1) reforms.

Below the Regulations, the SARB and the PA issue Directives (numbered D1, D2, and so on, with the most recent issuances in the current numbering series) and Guidance Notes (G1, G2). Directives are binding instructions to banks on the interpretation or application of the Regulations. Guidance Notes are advisory. The PA also issues Prudential Standards under the FSR Act, which are increasingly used for new prudential requirements such as the recovery and resolution planning rules. Practitioners working in capital, liquidity, or RWA will spend their lives in the Regulations Relating to Banks; risk-management, IRRBB, and stress-testing teams will spend their lives in Directives and Guidance Notes.

Resolution authority and CoDI

The Financial Sector Laws Amendment Act 23 of 2021 (FSLAA) was the most significant institutional reform of the SARB since the FSR Act of 2017. It designated the SARB as the resolution authority for South African banks and other designated financial institutions, gave the SARB the full toolkit of resolution powers (bail-in, bridge institution, asset separation, sale of business, write-down and conversion of capital instruments), and created the legal basis for the Corporation for Deposit Insurance.

The Corporation for Deposit Insurance (CoDI) is a wholly owned subsidiary of the SARB. It became operational on 1 April 2024 and provides deposit insurance cover of R100,000 per depositor per bank, funded by levies on member banks. CoDI's primary purpose is to make depositor protection credible enough that the SARB can use its resolution powers without triggering a wider deposit run. The R100,000 cover level is calibrated to protect roughly 90 per cent of South African depositors in full, though it covers a smaller share of total deposits by value, which is the standard design trade-off in deposit insurance scheme design.

The resolution authority has not yet been tested on a major bank. The most prominent post-FSLAA case was the curatorship of African Bank in 2014, which predated the FSLAA framework and was resolved under earlier statute. The SARB has published a Resolution Planning Framework and requires banks to maintain recovery plans (RRPs) under PA Prudential Standard FSR-RR. The interaction between the SARB as resolution authority, the PA as prudential supervisor, and CoDI as deposit insurer is governed by a Memorandum of Understanding among the three parties.

Other operational roles

Three additional SARB roles round out the picture. Banker to government: the SARB holds and operates the government's main bank account (the Exchequer Account), conducts government debt auctions on behalf of the National Treasury, and provides cash-management services. Reserves manager: the SARB holds and invests South Africa's gold and foreign-exchange reserves, which form the country's external buffer against balance-of-payments shocks. Payments-system operator: the SARB owns and operates the South African Multiple Option Settlement (SAMOS) system, which is the country's real-time gross settlement system through which interbank settlements, central counterparty payments, and large-value transfers settle in central bank money. Every commercial bank in South Africa has a settlement account at the SARB and settles its end-of-day position through SAMOS.

The SARB is also the issuer of South African banknotes and coins, through its wholly owned subsidiary the South African Mint (which produces coins) and the South African Bank Note Company (which prints banknotes). Currency in circulation is recorded on the SARB balance sheet as a liability and is one of the largest line items there.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The private-shareholder structure is a quirk, not a flaw

Foreign analysts often seize on the SARB's private ownership as evidence of a structural defect. It is not. The dividend is capped, voting rights are capped, shareholders have no influence over monetary or supervisory policy, and all profits beyond the dividend cap accrue to the National Revenue Fund. The structure is constitutionally and statutorily ringfenced. The political controversy around nationalisation is real but the practical impact of resolving it would be near-zero. For any exam question, mention the ownership structure as a historical fact, note that it does not affect the SARB's independence or its public-policy function, and move on.

SARB independence is unusually strong by global standards

The combination of constitutional entrenchment (sections 223 to 225), a clear primary object, and the explicit "without fear, favour or prejudice" formula gives the SARB a level of independence that exceeds most G20 central banks. The Bank of England's mandate is set by an Act of Parliament that can be amended by simple majority. The Federal Reserve's dual mandate is similarly statutory. The SARB's mandate cannot be changed without a constitutional amendment, which requires a two-thirds majority in the National Assembly. This is the single most important fact about the SARB for anyone trying to assess the credibility of its policy commitments.

The resolution power is untested

The FSLAA 2021 framework has been in place for several years and the resolution toolkit looks broadly aligned with the FSB Key Attributes. But it has not been used on a major bank. The 2014 African Bank curatorship predated the framework and was resolved under earlier statute. South African banks are well-capitalised by global standards, the system is highly concentrated (five banks account for roughly 90 per cent of banking assets), and there has been no sustained banking-system stress since the 2008-09 global crisis. Whether the SARB's resolution toolkit would actually be deployed against a major bank, and what political pressures would shape that deployment, are genuinely open questions.

Watch out Do not conflate the SARB with the Prudential Authority in exam answers. The SARB is the central bank. The PA is the prudential supervisor. The PA sits inside the SARB, but it has its own statutory decision-making body (the Prudential Committee), its own CEO, and its own published rulebook. The SARB sets the repo rate and the CCyB; the PA writes the prudential standards and runs supervision. Confusing the two costs marks.

Memorise the three statutory committees and their distinct memberships

For F107, the single highest-yield item of SARB content to memorise is the three statutory committees and their memberships. The MPC is Governor plus three Deputies plus four additional members, sets the repo rate. The FSC is the Governor plus senior officials of the SARB, PA, FSCA, NCR, and Treasury, sets the CCyB and designates D-SIBs. The Prudential Committee is the CEO of the PA plus the Governor plus three Deputies, and is the statutory decision-making body of the PA. An exam question that says "name the SARB's statutory committees and their respective remits" appears frequently; you should be able to write the answer from memory.

The legislative stack is layered, learn the order

The SARB operates under a layered legal framework. At the apex: the Constitution (sections 223 to 225). Below: the SARB Act 90 of 1989. Beside: the Banks Act 94 of 1990 (the bank-specific prudential statute, under which the Regulations Relating to Banks are issued). Beside: the Financial Sector Regulation Act 9 of 2017 (the Twin Peaks framework, which created the PA and the FSC and gave the SARB its financial-stability mandate). Beside: the Financial Sector Laws Amendment Act 23 of 2021 (which created CoDI and made the SARB the resolution authority). For any examiner who asks "under what statutory authority", you should be able to identify which of the five layers gives the SARB the relevant power.
