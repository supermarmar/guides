FSCA: the Financial Sector Conduct Authority

The Financial Sector Conduct Authority is South Africa's market-conduct and consumer-protection regulator, the second peak of the Twin Peaks model created by the Financial Sector Regulation Act of 2017. It supervises how banks, insurers, asset managers, financial advisers, exchanges and retirement funds behave towards their customers and how they operate in the markets, and it does so for every regulated financial institution in the country regardless of subsector.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The Financial Sector Conduct Authority (FSCA) is South Africa's market-conduct regulator for all financial institutions. It was established by the Financial Sector Regulation Act 9 of 2017 (the FSR Act) and became operational on 1 April 2018. It replaced the old Financial Services Board (FSB), which had supervised the non-bank financial sector since 1990, and at the same time absorbed the conduct-of-business functions that had previously sat inside the South African Reserve Bank's Bank Supervision Department. Its statutory objectives, set out in sections 57 to 60 of the FSR Act, are to enhance and support the efficiency and integrity of financial markets, to protect financial customers by promoting their fair treatment, to provide for financial education and financial literacy, and to assist in maintaining financial stability. It is based in Pretoria with offices in Cape Town and Durban and is headed by a Commissioner, currently Unathi Kamlana.

The FSCA's authority is exercised principally through Conduct Standards, which are binding rules issued under the FSR Act, and through Joint Standards issued together with the Prudential Authority (PA). Its supervisory perimeter is unusually wide: it licenses and regulates banks, insurers and retirement funds (jointly or in parallel with the PA), but it is also the sole regulator of about 11,000 financial services providers (FSPs) under the FAIS Act, of the JSE and the other licensed exchanges under the Financial Markets Act, of collective investment schemes, and of credit-rating agencies. It does not have criminal jurisdiction; its enforcement tools are administrative penalties, debarment of individuals, public censure, and licence withdrawal. The pending Conduct of Financial Institutions Bill (the COFI Bill) will, when enacted, consolidate the patchwork of conduct laws into a single activity-based framework and significantly expand the FSCA's rule-making authority.

Feynman test Imagine the South African financial system as a building with two regulators on duty. One stands by the foundations, checking that the walls are thick enough that the building will not fall down: that is the Prudential Authority, looking at solvency and capital. The other walks the corridors, checking that the staff are polite to customers, that the lifts have signs in the right places, that the brochures in reception do not lie about the rent, that the cleaners are properly trained, and that nobody is being charged for services they did not receive: that is the FSCA. Both regulators are looking at the same building. Neither cares much about what the other is doing. The Prudential Authority is happy if the building stands up. The FSCA is happy if everyone inside is treated fairly. Twin Peaks is just the architectural decision to have two separate watchmen rather than one.

Step 2

Why this matters

For any South African financial institution, conduct supervision is a separate, parallel, and equally consequential regulatory burden alongside prudential supervision. A bank can be perfectly capitalised, liquid, and Pillar 1 compliant, and still be in serious trouble with the FSCA. African Bank's collapse in 2014 was a prudential failure on paper, but the underlying business model that produced the failure (unsecured micro-lending into over-indebted customers) was a conduct failure first: customers were being sold products that performed badly and that they should not have been advised to buy. The post-2014 reforms in unsecured lending, in affordability assessments and in sales-incentive structures came predominantly through the conduct channel, not the prudential one. Understanding what conduct supervision looks like, how it produces enforcement, and how it interacts with prudential rules, is therefore essential for any actuary or risk professional working inside a South African bank.

The FSCA's perimeter is also wider than its name suggests. It is not just a banking regulator. It is the only regulator that talks to about 11,000 financial services providers (FSPs) under the FAIS Act, which means every independent financial adviser, every broker, every wealth manager, and every employee giving advice on behalf of a bank or insurer. It is the regulator that licenses every collective investment scheme manager, every retirement fund administrator, every licensed exchange, every central securities depository, and every clearing house. If you build a credit-risk model that feeds a customer-facing decision, the FSCA cares about that model not because of the Basel capital implications but because of the consumer-protection ones: are customers being lent to fairly, are pricing decisions explicable, is information being disclosed at the right point in the sales process.

Finally, the FSCA is by some distance the more visible of the two Twin Peaks regulators. The Prudential Authority publishes prudential standards and consultations but rarely names individual institutions. The FSCA names institutions, publishes administrative penalties, debars individuals, and pursues retail enforcement actions that make the financial pages. For an F107 candidate, this asymmetry has a practical consequence: the conduct regulator is the one whose public actions tell you what supervisory expectations look like in practice, because the prudential regulator's expectations are largely invisible to anyone outside the supervised institution.

Step 3

The mental model

The cleanest way to hold the FSCA in mind is as one half of a Twin Peaks pair. Every regulated financial institution in South Africa has two regulators sitting on top of it: the Prudential Authority for solvency, capital, liquidity, and large-exposure questions, and the FSCA for market-conduct, customer-treatment, and disclosure questions. For some institutions both regulators are active. For others, only the FSCA is, because their business model does not raise prudential concerns serious enough to warrant a parallel prudential regulator (for example, a small financial adviser firm or an asset manager).

Three flows of authority matter. Standards flow down from the FSCA to its regulated population through Conduct Standards, Joint Standards (with the PA), and codes of conduct. Information flows up to the FSCA through regulatory returns, complaints data, TCF self-assessments, and mystery-shopping output. And enforcement flows out from the FSCA in the form of administrative penalties, debarments, licence withdrawals, and public censures, every one of which is published on the FSCA website and most of which name the individual or firm concerned.

Step 4

The detail

History: from FSB to FSCA

The FSCA is the legal successor to the Financial Services Board (FSB), which had been the non-bank financial regulator in South Africa since 1990. The FSB was a single-purpose regulator covering insurance, retirement funds, collective investment schemes, financial markets and financial advisers, but explicitly excluding banks, which were the SARB's responsibility. The 2008 global financial crisis prompted a National Treasury policy paper, "A safer financial sector to serve South Africa better" (the 2011 "red book"), which proposed replacing this functional split with a Twin Peaks architecture along the lines already adopted in Australia and being implemented in the UK.

The FSR Act 9 of 2017 enacted the Twin Peaks reform. With effect from 1 April 2018, the FSB was dissolved and its functions, staff and assets transferred to the FSCA. At the same time, the conduct-of-business supervisory functions that the SARB had previously exercised over banks through the Bank Supervision Department were transferred to the FSCA, so that for the first time conduct supervision of banks was placed in the same institution as conduct supervision of insurers, asset managers and advisers. Prudential supervision of banks was simultaneously moved out of the SARB Bank Supervision Department into the new Prudential Authority, which sits inside the SARB but is administratively distinct.

Governance

The FSCA is headed by a Commissioner appointed by the Minister of Finance for a five-year term. The Commissioner is supported by up to four Deputy Commissioners and by a number of Heads of Department covering the main supervisory and operational divisions. The Commissioner reports to the FSCA Executive Committee internally and to the Minister of Finance externally. As of 2026 the Commissioner is Unathi Kamlana, who took office in 2021.

Commissioner
Statutory chief executive of the FSCA. Appointed by the Minister of Finance under section 60 of the FSR Act. Five-year renewable term. Has personal statutory powers over licensing and enforcement.
Deputy Commissioners
Up to four, also appointed by the Minister. Each takes the lead on a major area of supervision: market integrity, retail conduct, regulatory framework, and corporate functions are the usual portfolios.
Heads of Department
Senior officials below the Deputy Commissioners. Run the day-to-day supervisory teams in areas such as banking conduct, insurance conduct, retirement funds, financial advisory and intermediary services, market infrastructures, and enforcement.
Funding
Mostly self-funded by levies on the regulated population under the Financial Sector and Deposit Insurance Levies Act 11 of 2022. National Treasury appropriation covers a small residual share.

The statutory mandate

Sections 57 to 60 of the FSR Act set out the FSCA's objectives and functions. Section 57 lists the four statutory objectives, which need to be known by name for the exam:

  1. Enhance and support the efficiency and integrity of financial markets. Covers the FSCA's market-integrity role, including surveillance of trading on the JSE and other exchanges, oversight of clearing and settlement, and the policing of market abuse.
  2. Protect financial customers by promoting their fair treatment, providing financial education, and assisting customers to resolve complaints. This is the consumer-protection peak of the mandate, operationalised primarily through the Treating Customers Fairly framework.
  3. Assist in maintaining financial stability. A secondary objective, delivered through cooperation with the SARB, the PA and the Financial Stability Oversight Committee.
  4. Provide for the conduct supervision of financial institutions. The general rule-making, licensing and supervisory function across the perimeter.

Section 58 elaborates the functions. The FSCA must "regulate and supervise the conduct of financial institutions in providing financial products and financial services and in conducting other regulated activities", and must do so by issuing Conduct Standards, conducting supervisory on-site inspections, granting and withdrawing licences, monitoring compliance, and enforcing the law. Section 59 places parallel obligations on the FSCA to cooperate with the PA, the SARB and the Financial Intelligence Centre, and to share information across the regulatory network.

Powers

The FSCA's powers fall into four families. It licenses participants in the financial sector, principally FSPs under the FAIS Act, CIS managers under the Collective Investment Schemes Control Act, market infrastructures under the Financial Markets Act, and (under joint arrangements with the PA) banks and insurers. It investigates suspected misconduct, with statutory powers to compel production of documents, attendance of witnesses, and on-site inspections. It enforces, primarily through administrative penalties (which can be substantial: penalties in excess of R50 million have been imposed in market-abuse cases), public censure, and the debarment of individuals from holding key roles. And it makes rules through Conduct Standards and Joint Standards, which have the force of subordinate legislation.

What the FSCA cannot do is prosecute criminal offences. South African market abuse, including insider trading and market manipulation, is a criminal offence under the Financial Markets Act, but criminal prosecution is the responsibility of the National Prosecuting Authority. The FSCA's administrative penalty regime runs in parallel: it can fine an offender administratively under the FSR Act regardless of whether criminal prosecution is also pursued, and the standard of proof for an administrative penalty (balance of probabilities) is lower than for a criminal conviction (beyond reasonable doubt).

Scope of supervision

The FSCA's regulatory perimeter is broader than that of any predecessor institution. The principal supervised populations are:

Banks
Conduct supervision of the 17 registered banks under the Banks Act 94 of 1990. Prudential supervision sits with the PA. Bank-specific conduct standards cover complaint handling, debit-order abuse, retail credit advertising, and customer due diligence.
Insurers
Both long-term and short-term insurers licensed under the Insurance Act 18 of 2017. The FSCA handles policyholder protection, intermediary supervision, claims handling and the Policyholder Protection Rules; the PA handles capital and solvency under SAM (Solvency Assessment and Management).
Retirement funds
Approximately 5,500 funds registered under the Pension Funds Act 24 of 1956. The FSCA registers, supervises and regulates pension funds, approves rules and rule amendments, and enforces the duty of trustees under section 7C. Two-pot retirement reform (effective 1 September 2024) is currently the dominant supervisory project.
Financial services providers (FSPs)
About 11,000 entities licensed under the Financial Advisory and Intermediary Services Act 37 of 2002 (FAIS Act) to render advice or intermediary services on financial products. Includes independent advisers, brokers, wealth managers and tied agents of banks and insurers.
Collective investment schemes
Unit trusts, exchange-traded funds and hedge funds licensed under the Collective Investment Schemes Control Act 45 of 2002. The FSCA approves scheme documents, supervises management companies, and enforces conduct rules.
Market infrastructures
Licensed exchanges (JSE, A2X, ZAR X, 4 Africa Exchange, Cape Town Stock Exchange), the central securities depository (Strate), licensed clearing houses, and trade repositories under the Financial Markets Act 19 of 2012. Joint supervision with the PA for systemic firms.
Credit-rating agencies
Registered under the Credit Rating Services Act 24 of 2012. The FSCA is the sole regulator. About six active CRAs cover the South African market.
Crypto-asset service providers
A Declaration in 2022 designated crypto assets as a financial product under the FAIS Act. Crypto-asset service providers must therefore be FSP-licensed since 2023, and the FSCA has begun granting and refusing licences accordingly. Significant supervisory growth area.

Publication types

The FSCA's rule-making output, in declining order of legal force, falls into four categories.

Conduct Standards (CS)
Binding rules issued by the FSCA under section 106 of the FSR Act. Subordinate legislation. Must be published in the Government Gazette after Parliament's negative-resolution procedure. Cover specific topics such as effective resolution of complaints (Conduct Standard 3 of 2020), advertising of financial products, or fit-and-proper requirements for FSP key individuals.
Joint Standards
Issued jointly with the Prudential Authority under section 107 of the FSR Act. Used where a topic spans both conduct and prudential concerns, for example outsourcing, IT governance, or operational resilience. Joint Standards are formally issued by both regulators and have the same legal status as Conduct Standards or Prudential Standards.
Codes of conduct
Issued under sector-specific legislation, principally the FAIS Act. The FAIS General Code of Conduct for Authorised Financial Services Providers and Representatives (Board Notice 80 of 2003, as amended) is the most important, setting out the duty of care, suitability, and disclosure rules that apply to every FSP and representative.
Guidance notices
Non-binding statements of the FSCA's views on how a rule should be interpreted or applied. Cannot create new legal obligations but signal supervisory expectations. Roughly the equivalent of a PRA Supervisory Statement.

Treating Customers Fairly

The Treating Customers Fairly (TCF) framework is the backbone of the FSCA's consumer-protection mandate. It was originally adopted in 2014 by the FSB and was carried into the FSCA in 2018; the COFI Bill, when enacted, will give it explicit statutory footing as the organising framework for conduct supervision across all financial subsectors. TCF is built on six outcomes, each of which translates into a set of supervisory expectations and a set of evidence that the FSCA will look for during supervisory engagements:

  1. Outcome 1. Customers can be confident that they are dealing with firms where the fair treatment of customers is central to the corporate culture. Evidence: board minutes, executive incentive structures, customer-treatment metrics in management information.
  2. Outcome 2. Products and services marketed and sold in the retail market are designed to meet the needs of identified customer groups and are targeted accordingly. Evidence: product-approval and product-review processes, target-market definitions, complaints data by product.
  3. Outcome 3. Customers are provided with clear information and are kept appropriately informed before, during and after the point of sale. Evidence: disclosure documents, brochures, KFI documents, ongoing customer statements.
  4. Outcome 4. Where customers receive advice, the advice is suitable and takes account of their circumstances. Evidence: advice records, suitability assessments, file reviews of adviser output, complaints related to advice.
  5. Outcome 5. Customers are provided with products that perform as firms have led them to expect, and the associated service is of an acceptable standard and as they have been led to expect. Evidence: claims-paid statistics, lapse rates by reason, persistency reports, complaints data on product performance.
  6. Outcome 6. Customers do not face unreasonable post-sale barriers to change product, switch provider, submit a claim, or make a complaint. Evidence: switching statistics, complaints turnaround times, claims-handling timelines, mystery shopping output.

Outcomes 1, 4 and 5 are the three most frequently examined in F107 papers. Outcome 1 is the cultural one: the FSCA wants to see that fair treatment is owned at board level, not pushed down to a compliance function. Outcome 4 is the advice one: where advice is given (and FAIS-regulated advice is the main vehicle for this), the suitability of that advice is an explicit supervisory test, with the suitability test required by the FAIS General Code itself. Outcome 5 is the product-performance one: customers should not be sold products that systematically fail to perform as advertised, which links TCF to product oversight and governance (POG) requirements.

The FAIS Act

The Financial Advisory and Intermediary Services Act 37 of 2002 (FAIS Act) is the principal vehicle through which the FSCA regulates financial advisers and intermediaries. Every person rendering financial advice in South Africa must be either an authorised FSP or a representative of one. The FAIS Act establishes a four-category licensing regime:

Category I
Advice and intermediary services in respect of financial products other than discretionary management. The standard category for independent advisers and brokers. Most FSPs are Category I.
Category II
Discretionary FSPs. Authorised to make investment decisions on behalf of clients within a mandate. Asset managers and wealth managers running discretionary portfolios are typically Category II.
Category IIA
Hedge fund FSPs. A specialised sub-category for hedge fund management. Subject to additional capital and conduct requirements.
Category III
Administrative FSPs. Provide administration of financial products without giving advice. LISP (linked investment service provider) platforms typically operate under Category III.
Category IV
Assistance business FSPs. Specialised for funeral cover and similar low-value, mass-market products. Subject to lighter fit-and-proper requirements than Categories I to III.

The FAIS Act also imposes fit-and-proper requirements on every key individual and every representative of an FSP. These cover honesty and integrity, competence (qualifications and experience), operational ability, and financial soundness. The Determination of Fit and Proper Requirements for FSPs, Board Notice 194 of 2017, is the implementing instrument. Failure to meet fit-and-proper standards is the most common ground for debarment, and the FSCA's debarment list is published publicly.

The Financial Markets Act and other sector laws

The Financial Markets Act 19 of 2012 (FMA) governs the licensed exchanges, central securities depositories, clearing houses, and trade repositories. The FSCA is the sole regulator under the FMA, although it acts in consultation with the PA for systemically important market infrastructures and with the SARB on settlement-system matters. Market-abuse offences (insider trading, market manipulation, prohibited trading practices) are defined in Chapter X of the FMA, and the FSCA's Directorate of Market Abuse investigates suspected cases.

Adjacent legislation that the FSCA administers, in whole or in part, includes the Collective Investment Schemes Control Act, the Pension Funds Act, the Credit Rating Services Act, the Friendly Societies Act, and (after the 2022 Declaration) the FAIS Act as it applies to crypto assets. The Protection of Personal Information Act 4 of 2013 (POPI Act) is enforced by the Information Regulator rather than the FSCA, but data-protection failings often appear in FSCA enforcement files as evidence of broader conduct weaknesses.

The COFI Bill

The Conduct of Financial Institutions Bill (COFI Bill) is the most significant piece of pending financial-sector legislation in South Africa. First published for comment in 2018 and revised in 2020 and 2022, it has not yet been enacted as of mid-2026, although enactment is widely expected. Its purpose is to consolidate the patchwork of sector-specific conduct laws (the FAIS Act, the Long-Term Insurance Act, the Short-Term Insurance Act, the FMA conduct provisions, the CISCA conduct provisions, the Pension Funds Act conduct provisions) into a single activity-based conduct framework.

Under COFI, every regulated financial institution would be licensed for the activities it performs rather than for the legal form it takes, and would be subject to a common set of conduct obligations expressed in terms of the TCF outcomes. The FSCA's rule-making powers would expand considerably: it would be the principal source of detailed conduct rules across the sector. Practitioners are preparing for COFI through the FSCA's omni-conduct-based regulation (omni-CBR) framework, which is a transitional supervisory programme aligning current sectoral conduct rules to the activity-based COFI architecture.

Recent priorities

Four supervisory themes have dominated FSCA work in the period 2022 to 2026, and any of them is fair examination ground:

Retail distribution reform (RDR)
An ongoing programme inherited from the FSB to reform how advice and intermediary services are remunerated. Aims to remove conflicts of interest between adviser remuneration and product recommendation. Implemented in waves; Phase 1 in 2018 and 2019 covered commission structures for insurance, with later phases covering banking and investment products.
Crypto assets as financial products (2022)
By Declaration in the Government Gazette of 19 October 2022, crypto assets became a financial product under the FAIS Act. Crypto-asset service providers must therefore be FSP-licensed. The FSCA has been granting and refusing licences since 2023 and has imposed administrative penalties on unlicensed operators.
Two-pot retirement system (2024)
A reform to the Pension Funds Act effective 1 September 2024, splitting retirement-fund contributions into a "savings pot" (one third, with limited pre-retirement withdrawal allowed) and a "retirement pot" (two thirds, preserved to retirement). The FSCA supervises trustee implementation, member communication, and fund readiness.
BankServ Africa and payments conduct
The 2024 review of payment-system conduct, particularly around debit-order disputes (DebiCheck) and unauthorised debits, has resulted in fresh joint guidance from the FSCA and the PA. An indicator of how operational-resilience topics increasingly straddle the Twin Peaks boundary.
Transformation in financial services
A non-prudential, non-conduct objective placed on the FSCA by the FSR Act preamble: to support transformation of the financial sector. Operationalised through reporting requirements, supervisory attention to BBBEE compliance in the supervised population, and engagement with the Financial Sector Code.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Enforcement is the FSCA's most visible output

If you want to know what the FSCA actually cares about, read its enforcement actions before you read its standards. The standards say what the rules are. The enforcement actions tell you which rules are taken seriously, which sub-populations are under the most pressure, and which kinds of breach attract the heaviest penalties. Every administrative penalty is published on the FSCA website with the firm or individual named and the conduct described. This is a deliberate supervisory tool: public naming is itself a deterrent, and the published files are an evidence base for supervisory expectations.

The PA and FSCA boundary is occasionally blurred

The cleanest cases sit firmly on one side or the other: capital is prudential, advertising is conduct. But several topics straddle the line: outsourcing arrangements, operational resilience, IT governance, fraud prevention, and (increasingly) climate risk. These are typically dealt with through Joint Standards. For an actuary inside a regulated firm, the practical implication is that on these topics you may face two supervisory engagements running in parallel, asking similar but not identical questions. The Joint Standard text is the same; the supervisory style and emphasis are not.

Watch out The TCF framework is a set of outcomes, not a set of rules. The FSCA does not write a rule book that says "you must do X to achieve Outcome 4". It looks at the firm's conduct and the customer's experience and judges whether the outcome has been delivered. This is a different supervisory style from prudential rule-application, and it produces a different kind of supervisory pressure: a firm cannot demonstrate compliance simply by ticking the boxes in a checklist. It must produce evidence that the outcomes are being achieved in practice, and the FSCA reserves the right to disagree with the firm's interpretation of its own evidence.

TCF outcomes 1, 4 and 5 are the F107 staples

In the past five years of F107 papers, the TCF outcomes most likely to appear in scenario-based questions have been Outcome 1 (culture), Outcome 4 (advice suitability) and Outcome 5 (product performance). Outcomes 2 and 6 (product design and post-sale barriers) are less commonly tested but worth knowing. Outcome 3 (disclosure) is occasionally tested in the context of complex products such as guaranteed investment plans or with-profit life policies.

COFI will reshape this entire landscape

The current regime is built on a stack of sector-specific Acts (FAIS, FMA, CISCA, Pension Funds Act, Long-Term and Short-Term Insurance Acts, Banks Act conduct provisions). COFI replaces this with a single activity-based law. When that change happens, every firm in the FSCA's perimeter will need to migrate to a new licensing regime, and most of the existing Conduct Standards will be re-issued under COFI authority rather than under the sectoral Acts. For an F107 candidate, the exam-relevant points are: (a) COFI is pending and not yet law; (b) when enacted, it will consolidate the conduct framework; (c) the FSCA's omni-CBR programme is the transitional supervisory framework being prepared for COFI day-one.

The FSCA has limited resources relative to its scope

About 11,000 FSPs, 5,500 retirement funds, every CIS scheme, every exchange, every bank's retail business, every insurance product: this is an unusually wide perimeter for a single conduct regulator. The FSCA cannot inspect every firm every year, and supervisory engagement is therefore risk-based and intelligence-led. Firms that produce poor complaints data, that show up in mystery-shopping output, or that are flagged by whistleblowers get attention. Firms with clean records can go years without on-site supervisory engagement. The supervisory style is therefore reactive at the bottom of the population and intensive at the top.
