Lectures / Regulation / South Africa

NCA and the National Credit Regulator

The National Credit Act 34 of 2005 is the single statute that governs almost every consumer credit agreement in South Africa, from a R200 store account to a R3 million mortgage. It compels lenders to assess affordability before lending, prohibits reckless extension of credit, caps the cost of credit, and gives over-indebted consumers a statutory route into debt review. The National Credit Regulator, established by the Act and reporting to the Department of Trade, Industry and Competition, enforces the regime: it registers credit providers, debt counsellors, and credit bureaux, investigates contraventions, and refers matters to the National Consumer Tribunal. Unlike the PA and FSCA, the NCR sits outside the Twin Peaks architecture.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The National Credit Act 34 of 2005 (NCA) is the comprehensive consumer-credit statute for South Africa. It came into force in three phases between June 2006 and June 2007, replacing the Usury Act 1968, the Credit Agreements Act 1980, and a patchwork of microlending exemption notices. It does five main things. First, it requires every credit provider above a R500,000 net-asset threshold to register with the regulator. Second, it requires every credit provider, before extending credit, to conduct an affordability assessment using a methodology prescribed in regulations. Third, it prohibits reckless credit and gives the courts power to set aside agreements that breach the prohibition. Fourth, it caps the total cost of credit (interest, initiation fees, monthly service fees, credit-life premiums, default charges) through regulations recalibrated periodically. Fifth, it creates a statutory debt review process for over-indebted consumers, run by registered debt counsellors and supervised by the courts and the National Consumer Tribunal.

The National Credit Regulator (NCR) is the body the Act establishes to enforce all of this. It registers credit providers, debt counsellors, payment distribution agents, and credit bureaux; it investigates complaints and contraventions; it conducts compliance examinations; and it refers contested matters to the National Consumer Tribunal for adjudication. The NCR is a statutory body that reports to the Minister of Trade, Industry and Competition, not to the Minister of Finance. That detail matters: the NCR is functionally adjacent to the FSCA but legally separate, sitting outside the Twin Peaks framework established by the Financial Sector Regulation Act 2017.

Feynman test Imagine a country where, twenty years ago, anyone with a desk and a chequebook could lend to anyone, at any rate, for any purpose, with no obligation to check whether the borrower could afford to repay. Households got buried in debt. Parliament passed one statute that says three things to every lender. Before you lend, work out whether the borrower can afford the repayment using a sum we will write down for you. Do not lend more than this cap on the interest and fees. If we ever find you lent recklessly, a court can wipe out the loan. Then it sets up an office, the NCR, whose only job is to police that statute and license the credit-counselling profession that helps borrowers who are already drowning. The NCA is the law. The NCR is the office. Together they convert consumer credit from a caveat-emptor market into a lender-obligation market.

Step 2

Why this matters

Consumer credit in South Africa is enormous in scale and abusive in history. The NCR's Credit Bureau Monitor records roughly 28 million credit-active consumers and total outstanding consumer debt of approximately R2.3 trillion across mortgages, vehicle finance, credit cards, store accounts, and unsecured personal loans. Before the NCA, this market was governed by three statutes with overlapping scope and material gaps: the Usury Act capped interest rates but exempted microlenders below a small loan threshold; the Credit Agreements Act regulated instalment-sale credit but not unsecured lending; and a Department of Trade and Industry exemption notice from 1992 created the so-called microlending exception that allowed unsecured lenders to charge rates above the Usury cap provided the loan was under a specified ceiling. The result by the late 1990s was a hyper-active microlending sector with average effective annual rates above 100 per cent and routine garnishee-order abuse.

The De Jager Committee, appointed by the Department of Trade and Industry in 2002, recommended consolidation into a single consumer-credit statute. The NCA was the result. It came into force in three phases starting June 2006, with the affordability and reckless-credit provisions effective from June 2007. The reform shifted the regulatory model from caveat emptor to lender obligation: before the NCA, the borrower was expected to know what they were signing; after the NCA, the lender bears a statutory duty to verify ability to repay, and the legal consequence for breach is potential extinguishment of the debt.

For a bank, the NCA shapes the entire consumer-credit origination process. Application data must be sufficient to satisfy the affordability methodology. Scorecards must accept or reject in line with the affordability outcome, not just the predicted default risk. Pricing must respect the cost-of-credit caps under section 105. Front-line staff must be trained to recognise consumers in distress and to refer them to debt counsellors rather than to collections. For an actuary, three consequences matter most. The first is that the affordability regulations effectively impose a minimum loss-given-default characteristic on retail portfolios, because they constrain which consumers a bank may lend to, regardless of model output. The second is that reckless-credit litigation produces a tail of historical originations whose loss is determined by the courts rather than by behavioural performance, and which must be reflected in IFRS 9 stage 3 calibration. The third is that the cost-of-credit caps interact with risk-based pricing in a way that compresses margin on the highest-risk borrowers and pushes them out of the formal market into informal lending.

Step 3

The mental model

The NCA architecture is best understood as three layers. The top layer is the Act itself and its regulations. The middle layer is the regulator, the NCR, which licenses and supervises. The bottom layer is the actors the Act regulates: credit providers, credit bureaux, debt counsellors, and consumers. Sitting beside the NCR are two adjudicative venues, the National Consumer Tribunal for regulatory contraventions and the magistrates' courts for civil enforcement and confirmation of debt-review consent orders. The whole structure sits under the Department of Trade, Industry and Competition (the DTIC), not under National Treasury or SARB.

Three flows of authority matter. Registration and supervision flow from the NCR to the regulated actors. Adjudication flows in two directions: the NCR refers contraventions to the National Consumer Tribunal, while consumers and credit providers raise civil questions about specific agreements in the magistrates' courts. Data flows continuously between credit providers and credit bureaux, both directions mandatory, with the NCR setting the data standards. The NCR's parent department is the DTIC, so policy direction (including new regulations and amendments) flows from the Minister of Trade, Industry and Competition rather than from the Minister of Finance.

Step 4

The detail

History: from Usury Act to NCA

Before the NCA, consumer credit in South Africa was regulated by three overlapping pieces of legislation, each addressing a slice of the market and none covering it comprehensively. The Usury Act 73 of 1968 capped interest rates on most loans but left the cap unindexed for inflation, so by the late 1990s its effective bite was uneven. The Credit Agreements Act 75 of 1980 governed instalment-sale agreements and hire-purchase, with prescribed disclosure and cooling-off provisions, but did not cover open-ended credit such as store accounts or unsecured personal loans. The Microlending Exemption Notice of 1992, issued by the Department of Trade and Industry, allowed lenders to operate outside the Usury cap provided the loan was below a specified ceiling (raised to R10,000 by 1999) and the term was below 36 months. The unintended consequence was a microlending sector charging effective annual rates routinely above 100 per cent, with garnishee orders being abused to deduct repayments at source from borrower salaries without effective court supervision.

The De Jager Committee, appointed in 2002 by the Department of Trade and Industry, recommended consolidating the three statutes into a single consumer-credit framework with explicit lender obligations. The NCA was passed in 2005, signed by the President in March 2006, and brought into force in three phases. The first phase (1 June 2006) established the NCR and its registration regime. The second phase (1 September 2006) brought into force the provisions on debt counselling and the National Consumer Tribunal. The third phase (1 June 2007), the most consequential, brought into force the substantive lending provisions: affordability assessment, reckless credit, cost-of-credit caps, and the procedural rules for credit agreements.

Two later amendments reshaped the framework. The National Credit Amendment Act 19 of 2014 tightened the affordability methodology, expanded NCR investigative powers, and introduced clearer rules on credit-life insurance pricing. The National Credit Amendment Act 7 of 2019 introduced a statutory debt-intervention regime for over-indebted low-income consumers, allowing partial or total debt write-off after NCR review and Tribunal order. The 2019 amendment had its commencement deferred pending finalisation of supporting regulations and capacity building at the NCR, and the debt-intervention regime remains only partially operational as of 2026.

Scope: who and what is covered (section 4)

Section 4 of the NCA defines its scope expansively. The Act applies to every credit agreement between a credit provider and a consumer concluded or having effect within the Republic, subject to limited exclusions. The four categories of credit agreement are credit facilities (revolving credit, store accounts, credit cards), credit transactions (instalment sales, mortgages, vehicle finance, secured and unsecured loans), credit guarantees, and a residual category for any other agreement involving the provision of money or goods on deferred payment.

The exclusions are narrowly defined and worth knowing in detail. The NCA does not apply to credit agreements where the consumer is a juristic person (a company, close corporation, or trust) with an asset value or annual turnover of R1 million or more. It does not apply to credit agreements above R250,000 entered into by juristic persons with smaller balance sheets. Stokvels (rotating savings and credit associations) are excluded provided they meet specific registration requirements. Pawn transactions below a specified threshold are excluded. Policy-loan facilities under long-term insurance policies are excluded. Outside these carve-outs, every loan, lease, mortgage, instalment sale, store card, and credit-card account falls within scope.

Threshold of R500,000 for credit-provider registration
Section 40 requires registration as a credit provider for any person who, as a principal business or as part of its business, enters into credit agreements where the total principal debt under all outstanding agreements exceeds R500,000. Below the threshold, registration is voluntary. Every commercial bank and major retailer in South Africa is registered.
Small, intermediate, and large agreements
The NCA distinguishes three sizes of agreement, which affects which procedural rules apply. Small agreements are credit facilities up to R15,000 or other agreements up to R15,000. Intermediate agreements are credit facilities or other agreements between R15,000 and R250,000. Large agreements are above R250,000 or any mortgage. Disclosure requirements and cooling-off rights vary by size.
Developmental credit
A separate category for credit extended to low-income or small-enterprise borrowers under specific developmental objectives. Developmental credit providers register under a distinct sub-regime with lighter cost-of-credit constraints, intended to crowd in formal lending to underserved segments.

Registration of credit providers (sections 40 to 53)

A person who, in the ordinary course of business, enters into credit agreements as the credit provider must register with the NCR if the principal-debt total across all outstanding agreements exceeds R500,000. The registration application requires disclosure of the legal form of the applicant, ownership and control, fit-and-proper status of directors and senior management, the categories of credit the applicant intends to extend, and the projected book size. Registration is subject to renewal and to ongoing conditions, including the obligation to submit statutory returns to the NCR and to comply with the affordability and reckless-credit provisions.

Three other categories of registration sit alongside credit-provider registration. Debt counsellors must register under section 44 and meet prescribed training and fit-and-proper standards. Credit bureaux must register under section 43 and comply with data-quality, retention, and access rules. Payment distribution agents (PDAs), which collect debt-review repayments from consumers and distribute them to credit providers, must register and operate under prudential controls including trust-account requirements. Operating in any of the four roles without registration is a criminal offence under section 156 and an automatic referral to the National Consumer Tribunal.

Affordability assessment (section 81 and the Affordability Regulations 2015)

Section 81 places a positive duty on every credit provider, before entering into a credit agreement, to take reasonable steps to assess the consumer's existing financial means, prospects, and obligations, and the consumer's debt repayment history under previous credit agreements. The methodology for doing so was non-prescriptive until 2015, when the Affordability Regulations were published. Those Regulations replaced the previous methodology and set out a step-by-step calculation that every credit provider must apply (subject to limited exemptions).

The 2015 methodology has three steps. First, the credit provider must verify the consumer's gross income by reference to either (a) three months of payslips, (b) three months of bank statements showing salary credits, or (c) a combination of documentary evidence and bank statements where the income is irregular (self-employed, contract, or commission-based). Second, the credit provider must apply a minimum living-expense table prescribed in the Regulations, indexed to gross income, that sets a floor on the assumed monthly cost of food, utilities, transport, and other essentials. The table is structured so that lower-income consumers receive a higher relative living-expense allowance. Third, the credit provider must deduct existing debt service obligations (verified against the consumer's credit-bureau record) from gross income, after subtracting living expenses and statutory deductions (PAYE, UIF), to compute discretionary income available for new credit. The instalment under the proposed new agreement must fit within the discretionary-income envelope.

Minimum expenses table (Regulation 23A, table A)
A prescribed table of minimum monthly living expenses indexed to gross income. Bands run from below R800 (R 800 assumed minimum) up to above R20,000 (R6,251 plus a percentage of gross income above the band). Lenders may use higher figures if the consumer's declared expenses exceed the minimum, but never lower. The table is a floor, not a ceiling.
Verified income, not declared income
The 2015 Regulations are explicit that gross income must be verified by documentary evidence. A consumer's bare declaration is not sufficient. This is the single change that most reshaped retail credit origination in 2015, because it forced documentation collection on segments previously approved on declaration alone.
Exemptions: mortgages and student loans
A 2017 amendment to the Regulations narrowed the exemptions previously available for home loans and student credit. As of 2026, the exemption regime is limited and the prescribed methodology applies to almost all consumer credit, including first-time home loans.
Discretionary income
Gross income minus statutory deductions minus minimum living expenses minus existing debt service. The remaining envelope must accommodate the instalment on the proposed new credit at the contractual rate. If the instalment exceeds discretionary income, the credit must not be extended.

Reckless credit (sections 80 to 83)

Section 80 defines a credit agreement as reckless if, at the time the agreement was concluded, any of three conditions was true. The first condition is that the credit provider failed to conduct an affordability assessment as required by section 81. The second is that the credit provider did conduct an assessment but the assessment failed to identify that the consumer would become over-indebted by entering into the agreement. The third is that the credit provider, having conducted the assessment, entered into the agreement notwithstanding actual knowledge that the consumer would become over-indebted. The first ground is the most commonly litigated: where a credit provider has no documentary evidence of an affordability assessment, the agreement is recklessly extended as a matter of law.

The consequences are set out in section 83. A court (or the Tribunal in regulator-led proceedings) that finds an agreement reckless may make any of three orders. It may set aside, in whole or in part, all or part of the consumer's rights and obligations under the agreement. It may suspend the force and effect of the agreement for a period it considers appropriate. Or, where the reckless credit caused or contributed to over-indebtedness, it may restructure the consumer's obligations alongside debt review. The most severe form is total set-aside: the debt is declared extinguished, the credit provider loses the loan, and any judgment already obtained against the consumer is rescinded.

Section 80(1)(a): no assessment
Reckless credit by procedural failure. The credit provider did not assess affordability at all, or the assessment did not meet the prescribed standard. Easiest ground for a consumer to plead because it does not require proof of over-indebtedness at origination, only proof of methodological failure.
Section 80(1)(b)(i): assessment failed
Reckless credit by substantive failure. The assessment was conducted but produced the wrong answer: the consumer would have been over-indebted on a correctly applied methodology. Requires the court to reapply the methodology on the available facts.
Section 80(1)(b)(ii): assessment ignored
Reckless credit by deliberate breach. The assessment correctly identified that the consumer would be over-indebted, and the credit provider extended the credit anyway. Rare in practice because it requires evidence of subjective knowledge, but the most morally serious ground.

Reckless-credit defences are raised both proactively (a consumer asks a court to declare an agreement reckless) and defensively (a consumer in receipt of a summons or judgment raises reckless credit as a defence to enforcement). The 2014 amendment made the Tribunal a competent forum for reckless-credit findings in regulator-led proceedings, alongside the magistrates' courts. The seminal case for retail credit was the regulator's enforcement action against African Bank in 2013 to 2014, which contributed to the bank's curatorship in August 2014.

Cost of credit (sections 100 to 105 and the Regulations Relating to Cost of Credit)

Section 100 lists the only six categories of charge a credit provider may impose on a consumer under a credit agreement. Section 101 prescribes the maximum amounts for the categories that are quantitatively capped. Section 105 empowers the Minister to set the maximum rates and amounts by regulation. The current rates are in the Regulations Relating to Cost of Credit, last materially revised in 2016 with periodic indexation since.

Interest
Maximum annual interest rates vary by sub-category of credit: mortgage agreements at repo plus a multiple, credit facilities at repo plus a higher multiple, unsecured credit at repo plus a still-higher multiple, short-term credit (a separate category capped at 5 per cent per month for the first month). All caps are indexed to the SARB repo rate.
Initiation fee
A one-off charge for the cost of entering into the agreement. Capped at a rand amount that scales with the size of the agreement: small agreements at R165 plus a percentage above a threshold, larger agreements at higher caps. Cannot exceed 15 per cent of the principal debt.
Service fee
A monthly charge for the routine administration of the account. Capped at R60 per month (subject to indexation). Cannot be charged on credit facilities below a small balance threshold.
Credit-life insurance
Premiums for credit-life insurance, capped under the Credit Life Insurance Regulations 2017. The 2017 regulations set the maximum premium at R4.50 per R1,000 of deferred amount for unsecured credit, with lower caps for secured credit. Bundling and forced-product abuses are explicitly prohibited.
Default administration charges
Amounts that may be added to a consumer's account when the account falls into default. Capped at the amount provided for in the Magistrates' Courts Act tariffs. Cannot exceed the actual cost of administering the default.
Collection costs
Recoverable costs of collecting on a defaulted account. Capped at the Magistrates' Courts Act prescribed scale. Cannot include costs the credit provider would have borne in any event.

The combination of section 100 and the cost-of-credit cap produces an aggregate ceiling on what a credit provider can charge a consumer over the life of the agreement. The interaction with risk-based pricing matters for an actuary: the highest-risk borrowers in a price-rationing market would, on a pure expected-loss basis, attract rates above the cap. Because they cannot legally be priced into the formal market, they either receive credit at a loss-making rate (cross-subsidised by lower-risk borrowers) or are denied credit and migrate to the informal sector. The implication is that the cap creates a structural ceiling on the risk-acceptance frontier of the formal market.

Debt review (sections 86 to 88)

Sections 86 to 88 create a statutory debt-review mechanism for over-indebted consumers. A consumer who believes they are over-indebted may apply to a registered debt counsellor for debt review under section 86. The counsellor assesses the consumer's financial position, computes the consumer's monthly income and obligations, and proposes a debt-restructuring plan. If the credit providers agree to the plan, it becomes a consent order under section 86(7)(a). If they do not agree, the counsellor refers the matter to a magistrates' court under section 86(7)(c) for a court-ordered restructuring under section 87.

During the debt review process, the consumer is protected against legal action by credit providers for default under any agreement included in the review, with limited exceptions. Repayments are routed through a registered payment distribution agent (PDA), which collects from the consumer and distributes to the credit providers in line with the restructured plan. The review concludes when the consumer has either complied with the plan to the point of being no longer over-indebted (a clearance certificate is issued under section 71) or has failed to comply and the credit providers regain their enforcement rights.

Over-indebtedness (section 79)
A consumer is over-indebted if, having regard to all relevant information, it appears the consumer is or will be unable to satisfy in a timely manner all the obligations under all the credit agreements to which the consumer is a party. Test is forward-looking and based on prevailing circumstances. Standard differs from insolvency in being focused on debt-service capacity rather than balance-sheet condition.
Debt counsellor
A natural person registered with the NCR under section 44. Required to hold prescribed qualifications, complete debt-counselling training, and meet fit-and-proper requirements. Approximately 1,600 registered debt counsellors operate in South Africa. Counsellors charge consumers a prescribed fee for the initial application and ongoing administration.
Payment distribution agent (PDA)
A registered entity that collects monthly repayments from consumers under debt review and distributes them to the credit providers according to the restructured plan. PDAs must operate trust accounts and submit regular returns to the NCR. Two large PDAs (Hyphen, DCASA) handle most of the volume.
Clearance certificate
Issued by the debt counsellor under section 71 once the consumer has either repaid all the debts in the restructured plan or is no longer over-indebted. The certificate triggers removal of debt-review flags from the consumer's credit-bureau record and restores access to new credit.

Debt review has grown into a substantial sub-industry. The NCR's quarterly Consumer Credit Market Report records ongoing growth in active debt-review cases, with hundreds of thousands of consumers under review at any time. From a credit provider's perspective, debt review is operationally costly (case-by-case restructuring negotiations, PDA reconciliation, court applications) but provides a higher recovery than uncoordinated enforcement on over-indebted consumers. From an IFRS 9 perspective, accounts under debt review must be classified as stage 3 (credit-impaired) regardless of arrears status, because the consumer has formally acknowledged inability to meet original obligations.

The National Credit Amendment Act 7 of 2019: debt intervention

The 2019 amendment introduced a new statutory debt-relief mechanism, debt intervention, aimed at the segment of over-indebted consumers too poor to support a debt-review repayment plan. Eligibility is restricted to consumers with gross monthly income at or below R7,500 and total unsecured debt at or below R50,000, who are over-indebted. Eligible consumers apply to the NCR (not to a private debt counsellor) for debt intervention. The NCR investigates and, where appropriate, refers the matter to the National Consumer Tribunal, which may order the debts to be suspended for twelve months, with reinstatement subject to the consumer's circumstances at the end of the period. Where the consumer's circumstances have not materially improved, the Tribunal may order partial or total debt extinguishment.

The debt-intervention regime is administratively distinctive in three ways. It is the NCR's only statutory role as a primary case-handler for consumer applications (debt review is run by private counsellors). It empowers the Tribunal to extinguish debt without the credit provider's consent. And it implements a true debt-write-off mechanism in South African law, comparable in effect to a means-tested personal insolvency. The full regime was not in operation as of 2026 pending finalisation of supporting regulations and capacity building at the NCR. Credit providers should expect significant operational impact when it goes fully live, including write-off losses on small-balance unsecured portfolios and IFRS 9 provisioning effects for population-segment LGD assumptions.

The NCR: structure and enforcement record

The NCR is established by section 12 of the NCA as a juristic person funded by levies on regulated entities and by parliamentary appropriation. It is governed by a Board of Directors appointed by the Minister of Trade, Industry and Competition, with day-to-day operations led by a Chief Executive Officer. The NCR has approximately 200 staff organised across registrations, investigations and enforcement, statistics and research (publishing the Consumer Credit Market Report and the Credit Bureau Monitor), and consumer education.

Enforcement actions take three main forms. The NCR conducts compliance examinations of credit providers, debt counsellors, credit bureaux, and PDAs, with findings either resolved by a compliance notice (an administrative direction to remedy the breach within a specified period) or referred to the National Consumer Tribunal for adjudication and penalty. The NCR investigates complaints from consumers, either resolving them administratively or referring them to the Tribunal. And the NCR brings own-initiative enforcement actions against systemic breaches, including the high-profile African Bank reckless-lending investigation in 2013 to 2014 and a sequence of cases against unsecured lenders and microlenders since.

The National Consumer Tribunal is a separate statutory body established under the Consumer Protection Act 2008 and given jurisdiction over the NCA by the 2014 amendment. The Tribunal hears matters referred by the NCR and matters initiated by consumers, with the power to impose administrative penalties up to 10 per cent of the respondent's annual turnover (or R1 million, whichever is greater), to declare conduct prohibited, and to order restitution. Tribunal decisions are subject to appeal to the High Court.

Relationship to the Twin Peaks regulators

The NCR is functionally adjacent to the FSCA but legally separate. Both regulate financial-sector conduct, but the NCR's scope is limited to consumer credit agreements under the NCA, while the FSCA's scope is the broader market-conduct mandate under the FSR Act, the FAIS Act, and the various sector-specific statutes. Where a single transaction implicates both regimes (typically, the sale of credit-life insurance alongside a credit agreement), the credit aspects are NCR-regulated and the insurance-distribution aspects are FSCA-regulated. Coordination is through a memorandum of understanding between the NCR and the FSCA, originally signed under the predecessor FSB and renewed under the FSR Act.

The Prudential Authority's mandate (solvency and capital of banks) does not overlap with the NCR's mandate (conduct in consumer credit). A South African bank is therefore simultaneously regulated by the PA for capital and liquidity, by the FSCA for general market conduct and for the sale of insurance and investment products, and by the NCR for everything connected with its consumer-credit book. The Conduct of Financial Institutions Bill (the COFI Bill), in development since 2018, would in principle consolidate the conduct rules under a single statute and could in time absorb the NCR's mandate. As of 2026, the COFI Bill has not been enacted and the three-regulator architecture (PA, FSCA, NCR) remains in place.

Worked example: affordability assessment for a vehicle loan

Scenario. A consumer, Ms M, applies to a registered bank for a R200,000 instalment-sale agreement to finance a used vehicle over 60 months at a contractual rate of 18 per cent per annum (within the section 105 cap). Her verified gross monthly salary is R10,000. She has a credit-bureau record showing one existing unsecured personal loan with a monthly instalment of R900 and a credit card with a minimum monthly payment of R350. The bank must apply the 2015 Affordability Regulations to decide whether to approve.

Step 1: verify gross income. Three months of payslips confirm gross monthly salary of R10,000. (Step satisfied by documentary evidence; declaration alone is insufficient under the 2015 Regulations.)

Step 2: apply the minimum living-expense table. For a consumer with gross monthly income of R10,000, the prescribed minimum monthly living expense from the Regulation 23A table is approximately R2,400 (the exact figure depends on the most recent indexation of the table; this lecture uses representative figures). The bank may use a higher figure if Ms M's declared living expenses are higher, but cannot use a lower figure.

Step 3: compute statutory deductions. PAYE and UIF on a gross of R10,000 amount to approximately R600. Net of statutory deductions, gross-of-living-expenses income is R10,000 minus R600 = R9,400.

Step 4: compute existing debt service. Personal loan instalment R900 plus credit-card minimum R350 = R1,250 per month.

Step 5: compute discretionary income. R9,400 minus R2,400 (living expenses) minus R1,250 (existing debt) = R5,750 per month.

Step 6: compute proposed instalment. A R200,000 vehicle loan over 60 months at 18 per cent per annum produces an instalment of approximately R5,080 per month (standard amortisation formula).

Step 7: compare and decide. The proposed instalment of R5,080 fits within the discretionary-income envelope of R5,750. The bank may approve, subject to the broader credit-scoring decision and the cost-of-credit caps. Had the proposed instalment exceeded R5,750, the bank would have been legally required to decline or to reduce the loan amount to one whose instalment fits inside the envelope, regardless of the scorecard outcome.

Reckless-credit risk. If the bank had not collected payslips, or had used a lower living-expense figure than the prescribed minimum, or had missed the credit-bureau record of existing debt, the agreement would be vulnerable to a section 80(1)(a) reckless-credit challenge. The consequence under section 83 could be set-aside of the agreement and extinguishment of the R200,000 debt. The litigation risk is real and the bank must retain documentary evidence of every step.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The NCA has reshaped consumer credit more than any other modern SA statute

Between 2007 and 2026 the NCA produced the most material structural reform of South African consumer finance in living memory. Unsecured-credit losses peaked in the African Bank curatorship of 2014. The 2015 Affordability Regulations cut new origination volumes in unsecured personal loans by an estimated 20 to 30 per cent over the following two years, because the verified-income requirement excluded a swath of declaration-only originations. The 2017 credit-life insurance caps removed a multi-billion-rand annual revenue stream from credit providers. Each major step in the NCA timeline left a visible signature in retail-credit losses and origination volumes.

Reckless-credit litigation is now a portfolio-level risk, not a one-off

Plaintiff-side legal firms in South Africa have built debt-relief businesses around reckless-credit challenges. A successful claim wipes out the debt; the legal firm takes a percentage of the wiped-out value. The volume is meaningful enough that any bank's IFRS 9 stage 3 calibration on legacy retail portfolios must reflect a reckless-credit loss component, not just behavioural default. Documentation of the original affordability assessment is now the single most valuable record in the file, because its absence is itself an automatic ground for reckless credit under section 80(1)(a).

Watch out Do not confuse the NCR with the FSCA. Both regulate financial-sector conduct, but the NCR sits under the DTIC and is established by the NCA, while the FSCA sits under National Treasury and is established by the FSR Act. A bank reports to both. The conduct rules they apply are different, even when they target the same transaction (the sale of a credit-life policy alongside a loan is jointly regulated). Saying a bank is "FSCA-compliant" does not mean it is "NCR-compliant" and vice versa.

The affordability cap is binding before the scorecard

Credit-scoring models in South African retail banks predict default probability and inform pricing and limit-setting. The NCA-prescribed affordability calculation sits ahead of the scorecard in the approval logic: a consumer whose proposed instalment exceeds the discretionary-income envelope is declined regardless of the scorecard's prediction. The consequence for model design is that scorecard development populations are pre-filtered by affordability, so the observed default rates condition on having passed the affordability test. Recalibration after a change to the Affordability Regulations (as in 2015 and likely again at future revisions) shifts the development population materially and invalidates static calibrations.

Cost-of-credit caps compress risk-based pricing

Section 105 caps the interest rate on each category of credit. The cap is a binding constraint for high-risk segments where the actuarially fair rate (covering expected loss and required return) exceeds the cap. The bank cannot price above the cap, so it must either deny credit or accept a loss-making book. Most banks choose denial, which pushes the marginal high-risk borrower into informal lenders (loan sharks) operating outside the NCA. The cap is therefore a structural feature of the South African retail-credit risk frontier, not just a price control.

The 2019 debt-intervention regime is a contingent future loss

When the 2019 amendment's debt-intervention regime goes fully live, the NCR and Tribunal will be able to extinguish unsecured debt of consumers below the R7,500 income threshold and R50,000 unsecured-debt cap. The estimated affected population is hundreds of thousands of consumers. Banks with material exposure to the affected segment should treat the regime as a contingent loss against which to provision under IFRS 9 once commencement becomes credible. For F107 candidates, the 2019 amendment is the most likely "recent developments" question because it is the only major piece of consumer-finance legislation passed in the last decade still awaiting commencement.

The NCR's resource constraints define the enforcement frontier

The NCR's staff of approximately 200 cover registration, investigations, statistics, and consumer education across the entire South African consumer-credit market. The enforcement capacity is small relative to the regulated population and produces a long tail of unenforced contraventions. The credible enforcement risk for a credit provider is concentrated in two scenarios: large-scale industry-wide investigations (the African Bank model) and individual complaints from consumers (which are screened and only some referred to the Tribunal). The implication is that a bank with strong compliance documentation and few consumer complaints has a low probability of being the subject of a NCR enforcement action in any given year, but the consequence of being targeted in a major investigation is severe.
