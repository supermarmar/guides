Lectures / Regulation / South Africa

Banks Act 1990 and SARB Prudential Standards

The Banks Act 94 of 1990 is the parent statute for every commercial bank in South Africa. It vests prudential supervisory authority in the Prudential Authority and empowers the PA to make the Regulations Relating to Banks, the document that turns Basel III (and now Basel 3.1) into binding South African law. Sitting alongside the Regulations, Banks Act Directives and Prudential Standards under the Financial Sector Regulation Act fill in the operational detail. Knowing how the four layers fit together is the prerequisite for reading any South African bank's Pillar 3 disclosure.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The Banks Act 94 of 1990 is the foundational statute regulating commercial banks in South Africa. It provides for the registration of banks, prudential requirements, supervision, control over deposit-taking, and, since the 2021 amendments, an orderly resolution regime. Prudential powers under the Act were vested in the South African Reserve Bank's Bank Supervision Department until 2018; they now sit with the Prudential Authority (PA), a juristic person within SARB created by the Financial Sector Regulation Act 9 of 2017. The PA writes and enforces the Regulations Relating to Banks, a long, detailed instrument made under section 90 of the Banks Act that constitutes South Africa's transposition of the Basel III standards; the latest substantial amendments, gazetted in 2024, implement Basel 3.1. Supplementary instructions are issued through Banks Act Directives (the D-series), and cross-sectoral matters such as governance and recovery planning are covered by Prudential Standards issued under the FSR Act. Two companion statutes carve out separate regimes for non-commercial deposit-takers: the Mutual Banks Act 124 of 1993 and the Co-operative Banks Act 40 of 2007.

The architecture is more layered than the EU's or the UK's. In the EU, CRR is the directly applicable rulebook and EBA technical standards fill in the detail; in the UK, the PRA Rulebook plays the same role. In South Africa, four layers stack on top of each other: the Banks Act gives the PA its powers, the Regulations are the Basel rulebook, the Directives are supplementary instructions, and the Prudential Standards address topics that cut across banks, insurers, and financial conglomerates. The whole stack is enforced by the PA against six classes of registered entity: locally incorporated banks, branches of foreign banks, representative offices of foreign banks, controlling companies, mutual banks, and co-operative banks.

Feynman test Imagine you want to start a bank in Johannesburg. You walk into the Prudential Authority's office with R250 million and a business plan. The first question they ask is "have you read section 12 of the Banks Act?" That section says you cannot take a single rand of deposit from the public unless you are registered. The second question is "have you read the Regulations Relating to Banks?" Those regulations tell you how much capital you must hold against every loan you will ever make, in numbers that come directly from a room in Basel. The third question is "have you read the relevant Banks Act Directives?" Those tell you, for instance, exactly how to count your liquidity buffer. And the fourth question is "have you read the Prudential Standards?" Those tell you how your board must be composed and what your recovery plan must contain. If you can pass all four exams, the PA gives you a banking licence, signed by the CEO of the PA on behalf of the Governor of SARB. The Banks Act is the door. The Regulations are the rulebook. The Directives are the footnotes. The Prudential Standards are the etiquette.
What is beyond the source The source wiki note is a stub: a single reference link plus a sentence noting that the Regulations Relating to Banks implement Basel and a heading for Chapter VI. This lecture supplies the full statutory architecture, including the post-2017 split of regulatory authority between the PA and the FSCA under the Twin Peaks model; the 2024 amendments to the Regulations Relating to Banks that implement Basel 3.1 (revised standardised credit, SMA operational risk, FRTB market risk on a transitional path, an output floor); the Banks Act Directives system (the D-series); the structure of the Prudential Standards; the resolution regime introduced by the Financial Sector Laws Amendment Act 23 of 2021 (FSLAA), including the Corporation for Deposit Insurance (CoDI) and the SARB resolution function; the D-SIB designation methodology and the binding capital impact on the big four plus Investec; and the foreign-bank branch regime, which is notably restrictive in South Africa compared with the UK or Singapore.
Step 2

Why this matters

For any institution operating as a bank in South Africa, the Banks Act is the source of supervisory authority and the Regulations Relating to Banks are the prudential rulebook. Every capital adequacy calculation, every liquidity report, every large-exposure return, and every Pillar 3 disclosure flows from the Regulations. If you cannot read the Regulations and trace each requirement back to the Basel standard it implements, you cannot interpret your bank's capital position with any confidence. The Regulations are also the place where local tailoring is visible: where the PA has chosen to apply national discretion, the discretion is exercised here.

For an actuary working in credit risk, the Regulations contain the IRB rules. The treatment of probability of default, loss given default, exposure at default, the maturity adjustment, the asset-correlation formulas, and the supervisory floors all sit in the Regulations and follow the Basel III text closely, with the exceptions introduced by the 2024 Basel 3.1 amendments. The 72.5 per cent output floor, the constraints on A-IRB for large corporates and financial institutions, the new standardised approach for operational risk, and the FRTB transitional arrangements are all live and visible in the 2024 amendments.

For an actuary working in liquidity, the LCR and NSFR rules sit in the Regulations and in the supporting Directives. The LCR has been held at 100 per cent (with a temporary relaxation to 80 per cent during COVID, since reversed) and the NSFR at 100 per cent. The Regulations also house the local interpretation of high-quality liquid asset categories, which matters because South African government debt and SARB bills dominate the level 1 stock.

For F107 candidates, the Act and the Regulations are examinable together. Examiners expect candidates to know which prudential requirement sits in which instrument (capital ratios in the Regulations; bank licensing in the Act; D-SIB framework in Directive D7/2017 and now the Prudential Standards), and which post-crisis reform sits in which amendment cycle. The hierarchy is the examinable structure; the numbers are the examinable detail.

Step 3

The mental model

Two statutes meet at the Prudential Authority. The Banks Act 1990 is a single-sector statute, focused on banks, that empowers the PA to make the Regulations Relating to Banks. The FSR Act 2017 is a cross-sectoral statute, focused on all prudentially regulated financial institutions, that empowers the PA to make Prudential Standards. The PA enforces both. The Regulations Relating to Banks and the Prudential Standards are siblings, not parent and child.

Three flows of authority matter. Statutory power flows down from Parliament through the two statutes to the PA. Rule-making flows down from the PA through the Regulations, Directives, and Prudential Standards to regulated entities. And supervisory information flows up from regulated entities through prudential returns and reviews to the PA and, in turn, to the South African Reserve Bank's Financial Stability Committee.

Step 4

The detail

Banks Act 94 of 1990: structure

The Banks Act is organised in ten chapters, with subsequent amendment acts (most notably the Banks Amendment Acts of 2003, 2007, and 2015, and the Financial Sector Laws Amendment Act 2021) layered on top. The chapter structure has remained stable since the 1990 enactment and is worth memorising because it is the navigation key to the Act.

Chapter I, Administration
Definitions, the appointment of the Registrar of Banks (now the CEO of the Prudential Authority), and the powers of the supervisor. Section 1 contains the definitions, including the definition of "the business of a bank", which determines who falls inside the perimeter.
Chapter II, Registration of banks
Sections 11 to 22. The licensing regime. Only a registered bank or an authorised branch of a foreign bank may carry on the business of a bank in South Africa. Application requirements, refusal grounds, conditions of registration, and the controlling company concept.
Chapter III, Control of deposit-taking
The deposit-taking perimeter. Section 11 makes it an offence to conduct the business of a bank without authorisation. Section 78 grants powers to investigate unauthorised deposit-taking. The PA has used these powers against pyramid schemes and unauthorised crypto-asset operators.
Chapter IV, Control over banks
Sections governing day-to-day supervision. Conditions of registration, directives, inspections, on-site reviews. Section 60 provides the inspection power; sections 6 and 6A provide the directive-making power exercised through the D-series.
Chapter V, Control over controlling companies
Group-level prudential supervision. Any company that controls a bank must be separately registered as a controlling company. The PA supervises both the bank and its controlling company; consolidated capital requirements apply at the controlling-company level.
Chapter VI, Prudential requirements
The technical heart of the Act. Section 70 sets minimum share capital and unimpaired reserve funds; section 70A extends this to the banking group. Section 72 covers concentration of risk and large exposures. Section 73 covers minimum liquid asset requirements. Most of the detail is delegated to the Regulations Relating to Banks under section 90.
Chapter VII, Certain bank powers and duties
Special powers granted to banks, including the right to conduct business in foreign currency and certain duties towards depositors. Less heavily used than the surrounding chapters.
Chapter VIII, Reserves and dividends
Restrictions on dividend payments where capital is below regulatory minima. Section 79 requires PA approval before dividends are paid out of reserves.
Chapter IX, Resolution and curatorship (post-FSLAA)
Substantially rewritten by the Financial Sector Laws Amendment Act 23 of 2021. Designates SARB as the resolution authority. Provides for bail-in, bridge institutions, asset separation, and modified insolvency. Read alongside Chapter 12A of the FSR Act, which provides parallel resolution powers.
Chapter X, Offences, penalties, and general
Criminal offences under the Act, penalties, transitional provisions. Conducting bank business without authorisation is a criminal offence punishable by imprisonment.

Registration and the controlling company concept

The Banks Act distinguishes between three forms of foreign-bank participation: a locally incorporated subsidiary (a registered bank), a branch (subject to section 18A authorisation), and a representative office (which may not take deposits or do banking business at all). South Africa is restrictive on branches relative to the UK or Singapore: branches must be approved by the PA, are subject to local capital and liquidity requirements proportionate to their on-shore business, and most foreign banks therefore operate in South Africa as subsidiaries. Standard Chartered, Bank of China, HSBC, and Citibank all operate through registered branches; many other foreign players operate through smaller representative offices or have exited the market.

The controlling company concept is South Africa-specific in its precision. Any company that directly or indirectly controls a registered bank must itself be registered as a controlling company under Chapter V. Consolidated capital, leverage, and liquidity requirements apply at the controlling-company level. Among the big four, FirstRand Limited is the controlling company of FirstRand Bank; Standard Bank Group is the controlling company of The Standard Bank of South Africa; Nedbank Group is the controlling company of Nedbank Limited; and Absa Group is the controlling company of Absa Bank. Investec Limited is the controlling company of Investec Bank. The PA's published bank registers list controlling companies separately from banks.

Minimum capital on registration
Section 70 read with the Regulations requires a registered bank to have minimum share capital and unimpaired reserve funds of at least R250 million, or 8 per cent of risk-weighted assets, whichever is higher. This is the floor at registration; the Basel III capital ratios then operate on top.

The Regulations Relating to Banks

The Regulations Relating to Banks, made under section 90, are the working prudential rulebook. The current consolidated version was gazetted in 2012 (Government Notice R1029 of 12 December 2012) and has been amended approximately every two years since. The most significant recent amendments came in 2024 (Government Notice 4912 of 6 June 2024, with a follow-up gazette in late 2024) to implement Basel 3.1 with effect from 1 July 2025, with transitional arrangements through 2030.

The Regulations cover, in order:

Capital adequacy
CET1 minimum 4.5 per cent, plus AT1 1.5 per cent, plus T2 2 per cent, giving total capital 8 per cent of risk-weighted assets. Capital conservation buffer 2.5 per cent (CET1). Countercyclical buffer variable up to 2.5 per cent (currently set at zero for South Africa as of 2025). D-SIB higher loss absorbency surcharge of 1 to 2.5 per cent for designated systemically important banks. Full Basel III-aligned regulatory adjustments and prudential filters (goodwill, deferred tax assets, mortgage servicing rights, significant investments).
Leverage ratio
4 per cent of Tier 1 capital to total leverage exposure, above the 3 per cent Basel minimum. The 1 per cent uplift is a national discretion exercised by the PA. D-SIBs are subject to a further leverage buffer of half their CET1 G-SIB or D-SIB surcharge, in line with Basel III finalisation.
Liquidity Coverage Ratio
100 per cent of high-quality liquid assets to net stress outflows over 30 days. Temporarily relaxed to 80 per cent during COVID-19, restored to 100 per cent by January 2022. Level 1 HQLA dominated by South African government bonds, SARB bills, and central bank deposits.
Net Stable Funding Ratio
100 per cent of available stable funding to required stable funding over one year. In force since 1 January 2018. The NSFR has bound several South African banks at points in the cycle because of the structural reliance on wholesale funding in the local market.
Credit risk: standardised, F-IRB, A-IRB
All three Basel approaches available, subject to PA approval. From 1 July 2025, the 2024 amendments implement the Basel 3.1 constraints: A-IRB is no longer available for exposures to large corporates with revenue above EUR 500 million (rand-equivalent), banks, and other financial institutions, who must use F-IRB or the standardised approach. The revised standardised approach with greater risk sensitivity replaces the 2012 vintage. The 72.5 per cent output floor is phased in from 50 per cent in 2025 to 72.5 per cent in 2030.
Market risk
Transitional from 2024 to 2027. The pre-FRTB framework (standardised plus internal models for general and specific risk) continues during the transition. Full FRTB, with the Sensitivities-Based Method (SBM) and the Internal Models Approach (IMA) under expected shortfall, becomes mandatory from 1 January 2027. The PA's expectation is that few South African banks will adopt IMA; most will operate under SBM.
Operational risk: SMA
The new standardised approach to operational risk capital, based on a business indicator multiplied by a marginal coefficient with an internal loss multiplier (which the PA has fixed at 1 for South African banks). Replaces the legacy BIA, TSA, and AMA from 1 July 2025. Materially changes capital for several local banks because the internal loss multiplier choice is more conservative than the Basel option.
Large exposures
A bank's exposure to a single counterparty or group of connected counterparties may not exceed 25 per cent of its qualifying Tier 1 capital. For exposures between G-SIBs and D-SIBs, the limit tightens to 15 per cent. The Regulations follow the 2014 BCBS large-exposures standard.
Disclosure: Pillar 3
Quantitative and qualitative templates following the BCBS DIS standard. Tables KM1 (key metrics), OV1 (RWA overview), CR1 to CR10 (credit risk), MR1 to MR4 (market risk), OR1 to OR3 (operational risk), LIQ1 (LCR), LIQ2 (NSFR), and the remuneration and governance qualitative tables. Frequency is mostly quarterly for the largest banks.

Reading the Regulations is heavy work. The 2012 base text plus all subsequent amendments runs to over 1,000 pages, and several of the most technical provisions are written by reference to BCBS standards rather than reproduced inline. A practitioner reading the Regulations cold usually needs the underlying BCBS text on the next monitor.

Banks Act Directives (the D-series)

Directives are issued by the PA under sections 6 and 6A of the Banks Act. They are numbered sequentially and address specific topics where the Regulations need elaboration or where a one-off supervisory expectation needs to be set out in writing. Directives are binding on the bank to which they are addressed; "general" directives are binding on all registered banks. A small selection of the most important historical directives:

D1/2014: capital framework
Set out the PA's capital framework expectations under Basel III, including the components of CET1, the regulatory adjustments, and the buffer structure. Foundational alongside the 2012 Regulations.
D2/2015: liquidity
Operational expectations for LCR and NSFR reporting, including the treatment of certain South Africa-specific funding structures (committed liquidity facilities, the SARB's committed liquidity facility for the LCR).
D7/2017: domestic systemically important banks
The D-SIB designation framework. Sets out the assessment methodology (size, interconnectedness, substitutability, complexity), the supervisory bucket allocation, and the resulting higher loss absorbency requirement (1 to 2.5 per cent CET1).
Later directives on Basel 3.1 implementation
A series of 2023 to 2024 directives addresses implementation choices for FRTB, the revised standardised credit approach, the SMA, and the output floor. The PA tends to use directives to operationalise the Regulations rather than to add new substantive rules.

Prudential Standards under the FSR Act

The FSR Act 9 of 2017 introduced the Twin Peaks model of financial regulation in South Africa, splitting prudential and conduct authority between the PA and the Financial Sector Conduct Authority. Under section 105 of the FSR Act, the PA may make Prudential Standards applicable to all financial institutions under its jurisdiction. These cut across banks, insurers, and financial market infrastructures. Prudential Standards are designated by four-letter prefixes:

GOI (Governance and Operational Standards for Insurers) and GOB (for Banks)
Standards on board composition, risk management, internal control, and outsourcing. The GOB series replaces and updates older provisions that historically sat in the Regulations.
FCG (Financial Conglomerates)
Supervision of financial conglomerates (groups containing a bank and an insurer, or other regulated entities). Allocates capital and governance requirements at the group level.
RPS (Recovery Plan Standard)
Recovery planning requirements for systemically important banks and other designated entities. Aligned with the FSB Key Attributes and BCBS recovery-planning guidance.
FAP (Fit and Proper)
Fit and proper requirements for directors, senior managers, and key persons of regulated entities. Applies across banks and insurers.

The interaction between the Regulations Relating to Banks and the Prudential Standards is the most confusing part of the architecture for newcomers. The rule of thumb: if the requirement is a Basel III prudential calculation (capital, liquidity, RWA, leverage, large exposures, Pillar 3 disclosure), look in the Regulations. If the requirement is a qualitative governance, fit-and-proper, financial-conglomerate, or recovery-planning requirement, look in the Prudential Standards. The two instruments deliberately do not overlap, but on a few topics (for example, internal capital adequacy assessment) the same idea is referenced from both directions.

D-SIB designation and the big four

South Africa's domestic systemically important bank framework is set out in Directive D7/2017, now read alongside the Prudential Standards. The methodology mirrors the BCBS principles for D-SIBs: a quantitative scoring exercise across size, interconnectedness, substitutability, and complexity, supplemented by supervisory judgment. The PA conducts the assessment annually and publishes the list of designated D-SIBs without disclosing individual scores or buckets.

The market practice is well known. The "big four" South African banks (Absa Group, FirstRand, Nedbank Group, and Standard Bank Group) are designated D-SIBs, as is Investec Bank. Capitec Bank, although it has the largest retail customer base in the country, has historically been designated at a lower bucket because of its narrower wholesale interconnectedness. The HLA surcharge ranges between 1 and 2.5 per cent of CET1 depending on the bucket; the largest banks sit at 1.5 to 2 per cent.

The big four plus Investec
Standard Bank Group (the largest by assets), FirstRand, Absa Group, Nedbank Group, and Investec Bank. Together they account for over 80 per cent of South African banking system assets. The big four are publicly listed on the JSE; Investec has a dual listing in Johannesburg and London. All are designated D-SIBs subject to HLA surcharges and binding leverage and liquidity buffers.

Resolution: the Financial Sector Laws Amendment Act 2021

The Financial Sector Laws Amendment Act 23 of 2021 (FSLAA) is the most significant change to the South African bank regulatory framework since the original 2017 Twin Peaks reforms. FSLAA designates SARB as the resolution authority for banks and other designated systemically important financial institutions, creates the Corporation for Deposit Insurance (CoDI), and inserts a new Chapter 12A into the FSR Act setting out the resolution toolkit.

SARB as resolution authority
Empowered to place a designated institution in resolution where the institution is failing or likely to fail, resolution serves the public interest, and ordinary insolvency is not the right outcome. Resolution is administered by the Reserve Bank's Resolution Department, operationally separate from the PA.
Resolution toolkit
Four tools, mirroring the FSB Key Attributes: bail-in (statutory write-down or conversion of liabilities), bridge institution transfer (transfer of critical functions to a public-sector bridge), asset separation (transfer of non-performing assets to an asset management vehicle), and modified insolvency (sale of business or wind-down under court supervision).
CoDI (Corporation for Deposit Insurance)
Operational from 1 April 2024. Provides deposit insurance up to R100,000 per qualifying depositor per registered bank. Funded by levies on member banks (currently around 0.1 per cent of covered deposits per annum). Modest compared with the UK FSCS (GBP 85,000) or the EU minimum (EUR 100,000), but a meaningful protection for retail depositors in a country with a high informal-savings base.
FLAC (flac instruments)
Designated systemically important banks must hold a minimum amount of "first loss after capital" instruments: senior unsecured debt subordinated to ordinary creditors and contractually subject to bail-in in resolution. Equivalent to the UK MREL and EU TLAC concepts but with local terminology and calibration. The PA published the FLAC framework in 2023; phased in 2024 to 2028.

Companion statutes: mutual and co-operative banks

Two parallel statutes carve out separate regimes for non-commercial deposit-takers.

Mutual Banks Act 124 of 1993
Provides for the registration and supervision of mutual banks: deposit-taking institutions owned by their members rather than by shareholders. The supervisor is the same Prudential Authority. Prudential requirements broadly mirror those for commercial banks but with simplified standardised approaches and lower minimum capital (R10 million on registration). Very few institutions remain registered under this Act; GBS Mutual Bank and Finbond Mutual Bank are the principal examples.
Co-operative Banks Act 40 of 2007
Provides for co-operative banks (deposit-taking co-operatives serving a common bond of membership). Two tiers: co-operative financial institutions (smaller; supervised by the Co-operative Banks Development Agency) and co-operative banks (above R20 million in deposits; supervised by the PA). Small in aggregate but significant for financial inclusion in rural and township communities.

A worked example: capital stack for a South African D-SIB under Basel 3.1

Scenario. Consider Standard Bank of South Africa Limited as a designated D-SIB in 2026 under the post-Basel 3.1 Regulations, with a HLA surcharge of 2 per cent (an illustrative figure for the largest bucket). Assume the countercyclical buffer remains at zero and there are no bank-specific Pillar 2A or Pillar 2B add-ons disclosed publicly.

Construction. The total CET1 requirement is built up as follows:

  • Pillar 1 CET1 minimum, 4.5 per cent of RWA
  • Capital conservation buffer (CET1), 2.5 per cent of RWA
  • D-SIB higher loss absorbency surcharge (CET1), 2.0 per cent of RWA
  • Countercyclical buffer (CET1), 0 per cent of RWA
  • Total CET1 requirement, 9.0 per cent of RWA

Adding the Pillar 1 AT1 of 1.5 per cent and Pillar 1 T2 of 2 per cent gives a total capital requirement of 12.5 per cent of RWA, before any Pillar 2 add-ons.

On a notional RWA of R1,200 billion, the CET1 requirement translates to R108 billion of common equity Tier 1, plus R18 billion of AT1 and R24 billion of T2, for a total regulatory capital requirement of R150 billion.

The output floor. From 1 January 2025, the Regulations require that RWA computed under internal models cannot fall below 50 per cent of RWA computed under the revised standardised approach, rising in annual steps to 72.5 per cent by 2030. If the standardised RWA on the same portfolio is R1,500 billion, the floor in 2026 (assume 55 per cent transitional) gives a floor of 825 billion, which does not bind because IRB RWA of R1,200 billion is already above the floor. The floor becomes binding only when IRB savings exceed 27.5 per cent of standardised RWA in steady state.

Leverage. The 4 per cent leverage minimum applies to Tier 1 capital over total leverage exposure. With Tier 1 capital of R126 billion (R108 billion CET1 plus R18 billion AT1), the bank can support up to R3,150 billion of leverage exposure before breaching the leverage ratio. D-SIB leverage buffer of 1 per cent (half the 2 per cent HLA) raises the effective floor to 5 per cent, reducing the supportable leverage exposure to R2,520 billion.

Liquidity. LCR 100 per cent and NSFR 100 per cent apply unconditionally. A bank with net 30-day stress outflows of R250 billion must hold HQLA of at least R250 billion; a bank with required stable funding of R900 billion must hold available stable funding of at least R900 billion.

Where the binding constraint sits. In current market conditions, for most South African D-SIBs the leverage ratio and the LCR are the most binding requirements, not the CET1 ratio. The Basel 3.1 output floor is expected to become a binding constraint for the larger IRB users over the 2027 to 2030 transition, especially for the corporate book where the constraint on A-IRB use is sharpest.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Read the four layers in order, not in parallel

The Banks Act, the Regulations, the Directives, and the Prudential Standards are best read in that order, top to bottom. The Act gives you the statutory hook; the Regulations give you the quantitative requirement; the Directives give you the supervisory expectation around the requirement; the Prudential Standards give you the cross-sectoral governance overlay. Reading the Regulations cold, without the Act for context, leaves you guessing about what counts as a "bank"; reading the Directives without the Regulations leaves you implementing supervisory expectations without knowing what the underlying rule actually says.

The 2024 amendments are not a refresh, they are a generational change

The 2024 amendments to the Regulations Relating to Banks are the most significant single update since the 2012 base text. They implement Basel 3.1 in full: the revised standardised approach for credit risk, the constraints on A-IRB, the new SMA for operational risk, the 72.5 per cent output floor, and the framework for FRTB. The transitional path runs to 2030. For a credit-risk actuary in South Africa, the 2024 amendments redefine the model perimeter: A-IRB for large corporates and financial institutions is no longer available, the standardised approach has become risk-sensitive enough to compete on small portfolios, and the output floor will become binding for several banks once the transitional ramp is complete.

The branch regime is restrictive on purpose

South Africa's approach to foreign-bank branches has historically been more restrictive than the UK's or Singapore's. The PA has consistently required substantial local capital, local liquidity, and clear ringfencing for branch operations. The policy aim is to ensure that, in a parent-bank stress, South African depositors and counterparties are protected by capital that is on-shore and accessible to the resolution authority. The consequence is that most foreign banks operate in South Africa as locally incorporated subsidiaries, not as branches. When a textbook talks about "foreign bank branches" in a Basel sense, the South African reality is that there are fewer than two dozen such branches across the entire jurisdiction.

Watch out The Banks Act and the Regulations Relating to Banks are not the only regulatory instruments a South African bank must comply with. Conduct requirements sit with the FSCA under the Financial Sector Regulation Act and the Conduct of Financial Institutions Act (still progressing through Parliament at time of writing). Consumer credit rules sit with the National Credit Regulator under the National Credit Act. Anti-money-laundering rules sit with the Financial Intelligence Centre under FICA. A bank can be Basel-compliant under the Regulations and still in serious trouble with the FSCA, NCR, or FIC. Prudential compliance is necessary but never sufficient.

CoDI matters more than its R100,000 limit suggests

R100,000 per depositor per bank looks modest next to the UK's GBP 85,000 or the EU's EUR 100,000. But two design features make it materially protective. First, R100,000 captures the full balance of the overwhelming majority of South African retail depositors: median household deposit balances are well below this threshold. Second, CoDI's payout target is seven working days, which is among the fastest in the world; in the UK the FSCS payout target is also seven days but in practice many EU schemes take longer. The combination of broad coverage at the relevant depositor band and rapid payout makes CoDI a meaningful contributor to systemic stability, not just a regulatory tick-box.

The PA is part of SARB but not SARB

A surprisingly common confusion in exam answers is to treat "SARB" and "the Prudential Authority" as synonyms. They are not. The PA is a juristic person within SARB, with its own CEO appointed by the Minister of Finance, its own deputy CEOs, its own staff, and its own statutory mandate under the FSR Act. SARB the central bank conducts monetary policy and oversees the payments system; SARB also hosts the PA, the financial-sector resolution function, the Financial Stability Department, and CoDI. The PA's CEO reports to the Governor of SARB on operational matters but is statutorily independent in prudential decisions. In exam answers, attribute prudential rules to "the PA" and monetary policy to "SARB".

Three South African specifics that catch outsiders

First, the controlling company concept is more pervasive in South Africa than in most jurisdictions; the controlling company is itself a separately registered entity and is the primary point of consolidated supervision. Second, the leverage ratio minimum is 4 per cent, not 3 per cent; the 1 per cent uplift is national discretion exercised by the PA. Third, the FLAC framework for designated banks is South Africa's local answer to TLAC and MREL, with a separate name and a separate calibration; do not assume the EU or UK numbers transfer directly. These three details consistently catch out candidates relying on offshore textbooks.

Test yourself

  1. Identify the four principal layers of South African bank prudential regulation, in order from highest legal authority to most operational, and name the instrument that creates each layer.
  2. The Banks Act 94 of 1990 was amended materially in 2017 and 2021. Which two pieces of amending legislation are responsible, and what did each change?
  3. What is the minimum share capital required for a registered bank under section 70 of the Banks Act, and how does this compare with the operating capital adequacy requirements?
  4. Explain the concept of a "controlling company" under Chapter V of the Banks Act. Why does the PA supervise both the bank and its controlling company?
  5. The Regulations Relating to Banks set the South African leverage ratio at 4 per cent. The Basel III leverage ratio is 3 per cent. Why is South Africa's higher, and which authority chose the higher number?
  6. List the five D-SIBs in South Africa. What is the range of HLA surcharges they face?
  7. What does FSLAA stand for, and what three substantive changes did it make to the South African bank regulatory framework?
  8. CoDI provides deposit insurance up to R100,000 per depositor per bank. How does this compare with the UK FSCS and the EU minimum, and why might the lower South African limit nevertheless cover most retail depositors?
  9. A foreign bank wants to operate in South Africa. Compare its three options under the Banks Act in terms of capital requirements and the activities it may carry on.
  10. Explain how the 2024 amendments to the Regulations Relating to Banks restrict the use of A-IRB. For which types of exposure is A-IRB no longer available, and what must the bank do instead?

  • Financial Sector Regulation Act 9 of 2017 and Financial Sector Laws Amendment Act 23 of 2021: the FSR Act creates the PA and FSCA; FSLAA inserts the resolution regime and creates CoDI.
  • Mutual Banks Act 124 of 1993; Co-operative Banks Act 40 of 2007.
