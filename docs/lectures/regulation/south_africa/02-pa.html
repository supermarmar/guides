PA: the Prudential Authority

The Prudential Authority is South Africa's Twin Peaks prudential regulator. It was created by the Financial Sector Regulation Act of 2017 and went live on 1 April 2018. It sits operationally inside the South African Reserve Bank but is a separate juristic person, with its own CEO, its own balance sheet, and its own rulebook. It supervises every South African bank, every long-term and short-term insurer, every market infrastructure, and every designated financial conglomerate, and it is the body that writes the local interpretation of Basel III.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The Prudential Authority (PA) is South Africa's prudential regulator. It was established by the Financial Sector Regulation Act 9 of 2017 (FSR Act) and became operational on 1 April 2018, the day South Africa formally moved to a Twin Peaks supervisory model. The PA sits inside the South African Reserve Bank as a juristic person established by statute, with its own CEO appointed by the SARB Board with the concurrence of the Minister of Finance. It supervises roughly 17 registered banks, around 150 short-term and long-term insurers, the Johannesburg Stock Exchange and other market infrastructures, and the designated financial conglomerates. It implements Basel III for South African banks through the Regulations Relating to Banks (issued under the Banks Act 1990) and writes binding Prudential Standards under the FSR Act.

The simplest way to understand the PA is in contrast to what came before it. Until 31 March 2018, bank supervision sat inside SARB's Bank Supervision Department (BSD), and insurance supervision sat in a separate body called the Financial Services Board (FSB). The FSR Act folded the BSD and the prudential side of the FSB into a single new institution, the PA, and spun the conduct-of-business side of the FSB out into the new Financial Sector Conduct Authority (FSCA). Twin Peaks therefore did two structural things at once: it unified prudential supervision across banks and insurers, and it separated prudential from conduct.

Feynman test Imagine the SARB Governor's office is a large building. On one floor, called Monetary Policy, people decide what the repo rate should be. On a different floor, called the Prudential Authority, a separate team checks that each bank has enough capital and liquidity to survive a bad year. The PA team has the same landlord (SARB), shares the canteen, and the Governor still walks past their door, but their work is governed by a separate law, their decisions are their own, and they can be appealed independently. Down the road, in a completely different building, sits the FSCA, which checks that the same banks treat their customers fairly. A bank that mis-sells a product is the FSCA's problem. A bank that runs out of capital is the PA's problem. A bank that does both gets visits from both.

Step 2

Why this matters

For a South African bank, the PA is the day-to-day supervisor. It is the body that grants and revokes banking licences, that signs off on senior appointments, that conducts on-site inspections, that reviews the annual ICAAP and ILAAP submissions, and that imposes any capital add-on or restriction the bank ends up with. Every binding prudential requirement applied to a South African bank is either an Act of Parliament (the Banks Act 1990, the FSR Act 2017), a regulation made under one of those Acts (most importantly the Regulations Relating to Banks), or a Prudential Standard or directive issued by the PA. If you cannot tell which of those instruments a given requirement comes from, you cannot find the legal basis for it, and you cannot tell which regulator to talk to when it changes.

For an actuary or risk modeller working in a South African bank, the PA is the audience for the most important pieces of work the team produces. The annual ICAAP submission is read by PA supervisors and feeds the Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process equivalent. Model approvals for A-IRB credit risk, IMA market risk, and AMA or SMA operational risk go to the PA. Stress test results are reviewed by the PA. The Pillar 3 disclosure templates the bank publishes are prescribed by the PA. The boundary between what is purely a Basel decision and what is a local PA overlay (output floor calibration, D-SIB add-on, capital conservation buffer) determines a substantial part of the bank's required capital.

For an F107 candidate, the PA matters because it is the regulator examiners will assume you know. The exam is set in a South African context. The PA, the FSCA, the SARB, and the FSR Act 2017 are the institutional vocabulary of every regulatory answer. Confusing the PA with the FSCA, or attributing a Banks Act regulation to the FSR Act, or claiming that the Bank Supervision Department still exists, are easy ways to lose marks.

Step 3

The mental model

The mental picture is the Twin Peaks architecture. One peak is prudential safety and soundness; the other is market conduct and consumer protection. Each peak has its own regulator with its own statute. Both regulators supervise the same firms, but for different aspects of those firms' behaviour. Above both peaks sits the SARB, which retains responsibility for monetary policy and for overall financial stability through its Financial Stability Committee.

Three relationships matter inside this picture. First, the PA is operationally separate from SARB monetary policy but legally tied to SARB: the PA CEO is appointed by the SARB Board, is normally a SARB Deputy Governor by convention, and the SARB provides the PA's funding and infrastructure. Second, the PA and the FSCA supervise the same firms but for different purposes, so a South African bank has two regulators sending two sets of returns and writing two sets of rules. Third, the PA and the SARB Financial Stability Committee divide the macroprudential and microprudential work: the SARB FSC designates which firms are systemically important and sets the macroprudential overlay, the PA implements the resulting requirements at firm level.

Step 4

The detail

History: how South Africa got to Twin Peaks

South African bank supervision has lived inside the central bank since the early 1980s. The Bank Supervision Department (BSD) of the SARB was the bank regulator from 1987, when the Banks Act 94 of 1990 was being drafted in its modern form, until 31 March 2018. The BSD's mandate was extended after the 1985 banking crisis (the AB Industrial Bank failure and the broader Trust Bank distress) and again after the small-bank crisis of 2002 (Saambou, BoE, Unifer), which prompted the BSD to take a more intrusive line on liquidity and on small banks generally. The BSD implemented Basel II in South Africa with effect from January 2008 and Basel III in stages from 2013.

Insurance supervision sat in a different place. The Financial Services Board (FSB), established in 1990 by the Financial Services Board Act 97 of 1990, was a statutory body outside the SARB. The FSB supervised long-term insurers, short-term insurers, retirement funds, collective investment schemes, financial advisers, and a range of other non-bank financial activities. Its mandate combined what would today be called prudential supervision (capital, solvency, governance) with conduct supervision (advice standards, treating customers fairly, market integrity).

The post-2008 global rethink of supervisory architecture pushed many jurisdictions towards Twin Peaks. The UK split the FSA into the PRA (prudential) and the FCA (conduct) in 2013. Australia had already pioneered Twin Peaks in 1998. National Treasury published the South African Twin Peaks discussion paper in 2011, followed by the Financial Sector Regulation Bill in 2013. The FSR Act was signed into law in August 2017 and the operative date was set as 1 April 2018. On that date, the BSD ceased to exist as a separate SARB department, its staff and powers transferred to the new PA, and the FSB's prudential functions moved with them. The remaining FSB functions, principally conduct of business and consumer protection, became the FSCA.

Legal basis

The PA's powers come from several statutes layered on top of each other. The FSR Act 2017 is the constitutional statute that creates the institution and gives it its general mandate. The sector laws each then confer additional, sector-specific powers.

Financial Sector Regulation Act 9 of 2017 (FSR Act)
Establishes the PA (chapter 3) and the FSCA (chapter 4). Sets the mandate, governance, funding, and standard-making powers. Empowers the PA to make Prudential Standards binding across the financial sector. Sets up co-ordination bodies (FSOC, FSCR) and the Financial Services Tribunal for appeals. The umbrella statute.
Banks Act 94 of 1990
The sector law for banks. The PA is the "Registrar of Banks" for the purposes of this Act. Sets out licensing requirements, large-exposure limits, lending-and-investment restrictions, and the authority to make the Regulations Relating to Banks. The substantive prudential rulebook for South African banks is the regulations made under this Act, not the Act itself.
Insurance Act 18 of 2017
The sector law for insurers (life and non-life). Replaced the older Long-term Insurance Act 52 of 1998 and Short-term Insurance Act 53 of 1998. Houses the Solvency Assessment and Management (SAM) regime, the South African analogue of Solvency II. PA is the "Prudential Authority" for insurer purposes.
Financial Markets Act 19 of 2012
Governs exchanges, central securities depositories, clearing houses, and trade repositories. The PA shares supervisory responsibility with the FSCA: the PA covers the prudential side of CCPs and CSDs, the FSCA covers conduct and market integrity.
Co-operative Banks Act 40 of 2007 and Mutual Banks Act 124 of 1993
Govern the small mutual and co-operative banking sector. The PA supervises the larger entities; the Co-operative Banks Development Agency supervises the smaller ones.
National Payment System Act 78 of 1998
Governs the payment system. Supervised by SARB's National Payment System Department, not the PA. Worth knowing to avoid confusion: payments oversight is a SARB function, not a PA function.

Governance

The PA is headed by a Chief Executive Officer. The CEO is appointed by the SARB Board of Directors, subject to the concurrence of the Minister of Finance, for a five-year term renewable once. By convention, the PA CEO is also one of the SARB Deputy Governors, which gives the PA direct representation at the SARB Governors' Executive Committee and ensures that prudential supervision has a seat at the monetary-policy table without compromising operational independence.

Inside the PA, the operational structure is built around a Prudential Committee chaired by the CEO and including the heads of each supervisory department. The principal departments are Banking Supervision, Insurance Supervision, Financial Conglomerate Supervision, Market Infrastructure Supervision, Specialist Risk Support (which houses model approval and prudential analytics), and Policy, Statistics and Industry Support. The Specialist Risk Support team is the one a bank's risk-modelling function deals with most often: A-IRB model approvals, market-risk internal-model approvals, operational-risk approach approvals, and ICAAP reviews all pass through it.

Appeals against PA decisions go to the Financial Services Tribunal, an independent body established by the FSR Act. A bank that disputes a PA decision (a licence condition, a sanction, a capital add-on) can apply to the Tribunal for reconsideration. Tribunal decisions can themselves be reviewed by the High Court.

Mandate (FSR Act sections 32 to 35)

The FSR Act sets the PA's objectives in three clean statements:

Promote and enhance the safety and soundness of financial institutions
The classical prudential objective. Each individual firm should be solvent, well managed, well governed, and able to withstand reasonable shocks. Section 33(a) FSR Act.
Protect financial customers against the risk of those institutions failing to meet their obligations
Customer protection through prudential strength. Distinguished from conduct protection, which is the FSCA's brief. The PA protects depositors and policyholders by stopping firms from failing in the first place. Section 33(b).
Assist in maintaining financial stability
Co-operation with the SARB Financial Stability Committee on the macroprudential overlay. The PA does the microprudential work that feeds into the FSC's macroprudential decisions, and implements the resulting requirements. Section 33(c).

The three objectives must be balanced. The FSR Act does not rank them, and where they conflict the PA must take a reasonable view. In practice safety and soundness dominate the day-to-day work, with customer protection sitting underneath it and financial stability appearing in macroprudential decisions and resolution planning.

Powers

The FSR Act and the sector laws between them give the PA a substantial toolkit. Five categories matter.

Authorisation
Granting and revoking banking licences (Banks Act section 13), insurer licences (Insurance Act section 23), and approvals for senior appointments. The PA can attach conditions to a licence and can refuse approval of a chief executive, chief risk officer, or chief financial officer.
Rule-making
Issuing binding Prudential Standards under the FSR Act (cross-sector or sector-specific), making regulations under the Banks Act (with the Minister's concurrence), and issuing directives to individual firms. This is the lawmaking function.
Supervision
Conducting on-site inspections, requesting information, reviewing returns (the BA-series prudential returns for banks, the IFRS-based financial statements, the ICAAP submission), and engaging with firms through routine and ad-hoc supervisory meetings.
Enforcement
Imposing administrative penalties (up to R10 million per contravention under the FSR Act, recently raised in some Acts), debarring senior individuals, issuing public warnings, restricting business activities, and in extremis revoking licences.
Recommending resolution
When a bank is failing or likely to fail, the PA recommends to the SARB Governor that the SARB act as resolution authority. The South African resolution regime, modernised through the Financial Sector Laws Amendment Act 23 of 2021, sits inside SARB. The PA does not run resolution; it triggers it.

Publication types: how to read the PA rulebook

The PA produces several types of document with different legal force. Telling them apart is essential because only some are binding, only some can be made directly without consultation, and only some have RCAP equivalence.

Prudential Standards (PS)
Binding rules issued under the FSR Act. Apply across the financial sector or to a specific sector. Made after consultation under FSR Act sections 98 to 104. The most important new rule format introduced by Twin Peaks. Numbered as PS [topic]/[number], for example GOI 1 (Governance of Insurers, Standard 1) or FSP 1 (Financial Service Providers, Standard 1).
Regulations Relating to Banks
The substantive Basel-implementing rulebook for South African banks. Made under section 90 of the Banks Act with the Minister of Finance's concurrence. First version 1993; periodically replaced or amended. Current version implements Basel III; the 2024 amendments implement Basel 3.1 (the d424 finalisation). Several hundred pages long. The single most important document a South African bank credit-risk team reads.
Directives (Banks Act, D-series)
Binding instructions issued under section 6(6) of the Banks Act to all banks or to a specific bank. Numbered as Directive [number]/[year]. Used for fast-moving or operational matters, for example a directive on the treatment of COVID-19 restructured loans or on a temporary LCR relaxation. Faster to issue than a regulation because no ministerial concurrence is required.
Joint Standards
Issued jointly by the PA and the FSCA under FSR Act section 107. Cover topics that span both peaks: cyber resilience, outsourcing, financial customer education in a prudential context. Binding on the firms they apply to. Numbered as JS [topic]/[number].
Guidance Notes (G-series)
Non-binding clarifications of how the PA expects rules to be applied. Tell banks how the PA reads an ambiguous regulation. Have no direct legal force but in practice carry significant weight in supervisory dialogue. Numbered as Guidance Note [number]/[year].
Circulars and communications
Lower-level operational communications. Often used for reporting clarifications, deadlines, or transitional arrangements. Not binding in their own right.

The legal hierarchy runs Act of Parliament, then regulations made under the Act, then directives or Prudential Standards, then guidance. A directive cannot override the Banks Act. A guidance note cannot override a Prudential Standard. When two binding instruments appear to conflict, the more recent prevails for the points where they overlap.

Basel III implementation in South Africa

The South African Basel III regime is implemented through the Regulations Relating to Banks. The substantive content tracks the BCBS d424 finalisation with a small number of national choices and add-ons. The key quantitative requirements are below.

Common Equity Tier 1
Minimum 4.5 per cent of risk-weighted assets, plus a 2.5 per cent capital conservation buffer (CCoB) and a variable countercyclical capital buffer (CCyB) set by the SARB Financial Stability Committee. D-SIB add-on of 1 per cent to 2.5 per cent depending on systemic importance score. So a major D-SIB faces an effective CET1 minimum of around 8 per cent before any Pillar 2 add-on, before stress losses.
Total capital
Minimum 8 per cent total capital plus 2.5 per cent CCoB. The PA has set additional "Pillar 2A" requirements above the Basel minimum, set bank by bank to reflect risks not adequately captured under Pillar 1 (concentration, IRRBB, sometimes operational).
Leverage ratio
South Africa applies a 4 per cent leverage ratio (above the Basel minimum of 3 per cent). The 1 percentage-point overlay reflects historical South African supervisory caution and the dominance of a small number of large banks.
Liquidity Coverage Ratio
100 per cent LCR was phased in by January 2019 in line with the Basel timetable. During the COVID-19 stress in 2020 to 2021, the PA temporarily reduced the regulatory minimum to 80 per cent (Directive 1/2020 and successor directives) to allow banks to draw down their HQLA. The full 100 per cent has since been restored.
Net Stable Funding Ratio
100 per cent NSFR effective from January 2018. Calibrated with some local treatment of run-off rates on retail deposits, reflecting South Africa's historically sticky deposit base.
Output floor (Basel 3.1)
72.5 per cent of standardised RWA, phased in from 2025 to 2030 in line with the BCBS d424 timetable. Implemented through the 2024 amendments to the Regulations Relating to Banks. The output floor is the most economically significant Basel 3.1 change for South African D-SIBs because they all use A-IRB for most of their credit portfolios.

South Africa has no globally systemically important banks. The FSB's G-SIB methodology has never identified a South African institution as systemically important on the global scale. Instead, the SARB Financial Stability Committee designates domestic systemically important banks (D-SIBs) using a methodology that mirrors the BCBS framework but is calibrated to the South African banking system. The current D-SIBs are typically Absa, FirstRand, Nedbank, and Standard Bank (the "big four"), plus Investec. Each D-SIB faces a higher loss absorbency requirement set in CET1, scaled by its systemic importance bucket.

Insurance prudential framework: SAM

The PA's insurance brief is run on the Solvency Assessment and Management (SAM) regime, in force from 1 July 2018 alongside the Insurance Act 18 of 2017. SAM is closely modelled on Solvency II, with three pillars covering quantitative requirements (Pillar 1, the Solvency Capital Requirement and Minimum Capital Requirement on a one-year 99.5 per cent value-at-risk basis), governance and risk management (Pillar 2, including the Own Risk and Solvency Assessment), and disclosure (Pillar 3). The PA issues sector-specific Prudential Standards in series GOI (Governance of Insurers), FSI (Financial Soundness of Insurers), and others. Insurer ORSA submissions to the PA are the analogue of the bank ICAAP.

Financial conglomerate supervision

One of the structural innovations of the FSR Act is group-level supervision. Under FSR Act chapter 13, the PA can designate a financial conglomerate as a "designated financial conglomerate" and apply group-level prudential requirements: group capital adequacy, group risk management, intra-group exposures, and a single point of supervisory contact. Most of the major South African banking groups (FirstRand, Standard Bank, Absa, Nedbank) and the major insurance-led groups (Sanlam, Old Mutual) are designated. Group supervision matters because South African banks all sit within bancassurance and asset-management groups, and group risks (contagion, double-leverage, intra-group lending) are not visible from the bank standalone view.

ICAAP and ILAAP

South African banks submit an Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP) report to the PA at least annually. The ICAAP is the bank's own assessment of the capital it needs to support its risk profile, including risks not captured under Pillar 1 (concentration, IRRBB, business risk, strategic risk, pension risk, climate-related risks). The PA reviews the ICAAP and sets the Pillar 2A and Pillar 2B add-ons accordingly. From 2020 the PA has formalised the Internal Liquidity Adequacy Assessment Process (ILAAP) as a parallel exercise covering liquidity self-assessment, including intraday liquidity, funding plan robustness, and stress-test results against the LCR and NSFR minima.

IFRS 9 in South Africa

IFRS 9 has applied to South African banks since 1 January 2018, in line with the global IASB effective date. The PA reviews IFRS 9 models, in particular the staging criteria for Significant Increase in Credit Risk (SICR) and the forward-looking information (FLI) macroeconomic overlays. The interaction between IFRS 9 and the regulatory capital regime is governed by the Basel "transitional arrangements" that South Africa implemented in line with BCBS guidance, allowing partial add-back of the day-one IFRS 9 ECL hit to CET1 capital over a five-year transition. That transition has now expired and South African banks face the full IFRS 9 impact in their regulatory capital.

Reporting: the BA-series returns

South African banks submit a substantial suite of prudential returns to the PA, known by their BA-prefix form numbers. The principal returns include BA 100 (statement of financial position), BA 110 (off-balance-sheet exposures), BA 200 (credit risk), BA 300 (liquidity risk), BA 320 (LCR), BA 325 (NSFR), BA 330 (interest rate risk in the banking book), BA 400 (market risk), BA 410 (counterparty credit risk), BA 700 (capital adequacy and leverage), and BA 900 (institutional sector classification). The BA returns are the day-to-day raw material of supervisory analysis and are submitted monthly or quarterly depending on the form.

Pillar 3 disclosure

The PA prescribes Pillar 3 disclosure templates broadly aligned with the BCBS d520 standardised disclosure framework, with local adaptations. South African D-SIBs publish quarterly Pillar 3 reports covering capital adequacy, leverage, liquidity, credit-risk RWA breakdowns, counterparty credit risk, market risk, securitisation, and operational risk.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The PA is independent inside SARB, not independent of it

The PA's structural design is closer to the ECB Single Supervisory Mechanism or the Bank of England's PRA than to a fully external regulator. It is housed inside the central bank, shares the central bank's infrastructure, and is led by a SARB Deputy Governor. This proximity has benefits (information sharing, faster co-ordination on financial stability, no turf battles over crisis management) and costs (less visible separation from monetary policy in the public eye, occasional perception of capture). For an F107 candidate, the right framing is that the PA is operationally independent and legally a separate juristic person, but institutionally embedded in SARB.

The South African D-SIB list is short and the supervision is intensive

South Africa has roughly 17 registered banks, but the five D-SIBs dominate the system. This concentration shapes how the PA supervises: there is intensive, named-supervisor engagement with each big-five bank, supervisory dialogue is dense, and the PA has the resources to look at individual model files in detail. By contrast, smaller banks face a lighter-touch supervisory regime that is more rules-based and less judgement-based. The supervisory style for a D-SIB looks more like UK PRA continuous supervision than US OCC examiner cycles.

Watch out Do not confuse the PA with the FSCA, and do not refer to the "Bank Supervision Department" as if it still existed. The BSD ceased to exist as a separate SARB department on 1 April 2018. Its successor is the PA. The FSB also ceased to exist on the same date; its conduct successor is the FSCA and its prudential successor is the PA. The acronym FSB is sometimes used in older textbooks for the Financial Services Board and sometimes globally for the Financial Stability Board. They are not the same body. In a F107 exam, "FSB" should be reserved for the global FSB; the South African body is the "former FSB", succeeded by FSCA and PA.

The Banks Act regulations are where the substance lives

If you are looking for the local interpretation of a Basel rule, do not search the FSR Act. Search the Regulations Relating to Banks (the regulations made under section 90 of the Banks Act). Almost every numerical requirement, formula, parameter, and disclosure template lives there. The FSR Act is the institutional statute; the Banks Act regulations are the technical rulebook. Many F107 candidates lose marks by attributing the Basel III capital ratios to the wrong statute.

2018 was a clean handover, not a fresh start

The Twin Peaks transition was deliberately designed to preserve continuity. The BSD staff moved to the PA. The FSB prudential staff moved to the PA. The existing regulations remained in force. New PA Prudential Standards were issued progressively from 2018 onwards, but the day-1 rulebook was effectively the inherited BSD and FSB rulebook with a new wrapper. This continuity is why South Africa did not see the supervisory disruption that the UK saw in 2013 around the FSA-to-PRA-and-FCA split. Supervisors and banks knew each other already.

Basel 3.1 implementation is the live workstream

The 2024 amendments to the Regulations Relating to Banks are the most consequential PA rule change in a decade. They implement the BCBS d424 finalisation: revised credit-risk standardised approach, new operational-risk standardised approach, output floor (phasing 2025 to 2030), revised CVA framework, and FRTB market-risk framework. Any credit-risk model built before 2024 needs a re-check of its standardised RWA reporting because the output floor will, for many South African D-SIB portfolios, become a binding constraint by 2030.

Climate risk is creeping in

The PA published a guidance note on climate-related risk practices for banks in 2024 and is developing a sector-wide approach to climate stress testing in co-ordination with the SARB Financial Stability Committee. South African banks have not yet faced binding climate capital requirements, but the supervisory direction of travel is clear and ICAAP submissions are already expected to address climate exposures.
