FCA: the Financial Conduct Authority

The Financial Conduct Authority is the UK's conduct and market integrity regulator and the prudential regulator for the firms the PRA does not supervise. It is the twin-peaks counterpart of the PRA: where the PRA asks whether a bank is safe, the FCA asks whether it treats its customers fairly and behaves properly in markets. It supervises roughly fifty thousand firms across retail and wholesale finance, owns the FCA Handbook, and has reshaped the regulator-firm relationship through the 2023 Consumer Duty.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) is the UK's conduct regulator for every authorised financial services firm, and the prudential regulator for those firms not supervised by the PRA. It was created by the Financial Services Act 2012 and took over the conduct function of the former Financial Services Authority on 1 April 2013. Its constitutional foundation is the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (FSMA), as amended. It has three statutory operational objectives, consumer protection, market integrity, and effective competition, plus a single strategic objective (ensuring markets work well) and, since the Financial Services and Markets Act 2023, a secondary objective to facilitate the international competitiveness and medium-to-long-term growth of the UK economy. It supervises roughly fifty thousand firms, ranging from the largest banks (jointly with the PRA, on the conduct side) to sole-trader investment advisers and crypto-asset firms.

The FCA is funded by an annual levy on the firms it regulates, governed by a board appointed by HM Treasury, and accountable to Treasury and Parliament. It does not write legislation; it writes the FCA Handbook, the rulebook every authorised UK firm must comply with on the conduct side. The Handbook sits on top of FSMA and is the practical text supervisors and compliance teams read every day. The 2023 Consumer Duty (Policy Statement PS22/9) added a new overarching principle (Principle 12) requiring firms to act to deliver good outcomes for retail customers, and is the most consequential conduct reform in a decade.

Feynman test Think of the UK as having two safety inspectors looking at the same bank. One walks around the building checking the structural beams, the fire suppression, and the load-bearing walls. That is the PRA, asking whether the bank will collapse. The other inspector walks the corridors checking that the staff are not selling residents broken kettles, that the lift signage is honest, and that the customer services desk does not lie about the rules. That is the FCA, asking whether the bank treats its customers fairly. A dual-regulated bank has both inspectors visiting; a mortgage broker or an investment adviser has only the conduct one. The PRA closes a bank down if it is unsafe. The FCA fines, bans, and prosecutes if it is unfair.

Step 2

Why this matters

Every UK bank has two supervisors. The PRA owns capital, liquidity, governance for safety, and recovery and resolution planning. The FCA owns everything else: product design, sales practices, complaints handling, financial promotions, market conduct, transaction reporting, financial crime, and the personal conduct of senior managers and certified staff. If you work in a UK bank you will have a PRA supervisor calling about your ICAAP and a separate FCA supervisor calling about your treating-customers-fairly metrics. They sit in different buildings (PRA in the Bank of England at Threadneedle Street, FCA in Stratford), report to different boards, and pursue overlapping but distinct mandates.

Conduct failings have cost the UK banking system more than the 2008 recapitalisation. Payment protection insurance redress alone is estimated at over £38 billion across the industry between 2011 and 2019. LIBOR manipulation, foreign-exchange rigging, mortgage mis-selling, packaged-bank-account mis-selling, and pension transfer scandals each generated multi-billion-pound provisions. The FCA's predecessor regimes had not designed themselves to police these behaviours systematically; the conduct turn after 2013 is the regulatory response. Understanding the FCA is therefore not optional for anyone working inside a UK bank: the bank's financial performance, capital headroom, and reputational capital are all materially exposed to conduct supervision.

The Consumer Duty is the most consequential single piece of conduct regulation since FSMA itself. From 31 July 2023 (for new and existing open products, with closed products following on 31 July 2024) every firm in the retail distribution chain must demonstrate that it is acting to deliver good outcomes for retail customers across four dimensions: products and services, price and value, consumer understanding, and consumer support. The Duty shifts the FCA's stance from a rule-checking regulator to an outcomes-checking regulator: a firm can be in literal compliance with every detailed Handbook rule and still be in breach if its outcomes for customers are poor. For a banking practitioner, the Duty is the most likely future source of supervisory pressure, fines, and remediation cost.

Step 3

The mental model

The UK runs a twin-peaks regulatory model. The PRA and FCA share the regulatory perimeter set by FSMA, but they ask different questions of the firms they supervise. Dual-regulated firms (banks, building societies, insurers, large investment firms) answer to both; solo-regulated firms (asset managers, IFAs, brokers, payment institutions, crypto firms) answer only to the FCA.

Three flows of authority matter. Authorisation flows from Parliament through FSMA to HM Treasury, which sets the regulatory perimeter and appoints the FCA's chair. Rule-making authority flows within the FCA: the board approves Handbook changes after public consultation. Supervision flows from the FCA to firms, through dedicated supervisory teams for the largest firms (so-called "fixed portfolio" firms) and through thematic and event-driven reviews for the rest of the population (the "flexible portfolio").

Step 4

The detail

History and creation

The FCA succeeded the conduct function of the Financial Services Authority (FSA), which had existed from 1997 to 2013. The FSA was itself a creation of the New Labour government, intended to consolidate eight separate regulators (banking, building societies, friendly societies, insurance, investment business, securities, and so on) into a single integrated supervisor under FSMA 2000. The FSA combined prudential and conduct supervision in one body and reported to HM Treasury.

The 2007 to 2009 financial crisis exposed two failings in the integrated FSA model. The first was the so-called tripartite failure: the FSA, the Bank of England, and HM Treasury had overlapping but unclear responsibilities for financial stability, and none of them had clear ownership of macroprudential risk. The Northern Rock run in September 2007 played out as a public embarrassment for the tripartite arrangement. The second failing was that the FSA's culture had tilted heavily towards conduct issues during the years before the crisis (treating-customers-fairly was a major workstream from 2006) and prudential supervision had received insufficient senior attention.

The Conservative-Liberal Democrat coalition government legislated the dismantling. The Financial Services Act 2012 abolished the FSA and split it into two bodies: the PRA, a subsidiary of the Bank of England responsible for prudential supervision of systemic firms; and the FCA, an independent body responsible for conduct supervision of all firms plus prudential supervision of firms not designated to the PRA. Both regulators went live on 1 April 2013. The Financial Policy Committee inside the Bank of England was given the macroprudential mandate.

Governance and accountability

The FCA is a company limited by guarantee, independent of government but accountable to it. Its governance has four layers.

Board
The FCA Board comprises a chair, the CEO, executive directors, and non-executive directors. The chair is appointed by HM Treasury for a fixed term; the CEO is appointed by the board with HM Treasury approval. As of 2026 the chair is Ashley Alder and the CEO is Nikhil Rathi (appointed October 2020). The board sets strategy, approves the budget and the annual business plan, and approves major Handbook changes.
Executive committees
Day-to-day decisions are taken by the Executive Committee (ExCo) and a series of executive committees covering authorisations, supervision, enforcement, policy, and operations. The Regulatory Decisions Committee (RDC) is a separate, independent committee that takes contested enforcement decisions on behalf of the FCA.
Accountability
The FCA reports annually to HM Treasury, which lays the report before Parliament. The Treasury Select Committee scrutinises the FCA's work and has wide-ranging powers to summon the chair and CEO. The Complaints Commissioner is an independent body that hears complaints about how the FCA itself has behaved.
Funding
The FCA is funded by an annual levy on the firms it regulates, banded by firm size and activity. There is no taxpayer subsidy. The annual budget is roughly £750 million (2024-25). This funding model creates an inherent tension: the FCA must remain credible as an independent regulator while being financially dependent on the firms it polices.

Statutory objectives

The FCA's mandate is set out in FSMA 2000 (as amended by the Financial Services Act 2012 and the Financial Services and Markets Act 2023). It has one strategic objective, three operational objectives, and a secondary objective.

Strategic objective
"Ensuring that the relevant markets function well." This is the overarching purpose; the operational objectives explain what "functioning well" means in practice.
Consumer protection (operational)
"Securing an appropriate degree of protection for consumers." The FCA must consider the differing degrees of experience and expertise of consumers, the differing degrees of risk in different investments, the general principle that consumers should take responsibility for their decisions, and the general principle that firms should provide a level of care commensurate with the risk involved.
Market integrity (operational)
"Protecting and enhancing the integrity of the UK financial system." Integrity includes stability, lack of fraud and financial crime, soundness of market infrastructure, transparency of price formation, and orderly operation of financial markets.
Effective competition (operational)
"Promoting effective competition in the interests of consumers" in the markets for regulated services. The FCA has explicit competition powers, including the ability to make market investigation references to the Competition and Markets Authority, and has used them across cash savings, mortgages, and investment platforms.
Secondary international competitiveness and growth objective (FSMA 2023)
Introduced by the Financial Services and Markets Act 2023 and in force from August 2023. The FCA must, when discharging its general functions, "act in a way which, so far as reasonably possible, facilitates the international competitiveness of the UK economy (including in particular the financial services sector) and its growth in the medium to long term", subject to the primary objectives. Politically, this objective was a signal from a post-Brexit Treasury that the FCA had become too cautious.

Powers

The FCA's powers under FSMA are extensive. They are the practical tools by which the statutory objectives are pursued.

Authorisation
No firm may carry on regulated activity in the UK without FCA (or PRA-and-FCA) authorisation. The FCA authorises new firms, varies existing permissions, and withdraws permission where the firm no longer meets the Threshold Conditions. Authorisation is the gatekeeper power.
Rule-making
The FCA makes binding rules through the FCA Handbook, after public consultation. The Handbook is law in the sense that breach of a rule may give rise to civil liability and to enforcement.
Supervision
Ongoing monitoring of authorised firms through dedicated supervision teams (for the largest firms), thematic reviews (industry-wide), and event-driven supervision (triggered by complaints, market events, whistleblowing). Supervisors can require information, conduct on-site visits, commission skilled-person (Section 166) reports paid for by the firm, and require remedial action.
Enforcement
Where firms or individuals breach rules, the FCA may impose unlimited financial penalties, public censure, restitution orders (requiring firms to compensate harmed consumers), prohibition orders against individuals, and, for market abuse and certain other offences, criminal prosecution. The largest single FCA fine to date is £1.1 billion on Goldman Sachs International for failings related to 1MDB (October 2020, jointly with US authorities).
Product intervention
Since FSMA was amended in 2012, the FCA has power to ban or restrict the sale of specific products it considers harmful, without going through full rule-making. It has used this power against contracts for difference on retail clients, binary options, and certain crypto-asset products.

The FCA Handbook

The FCA Handbook is the rulebook that every authorised firm must comply with. It is divided into seven blocks containing fourteen books (sourcebooks) covering high-level standards, prudential rules, business standards, regulatory processes, redress, specialist sourcebooks, and listing/prospectus/disclosure rules. The Handbook is updated continuously and published in full on the FCA website.

High Level Standards (PRIN, SYSC, COCON, FIT, etc.)
The "Principles for Businesses" (PRIN) are eleven (now twelve, with the Consumer Duty) high-level statements of expected firm conduct. Senior Management Arrangements, Systems and Controls (SYSC) sets out governance and control expectations. COCON contains the Conduct Rules for individuals. FIT is the fit-and-proper test.
Business Standards
Includes the Conduct of Business Sourcebook (COBS, the main rules for investment business), the Insurance Conduct of Business Sourcebook (ICOBS), the Mortgages and Home Finance Conduct of Business Sourcebook (MCOB), the Banking Conduct of Business Sourcebook (BCOBS), and the Consumer Credit Sourcebook (CONC). Each contains the detailed conduct rules for the relevant market.
Prudential standards
Includes the Prudential sourcebook for Banks (BIPRU), the Investment Firms Prudential Regime (MIFIDPRU, the UK successor to MiFID II prudential rules), and the Interim Prudential Sourcebooks. These rules apply to FCA solo-regulated firms; dual-regulated firms follow PRA prudential rules.
Redress and dispute resolution
The Dispute Resolution sourcebook (DISP) sets out complaints-handling requirements and the route to the Financial Ombudsman Service (FOS). The Compensation sourcebook (COMP) sets out the rules of the Financial Services Compensation Scheme (FSCS). Together these are the consumer-redress backbone.

The eleven (now twelve) Principles for Businesses are particularly important. They are the high-level statements through which the FCA articulates its expectations and which underpin enforcement when a more detailed rule is silent. Principles 6 ("treat customers fairly") and 7 ("communications must be clear, fair and not misleading") were historically the most enforcement-active. Principle 12, introduced by the Consumer Duty in 2023, now sits above the others for retail customers.

Threshold Conditions

The Threshold Conditions, set out in Schedule 6 to FSMA, are the minimum standards every authorised firm must meet to obtain and retain permission. They cover legal status, location of offices, effective supervision (the firm must be capable of being effectively supervised), appropriate non-financial and financial resources, suitability (of the firm and of its controllers), and a sustainable business model. The FCA must be satisfied of all Threshold Conditions on initial authorisation and on a continuing basis; failure of a condition is grounds for varying or removing permission.

The Consumer Duty (PS22/9)

The Consumer Duty is the most significant FCA reform since FSMA itself. Final policy statement PS22/9 was published in July 2022. The Duty came into force on 31 July 2023 for new and existing open products, and on 31 July 2024 for closed products. It comprises a new Principle 12, three cross-cutting rules, and four outcomes.

Principle 12
"A firm must act to deliver good outcomes for retail customers." This sits above all other principles for retail business and disapplies Principle 6 (treating customers fairly) for retail business; for non-retail business, the existing principles remain.
Cross-cutting rules
Three behavioural requirements: act in good faith towards retail customers, avoid causing foreseeable harm, and enable and support retail customers to pursue their financial objectives.
Products and services outcome
Products and services must be designed to meet the needs, characteristics, and objectives of an identified target market, and distributed appropriately. Manufacturers and distributors both have responsibilities.
Price and value outcome
The price the customer pays must be reasonable relative to the benefits they receive. Firms must conduct and document a "fair value assessment" for every retail product. Poor value is grounds for enforcement.
Consumer understanding outcome
Communications must support customers in making informed decisions. Firms must test communications, monitor outcomes, and adjust where customers are clearly not understanding.
Consumer support outcome
Customer support must be at least as good as the sales support, and barriers to switching, complaining, or cancelling must be no greater than the barriers to buying.

The Duty's evidential demands are substantial: firms must collect outcomes data, report it through governance, evidence "fair value" for every retail product, and have a board-approved annual Consumer Duty report. Through 2024 and 2025 the FCA's supervisory focus shifted from implementation to enforcement, with thematic reviews of cash savings rates, premium finance, and GAP insurance all citing the Duty as the legal hook.

Senior Managers and Certification Regime (SM&CR)

SM&CR is the regime through which the FCA (and the PRA for dual-regulated firms) holds individuals personally accountable for conduct in their areas of responsibility. The regime was rolled out to banks in 2016, to insurers in 2018, and to all other FCA solo-regulated firms in December 2019.

Senior Manager Functions (SMFs)
Defined senior roles (CEO, CFO, Chief Risk Officer, head of compliance, etc.) require pre-approval by the regulator. Each senior manager has a Statement of Responsibilities setting out their personal accountabilities, plus the firm's Management Responsibilities Map showing how responsibilities fit together. The "duty of responsibility" makes each senior manager personally liable for misconduct in their area unless they took reasonable steps to prevent it.
Certification Regime
A wider pool of staff (those whose role could cause significant harm to the firm or its customers) must be certified by the firm annually as fit and proper. The FCA does not pre-approve these individuals, but the firm bears the responsibility for the certification.
Conduct Rules
A short set of personal conduct rules (COCON) applying to almost all staff at authorised firms. Breach of a conduct rule can be enforced against the individual personally, including by prohibition order.

Operational resilience (PS21/3)

The Operational Resilience policy statement (PS21/3, March 2021) introduced a duty for firms to identify their "important business services", set "impact tolerances" beyond which disruption would cause intolerable harm, and conduct severe-but-plausible scenario testing to demonstrate they can remain within those tolerances. Firms had until 31 March 2025 to be fully able to remain within their impact tolerances for severe-but-plausible disruptions. The policy is jointly owned by FCA and PRA and is one of the clearest examples of post-pandemic regulatory thinking on cyber, third-party, and infrastructure risk.

Market regulation

The FCA inherits the UK's regime for wholesale markets, post-Brexit largely an onshored version of EU law.

UK MiFID
The onshored Markets in Financial Instruments Directive (MiFID II) and Regulation (MiFIR). Governs conduct of investment business, best execution, transaction reporting, market structure (regulated markets, MTFs, OTFs), and product governance. The FCA is consulting on a wholesale-markets review to diverge from EU rules.
UK MAR
The Market Abuse Regulation, onshored from EU law. Prohibits insider dealing, unlawful disclosure of inside information, and market manipulation. The FCA brings both civil and criminal proceedings; criminal market abuse prosecutions are brought under the Criminal Justice Act 1993 and the Financial Services Act 2012.
Listing Rules and Prospectus Regulation
The FCA in its capacity as UK Listing Authority sets the rules for admission of securities to listing on the London Stock Exchange, and the prospectus regime for public offerings. Major reform consulted on in 2023 and 2024 to make UK listings more competitive.

Financial crime and AML

Anti-money-laundering supervision is shared in the UK between the FCA, HMRC, the Gambling Commission, and various professional-body supervisors. The FCA supervises banks, building societies, electronic money and payment institutions, and crypto-asset firms for compliance with the Money Laundering, Terrorist Financing and Transfer of Funds (Information on the Payer) Regulations 2017 (MLR 2017). The FCA has fined major banks repeatedly for AML failures (HSBC, Standard Chartered, NatWest among others).

Sustainability disclosure

Three regimes overlap. The FCA has required TCFD-aligned disclosures from listed issuers and asset managers since 2022. The Sustainability Disclosure Requirements (SDR), in policy statement PS23/16 from November 2023, introduced a UK-specific labelling regime for sustainable investment funds (four labels: Sustainability Focus, Sustainability Improvers, Sustainability Impact, Sustainability Mixed Goals) and anti-greenwashing rules across all FCA firms (in force May 2024). The transition plan disclosure framework (TPT, now folded into ISSB-aligned UK Sustainability Reporting Standards) is being developed for large firms.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The FCA is more visible than the PRA, and that is by design

PRA supervision is mostly conducted in private. Capital decisions, ICAAP feedback, and Pillar 2 add-ons rarely reach the press unless something has gone badly wrong. FCA supervision, by contrast, has a public face: high-profile fines, public censures, thematic reviews published in full, "Dear CEO" letters posted on the website. The visibility is deliberate. The conduct mandate depends on deterrence, and deterrence depends on the rest of the industry seeing what happens when a firm fails. For a banking practitioner this means an FCA enforcement action has a reputational dimension far beyond the financial penalty.

Consumer Duty changed the regulator-firm relationship from rules to outcomes

Under the pre-Duty regime, a firm could mount a credible defence by showing it had followed the rules to the letter. Under the Duty, that defence narrows considerably: the firm must also be able to show its rules-compliance produced good customer outcomes. "We followed the manual" is no longer sufficient. The implication for compliance functions is structural: they must now collect, monitor, and act on outcomes data, not just police process compliance. The implication for product teams is that fair-value assessments are now part of the product lifecycle, not an afterthought.

Watch out SM&CR makes individuals personally liable. A senior manager who is found to have failed to take reasonable steps in their area of responsibility can be fined personally, prohibited from financial services, and named publicly. The duty of responsibility is the real teeth: it is not enough to plead ignorance, the question is whether reasonable steps were taken. The standard is high, and the FCA has pursued senior managers (most prominently in the case of Andrew Tinney at Barclays in 2014, and successive cases since) where the firm itself has been the primary defendant.

The secondary growth objective is a political signal, not a policy revolution

The 2023 secondary objective requires the FCA to facilitate UK competitiveness and growth, subject to the primary objectives. It does not, on its face, change anything: the FCA still has consumer protection, market integrity, and competition as its primary mandate, and the secondary objective only binds when there is no conflict. In practice the signal was clear: Parliament wanted a less cautious FCA, more willing to take regulatory risk where the benefit to UK competitiveness was clear. The FCA has responded with the Edinburgh Reforms work, the wholesale markets review, the listing-regime reform, and a more permissive stance on certain crypto and DeFi activities. Whether this is durable depends on whether the next consumer-detriment scandal arrives before the next political cycle.

The FCA-PRA boundary is porous in practice

The textbook says PRA owns prudential and FCA owns conduct. In practice the two overlap on retail lending (where conduct failings can become prudential through redress costs), on culture and governance (both regulators care, neither owns it cleanly), and on operational resilience (where both supervise jointly). The MoU between the two regulators has a veto mechanism: either can ask the other to reconsider an action that affects its objectives. In a serious case, the Treasury can be the tie-breaker. For a practitioner, the practical advice is to know which regulator owns the file you are working on, because supervisory style and tolerance differ.

The flexible portfolio is where the failures hide

The largest firms have dedicated FCA supervisors who know them well. The "flexible portfolio" of smaller firms is supervised thematically and reactively, which is where most consumer detriment is generated and where most enforcement effort is spent. If you are advising a smaller authorised firm, do not assume FCA invisibility means FCA tolerance: the regulator is looking, just not at you specifically, until something draws its attention. The Consumer Duty raises the stakes because every firm in a distribution chain bears responsibility for outcomes, including those whose direct customer contact is minimal.
