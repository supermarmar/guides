UK CRR and Basel 3.1: PS12/25, SS3/24, SS4/24

After Brexit the UK kept the EU Capital Requirements Regulation on its statute book as onshored law, then began the slow business of pulling it back into the PRA's own rulebook. PS12/25 finishes that job and bolts on Basel 3.1, the BCBS's 2017 finalisation package. Two companion supervisory statements, SS3/24 on the definition of default and SS4/24 on the IRB approach, set out the PRA's expectations for how the rules should be applied in practice. Together they form the binding text for UK banks from 1 January 2027.

Step 1

The 30-second version

When the UK left the EU at the end of 2020, the EU Capital Requirements Regulation (CRR) did not disappear. It was retained as "EU-derived domestic legislation" under the European Union (Withdrawal) Act, with the Bank of England and the PRA absorbing the functions previously carried out by the European Banking Authority. From that point the UK had two parallel objectives: first, to repatriate the CRR from a statutory instrument into the PRA Rulebook so that the prudential regulator could amend it directly; and second, to implement the BCBS's December 2017 Basel III finalisation package (popularly "Basel IV", officially "Basel 3.1"). PS12/25, published in July 2024 in near-final form, does both jobs at once. It restates the CRR's substance as PRA rulebook chapters and inserts the Basel 3.1 changes: the revised standardised approach for credit risk, constraints on the advanced internal ratings based (A-IRB) approach, a 72.5 per cent output floor phased in from 50 per cent in year one, the Standardised Measurement Approach (SMA) for operational risk, the Fundamental Review of the Trading Book (FRTB) for market risk, and a revised credit valuation adjustment (CVA) framework.

Two supervisory statements travel with the rulebook. SS3/24 sets out the PRA's expectations for how to apply the definition of default: a 90-days-past-due trigger, a materiality threshold of £100 (retail) or £440 plus 1 per cent (non-retail), unlikeliness-to-pay indicators, technical past-due exclusions, and the conditions for returning an obligor to non-defaulted status. SS4/24, in 21 chapters, sets out the PRA's expectations on the IRB approach end to end: permission, partial use, the use and experience test, rating systems, data representativeness, model deficiencies and the margin of conservatism (MoC) framework, PD development and calibration, LGD general and downturn calibration, in-default LGD, EAD model and calibration, slotting, application of risk parameters, stress testing, and validation. The whole package was scheduled to take effect on 1 January 2026 but the implementation date has been deferred to 1 January 2027.

Feynman test Imagine the EU CRR as a tenant in the UK statute book. After Brexit, the UK landlord (parliament) decided the tenant could stay but had to follow new house rules written by the PRA. PS12/25 is the landlord rewriting the lease in his own words. While he was at it, he added the Basel 3.1 clauses the world had agreed to in 2017: a tighter standardised approach, a floor that says your internal models cannot give you less than 72.5 per cent of standardised capital, a single non-modelled approach for operational risk, and a rebuilt market risk regime. Then he hung two notices on the wall, SS3/24 and SS4/24, telling existing IRB tenants exactly how he expects them to behave: what counts as default, how to estimate PD and LGD and EAD, what to add for model uncertainty, and how to validate everything. The whole thing was meant to take effect on 1 January 2026; the landlord gave everyone an extra year to find their keys.
What is beyond the source The source wiki documents the PS12/25 restatement, the SS3/24 default chapters, and the SS4/24 IRB chapter structure including the Chapter 9 MoC framework and Chapter 17 EAD expectations. This lecture adds: the post-Brexit constitutional pathway that produced PS12/25 (the Edinburgh Reforms of December 2022 and the Financial Services and Markets Act 2023); the specific UK divergences from EU CRR3 that matter for capital planning (output floor phase-in, residential mortgage risk weights, SME treatment, CVA exemptions, IRRBB); the relationship between SS4/24 and the companion SS1/23 on model risk management; a worked example showing the output floor binding for a UK retail mortgage book; and a grandmaster perspective on why other Anglosphere jurisdictions are watching UK divergence carefully.
Step 2

Why this matters

The UK is the first major Basel jurisdiction to publish its full Basel 3.1 implementation in near-final form, ahead of both the EU (whose CRR3 was finalised in 2024 but applies from 2025 with long phase-ins) and the US (where Basel III "endgame" remains under consultation as of 2026). For UK banks, the PRA Rulebook is the binding text; the BCBS standard is the policy origin, but the rulebook is what supervisors will examine. If you cannot read the PRA Rulebook chapters that replace the CRR articles, you cannot quote your own capital position back to the regulator with confidence.

The institutional change matters as much as the technical change. Before PS12/25, amending CRR for the UK required either a statutory instrument under the Withdrawal Act or primary legislation. After PS12/25, the PRA can amend the rules directly under its rulebook-making powers, subject to consultation and the Financial Services and Markets Act 2023 framework. The UK has moved from an EU rule-taker that occasionally onshored variations to a rule-maker that writes its own prudential code. The first round of divergences from EU CRR3 has already produced visible differences: a faster output floor phase-in than the BCBS minimum allows, a bespoke LTV-based residential mortgage risk-weight table, a different SME supporting factor architecture, and broader non-financial counterparty exemptions from the CVA charge.

For an actuary inside a UK bank, three operational consequences follow directly. First, the IRB model estate has to be reviewed end to end against SS4/24, with particular attention to the MoC framework in Chapter 9 (Group A data and methodological deficiencies, Group B underwriting and policy uncertainty, Category C general estimation error) and the EAD expectations in Chapter 17. Second, the definition of default in SS3/24 may require parameter recalibration: the £100 retail materiality threshold differs from the previous EBA-aligned €100, and the unlikeliness-to-pay indicators are operationalised more prescriptively. Third, the output floor will become binding for some UK banks well before the 2030 terminal value of 72.5 per cent, because the standardised approach has itself been tightened in PS12/25 and the floor is phased in from 50 per cent at start of 2027, rising in equal increments to 72.5 per cent at start of 2030. Capital plans must reflect both the level and the path.

Step 3

The mental model

The post-Brexit UK prudential architecture is best understood as a stack of three layers. At the bottom, primary legislation gives the PRA the power to make rules: the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000, as amended by FSMA 2023, is the constitutional foundation. In the middle, the PRA Rulebook contains the binding rules, including the CRR Parts that PS12/25 restated. On top, supervisory statements (SSs) and statements of policy set out the PRA's expectations on how firms should apply those rules. SSs are not legally binding but they are enforcement-grade: a firm that ignores an SS without good reason will struggle to satisfy the PRA that its rule compliance is adequate.

Three flows of authority matter. Rules flow down from FSMA 2023 (parliament) through the PRA Rulebook (delegated rule-making) to firm-level implementation. Expectations flow down from supervisory statements alongside the rules and are tested through the supervisory review process. Reporting and disclosure flow up from firms through COREP/FINREP (the carry-over of EBA reporting templates, retained by the PRA) and through Pillar 3 disclosures. The output floor cuts horizontally across the whole stack: it compares total RWAs under the IRB and standardised approaches and forces the lower to be marked up to 72.5 per cent of the upper, once fully phased in.

Step 4

The detail

Post-Brexit context: from onshoring to the Edinburgh Reforms

At 11pm on 31 December 2020 the UK left the EU's regulatory orbit. The European Union (Withdrawal) Act 2018 onshored the entire EU acquis, including the CRR and CRD IV, as "retained EU law". For prudential purposes the regulation continued to apply more or less unchanged. The PRA absorbed the technical standards and guidelines previously issued by the EBA, and the EBA's binding role disappeared. Where the CRR referred to the EBA, references were reinterpreted as references to the PRA; where the CRR allowed national discretion, that discretion now sat with HM Treasury or the PRA depending on the article. Initial UK divergences during 2021 to 2022 were limited and pragmatic: the PRA implemented the Basel III phase-in of the leverage ratio for major UK banks, made adjustments to liquidity reporting to remove EBA references, and consulted on the standardised approach to counterparty credit risk (SA-CCR). The wholesale Basel 3.1 redesign waited for the new institutional architecture.

That architecture arrived in December 2022 with the Edinburgh Reforms, a package of thirty announcements by the Chancellor designed to reshape UK financial services regulation post-Brexit. Two strands matter for prudential rules. First, the announcement of a "Smarter Regulatory Framework" under which retained EU law in financial services would be repealed and replaced by domestic rules made by the PRA and FCA. Second, an explicit commitment to implement Basel 3.1 with UK-specific tailoring. The implementing legislation was the Financial Services and Markets Act 2023, which received royal assent in June 2023 and gave the PRA the power to make, vary, and revoke rules in areas previously governed by CRR. With that power, the PRA published a consultation paper (CP16/22 in November 2022) and, after consultation, finalised the framework as PS12/25 in July 2024.

What PS12/25 does to the rulebook

PS12/25 is not a single new regulation. It is a restatement: the substance of the CRR is transposed into the PRA Rulebook as a set of named "CRR Parts", each addressing one of the original CRR titles. The Own Funds Part replaces CRR Part Two. The Credit Risk Standardised Approach Part replaces CRR Part Three, Title II, Chapter 2. The Credit Risk Internal Ratings Based Approach Part replaces Chapter 3 of the same title. The same logic applies to operational risk, market risk, large exposures, liquidity (LCR and NSFR), leverage ratio, disclosure (Pillar 3), and securitisation. The article numbering is preserved where possible, which is why the source wiki quotes Article 92 (own funds requirements), Article 111 (exposure value, standardised approach), Article 144 (high-level IRB requirements), Article 163 (retail PD floors), Article 166D (exposure value for A-IRB), Article 178 (definition of default), Article 180 (PD estimation), and Article 182 (EAD estimation) as PRA Rulebook articles.

The substantive changes inside the restated text are the Basel 3.1 changes. The most consequential ones are:

Revised standardised approach for credit risk
More granular risk-weight buckets for unrated exposures, a new LTV-based table for residential mortgages, new buckets for commercial real estate, revised treatment of specialised lending and project finance, and changes to the conversion factors in Table A1. The Table A1 in the source wiki article 111 shows the new conversion factor grid: 100 per cent for credit substitutes and transactions with recourse; 50 per cent for note issuance facilities and UK residential mortgage commitments; 40 per cent for other commitments; 20 per cent for documentary credits and warranties; 10 per cent for unconditionally cancellable commitments.
Constraints on the advanced IRB approach
A-IRB is removed for certain portfolios: large corporates with consolidated revenue above EUR 500 million and equivalent (now expressed in GBP), banks and other financial institutions, and equities. For these portfolios firms must use either the foundation IRB approach (corporates and banks) or the standardised approach (equities). The change reduces the scope of internal model use and addresses BCBS concerns about unwarranted variability of risk-weighted assets across banks.
Input floors on PD, LGD, EAD
Article 163 sets retail PD floors at 0.10 per cent for qualifying revolving retail (non-transactor), 0.10 per cent for retail mortgages on UK residential property, and 0.05 per cent for other retail. Analogous floors apply to LGD (for example, 5 per cent for unsecured corporate, 10 per cent for unsecured retail mortgage) and to A-IRB conversion factors. The floors are a hard backstop against unrealistically low parameter estimates.
Standardised Measurement Approach (SMA) for operational risk
Replaces all three previous approaches (BIA, TSA, AMA) with a single non-modelled approach. Capital is a function of the Business Indicator (a gross income proxy with three components) multiplied by a Business Indicator Component coefficient, with an Internal Loss Multiplier (ILM) scaling the result by the bank's own loss history. The PRA in PS12/25 has retained discretion over whether to apply the ILM, and the near-final position is to set ILM = 1 for all firms, removing the loss-sensitivity element. This is a significant simplification compared to BCBS d424 and a UK-specific design choice.
Fundamental Review of the Trading Book (FRTB)
Replaces the previous market risk framework. New standardised approach is the Sensitivities-Based Method (SBM), with default risk charge and residual risk add-on. Internal models approach uses Expected Shortfall (ES) at 97.5 per cent rather than Value-at-Risk (VaR) at 99 per cent, with non-modellable risk factor (NMRF) charges and profit and loss attribution testing. UK implementation in PS12/25 retains the BCBS architecture with limited national discretion on the trading book / banking book boundary.
Revised CVA framework
Replaces the internal model method for CVA capital with two non-model approaches: the Basic Approach (BA-CVA) and the Standardised Approach (SA-CVA). UK retains exemptions for transactions with non-financial counterparties below specified thresholds, broader than EU CRR3 which has narrower scope.
Output floor
Article 92(3a) of the restated rules. Total RWAs cannot fall below 72.5 per cent of what they would be under the standardised approaches for all risks (the "fully standardised RWA"). Phased in from 1 January 2027 at 50 per cent, rising in 5.625 per cent increments to 72.5 per cent at 1 January 2030. This is the BCBS minimum path. The PRA has retained discretion to accelerate or modify but has chosen not to.

Key UK divergences from EU CRR3

Both the UK and the EU implement BCBS d424, but they differ in several specific areas where national discretion is preserved or has been exercised. For a UK bank operating in both jurisdictions, or for an EU bank operating in the UK, the divergences are operationally material.

SME supporting factor
EU CRR3 retains an SME supporting factor that scales down RWAs for exposures to small and medium-sized enterprises, applying a 76.19 per cent multiplier for exposures up to EUR 1.5 million and 85 per cent above. UK PS12/25 has retained a UK-tailored SME adjustment but with a different threshold structure and a flat 85 per cent multiplier across the relevant exposures, rather than the two-band EU design. The practical effect: UK SME risk weights are slightly less favourable than EU CRR3 below the EUR 1.5m threshold, slightly more aligned above.
Output floor phase-in
Both UK and EU follow the BCBS path: 50 per cent in year one, rising to 72.5 per cent by year five. EU CRR3 adds a transitional cap that effectively delays the bite of the floor for residential mortgage exposures with low historical losses and for certain corporate exposures without external ratings. UK PS12/25 does not include these transitional caps in the same form. The UK floor is closer to the BCBS minimum; the EU floor is softer in the early years.
Residential mortgage risk weights
The standardised approach in PS12/25 uses an LTV-based table specific to UK residential property. Risk weights start at 20 per cent for LTVs below 50 per cent, rise through 25 per cent (50-60), 30 per cent (60-70), 40 per cent (70-80), 50 per cent (80-90), 60 per cent (90-100), and 75 per cent (above 100), broadly aligned with BCBS d424 but with UK-specific calibration of the bands. EU CRR3 uses a similar architecture but with subtly different LTV thresholds and different treatment of buy-to-let exposures.
CVA exemptions
EU CRR3 narrows the previous exemption from the CVA charge for transactions with non-financial counterparties (NFCs) below the clearing threshold. UK PS12/25 retains the broader EU CRR1 exemption substantively unchanged, so UK banks face a lower CVA capital requirement for their corporate derivatives book than EU banks of comparable size. This is one of the more visible UK softenings.
Operational risk ILM
EU CRR3 sets ILM at 1 for Bucket 1 banks (Business Indicator below EUR 1 billion) and allows national discretion above that. UK PS12/25 has chosen ILM = 1 for all firms regardless of size. UK operational risk capital is therefore insensitive to historical losses, which is simpler but loses the incentive effect BCBS intended.
IRRBB treatment
EU CRD VI implements BCBS 368 with a Pillar 1 capital charge proposal under consultation. UK retains IRRBB as a Pillar 2 risk, monitored through the supervisory review process and the PRA's IRRBB supervisory statement (SS31/15 as updated), without a binding Pillar 1 capital number. This is the largest single divergence in approach, although the practical capital impact for most UK banks is small because Pillar 2A already requires explicit IRRBB capital add-ons.
Implementation date
EU CRR3 applies from 1 January 2025 with phase-ins for the output floor stretching to 2030 and beyond. UK PS12/25 was originally scheduled for 1 January 2026 but was deferred in 2025 to 1 January 2027, partly in response to the US delay of the Basel III "endgame" and the desire to keep UK and US implementation roughly aligned. The deferral gives UK banks an extra year for system build and model recalibration.

SS3/24: the definition of default in practice

SS3/24, published 12 September 2024, sets out the PRA's expectations on how firms should apply Article 178 of the restated CRR Internal Ratings Based Approach Part. The supervisory statement has eight chapters: introduction, past due criterion in the identification of default, indications of unlikeliness to pay, application of the definition of default in external data, return to non-defaulted status, consistency in the application of the definition of default, application of the definition of default for retail exposures, and documentation, internal policies, and risk management processes. The substance largely tracks EBA Guidelines GL/2016/07 on the application of the definition of default, with UK-specific terminology and the materiality threshold converted from euros to sterling.

The two triggers for default are Article 178(1)(a) unlikeliness to pay and Article 178(1)(b) the 90-days-past-due criterion. Either alone is sufficient. The 90-days count begins when any principal, interest, or fee has not been paid by its contractual due date; modifications of the payment schedule reset the count to the modified schedule. Where a payment holiday is granted under a contractual right or by law, the days past due count is suspended, but the firm must still consider unlikeliness-to-pay indicators. The "months in arrears" approach commonly used by UK lenders is accepted in principle (paragraph 2.7).

The materiality threshold has two limbs and varies by exposure type. For retail exposures, the credit obligation is past due if the absolute sum of all amounts past due is greater than zero (i.e. any amount counts) and the relative threshold (past-due amount divided by total on-balance-sheet exposure excluding equities) is greater than zero per cent. For non-retail exposures, the absolute threshold is GBP 440 (broadly equivalent to the EU's EUR 500) and the relative threshold is 1 per cent. These thresholds reflect a UK-specific choice that diverges slightly from EBA GL/2016/07.

Unlikeliness-to-pay (UTP) indicators are listed in Article 178(3) and elaborated in SS3/24 Chapter 3. The headline indicators are: the firm puts the credit obligation on non-accrued status (interest stops accruing); the firm makes a specific credit risk adjustment (impairment) resulting from a significant decline in credit quality; the firm sells the credit obligation at a material credit-related economic loss; the firm consents to a distressed restructuring that involves forgiveness or postponement of principal, interest, or fees; the obligor has filed or been put into bankruptcy or similar protection. The PRA expects firms to operationalise each of these into observable triggers in their systems.

A "technical past due situation" (paragraph 2.8) is not a default. The four cases are: a data or system error of the firm; failure of the payment system; a time lag between receipt of payment and allocation to the relevant account; and certain factoring arrangements where receivables are not themselves past due. Importantly, "wrong credit decisions" are explicitly excluded from technical past due, which means that a firm cannot avoid a default classification by arguing that it should have granted forbearance earlier.

Return to non-defaulted status (Chapter 5) requires a probation period during which the obligor must demonstrate sustained payment behaviour. The default cure conditions include: full repayment of all material past-due amounts; the absence of any other UTP indicator; and a minimum cure period (typically 12 months for distressed restructuring, 3 months for past-due-only defaults), during which the obligor's behaviour is monitored. The cure period is a key calibration parameter for PD models because it directly affects the observed default rate over a given window.

SS4/24: the IRB approach end to end

SS4/24, also published 12 September 2024, sets out the PRA's expectations on the IRB approach to credit risk. It has 21 chapters, organised in roughly the same sequence a model developer would follow from permission to validation. The structure is:

  1. Chapter 1. Introduction and definitions.
  2. Chapter 2. Permission to use the IRB approach: application requirements, the supervisory assessment process, change permissions for material model changes.
  3. Chapter 3. Partial use and reversion to less sophisticated approaches: the conditions under which a firm may keep some exposures on the standardised approach while using IRB elsewhere, and the conditions for forced reversion.
  4. Chapter 4. Use and experience test: the requirement that IRB ratings and parameters be used in actual credit decisioning, monitoring, pricing, and capital allocation, not just for regulatory capital. Article 144(1)(b) requires "essential role" in risk management.
  5. Chapter 5. Qualifying revolving retail exposures (QRRE): the subset of revolving retail exposures (credit cards, overdrafts) that receive lower correlation factors in the IRB formula, subject to volatility tests.
  6. Chapter 6. High-level expectations: governance, model risk management, documentation.
  7. Chapter 7. Rating systems: the design of rating philosophies (point-in-time versus through-the-cycle), grade structures, and assignment processes.
  8. Chapter 8. Data representativeness: the requirement that the reference data set used for model development reflects the current portfolio in terms of obligor and facility characteristics.
  9. Chapter 9. Model deficiencies and margin of conservatism: the MoC framework described below.
  10. Chapter 10. PD model development: discriminatory power requirements, treatment of low-default portfolios, segmentation, expert judgement.
  11. Chapter 11. PD calibration: long-run average default rate, choice of historical observation period (at least five years per Article 180), adjustments for portfolio mix.
  12. Chapter 12. LGD general expectations and model development: economic loss concept, discount rates, recoveries, costs.
  13. Chapter 13. LGD calibration general: structure of the calibration process.
  14. Chapter 14. LGD long-run average calibration: estimation of LRA LGD over good and bad years.
  15. Chapter 15. LGD downturn calibration: identification of downturn period, downturn LGD methodology.
  16. Chapter 16. LGD in-default estimation (ELBE and LGD-in-default): the PRA's expectations for treating defaulted exposures.
  17. Chapter 17. EAD model and calibration: the conversion factor framework described below.
  18. Chapter 18. Slotting approach: the supervisory slot table for specialised lending where firms do not estimate own parameters.
  19. Chapter 19. Application of risk parameters: how PD, LGD, and EAD feed into the RWA formula.
  20. Chapter 20. Stress tests used in the assessment of capital adequacy: ICAAP linkage and IRB-specific stress methodologies.
  21. Chapter 21. Review of estimates (validation): the validation process, frequency, scope, escalation.

The MoC framework (SS4/24 Chapter 9)

Chapter 9 is the most heavily applied chapter in practice because it governs how firms quantify and add a margin of conservatism (MoC) to risk parameter estimates. The MoC is required by Article 179(1)(f) of the IRB Part and is intended to cover uncertainty that the central estimate cannot capture.

The framework splits deficiencies into two groups and the resulting MoC into three categories. Group A covers identified data and methodological deficiencies: missing or materially changed default triggers in historical observations, missing or inaccurate date of default, missing or outdated rating assignments, missing information on cash flow sources, missing or outdated risk drivers, missing or inaccurate information on future recoveries, missing or inaccurate data for economic loss calculation, limited representativeness of historical observations when external data are used, potential bias from the choice of approach to averaging one-year default rates, the necessity of adjusting that average, and missing information for downturn LGD estimation. Group B covers relevant changes to underwriting standards, risk appetite, collection and recovery policies, and any other source of additional uncertainty. The corresponding MoC categories are Category A for Group A deficiencies, Category B for Group B deficiencies, and Category C for the general estimation error, which reflects the dispersion of the statistical estimator at the calibration segment level.

The PRA expects firms to identify and where possible correct the underlying deficiencies through "appropriate adjustments" to the modelled estimate, and only then add the MoC to cover residual uncertainty. The final MoC is the sum of the three category MoCs (paragraph 9.10) and is added to the modelled estimate (paragraph 9.11). The PRA is explicit that the MoC cannot reduce the parameter estimate (paragraph 9.12) and that adopting a MoC does not replace the need to fix the underlying model (paragraph 9.15). The framework is therefore a temporary backstop, not a permanent fix, and the PRA monitors trends in MoC levels as an indicator of model estate health.

The EAD framework (SS4/24 Chapter 17)

Chapter 17 governs the estimation of exposure at default (EAD) or, equivalently, conversion factors (CF) for off-balance-sheet exposures. The PRA accepts that the Limit Factor estimation approach (expressing EAD as a percentage of total limit) is one of several compliant routes (paragraph 17.2). Firms may use Limit Factors either as an intermediate step to derive CFs that go into the standard formula (Article 166D), or directly as the basis for EAD (paragraph 17.3).

The downturn requirement is explicit (paragraph 17.6): downturn EAD or CF estimates should reflect the exposure expected to be outstanding if default occurs in the next year, assuming economic downturn conditions in that year and unchanged firm policies on exposure control other than changes induced by the downturn itself. The PRA's eleven detailed expectations in paragraph 17.8 cover everything from facility-level application of estimates, treatment of paucity of data, granular documentation of how internal and external data plus expert judgement combine to produce estimates, awareness of economic cycle impact, demonstration of weakness awareness, discrimination by product features and customer type, and identification of dependencies between default rates and EADs in downturn.

Two operational details often trip firms up. First, paragraph 17.11 requires firms to investigate the distribution of realised CFs in the reference data set, base the estimate on an appropriate point along that distribution rather than blindly taking the median, consider further segmentation for facilities close to full utilisation, and apply the more conservative of long-run average or downturn CF estimates. Second, paragraph 17.14 requires accrued interest, other due payments, and limit excesses to be included in EAD reference data; the corresponding paragraph 17.17 says realised EADs and CFs should not be capped at the principal amount outstanding or facility limits when measuring the reference data, even though the regulatory EAD itself may be subject to other constraints.

SS1/23: the companion model risk management framework

SS1/23, published in May 2023, predates PS12/25 and SS4/24 but is the indispensable companion. It applies to all PRA-authorised firms that meet specified materiality thresholds (broadly, the same firms that use IRB models for capital). SS1/23 introduces a tiered model categorisation: Tier 1 (high materiality, complex, high risk of error), Tier 2 (medium materiality), Tier 3 (low materiality). The framework's five principles cover model identification and inventory, governance, model development and implementation, validation, and model risk mitigants. SS4/24's IRB-specific expectations sit on top of SS1/23: a UK IRB PD model is almost always Tier 1 under SS1/23, with all the governance and validation cadence that implies, and it must also meet the IRB-specific expectations in SS4/24 chapters 7 through 21.

The implementation timeline

The PRA's original PS17/23, published in December 2023, set 1 January 2026 as the implementation date for the Basel 3.1 changes, with the output floor phasing in 50 / 55.625 / 61.25 / 66.875 / 72.5 per cent over five years to 1 January 2030. In September 2024 the PRA published PS9/24, finalising the credit risk and operational risk chapters of PS17/23. In July 2024 the broader near-final policy statement PS12/25 was issued, covering the remaining strands including the output floor, large exposures, and market risk. In 2025, after the US Federal Reserve confirmed that the US Basel III endgame would be substantially delayed and the EU confirmed its phased CRR3 timetable, the PRA announced a one-year deferral, moving UK implementation to 1 January 2027.

Worked example: output floor on a UK mortgage book

Setting. A UK retail bank has a residential mortgage book of GBP 50 billion, all owner-occupied and entirely on the A-IRB approach. The average LTV at origination is 65 per cent and the weighted-average risk weight under A-IRB at year-end 2026 was 12 per cent (so IRB RWAs of GBP 6 billion). Under the standardised approach in PS12/25, applying the LTV-based table, the weighted-average risk weight would be about 30 per cent (so standardised RWAs of GBP 15 billion). The bank holds GBP 600 million of CET1 against the IRB RWAs (10 per cent CET1 ratio against the mortgage book).

Question. How does the output floor change the bank's required capital from 1 January 2027 through 1 January 2031?

Working. The output floor sets total RWAs equal to the maximum of IRB RWAs and (floor percentage) times standardised RWAs. For the mortgage book in isolation:

Floor applied to standardised RWAs:
2027 (50%):    0.500 * 15.0 bn = 7.50 bn   > IRB 6.00 bn  --> floor binds, RWA = 7.50 bn
2028 (55.625%): 0.55625 * 15.0 bn = 8.34 bn  --> floor binds, RWA = 8.34 bn
2029 (61.25%): 0.6125 * 15.0 bn = 9.19 bn   --> floor binds, RWA = 9.19 bn
2030 (66.875%): 0.66875 * 15.0 bn = 10.03 bn --> floor binds, RWA = 10.03 bn
2031 (72.5%):  0.725 * 15.0 bn = 10.875 bn  --> floor binds, RWA = 10.88 bn

Required CET1 at 10 per cent ratio (illustrative; ignores buffers):
2026 baseline: 600 m
2027:           750 m   (+25%)
2028:           834 m   (+39% on baseline)
2029:           919 m   (+53%)
2030:         1,003 m   (+67%)
2031:         1,088 m   (+81%)

Conclusion. The output floor binds from the first day of implementation because the IRB risk weight (12 per cent) is well below the floored standardised risk weight (15 per cent at 50 per cent of 30 per cent in 2027). By the time the floor is fully phased in, this book consumes 81 per cent more CET1 than under the unconstrained A-IRB. In practice the bank would respond by (i) raising mortgage pricing to recover the marginal capital cost; (ii) reviewing whether to retain A-IRB for the mortgage book at all, given that the floored capital approximates a heavy-tailored standardised number; (iii) considering risk transfer (significant risk transfer securitisation) on the highest-LTV tranches where the floor bites hardest. The output floor is the single largest structural change in UK Basel 3.1 for mortgage-heavy retail banks.

Pillar 3, large exposures, liquidity, and leverage in PS12/25

The headline credit, operational, and market risk changes attract most attention, but PS12/25 also restates and updates several other CRR strands. The Pillar 3 disclosure Part replicates the revised BCBS disclosure templates and aligns UK disclosures with the BCBS DIS framework. The large exposures Part retains the 25 per cent of Tier 1 limit for exposures to non-G-SIB counterparties and the 15 per cent G-SIB-to-G-SIB limit, with the BCBS d424 changes to exposure measurement and counterparty definitions. The liquidity Part keeps the LCR and NSFR substantively unchanged from the onshored CRR text, with PRA-specific clarifications on inflows and outflows. The leverage ratio retains the 3.25 per cent minimum for major UK banks with an additional G-SIB add-on, alongside the BCBS d424 changes to off-balance-sheet exposure measurement. None of these is in itself a revolution; together they tidy up the post-Brexit rulebook and align it with current BCBS thinking.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

From rule-taker to rule-maker

For two decades the UK was the most influential EU member at the negotiating table on bank capital but was ultimately bound by whatever CRR text the EU institutions adopted. Post-Brexit the UK has flipped that relationship: it now writes its own rules and other jurisdictions watch what it does. Switzerland, Singapore, Australia, and Canada have all referred to PS12/25 in their own Basel 3.1 consultations. The UK approach is more principles-based than EU CRR3 in some respects (IRRBB, CVA exemptions, operational risk ILM) and more strictly aligned with BCBS in others (output floor phase-in without transitional caps). The mix is best read as a deliberate "Basel-plus-targeted-tailoring" strategy, not a pure regulatory race to the bottom or top.

Supervisory statements are enforcement-grade

SS3/24 and SS4/24 are formally non-binding, but they are written in the same drafting style as the rulebook and are referred to in PRA examinations and supervisory letters. A firm that ignores SS4/24 Chapter 9 on the MoC framework without a defensible justification will not pass an IRB model review. Treat the SSs as binding for practical purposes; the only places where the "non-binding" status matters are where you can show that a deviation produces a more conservative outcome and the supervisor agrees.

The 2027 deferral is a working window, not a holiday

The extra year between January 2026 and January 2027 is an opportunity to finish model recalibrations, data lineage work, and capital planning under the new rules, not an opportunity to wait. The PRA has signalled that it will use the extra time to do additional model reviews under SS4/24 and to assess firms' readiness for the output floor. Banks that interpret the deferral as a relaxation of pressure are likely to find themselves under intensified scrutiny in the second half of 2026.

Watch out The "near-final" label on PS12/25 is technical: it indicates that the PRA reserves the right to make minor technical adjustments before the rules formally come into force. It does not mean the substance is provisional. Treat the published near-final text as the rulebook from your planning perspective; the only material residual uncertainty is around the precise calibration of certain transitional caps and the application of the ILM in operational risk if HMT directs a change.

Watch the output floor, not the input floors

The input floors on PD (Article 163), LGD, and CF are operationally visible but they are calibrated to bind only on the lowest-risk segments. The output floor is calibrated to bind on entire portfolios. For a UK A-IRB bank with a low-risk mortgage book, the output floor will bind from day one; for a corporate bank with a balanced book including SMEs and unsecured retail, the floor may bind only in later phase-in years or not at all. Map your portfolio against the standardised RWA equivalents now and you will know which year the floor starts to dictate your capital position.

Read SS4/24 as a model risk framework, not just an IRB framework

The chapters of SS4/24 on data representativeness (Chapter 8), model deficiencies (Chapter 9), validation (Chapter 21), and stress testing (Chapter 20) are written in a way that applies as much to IFRS 9 ECL models, behavioural liquidity models, and ICAAP models as to IRB capital models. The PRA's broader thinking on model risk management (SS1/23) is visible throughout. A team that builds a strong SS4/24 process for IRB will find it transports well to the other model families.

Other Anglosphere jurisdictions are watching

Australia (APRA's APS 113), Singapore (MAS Notice 637), Canada (OSFI CAR Guideline), and Hong Kong (HKMA SPM CA-G-1 series) all face the same Basel 3.1 implementation challenge. None has yet published a complete restatement comparable to PS12/25. Several have explicitly cited UK PS12/25 as a reference point during their own consultations. If the UK approach proves operationally workable for the major banks during 2027 to 2028, expect a quiet convergence by other Anglosphere supervisors towards similar architectural choices. If UK divergences (particularly on CVA exemptions and operational risk ILM) attract BCBS criticism in the next RCAP review, expect those choices to be wound back.
