PRA: the Prudential Regulation Authority

The PRA is the UK's prudential supervisor for banks, building societies, designated investment firms, and insurance firms. Sitting alongside the FCA in the post-2012 twin-peaks model, and operating as a subsidiary of the Bank of England since 2017, the PRA writes the rulebook that turns Basel into binding UK law, supervises around 1,500 firms through a risk-based categorisation framework, and makes the day-to-day judgements that decide whether a UK financial institution can operate, expand, or must withdraw permissions.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA) is the UK's prudential regulator and supervisor for banks, building societies, credit unions, insurers, and major investment firms. It was created by the Financial Services Act 2012, which abolished the Financial Services Authority and split its responsibilities along the twin-peaks model: prudential supervision to the PRA, conduct supervision to the new Financial Conduct Authority (FCA). The PRA opened for business on 1 April 2013 as a subsidiary of the Bank of England with its own legal personality. The Bank of England and Financial Services Act 2016 then collapsed that separate legal entity into the Bank itself with effect from 1 March 2017, so that the PRA today exists as a function of the Bank rather than as an independent company. Policy is set by the Prudential Regulation Committee (PRC), chaired by the Governor of the Bank of England, with the Deputy Governor for Prudential Regulation acting as chief executive of the PRA.

The PRA has three statutory objectives. The general objective is to promote the safety and soundness of the firms it regulates. The insurance objective, which applies only to insurers, is to contribute to securing an appropriate degree of protection for policyholders. The secondary competition objective requires the PRA, when discharging its general functions, to facilitate effective competition in the markets for services provided by PRA-authorised persons, so far as is consistent with the two primary objectives. The Financial Services and Markets Act 2023 added a further secondary objective of facilitating the international competitiveness and medium- to long-term growth of the UK economy, subject to international standards. The PRA publishes binding rules in the PRA Rulebook and communicates expectations and policy approach through Supervisory Statements, Statements of Policy, Policy Statements, and Consultation Papers.

Feynman test Imagine the UK financial system as a fleet of vessels at sea. The Bank of England is the harbourmaster, watching the weather and the channel; the FCA is the coastguard, checking that crews treat their passengers well; the PRA is the surveyor that walks aboard each ship, opens the hold, taps the hull, measures the ballast, and decides whether the ship can sail at all. It has a binder of rules that say how thick the hull must be, how much ballast each class of ship must carry, and how often the captain must report his readings. It rates each ship from Category 1 (an ocean liner whose sinking would close the harbour) down to Category 5 (a fishing boat whose sinking would inconvenience nobody but its owner), and it visits the liner constantly while leaving the fishing boat mostly alone. When it decides a ship is unseaworthy it can refuse to renew its licence, force it to reduce its load, or order it back to port. It does not punish the captain for rudeness to passengers; that is the coastguard's job. It cares whether the ship will float.

Step 2

Why this matters

For any UK-authorised bank or insurer the PRA is the day-to-day regulator. The PRA Rulebook is the binding text; PRA Supervisory Statements are how the regulator tells the firm what it expects in practice. If you work in a UK bank or building society, almost every prudential policy document you produce, from the ICAAP to the recovery plan to the model change request, is written for a PRA audience. If you have ever sat opposite a regulator across a model validation meeting in London, the regulator was almost certainly from the PRA.

The PRA matters for a second, less obvious reason: it is one of the more prescriptive supervisors among the major jurisdictions. The PRA has a reputation for thick supervisory statements, detailed expectations, and a willingness to enforce. SS4/24 alone runs to twenty-one chapters of expectations on the IRB approach. SS1/23 sets out model risk management principles that go further than most peer jurisdictions. Firms that operate in multiple jurisdictions consistently find that the PRA's documentary requirements are heavier than those of, say, the OCC or APRA, although less prescriptive than the SSM in some respects. Understanding the PRA's published expectations is therefore a substantial part of compliance work for any UK firm.

For an actuary working in a UK bank, three institutional facts have direct day-to-day consequences. First, the PRA's categorisation of your firm dictates how often you meet your supervisors and how much scrutiny each capital plan or model change attracts; Category 1 firms have full-time supervision teams, Category 5 firms may interact with the PRA only at annual or biennial intervals. Second, the PRA writes its own rulebook now: post-Brexit, the EU CRR is being progressively restated into PRA rules through the near-final CRR exercise, and the PRA has used the restatement to make UK-specific adjustments such as the implementation of Basel 3.1. Third, the PRA's supervisory statements, although not strictly binding, are treated as binding in practice; the PRA expects firms to comply or to explain credibly why they do not.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture three layers. At the top, the Bank of England as a single statutory entity. Inside it, the Prudential Regulation Committee as the PRA's decision-making body. Around it, the FCA as the twin-peaks conduct counterpart. Below it, the universe of PRA-authorised firms organised into five supervisory categories. The arrows that connect these layers are the powers and obligations created by FSMA and the PRA Rulebook.

Three flows of authority matter. Rules and supervisory expectations flow downward from the PRC through the PRA Rulebook and Supervisory Statements to firms. Reporting and approvals flow upward from firms to the PRA: regulatory returns, ICAAP packs, model change requests, senior manager applications. And coordination flows sideways between the PRA, the FCA, and other parts of the Bank, including the Resolution Directorate that prepares for and executes bank failure, and the Financial Policy Committee that sets macroprudential policy.

Step 4

The detail

Legislative history: from tripartite to twin peaks to subsidiary to function

Until 2013 the UK operated the so-called tripartite system established by the Bank of England Act 1998 and FSMA 2000. The Bank of England held responsibility for monetary policy and high-level financial stability. The Financial Services Authority, created in 2001, was a single regulator responsible for both prudential and conduct supervision of all financial firms. HM Treasury provided the legislative framework and held the ultimate political responsibility. The arrangement was widely criticised after the 2007 to 2008 financial crisis on the ground that no single body had been responsible for the prudential safety of UK banks at the system level, with the Northern Rock failure cited as the most visible illustration of supervisory gaps.

The Financial Services Act 2012, given Royal Assent in December 2012 and brought into force on 1 April 2013, dissolved the FSA and replaced it with the new architecture. The PRA was established as a subsidiary company of the Bank of England, the FCA as an independent statutory body, the Financial Policy Committee was placed inside the Bank to take macroprudential decisions, and the Bank itself was given a new objective of protecting and enhancing the stability of the UK financial system. The Bank of England and Financial Services Act 2016, with effect from 1 March 2017, ended the PRA's separate legal personality and brought it inside the Bank as a function rather than a subsidiary. From that date the PRA's name appears on Supervisory Statements and Policy Statements but the legal entity that issues them is the Bank of England, acting through the Prudential Regulation Committee.

Tripartite system (1998 to 2013)
HM Treasury, Bank of England, and FSA. Single regulator (FSA) for both prudential and conduct. Criticised after 2008 for blurred accountabilities between prudential safety, conduct of business, and systemic stability.
Twin peaks (from 2013)
Prudential supervision separated from conduct supervision. PRA takes prudential responsibility for banks, building societies, credit unions, designated investment firms, and insurers. FCA takes conduct responsibility for all firms and prudential responsibility for non-PRA firms.
PRA as subsidiary (2013 to 2017)
PRA Ltd was a separate company wholly owned by the Bank of England. It had its own board chaired by the Governor. Decisions were made by the PRA Board, with formal communications and rule-making in the PRA's name.
PRA as Bank function (from 2017)
PRA Ltd dissolved. The PRA's powers vest in the Bank of England, exercised by the Prudential Regulation Committee. The Bank publishes documents under the PRA name as a matter of convention and statutory branding, but no separate legal entity exists.

Statutory objectives

The PRA's objectives are set out in FSMA 2000 as amended. There are two primary objectives, two secondary objectives, and a duty to have regard to a number of further matters when discharging its functions.

General objective
To promote the safety and soundness of PRA-authorised persons. Section 2B of FSMA. Pursued primarily by seeking to ensure that the business of regulated firms is carried on in a way that avoids any adverse effect on the stability of the UK financial system, and that the failure of a regulated firm does not have an adverse effect on the stability of the system. Note the language: the PRA does not have a zero-failure regime, it has a no-adverse-effect-on-stability regime.
Insurance objective
To contribute to the securing of an appropriate degree of protection for those who are or may become policyholders. Section 2C of FSMA. Applies only to PRA-authorised insurers and not to banks. Distinct from the FCA's conduct duty toward consumers.
Secondary competition objective
When the PRA discharges its general functions, it must, so far as is reasonably possible, act in a way that facilitates effective competition in the markets for services provided by PRA-authorised persons. Section 2H of FSMA. Inserted by the FS (Banking Reform) Act 2013. Sits below the primary objectives.
Secondary international competitiveness and growth objective
Added by FSMA 2023, in force from 29 August 2023. Requires the PRA to act in a way that facilitates the international competitiveness of the UK economy and its medium- to long-term growth, subject to alignment with international standards. The PRA must report annually on how it has discharged this objective. The objective has been politically prominent post-Brexit as a counterweight to perceived over-regulation.

The PRA must also have regard, when making rules and supervising firms, to the principles of good regulation set out in FSMA: proportionality, efficient and economic use of resources, transparency, sustainable economic growth, international standards, and others. The annual remit letter from HM Treasury elaborates these "have regards" with current government priorities, such as climate transition or the application of the Edinburgh Reforms.

Powers

The PRA's statutory powers fall into four families. The first is authorisation: no firm may carry on a regulated activity in the UK without permission under Part 4A of FSMA. For PRA-regulated activities the PRA grants the permission, with the FCA's consent on conduct matters. The second is rule-making: the PRA may make rules binding on PRA-authorised firms under section 137G of FSMA, and these rules are published in the PRA Rulebook. The third is supervision: the PRA may impose individual capital and liquidity requirements (Pillar 2), require a firm to take or refrain from action (a "section 55J variation" or an "own-initiative" variation of permission), require information, and direct a skilled person review under section 166. The fourth is enforcement: the PRA may impose financial penalties, public censure, prohibition orders against individuals, and ultimately the withdrawal of permission to carry on regulated activities.

Authorisation
Part 4A FSMA. PRA-regulated activities include accepting deposits, effecting and carrying out contracts of insurance, dealing in investments as principal where the firm is a designated investment firm. A firm authorised for PRA activities is dual-regulated.
Rule-making
Section 137G FSMA. Subject to consultation under section 138I. Affirmed by the PRC. Onshored EU regulations and parts of the CRR are being progressively transferred into the PRA Rulebook through the near-final CRR restatement that gives the PRA the power to amend that material directly.
Pillar 2 capital and liquidity
The PRA sets each firm's Total Capital Requirement (TCR = Pillar 1 + Pillar 2A) and PRA buffer (Pillar 2B) after reviewing the ICAAP. Decision communicated via Capital Requirements Direction. Pillar 2A covers risks not captured or not adequately captured by Pillar 1. The PRA buffer covers stress losses.
Skilled person reviews (s166)
Section 166 FSMA. Allows the PRA to commission an independent expert at the firm's expense to investigate a specific concern. Common precursor to enforcement, but also used as a diagnostic tool. Skilled persons are typically the Big Four or specialist consultancies on a panel arrangement.
Enforcement
Financial penalties under section 206 FSMA. Public censure. Prohibition orders against individuals under section 56. Withdrawal of permission under section 55J. Notable PRA enforcement actions include penalties against Citigroup (2018), Goldman Sachs International (2019), and Credit Suisse International (2020 to 2023, after Archegos).

Publication types

The PRA produces a defined set of document types, each with a different status. Understanding the hierarchy is essential when interpreting a piece of PRA material: the type of document tells you whether it is binding, whether it is open to challenge, and how the PRA expects it to be applied.

PRA Rulebook
The compendium of binding rules made by the PRA under FSMA. Organised by topic (Capital Buffers, Disclosure (CRR), Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment, and so on) and by firm category (CRR firms, non-CRR firms, Solvency II firms). Replaced the older PRA Handbook (inherited from the FSA) in 2014. Subject to consultation before changes are made. prarulebook.co.uk.
Supervisory Statements (SS)
The PRA's published expectations on a specific topic. Not legally binding rules, but firms are expected to apply them. Numbered sequentially within a year, for example SS1/23 (Model Risk Management) or SS4/24 (IRB Approach). Firms that depart from a Supervisory Statement should be able to explain why.
Statements of Policy (SoP)
The PRA's stated approach to using a specific power, such as the framework for setting Pillar 2A capital, the methodology for the PRA buffer, or the approach to enforcement. Drives consistency across firms.
Policy Statements (PS)
The outcome document of a consultation. Sets out the PRA's final response to feedback on a Consultation Paper and announces the final form of any new rules or supervisory statements. PS12/25, for example, finalised the CRR near-final restatement on credit risk.
Consultation Papers (CP)
Proposals for new rules or supervisory statements, issued for public consultation. Typically open for three to four months. Include a cost-benefit analysis and a compatibility assessment against the PRA's objectives.
Discussion Papers (DP)
Early-stage thinking on emerging issues, intended to invite views before specific proposals are formulated. Less common than CPs.

The categorisation framework

The PRA's Approach to Banking Supervision and Approach to Insurance Supervision set out how the PRA decides what intensity of supervision to apply to each firm. The starting point is the firm's "potential impact" on financial stability and on policyholders, judged on size, complexity, interconnectedness, and substitutability of services. Firms are placed into one of five categories.

Category 1
The most systemically important firms. Failure would have a significant adverse effect on financial stability. In banking, the UK G-SIB and D-SIB group: HSBC, Barclays, Lloyds Banking Group, NatWest Group, Standard Chartered, Santander UK. In insurance, the largest life and composite insurers. Continuous supervision by a dedicated team; full-scope assessment annually.
Category 2
Significant firms that nevertheless do not pose the same systemic threat as Category 1. In banking, Nationwide Building Society, large branches and subsidiaries of foreign banking groups, smaller domestic banks above the ring-fencing threshold. Intensive supervision but less than continuous; full periodic assessment.
Category 3
Medium-impact firms where failure would have moderate adverse effect on policyholders or on parts of the market. Regular supervision with proportional intensity. The largest segment of mid-tier UK banks and building societies fits here.
Category 4
Smaller firms where failure would have limited stability impact. Periodic supervision focused on key risks. Includes most small banks, building societies, and smaller insurers.
Category 5
The smallest firms, where supervisory contact is largely baseline reporting and exception-driven. Includes the smallest deposit takers and run-off insurance vehicles.

Categorisation drives every aspect of supervision: how often the firm meets its supervisors, the depth of the Periodic Summary Meeting (PSM) cycle, the scope of skilled person reviews, the depth of Pillar 2 analysis, and the speed with which authorisations and model change applications are processed. A Category 1 firm will have a supervisory team of 20 to 40 staff embedded in close to continuous contact; a Category 5 firm may speak to the PRA only when it files its annual returns.

The Senior Managers and Certification Regime

The Senior Managers and Certification Regime (SM&CR) is the PRA's and FCA's joint framework for individual accountability. Introduced for banks in March 2016 (Banking Reform Act 2013) and extended to insurers in December 2018 and to other FSMA firms in December 2019, the regime replaced the older Approved Persons Regime with a sharper, more accountability-driven structure.

SM&CR has three components. The Senior Managers Regime applies to a defined list of senior management functions: the chief executive, chief finance officer, chief risk officer, head of internal audit, chair, board members, and certain other roles. Each senior manager must be pre-approved by the PRA (for PRA-designated functions) or the FCA. Each must have a Statement of Responsibilities and there must be a Management Responsibilities Map for the firm that shows who is accountable for what. Senior managers have a statutory Duty of Responsibility under section 66A of FSMA: if a regulatory breach occurs in an area within the senior manager's responsibility, the regulator may take enforcement action against the senior manager unless the senior manager took such steps as a person in their position could reasonably be expected to take to avoid the breach.

The Certification Regime applies to a broader population of staff who, although not senior managers, perform roles where their conduct could materially affect the firm or its customers. The firm itself, not the PRA, certifies these staff annually as fit and proper for their role. The Conduct Rules apply to all employees and set out basic standards of behaviour (act with integrity, act with due skill, be open and cooperative with regulators, observe proper standards of market conduct, and so on).

ICAAP, Pillar 2, and the PRA's capital framework

Every PRA-authorised bank must conduct an annual Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process. The ICAAP document is the firm's own analysis of the capital it needs to hold against all material risks, including risks not captured by Pillar 1. The PRA reviews the ICAAP through the Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process and issues two outputs.

Total Capital Requirement (TCR)
Pillar 1 capital plus Pillar 2A capital. Pillar 2A is a firm-specific add-on to cover risks not captured (or not adequately captured) in Pillar 1, such as concentration risk, interest rate risk in the banking book, pension risk, and any model deficiencies. Communicated via a Capital Requirements Direction.
PRA buffer (Pillar 2B)
A firm-specific buffer over and above the TCR plus the regulatory buffers (Capital Conservation Buffer, Countercyclical Buffer, G-SII/D-SII buffer). Sized to cover stress losses identified through the firm's own stress tests and the PRA's solvency stress test. The PRA expects firms to use the PRA buffer to absorb stress losses; using the buffer is not a breach of TCR but does trigger heightened supervisory dialogue.

The PRA's Statement of Policy on Pillar 2 (last refreshed in 2025) sets out the methodologies the PRA uses to assess each risk category for Pillar 2A. Credit concentration risk uses a Herfindahl-Hirschman based approach; interest rate risk uses a parallel shock combined with non-parallel scenarios; operational risk uses both a scenario-based approach and a benchmark derived from the standardised approach. The methodology is mechanical but the PRA reserves judgement: in practice, Pillar 2A add-ons are negotiated between supervisors and the firm during the SREP cycle.

The Resolvability Assessment Framework

The Bank of England is the UK resolution authority and the PRA is the prudential regulator that supervises a firm's preparedness for resolution. The Resolvability Assessment Framework (RAF), introduced in 2019, requires the eight major UK banks (HSBC, Barclays, Lloyds, NatWest, Standard Chartered, Santander UK, Nationwide, Virgin Money) to assess themselves against three resolvability outcomes: adequate financial resources, the ability to continue doing business through resolution and restructuring, and the ability to coordinate and communicate effectively through a resolution. Firms must publish a summary of their RAF self-assessment every two years and the Bank publishes its own assessment alongside.

The framework was first reported in June 2022, with second-round reports in 2024. The Bank has used the published reports to flag specific firms as having residual issues to remediate, naming the issues publicly. For an actuary in a UK bank, the RAF is a major source of model and process work: the financial resources outcome requires the firm to be able to demonstrate that capital and liquidity will be sufficient through resolution, which means stress-testing and reverse-stress-testing capabilities that go beyond the regular ICAAP.

The recent supervisory programme

The PRA's published agenda for 2024 to 2026 is dense. The most important elements for a credit risk modeller are the near-final CRR restatement (the conversion of EU CRR articles into the PRA Rulebook with UK-specific adjustments), the implementation of Basel 3.1, and the IRB-related supervisory statements. SS3/24 (Credit Risk: Definition of Default) and SS4/24 (Credit Risk: Internal Ratings Based Approach), both published in September 2024, are the substantive PRA expressions of the EBA's earlier definition-of-default and IRB guidelines, adapted for UK conditions and now lodged inside the PRA's own framework rather than referring out to EU material.

Other strategically important recent publications include SS1/23 on Model Risk Management Principles for Banks (effective May 2024, the PRA's first standalone statement on model risk that goes beyond the credit risk perimeter), SS1/21 and the joint Bank-FCA-PRA policy on operational resilience (effective March 2022, fully embedded by 2025), SS2/21 on outsourcing and third-party risk management, and the ongoing implementation of the Solvent Exit Planning rules for non-systemic banks (effective from October 2025). PS12/25 finalised the near-final CRR position on credit risk and is the basis for the Basel 3.1 capital regime in the UK from 1 January 2027.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The PRA is prescriptive by design

Compared with its peer supervisors, the PRA is at the prescriptive end of the spectrum. SS-by-SS, expectation-by-expectation, the PRA builds up a documentary apparatus that goes further than the Federal Reserve's guidance and matches the SSM in many areas. The reason is partly historical (the PRA inherited a tradition of detailed handbook drafting from the FSA) and partly cultural (UK supervisors prize a written expectation that can be referenced in a model file). The practical consequence is that compliance with PRA expectations is documentation-heavy: the firm must show that it has read the Supervisory Statement, applied it, and recorded how it has done so.

Supervisory Statements are softer than rules but harder than guidance

An SS is not a rule. A firm that departs from an SS has not breached the PRA Rulebook. But the PRA's standard supervisory approach is to ask the firm to justify the departure in writing and to escalate if the justification is inadequate. In practice the cost of departing from an SS approaches the cost of departing from a rule, and the regulatory expectation is therefore comply-by-default. The exam point is to distinguish the legal status (non-binding) from the practical status (treated as binding).

Watch out Saying that a firm "complies with the PRA Rulebook" is a more precise statement than saying it "complies with PRA expectations". The former covers binding rules; the latter covers Supervisory Statements as well. In a regulatory document, never use the second phrase where the first will do, and never use the first phrase to cover the second. Supervisors will read the difference.

Read the Statement of Policy alongside the rule

The PRA Rulebook contains the binding text, but the policy rationale and the supervisory methodology usually sit in an accompanying Statement of Policy. The Pillar 2 framework is the canonical example: the rule says firms must hold capital against material risks not captured in Pillar 1, but the methodology the PRA uses to assess those risks is in a Statement of Policy that runs to over a hundred pages. Reading the rule alone misses how the PRA actually applies it.

Categorisation is destiny

Your firm's category determines almost everything about your relationship with the PRA: the frequency of contact, the depth of the SREP, the seniority of supervisors, the speed of model approvals. A move from Category 3 to Category 2, which can be triggered by growth in retail deposits or by an acquisition, can double or triple the amount of supervisory engagement the firm experiences. Strategic planners should always ask the category question first.

The post-Brexit equilibrium is still settling

From the 1 January 2021 onshoring date until the near-final CRR restatement, the UK operated under a "Retained EU Law" version of the CRR that was substantively identical to the EU version. The PRA has used the restatement to make UK-specific divergences: a different timeline for Basel 3.1, narrower output-floor interactions, and small adjustments to the IRB framework. The direction of travel is clear but the equilibrium is not yet stable; expect further divergence in the second half of the 2020s, especially in response to the secondary international competitiveness objective. Tracking what the PRA does and what the EBA does in parallel is now part of any UK credit risk modeller's job.

The PRC is not the PRA in the colloquial sense

When a Supervisory Statement is published "by the PRA", it is in fact issued by the Bank of England acting through the PRC. The day-to-day supervision is done by PRA staff inside the Bank, but the decisions that bind the firm are PRC decisions. This matters in two contexts: when judicial review is contemplated (the decision-maker is the Bank), and when reading meeting minutes (the PRC minutes, published with a lag, are often the clearest indication of the supervisor's strategic direction).
