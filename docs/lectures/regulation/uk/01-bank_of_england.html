Bank of England: monetary and financial stability

The Bank of England is the United Kingdom's central bank, operationally independent since 1997, and the seat of three statutory committees that together define UK macroeconomic and prudential policy: the Monetary Policy Committee sets Bank Rate, the Financial Policy Committee runs the macroprudential toolkit, and the Prudential Regulation Committee, sitting at the head of the PRA, supervises every UK bank, building society, designated investment firm, and insurer of consequence.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The Bank of England (BoE) is the UK's central bank. It was founded in 1694 by Royal Charter as a private bank to fund William III's war with France, nationalised in 1946, and granted operational independence for monetary policy in 1997. After the 2008 crisis the post-1997 architecture was reversed: the Financial Services Act 2012 created the Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA) as a BoE subsidiary and the Financial Policy Committee (FPC) as the UK's macroprudential authority, and the Bank of England and Financial Services Act 2016 merged the PRA fully into the Bank. The Bank today runs three statutory committees: the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), which sets Bank Rate to hit the 2 per cent CPI inflation target; the FPC, which sets the UK countercyclical capital buffer and other macroprudential tools; and the Prudential Regulation Committee (PRC), which sits at the head of the PRA and supervises around 1,500 firms.

Beyond these three policy mandates, the Bank is the UK's resolution authority under the Banking Act 2009, runs the annual concurrent stress test of the largest UK banks, operates the sterling money markets through open-market operations and standing facilities, issues English banknotes, and remains the lender of last resort. The Bank's address, Threadneedle Street in the City of London, has been the operational centre of UK finance since 1734.

Feynman test The Bank of England is one building in the City of London that does four jobs at once. Job one, it decides what interest rate borrowers in the UK pay, by setting the rate at which it lends to commercial banks overnight. Job two, it decides how much capital UK banks have to hold against a downturn, by turning a single dial called the countercyclical buffer up or down. Job three, it reads the books of every UK bank to make sure they are not lying, through a subsidiary called the PRA. Job four, when a bank fails, it is the Bank of England that decides who takes the loss and who keeps their deposits. Four jobs, one building, one Governor who chairs each of the four committees. The only thing the Bank does not do is conduct duty: that lives at the FCA, down the road in Stratford.

Step 2

Why this matters

The Bank of England is the parent institution to almost every other UK financial regulator you will encounter in this course. The PRA is a committee of the Bank. The FPC is a committee of the Bank. The UK's resolution authority is the Bank. The UK's monetary authority is the Bank. Understanding the BoE's internal architecture is the prerequisite to understanding any other piece of UK bank regulation, because every other regulator is either inside the Bank or interacts with it daily. The one substantive exception is the Financial Conduct Authority, which sits outside the Bank and handles market conduct rather than prudential safety; that is the subject of lecture 19.

The post-2008 reorganisation also matters as a story about regulatory design. Between 1997 and 2013, UK bank supervision was outsourced to the Financial Services Authority (FSA), a single regulator covering banks, insurers, brokers, and conduct, sitting outside the Bank. The arrangement worked until it didn't: Northern Rock failed in 2007 with the Bank, the FSA, and HM Treasury blaming one another for the supervisory gap. The 2012 Act dismantled the FSA, returned prudential bank supervision to the Bank through the PRA, and created the FPC to address the macroprudential gap that nobody had owned in 2007. The current arrangement, often called "twin peaks", reflects a deliberate decision that prudential supervision and macroprudential policy belong inside the central bank, but conduct regulation does not.

For an actuary working in a UK bank, three institutional facts have direct day-to-day consequences. First, the rules in the PRA Rulebook ultimately answer to the PRC, which is chaired by the Governor of the Bank: there is no daylight between PRA policy and BoE policy. Second, the Bank's annual concurrent stress test sets the size of the firm-specific capital buffers under Pillar 2B for the largest banks; the stress scenario is the Bank's, not the firm's. Third, the Bank is the firm's resolution authority, which means it owns the firm's resolution plan, sets its MREL requirement, and would lead any bail-in. The Bank is therefore both supervisor and undertaker.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture the Bank as one institution that operates three policy committees, each with a different mandate and a different set of tools, but all chaired or co-chaired by the Governor. Above them sits the Court of Directors, the Bank's governance body. Beside them sit HM Treasury (the political principal) and the FCA (the conduct counterpart outside the Bank). Below them sit the supervised firms, the resolution counterparties, and the gilt and money-market participants.

Three flows of authority matter. Policy flows down from the three committees to firms and markets, through the PRA Rulebook, FPC directions, and MPC operational decisions. Information flows up from supervised firms through PRA returns, from the stress test exercise, and from the Bank's market intelligence to inform each committee's decisions. And accountability flows outward from the Bank to Parliament through the Treasury Committee, to HM Treasury through the annual remit letter for the MPC and the recommendations and remits for the FPC and PRC, and to the public through the published minutes of every committee meeting.

Step 4

The detail

History: from war finance to central bank to triple-mandate institution

The Bank of England was founded on 27 July 1694 by Royal Charter granted to a group of subscribers led by William Paterson, a Scottish merchant. Its purpose was to lend 1.2 million pounds to the government of William III to fund the Nine Years' War against France. The subscribers were granted, in exchange, the right to issue banknotes and to take deposits as a joint-stock company. For its first 250 years the Bank was a privately owned commercial bank with a special relationship with the Crown: it managed the national debt, acted as the government's banker, and, from the 1844 Bank Charter Act onwards, held a monopoly on banknote issuance in England and Wales.

The Bank moved to its current Threadneedle Street site in 1734. The current building, designed by Sir Herbert Baker and completed in 1939, sits inside the perimeter of the eighteenth-century Soane building whose external walls were preserved. The cellars hold one of the world's largest gold reserves, including the bulk of the UK's official reserves and significant custodial holdings for other central banks.

The legislative chain that produced the modern Bank runs through six statutes:

Bank Charter Act 1844
Separated the Bank's Issue Department (banknotes) from its Banking Department (commercial activities). Gave the Bank a monopoly on English banknote issue. The first legislative recognition of the Bank's central-bank role.
Bank of England Act 1946
Nationalised the Bank. Its stock was transferred from private shareholders to HM Treasury, which has been the sole owner ever since. The Governor and Court became Crown appointments. The Bank's policy was, in practice, directed by the Treasury.
Bank of England Act 1998
Codified the operational independence for monetary policy that Chancellor Gordon Brown had announced in May 1997. Created the statutory MPC. Set the inflation target framework: Treasury sets the target by annual remit letter, the MPC chooses Bank Rate to achieve it. Transferred banking supervision out of the Bank to the new FSA.
Banking Act 2009
Created the Special Resolution Regime (SRR) for failing banks. Established the Bank of England as the UK resolution authority. Introduced the resolution toolkit: sale of business, bridge bank, asset management vehicle, and (added in 2014 to implement the EU BRRD) the bail-in tool. The post-Northern Rock fix.
Financial Services Act 2012
Abolished the FSA. Created the PRA as a subsidiary of the Bank, the FCA as a separate conduct regulator, and the FPC as a statutory committee inside the Bank. The "twin peaks" reform: prudential supervision and macroprudential policy returned to the Bank; conduct went elsewhere. Took effect 1 April 2013.
Bank of England and Financial Services Act 2016
Merged the PRA fully into the Bank, replacing the PRA Board with the PRC as a statutory committee directly inside the Bank. Removed the separate-subsidiary structure. Aligned PRA accountability with the Bank's own. Took effect 1 March 2017.

Governance: the Governor, the Deputies, and the Court

The Bank is led by a Governor, appointed by the monarch on the advice of the Prime Minister for an eight-year term. The Governor chairs all three statutory committees and the Court. Four Deputy Governors, also Crown-appointed, hold portfolios:

Deputy Governor, Monetary Policy
Leads the Bank's economic analysis and the operational side of monetary policy. Sits on the MPC and the FPC. Owns the Monetary Policy Report (quarterly).
Deputy Governor, Financial Stability
Leads the FPC's analytical work and the macroprudential framework. Owns the Financial Stability Report (twice-yearly). Sits on the MPC, FPC, and PRC, providing the cross-committee bridge.
Deputy Governor, Markets and Banking
Runs the Bank's market operations: the Sterling Monetary Framework, the Asset Purchase Facility, foreign-exchange operations, and the Bank's balance-sheet management. Sits on the MPC and FPC.
Deputy Governor, Prudential Regulation
Chief Executive of the PRA. Runs day-to-day prudential supervision. Sits on the PRC and FPC. Reports through the PRC to the Court for governance and to the Treasury for political accountability.

The Court of Directors is the Bank's governing body: it does for the Bank what a board does for a company. The Court comprises the Governor, the four Deputies, and up to nine non-executive directors appointed by the Chancellor. Its job is governance, not policy: it sets the Bank's strategy and budget, reviews internal controls, and approves the annual report. It does not direct the policy committees, which are statutorily independent. A separate Oversight Committee of the Court's non-executives reviews the policy committees' procedures and conducts retrospective reviews of major decisions.

The Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)

The MPC has nine members: the Governor (who chairs), the three Deputy Governors for Monetary Policy, Financial Stability, and Markets and Banking, the Bank's Chief Economist, and four external members appointed by the Chancellor for three-year terms (renewable once). Externals are typically academic economists, former policymakers, or City practitioners.

The MPC's statutory objective, set by the Bank of England Act 1998 and operationalised by the Chancellor's annual remit letter, is to maintain price stability, and subject to that, to support the government's economic policy including its objectives for growth and employment. Price stability is defined as 2 per cent annual CPI inflation. If inflation deviates by more than 1 percentage point from the target, the Governor must write an open letter to the Chancellor explaining why, what the Bank is doing about it, and how long the deviation is expected to last.

The MPC meets eight times per year on a published schedule. Each meeting comprises two policy days plus pre-meeting briefing sessions. Decisions are by majority vote on the level of Bank Rate, the size of the Asset Purchase Facility, and any operational decisions on the gilt purchase programme. The Committee publishes the minutes of every meeting (with the individual vote of each member) immediately after the decision. A Monetary Policy Report is published quarterly, setting out the Committee's view of the economic outlook with fan charts for inflation and GDP.

Bank Rate
The interest rate the Bank pays on commercial banks' reserve accounts and charges on the Operational Standing Facility. The MPC's primary policy instrument. Transmits to interbank rates (SONIA), then to lending and deposit rates across the economy.
Asset Purchase Facility (APF)
The vehicle through which the Bank conducts quantitative easing. Created 2009. Purchases gilts (and, in earlier programmes, corporate bonds) financed by issuing reserves. Held 875 billion pounds of gilts at peak in 2021. Now in run-off (active gilt sales and maturity-driven decline) since 2022.

The Financial Policy Committee (FPC)

The FPC has 13 members: the Governor (chair), the four Deputies, the Chief Executive of the FCA, two HM Treasury-nominated externals (currently five external members under the post-2013 expansion), and a non-voting Treasury representative. Created by the Financial Services Act 2012, it is the UK's macroprudential authority. Its statutory objective is to identify, monitor, and take action to remove or reduce systemic risks to the UK financial system.

The FPC has two categories of tool. Powers of direction allow it to require the PRA and the FCA to implement specified macroprudential measures: these are legally binding on the prudential and conduct regulators. Powers of recommendation allow it to make non-binding recommendations to the PRA, the FCA, HM Treasury, and other parts of government, with a "comply or explain" expectation. Specific tools include:

UK countercyclical capital buffer (CCyB)
An additional CET1 capital requirement on UK exposures, set by the FPC quarterly. Range 0 to 2.5 per cent (or higher in exceptional cases). The headline macroprudential tool. Currently set at 2 per cent (set January 2023, took effect July 2023). The pre-pandemic neutral rate was set at 1 per cent.
Sectoral capital requirements
Power to require higher risk weights or higher capital requirements against specific sectors (e.g. commercial real estate, high-LTV mortgages). Used sparingly.
Loan-to-income (LTI) flow limit on mortgages
FPC direction in force since 2014: no more than 15 per cent of a lender's new residential mortgage flow may be at LTI of 4.5 or above. Reviewed annually. The headline borrower-based macroprudential measure in the UK.
Affordability test for mortgages
Lenders must stress mortgage affordability against a rate 3 percentage points above the lender's standard variable rate (SVR). Withdrawn in 2022 after FPC review concluded the LTI flow limit was the primary tool.
Annual concurrent stress test
FPC and PRC jointly set the scenario. PRA-supervised major UK banks run their balance sheets through it. Results published. Determines the Pillar 2B capital add-on for participating banks.

The FPC meets formally four times per year and publishes a Financial Stability Report twice a year (June and December, on a rolling basis with two FSRs straddling the four policy meetings). The FSR is the macroprudential analogue of the Monetary Policy Report: a comprehensive review of UK and global financial-stability risks, the FPC's view of those risks, and an explanation of policy decisions.

The Prudential Regulation Committee (PRC)

The PRC has 14 members: the Governor, the Deputy Governors for Prudential Regulation (Chief Executive of the PRA) and Financial Stability, the Chief Executive of the FCA, and external members appointed by the Chancellor. Created by the Bank of England and Financial Services Act 2016, replacing the previous PRA Board. The PRC sits at the head of the PRA and makes all major supervisory decisions: it approves the PRA Rulebook, sets supervisory strategy, and decides individual firm matters that fall above the threshold of delegation to PRA executives.

The PRA itself, the operational supervisor, is the subject of the next lecture in this series. For the BoE perspective, the relevant facts are: the PRA is not legally separate from the Bank since 2016, the PRC chairs it, and its statutory objectives (safety and soundness of supervised firms, plus insurance policyholder protection) flow from the Bank's overall mandate.

Resolution authority under the Banking Act 2009

The Banking Act 2009 made the Bank of England the UK's resolution authority. The Bank holds a dedicated Resolution Directorate, separate from the PRA but inside the Bank, with the staff and powers to take a failing bank into resolution. The toolkit, expanded by the Banking Act 2009 (Restriction of Special Bail-In Provision, etc.) Order 2014 to implement the EU Bank Recovery and Resolution Directive (BRRD), comprises four stabilisation options:

Sale of business
Transfer of all or part of the failing bank to a private-sector purchaser. Used for Bradford and Bingley (2008) and parts of Northern Rock.
Bridge bank
Transfer of critical functions to a temporary bank wholly owned by the Bank of England, pending sale to a private purchaser. A holding pattern when no immediate buyer is available.
Asset management vehicle
Transfer of bad assets to a separate vehicle for run-off. The Northern Rock split into Northern Rock plc (good bank, sold) and Northern Rock (Asset Management) plc (bad bank, run-off) was the prototype.
Bail-in
Write-down and conversion of unsecured creditor claims to absorb losses and recapitalise the failing bank in place. The post-2014 preferred tool for systemic banks. Requires the failing bank to hold a stock of bail-inable liabilities, measured by MREL (the subject of lecture 21).

The Bank chooses the resolution tool in consultation with HM Treasury (which retains a veto where public funds would be at risk) and the PRA. A bank-insolvency procedure (for smaller, non-systemic failures) and a bank-administration procedure (for residual entities after partial transfer) sit alongside the stabilisation tools.

The annual concurrent stress test

Since 2014 the Bank has run an annual stress test of the largest UK banks. The 2024 exercise covered the seven largest groups: Barclays, HSBC, Lloyds, NatWest, Santander UK, Standard Chartered, and Nationwide Building Society. The scenario is set by the FPC and PRC jointly and is published in the spring; banks run their balance sheets through it over the summer; results are published in December.

The stress test feeds directly into the Pillar 2B capital framework. Banks are required to hold capital sufficient to absorb the modelled losses in the stress scenario while remaining above their hurdle rates (CET1 4.5 per cent plus G-SIB or other systemic buffers). The difference between the projected stressed CET1 and the hurdle rate, taken as a percentage of risk-weighted assets, is the firm-specific PRA buffer. The test is therefore the binding determinant of capital for the largest UK banks: the Pillar 1 and Pillar 2A capital requirements are usually exceeded by the stress-test-derived buffer.

Balance-sheet operations: the Sterling Monetary Framework

The Bank conducts monetary policy through the Sterling Monetary Framework (SMF), a published set of facilities through which it lends to and borrows from eligible counterparties. The framework has been progressively rebuilt since the 2008 crisis to deliver Bank Rate to the money market under a wide range of balance-sheet conditions.

Open market operations (OMOs)
Regular short-term repo transactions that supply reserves to the banking system at Bank Rate against eligible collateral. The day-to-day instrument of monetary policy.
Operational Standing Facility (OSF)
Bilateral standing facilities allowing counterparties to deposit reserves with the Bank or borrow against eligible collateral at Bank Rate plus or minus a spread. The corridor system: caps and floors on the money-market rate. Floor is the deposit rate, ceiling is the lending rate.
Indexed Long-Term Repo (ILTR)
Monthly auctions of six-month repos against a wider collateral set. Designed to supply term liquidity under varying degrees of stress. Pricing varies with the demand for liquidity and the quality of collateral pledged.
Contingent Term Repo Facility (CTRF)
Stand-by facility activated by the Bank in periods of stress to supply additional term liquidity against a broad collateral set. Most recently activated during the 2020 COVID-19 dislocation and the September 2022 gilt market crisis.
Asset Purchase Facility
The vehicle through which the Bank purchases gilts (and historically corporate bonds) for quantitative easing. Indemnified by HM Treasury. Peak gilt holdings 875 billion pounds in 2021. Now in active run-off via sales and non-replacement of maturities.

Outside the SMF, the Bank runs the CHAPS sterling real-time gross settlement system and the underlying RTGS service, the plumbing through which all sterling wholesale payments flow. A new generation of the RTGS system, called RT2, has been under development since 2017 and entered service in 2024.

Banknote issuance

The Bank holds a statutory monopoly on the issuance of banknotes in England and Wales, granted under the Bank Charter Act 1844 and consolidated under the Currency and Bank Notes Act 1954. Scottish (Bank of Scotland, Royal Bank of Scotland, Clydesdale Bank) and Northern Irish (Bank of Ireland, Danske Bank, Ulster Bank, AIB Group UK) commercial banks may issue their own banknotes under a separate framework, but must hold matching reserves at the Bank as cover. The Bank supervises these issuers under the Banking Act 2009.

The PRA Rulebook

The substantive bank-supervision rulebook produced by the PRC and applicable to authorised banks, building societies, designated investment firms, and credit unions is the PRA Rulebook. It is the legally binding UK implementation of Basel III (and the UK's "Basel 3.1" reforms), drawing on (and from 1 January 2025 onwards superseding) the EU CRR rulebook that the UK inherited at the end of the Brexit transition. The PRA Rulebook is the day-to-day reference for any UK bank's capital, liquidity, large-exposures, governance, and reporting requirements. It is the subject of lecture 20.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

One roof, one Governor, one accountability chain

The Bank of England is unique among major central banks in housing monetary policy, macroprudential policy, prudential supervision, and resolution authority under one roof. The ECB has the Single Supervisory Mechanism but it operates as a distinct function, and the US Federal Reserve shares prudential supervision with the OCC and FDIC. In the UK, the Governor sits at the head of every relevant committee, and the same building owns every prudential lever that matters. The benefit is coordination: monetary policy and macroprudential policy talk to each other every week, supervisors and resolvers share desks. The cost is concentration of policy authority in one institution, and a need for credible internal "Chinese walls" between functions whose objectives can pull in opposite directions.

The twin peaks model and the FCA's place outside the Bank

The 2012 reform deliberately put prudential regulation inside the Bank and conduct regulation outside it. The reasoning is that conduct supervision (mis-selling, market abuse, consumer protection) is a different discipline from prudential supervision (solvency, liquidity, model validation), and that combining them in a single regulator (as the FSA did between 1997 and 2013) produced a body too broad to do either well. The FCA therefore sits outside the Bank, although the Governor sits on the FCA's Board and the FCA's Chief Executive sits on the FPC and the PRC. The arrangement looks like separation on paper and like close cooperation in practice.

Watch out The Bank's three statutory committees have statutorily independent mandates, but the Governor chairs all three and the senior staff cycle between them. Do not take "independent" to mean "isolated". The MPC and FPC will lean on one another when the cycle is unusually severe (e.g. the FPC asking the MPC to factor financial-stability risks into its rate path, or the MPC asking the FPC about the macroprudential consequences of a tight monetary stance). The legal independence is real, but the institutional coordination is intense.

The Bank is supervisor and undertaker

For any large UK bank, the Bank of England is both the institution that writes its capital and liquidity rules through the PRA, and the institution that will dismantle it if it fails through the Resolution Directorate. The dual role is by design: the resolver wants the supervisor to set high capital requirements ex ante so that resolution is less likely; the supervisor wants the resolver to credibly threaten resolution ex ante so that a failing bank does not assume a bail-out. Practically, this means the resolution planning conversation and the supervisory conversation are coordinated, and a bank cannot play one off against the other.

Threadneedle Street is the political centre of UK finance

The geography matters. The Bank's policy committees meet in a building that has been on the same site since 1734, with a working relationship with the Treasury (across St James's Park) and with the City (immediately surrounding it) that is measured in centuries. When a UK financial crisis unfolds, the operational response runs through Threadneedle Street: the 2007 Northern Rock run, the 2008 RBS and HBOS rescues, the 2020 COVID liquidity provision, the September 2022 LDI gilt market intervention. Knowing where in that building each decision is taken (the Court Room, the MPC room, the Markets directorate trading floor) tells you who owns which lever.

The Bank does not do conduct, payments, or competition

For all the breadth of the Bank's prudential and monetary mandate, three things sit outside it. Conduct is at the FCA. Payment system regulation (Faster Payments, Bacs, LINK as schemes, not the underlying CHAPS RTGS) is at the Payment Systems Regulator (PSR), a subsidiary of the FCA. Competition in financial services is shared between the FCA and the Competition and Markets Authority (CMA). When a question lands on a regulator's desk, the first triage question is which of the four jurisdictions it falls into: prudential (Bank), conduct (FCA), payments (PSR), or competition (CMA).

Read the FSR and the Monetary Policy Report, not the press releases

The Bank's substantive thinking is in its two flagship publications, the Monetary Policy Report (MPC, quarterly) and the Financial Stability Report (FPC, twice-yearly). Both are roughly 60 to 80 pages, written in accessible English, and contain the analytical basis for the Committee's decisions. The press releases and minutes are the short summaries. The reports are the source. For an actuary or risk practitioner, the FSR is the single most important document published in the UK each half-year: it is the Bank's view of where systemic risk sits and what the FPC is going to do about it.

Test yourself

  1. The Bank of England has three statutory committees. Name each, give its membership count, and state its principal policy tool.
  2. What did the Bank of England Act 1998 do, and what did the Bank of England and Financial Services Act 2016 do? Why are the two changes sometimes described as opposite movements in policy authority?
  3. Explain the "twin peaks" model. Where do prudential supervision and conduct regulation sit, and why was this split chosen in 2012?
  4. The UK countercyclical capital buffer is set by the FPC. What is its statutory range, what does it apply to, and where does the FPC currently stand on it?
  5. List the four resolution stabilisation tools under the Banking Act 2009. Which was the preferred tool for the Northern Rock failure in 2007-2008, and which is the preferred tool for a systemic G-SIB today?
  6. What is the Sterling Monetary Framework? Distinguish between the Operational Standing Facility, the Indexed Long-Term Repo, and the Contingent Term Repo Facility.
  7. Why does the Bank publish a Financial Stability Report separately from the Monetary Policy Report, and how do the two relate?
  8. The MPC's inflation target is 2 per cent CPI. Who sets the target, what happens if inflation deviates by more than 1 percentage point, and what is the Governor's obligation in that case?
  9. Explain how the annual concurrent stress test feeds into the Pillar 2B capital buffer for the largest UK banks.
  10. An industry colleague tells you a UK bank "is supervised by the Bank of England". Is this strictly correct, or should they say something different? What is the precise statutory position?
