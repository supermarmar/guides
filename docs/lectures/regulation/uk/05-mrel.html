MREL: minimum requirement for own funds and eligible liabilities

The UK's answer to "no more taxpayer bailouts". MREL forces every bank above a size threshold to hold a stack of equity and bail-inable debt large enough that, if the firm fails, the Bank of England as resolution authority can write down the liability stack, recapitalise the going concern, and keep the lights on for depositors and counterparties, without writing a single Treasury cheque. It is set by the Bank of England under the Banking Act 2009, derived in design from the EU's BRRD, and calibrated to align with the FSB's TLAC standard for G-SIBs while extending the principle to a much wider population of UK firms.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The Minimum Requirement for own funds and Eligible Liabilities (MREL) is a UK regulatory requirement, set by the Bank of England in its capacity as resolution authority, that forces banks and building societies above a size threshold to maintain a stack of bail-inable financial instruments sufficient to absorb losses and recapitalise the firm in resolution without recourse to public funds. The instruments that count are own funds (CET1, AT1, Tier 2) and eligible liabilities (typically senior debt issued by a holding company, or, where the group has no HoldCo, senior non-preferred debt issued by the operating bank). MREL is the UK descendant of the EU's Bank Recovery and Resolution Directive (BRRD) requirement, onshored after Brexit, and is the UK's implementation route for the FSB's Total Loss-Absorbing Capacity (TLAC) standard for global systemically important banks.

The headline number, for a bank whose preferred resolution strategy is bail-in, is roughly twice the firm's risk-based capital requirement. One copy of the capital stack absorbs the losses that pushed the firm into resolution; a second copy recapitalises the surviving entity to a level that allows it to continue operating and meet the going-concern capital requirements again. On top of that sits the firm's combined buffer requirement (capital conservation buffer, countercyclical buffer, and, where relevant, the G-SII or systemic risk buffer). If the firm fails to meet MREL plus buffers, the same Maximum Distributable Amount (MDA) restrictions that apply to capital breaches kick in: dividends, AT1 coupons, and discretionary bonuses are constrained. For smaller firms whose preferred resolution strategy is sale of the business or modified insolvency funded by the Financial Services Compensation Scheme, MREL equals the capital requirement only. There is no recapitalisation component because there is no plan to recapitalise.

Feynman test Think of a fire-prone wooden building. The Basel capital ratios say: hold a thick layer of fireproof bricks at the base so an ordinary fire does not bring the building down. The MREL ratios say: in addition, keep a second pre-built brick layer in the basement, ready to be hoisted into place if the first one cracks. The first layer is "going concern" capital, used to absorb losses while the firm trades through trouble. The second layer is "gone concern" capital, used to rebuild the bank to going-concern standards the morning after the resolution weekend. Without the second layer, the only options are taxpayer money or letting the building collapse. With it, the Bank of England can convert subordinated and senior debt into equity overnight, hand the recapitalised firm back to its new owners (the bailed-in creditors), and reopen for business on Monday morning.

Step 2

Why this matters

The political mandate behind MREL is the post-2008 promise that no future bank failure would cost the taxpayer. In October 2008 the UK government injected GBP 37 billion of capital into RBS, Lloyds, and HBOS; the fiscal commitment, including guarantees and liquidity facilities, peaked at over GBP 1 trillion. The cost to the public purse was politically intolerable, but the alternative, letting a clearing bank collapse, would have brought down the payment system. The G20 set the global policy direction at the 2009 Pittsburgh summit: large banks must be resolvable without taxpayer support. The FSB translated this into the TLAC standard for G-SIBs in 2015. The EU broadened the principle to all banks through the BRRD in 2014. The UK, then a Member State, implemented BRRD through the Banking Act 2009 (as amended by the Banking Reform Act 2013) and the BoE's Statement of Policy on MREL. After Brexit, the BRRD provisions were onshored and the BoE retained discretion to recalibrate.

For a UK bank, MREL has three direct consequences. First, it dictates the size of the bail-inable liability stack the firm must carry. For a typical UK ring-fenced clearer, MREL is around 20 to 25 per cent of risk-weighted assets, comfortably above the Basel III minimum capital requirement of 8 per cent plus buffers. Second, it shapes the firm's legal structure: most UK groups now operate a holding company above the regulated bank specifically so that HoldCo senior debt can be issued as MREL-eligible without disturbing the operating company's creditors. Third, it raises funding cost: senior debt issued by a HoldCo is structurally subordinated to operating company creditors and prices at a spread above OpCo senior, typically 30 to 60 basis points wider. A bank with GBP 20 billion of MREL-eligible HoldCo senior debt outstanding is paying tens of millions a year of additional spread to maintain its resolution stack.

For an actuary working inside the bank, MREL changes capital-planning arithmetic in three places. Capital plans now project a parallel MREL stack alongside the CET1, T1, and total capital stacks, with separate transition paths and buffer requirements. The internal capital adequacy assessment process (ICAAP) and the recovery and resolution planning (RRP) work converge: the same Pillar 2 capital add-ons that feed the capital requirement feed the MREL recapitalisation amount. Stress testing must check that MREL is met under stress, not just capital. And funds transfer pricing (FTP) must price the cost of HoldCo issuance back to the businesses that consume the recapitalisation capacity, otherwise a business unit chooses its risk-weighted assets without seeing the full marginal funding cost.

Step 3

The mental model

Two pictures together do most of the work. The first is the creditor hierarchy: the order in which different categories of liability absorb losses in a bail-in. The second is the MREL stack itself: how the various layers of capital and eligible debt build up to the binding MREL ratio for a UK bail-in firm.

Two flows of authority matter. The Bank of England sets MREL bank by bank, after consultation with the PRA, based on a preferred resolution strategy decided in advance and reviewed regularly. The firm builds and maintains the eligible stack, reports compliance to the BoE, and works with the BoE's Resolution Directorate on annual resolvability assessments. If the firm fails, the BoE triggers the resolution tools provided by the Banking Act, of which bail-in is the principal instrument. The Treasury approves any use of public funds; in a proper MREL-funded bail-in, none are used.

Step 4

The detail

Statutory and policy architecture

The legal foundation for MREL in the UK is the Banking Act 2009, which created the Special Resolution Regime (SRR) and gave the Bank of England a suite of resolution tools (transfer of business, bridge bank, bail-in, asset management vehicle, temporary public ownership) for failing banks. The Act was substantially amended by the Banking Reform Act 2013 to add the bail-in tool, and by secondary legislation implementing the EU Bank Recovery and Resolution Directive (BRRD), Directive 2014/59/EU, which introduced the concept of MREL. After the UK left the EU, the BRRD provisions were retained as onshored law and have since been amended by the BoE's evolving Statement of Policy.

The policy detail sits in the BoE's Statement of Policy on the Bank of England's approach to setting a minimum requirement for own funds and eligible liabilities (MREL), first published in 2016, substantially revised in 2018 to align with the FSB TLAC standard and the EU BRRD II, further revised in 2021 to refine transitional arrangements for medium-sized firms, and updated again in 2025 to simplify the framework for non-systemic firms. The BoE issues MREL requirements to individual firms by way of a published list of resolution entities and their indicative MREL levels, refreshed annually.

Banking Act 2009
UK statutory basis for bank resolution. Establishes the Special Resolution Regime, the resolution objectives, and the resolution tools. The Bank of England is designated resolution authority for banks and building societies; the PRA for credit unions and small investment firms; the FCA for non-PRA-regulated firms.
BRRD (2014, onshored)
EU Directive 2014/59/EU introducing harmonised resolution frameworks across the EU, including MREL. The UK implemented BRRD during its EU membership and retained the substance after Brexit, with the BoE assuming the discretion that the EU Single Resolution Board exercises in the euro area.
FSB TLAC standard (2015)
FSB Principles on Loss-absorbing and Recapitalisation Capacity of G-SIBs in Resolution. Sets a floor of 18 per cent of RWA and 6.75 per cent of leverage exposure for external TLAC at G-SIB resolution entities. UK G-SIBs (HSBC, Barclays, Standard Chartered) meet TLAC through MREL; the BoE confirms that the UK's MREL framework is the route to TLAC compliance.
BoE Statement of Policy on MREL
The operational document. Sets out how the BoE calibrates MREL for each firm, the resolution strategies, the eligibility criteria, the disclosure regime, and the transitional path for firms growing into MREL. Revised in 2018, 2021, and 2025.

Resolution strategies drive MREL calibration

Before setting MREL, the BoE assigns each firm a preferred resolution strategy, reviewed at least every two years and after any material change in the firm's structure or business. The strategy answers the question: if this firm fails on a Friday evening, what does the BoE do over the weekend? Three broad strategies exist, and each implies a different MREL calibration.

Bail-in
For large, complex, systemic firms. The firm enters resolution as a going concern; the BoE writes down equity and converts subordinated and senior debt into new equity; the recapitalised firm continues operating under new ownership. MREL = loss absorption (capital requirement) + recapitalisation (second capital requirement) = approximately 2 × (P1 + P2A). All UK G-SIBs and ring-fenced clearers fall here.
Partial transfer
For medium-sized firms with critical functions, but where bail-in is not practicable. The BoE transfers the critical parts of the business (typically retail and SME deposits and the related franchise) to a private-sector purchaser or a temporary bridge bank, and places the residual into modified insolvency. MREL covers losses plus the recapitalisation cost of the transferred business. The 2023 transfer of SVB UK to HSBC was a real-world instance.
Modified insolvency, including Bank Insolvency Procedure (BIP)
For smaller firms whose failure can be handled by paying out covered depositors through the Financial Services Compensation Scheme and winding the rest down through a special insolvency procedure. There is no recapitalisation because the firm is not continuing. MREL = capital requirement only (the loss-absorption component).

The BoE publishes an indicative threshold for each strategy. Firms with total assets above GBP 15 to 25 billion, or with significant transactional or current-account business, will normally be assigned a bail-in or partial-transfer strategy. Firms below this size band typically follow modified insolvency, and their MREL therefore equals their capital requirement, sparing them the cost of recapitalisation issuance.

The two components: loss absorption and recapitalisation

For a bail-in firm, MREL is built from two components, each calibrated as a function of the firm's risk-based capital requirement. The formula, in the BoE's preferred phrasing, is:

External MREL = Loss-absorption amount (LAA) + Recapitalisation amount (RCA)
LAA = Pillar 1 + Pillar 2A (the firm's going-concern capital requirement, excluding buffers)
RCA = Pillar 1 + Pillar 2A (a second copy of the same, sized to recapitalise the surviving firm)
Hence: External MREL approximately = 2 × (P1 + P2A) of RWA

This 2 × formula is the workhorse calibration. For a typical UK clearing bank with 8 per cent Pillar 1 and 2 to 4 per cent Pillar 2A, external MREL lands around 20 to 24 per cent of RWA. The combined buffer requirement (capital conservation buffer of 2.5 per cent, plus any countercyclical buffer and any G-SII or systemic risk buffer) sits on top. The BoE may also impose a leverage-based MREL floor for G-SIBs to ensure alignment with the TLAC leverage requirement of 6.75 per cent.

Watch out The recapitalisation amount can be adjusted by the BoE to reflect post-resolution conditions. If the BoE judges that the surviving entity will be smaller (because some assets were transferred or wound down during resolution), the RCA can be set below Pillar 1 + Pillar 2A. Equally, if the BoE expects market confidence to require an additional capital cushion in the surviving entity, the RCA can include a "market confidence charge" equal to the combined buffer requirement less the countercyclical buffer. Reading the BoE's annual indicative MREL table without understanding these adjustments will give the wrong number.

Eligibility criteria for MREL instruments

Not every liability counts towards MREL. The instrument must satisfy a battery of tests designed to ensure that it can be written down or converted into equity at the resolution weekend without legal challenge and without disrupting the firm's continuing operations. The eligibility criteria are drawn from BRRD Article 72b (as onshored) and elaborated in the BoE Statement of Policy.

  1. Issued and paid up. The instrument must be in issue and fully paid; commitments to issue do not count.
  2. Not owed to, secured or guaranteed by, the firm or its subsidiaries. Self-funded instruments are excluded; the bank cannot lend money to a buyer who then lends it back as MREL.
  3. Remaining maturity of at least one year. Short-dated instruments do not count, because they may mature before they can be bailed in. As the residual maturity falls below one year, the instrument falls out of MREL eligibility on a straight-line basis.
  4. Not arising from a derivative. Derivative payables are excluded because their valuation in resolution is too uncertain.
  5. Not a deposit benefiting from preferred treatment. Covered and preferred deposits are explicitly excluded; their bail-in would defeat the depositor-protection purpose.
  6. Contractual or statutory subordination. The instrument must rank below operating-company senior unsecured creditors, either contractually (senior non-preferred) or structurally (HoldCo senior issued above an OpCo).
  7. Bail-in clause. The instrument's terms must acknowledge the BoE's statutory bail-in power, and for instruments governed by non-UK law, must include a contractual recognition of that power.
  8. For external MREL, issued by the resolution entity. Instruments issued by operating subsidiaries do not count as external MREL; they may count as internal MREL on a look-through basis.

OpCo senior unsecured debt issued by the regulated bank itself is generally not eligible for MREL. Bailing in OpCo senior would force losses onto trade creditors, derivative counterparties, and large corporate depositors of the operating company, which the BoE has judged would be operationally disruptive and could trigger contagion. The structural answer adopted by every UK G-SIB has been to create a holding company above the operating bank and issue MREL-eligible senior debt from the HoldCo, where there are no operating creditors to disturb.

Internal MREL: pre-positioning resources at sub-groups

External MREL is set on the resolution entity, typically the top UK holding company. But the losses that drive a resolution might originate in any material subsidiary. To ensure that losses can flow up to the resolution entity, where they can be absorbed by the bail-in of external creditors, the BoE imposes an internal MREL on material sub-groups within the resolution group. Internal MREL is issued by the subsidiary to the resolution entity (its parent), and is calibrated as a percentage of the equivalent external MREL the subsidiary would face if it were resolved on a standalone basis. The BoE's standard calibration is between 75 per cent and 90 per cent of the standalone equivalent.

The mechanics are: when a material subsidiary suffers losses, the internal MREL instruments held by the parent are written down or converted into equity at the subsidiary, transmitting the loss to the parent. The parent absorbs the loss against its own external MREL, which is then bailed in by writing down or converting the external instruments held by third-party investors. Internal MREL is the plumbing that connects local losses to centralised loss absorption, and it is what makes a single point of entry resolution strategy operationally feasible.

Single point of entry versus multiple point of entry

The BoE generally favours a single point of entry (SPE) resolution strategy for UK-headquartered groups: bail-in is executed only at the top resolution entity (the UK HoldCo), and losses flow up from operating subsidiaries through internal MREL. This is operationally simpler, avoids fragmenting the group during resolution, and aligns with the FSB's preferred approach for G-SIBs. Barclays, Lloyds, NatWest, Standard Chartered, and HSBC's UK ring-fenced bank all have SPE strategies executed in London.

The alternative is multiple point of entry (MPE), where bail-in is executed at several regional resolution entities simultaneously. HSBC Group, the only true global UK-headquartered banking group, follows a partial MPE approach: separate resolution entities in the UK, Hong Kong, and the US allow each region to be resolved independently of the others, reflecting the firm's federal structure and the political reality that no single jurisdiction wants to depend on another to resolve a major subsidiary. MPE requires more loss-absorbing capacity overall, because each entity must be self-sufficient, but it is more robust to cross-border resolution friction.

Relationship with TLAC

The FSB's TLAC standard applies only to G-SIBs and sets a floor: external TLAC must be at least the higher of 18 per cent of RWA and 6.75 per cent of the Basel III leverage exposure measure (the post-2022 calibration). MREL applies to all UK firms above the resolution threshold, not just G-SIBs, and is set firm-by-firm by the BoE. For UK G-SIBs, MREL must be at least as high as TLAC; in practice, BoE-set MREL is higher than the TLAC floor for most G-SIBs because the 2 × (P1 + P2A) calibration exceeds 18 per cent once Pillar 2A is added in. There is no double-counting: the same instruments meet both requirements, and the BoE confirms TLAC compliance through the MREL framework.

Two structural differences between TLAC and MREL are worth memorising. First, TLAC has an explicit minimum subordination requirement (the bulk of TLAC must be subordinated to excluded liabilities), whereas MREL has a similar but firm-specific subordination expectation set by the BoE. Second, TLAC excludes deposits regardless of size, whereas MREL allows certain large corporate deposits to count in narrow circumstances; in practice, no UK bank includes deposits in its MREL stack.

The MDA-style restriction

If a firm fails to meet its MREL plus combined buffer requirement, the firm becomes subject to a Maximum Distributable Amount (MDA)-style restriction, mirroring the capital MDA framework in Article 141 of the CRR. Discretionary distributions (ordinary share dividends, AT1 coupons, discretionary bonuses) are capped by reference to the firm's interim profits, and the cap tightens as the buffer breach deepens. The MDA trigger sits at the top of the combined buffer; firms typically aim to maintain a "management buffer" above the trigger so that ordinary volatility does not push them into restriction.

UK eligible-instrument structures

In practice, UK banks meet MREL through three instrument types issued in roughly fixed proportions. Common equity Tier 1 forms the base (10 to 12 per cent of RWA for a typical UK clearer). AT1 contingent convertibles and Tier 2 subordinated debt sit above CET1 (typically 1 to 2 per cent of RWA each). The recapitalisation amount is funded almost entirely by HoldCo senior debt issued from the top UK resolution entity. Barclays plc, Lloyds Banking Group plc, NatWest Group plc, Standard Chartered plc, and HSBC Holdings plc all issue HoldCo senior under English law with explicit bail-in clauses, distributed primarily into the international wholesale market.

The HoldCo senior market in London is now one of the deepest in the world, with annual issuance of GBP 30 to 50 billion across the UK G-SIBs and other large firms. Pricing is at a spread above OpCo senior of around 30 to 60 basis points, reflecting the structural subordination, the bail-in risk, and the absence of a deposit-insurance floor for senior debt. The cost of carrying MREL is one of the largest items in a UK bank's wholesale funding budget, and a meaningful constraint on the marginal economics of RWA-intensive business lines.

The 2021 revisions

The BoE updated the Statement of Policy in December 2021 after a public consultation on whether the regime, designed during the BRRD era for the largest firms, was bearing too heavily on medium-sized banks growing into MREL. The revisions extended the transitional period for firms newly subject to MREL, allowed a glide-path of three to six years from indicative to end-state MREL, and clarified that the BoE may set MREL below the 2 × (P1 + P2A) headline where the firm's resolution strategy implies a smaller surviving entity. The revisions also tightened internal MREL calibration to align with the FSB's i-TLAC standard for G-SIBs.

The 2025 reforms

In 2025 the BoE published further proposals to simplify MREL for non-systemic firms, in response to industry feedback that the framework was insufficiently differentiated between firms with truly systemic functions and those whose failure could be handled through modified insolvency. The 2025 reforms raised the indicative size threshold at which a firm moves from modified insolvency to a transfer or bail-in strategy, eased the eligibility tests for instruments issued by smaller resolution entities, and provided greater proportionality in disclosure and reporting requirements. The reforms are an explicit recognition that the original BRRD calibration, designed for a small population of systemically important banks, scaled poorly to the much wider population to which MREL formally applies.

A worked example

UK bail-in bank: external MREL computation.

Take a UK ring-fenced clearing bank with the following profile:

  • Risk-weighted assets: GBP 100 billion
  • Pillar 1 capital requirement: 8 per cent of RWA = GBP 8 billion
  • Pillar 2A capital requirement: 2 per cent of RWA = GBP 2 billion
  • Capital conservation buffer: 2.5 per cent of RWA = GBP 2.5 billion
  • Countercyclical buffer: 1.0 per cent of RWA = GBP 1.0 billion
  • Preferred resolution strategy: bail-in (single point of entry)

The MREL calculation proceeds as follows:

Loss absorption amount (LAA) = P1 + P2A
                              = 8 per cent + 2 per cent
                              = 10 per cent of RWA
                              = GBP 10 billion

Recapitalisation amount (RCA) = P1 + P2A
                              = 8 per cent + 2 per cent
                              = 10 per cent of RWA
                              = GBP 10 billion

External MREL                  = LAA + RCA
                              = 20 per cent of RWA
                              = GBP 20 billion

Combined buffer requirement   = CCB + CCyB
                              = 2.5 per cent + 1.0 per cent
                              = 3.5 per cent of RWA
                              = GBP 3.5 billion

MREL + CBR (the MDA trigger)   = 23.5 per cent of RWA
                              = GBP 23.5 billion

The bank must therefore carry GBP 20 billion of MREL-eligible instruments (equity, AT1, Tier 2, and HoldCo senior debt) at all times, plus GBP 3.5 billion of CET1 in the combined buffer. A typical composition might be: GBP 11 billion CET1, GBP 1.5 billion AT1, GBP 1.5 billion Tier 2, and GBP 6 billion HoldCo senior debt. The HoldCo senior is the marginal instrument issued specifically to meet the recapitalisation amount; it would not be on the balance sheet but for MREL.

If the bank's CET1 ratio falls by 100 basis points due to credit losses, MREL is unaffected as long as the firm continues to hold the eligible stack. But the combined buffer is consumed, the MDA trigger is breached, and dividends, AT1 coupons, and discretionary bonuses are restricted. Restoring the buffer requires either retained earnings, a fresh equity issuance, or shrinking RWA.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

MREL has rewritten UK bank capital structure

Before MREL, the UK clearing banks ran balance sheets consisting of equity, some subordinated debt, and a large slug of OpCo senior unsecured. After MREL, the same banks run holding-company structures above the operating bank, fund the recapitalisation amount through HoldCo senior issuance, and price the carry cost back to the business lines through FTP. Every UK G-SIB has moved through the same capital-structure transformation between roughly 2015 and 2022. The transformation was not driven by capital optimisation; it was driven by the BoE's preferred resolution strategy and the eligibility rules. MREL is the rare prudential rule that has reshaped how banks are owned, not just how they are capitalised.

Resolution authorities can vary MREL for firm-specific factors

The 2 × (P1 + P2A) headline calibration is a starting point, not a constraint on the BoE. In setting MREL for individual firms, the BoE may adjust the recapitalisation amount downwards where the resolution plan implies a smaller surviving entity, or upwards where the firm's business model implies a higher post-resolution capital need. The BoE may also impose subordination requirements firm-by-firm. The published indicative MREL list shows the headline percentages; the binding firm-specific MREL is communicated to each firm directly. Reading the indicative list as if it were the firm-specific number is a common analyst error.

SVB UK (March 2023): the regime in action, but not via bail-in

When Silicon Valley Bank UK Limited failed on the night of 10 March 2023 alongside its US parent, the BoE did not use bail-in. SVB UK was small enough that its preferred resolution strategy was sale to a private-sector purchaser; HSBC bought it for GBP 1 on the Sunday evening, with the BoE having used the partial-transfer tool from the Banking Act 2009. The case demonstrated that MREL is one option among several, that the BoE's resolution framework can complete a transfer in 48 hours when the buyer is ready, and that an MREL stack is not the only way to avoid taxpayer support. It also showed an uncomfortable truth: SVB UK had not yet built a full MREL stack because it was below the bail-in threshold, and the rescue worked because a buyer existed. Without HSBC, the outcome would have been depositor payout through the FSCS and franchise destruction.

Credit Suisse (March 2023): resolved via merger, not bail-in

One week after SVB UK, Credit Suisse failed and was merged into UBS over a single weekend by the Swiss authorities. The Swiss regime, in principle, supports bail-in resolution; in practice, the authorities chose to wipe out the AT1 instruments (USD 17 billion of CoCo bonds) while preserving some value for equity, an outcome that was the reverse of the orthodox bail-in waterfall. The episode raised hard questions about whether large banks can actually be resolved through bail-in under acute stress, and whether the merger-rescue precedent will become the default. UK and EU resolution authorities have publicly reaffirmed their commitment to bail-in for their own G-SIBs, but the credibility of the regime depends on whether it can survive the political pressure of a real failure.

EU SRMR and the BRRD II nuances

The EU's equivalent regime sits in the Single Resolution Mechanism Regulation (SRMR) and an amended BRRD (BRRD II). The Single Resolution Board (SRB) plays the role the BoE plays in the UK for euro-area significant institutions; national resolution authorities cover smaller firms. The eligibility criteria are essentially the same as in the UK, but the calibration formula was modified by BRRD II to use the term "MREL-TREA" (a ratio to total risk exposure amount) and to introduce a leverage-based component for G-SIIs. Subordination requirements differ in detail: French and Italian banks have made heavy use of senior non-preferred debt (a contractually subordinated instrument created specifically for this purpose), whereas UK banks rely on structural subordination through a HoldCo. For a cross-border banking group, the divergence between UK and EU calibration is a live operational issue.

MREL is gone-concern, not going-concern

A persistent confusion is the relationship between MREL and capital. Capital is going-concern: it absorbs losses while the firm continues to trade. MREL is gone-concern: it absorbs losses and recapitalises the firm at the point of failure. The same CET1, AT1, and Tier 2 instruments count towards both, but the eligible-liability layer (HoldCo senior) counts only towards MREL, not towards capital. Reading a UK bank's balance sheet, the analyst should identify the capital ratios first (CET1, T1, total capital) and then the MREL ratio separately; the two are calculated against the same RWA denominator but use different numerators.

Watch out Always check whether a published MREL figure is on the "MREL excluding buffers" basis (the legal requirement) or "MREL including buffers" basis (the operational threshold). The BoE typically reports both; analysts often quote one and reason with the other. The MDA-style restriction kicks in on the latter; the binding legal requirement is the former.

Further reading

  • BoE Resolvability Assessment Framework (RAF): annual public disclosures by major UK banks on their resolution readiness, including MREL build progress.
  • FSB Resolution Report (annual): tracks implementation of the TLAC standard and the broader resolution toolkit across G-SIB home jurisdictions.
  • Hellwig, "Capital Regulation after the Crisis", Max Planck Institute working paper: a critical perspective on whether the loss-absorbing-capacity regime can substitute for higher equity.
  • HM Treasury, "Resolution regime for central counterparties": parallel statutory framework for non-bank financial market infrastructures, increasingly cross-referenced in MREL policy work.
