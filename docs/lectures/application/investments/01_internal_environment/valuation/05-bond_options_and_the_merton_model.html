Bond options and the Merton model: a lognormal price for an underlying that has a ceiling

Black's formula assumes the underlying is unbounded above. A bond price is capped by the undiscounted sum of its remaining cash flows, and the cap falls as the bond shortens while Black's tail grows with the square root of time, so the two must cross. On the worked bond they cross between five and eight years. The same lecture reads a company's equity as a call on its assets, which makes a corporate bond a risk-free bond less a put worth 1.484769, and then finds the model sending short-dated credit spreads to zero.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The source gives three forms of the bond forward price, a Black formula for bond options, a conversion from yield volatility to price volatility, and the Merton model, all without derivations or conditions. Three of the four have conditions worth knowing, and one of them fails in a way that produces an arithmetically impossible answer.

The bond forward is carry, with the coupon in the role of the dividend yield. On a 6 per cent ten-year bond priced at 86.579837 to yield 8 per cent, financed at 5 per cent, the one-year forward is 85.178485 using the discrete-coupon form. The continuous-yield form gives 84.924851, out by 0.2978 per cent, which is the cost of pretending a lumpy coupon is a smooth yield. And the source's note that bond derivatives use dirty prices matters: a repo lender finances the dirty price, so using a clean price understates the financing base.

The yield-to-price volatility conversion is a duration-only approximation and the source does not say so. Testing it by taking the yield as lognormal and repricing the bond exactly at every yield, the true price volatility is below the duration estimate at every level: 11.0100 against 11.2816 per cent at a 20 per cent yield volatility, and at 40 per cent the rule overstates by 8.96 per cent. Convexity explains both halves of the discrepancy, raising the mean price above the price at the mean yield and simultaneously damping the dispersion.

The Merton model is Black-Scholes with the firm's assets as the underlying and the face of its debt as the strike, because limited liability gives shareholders max(V − D, 0). On a firm worth 100 with debt of 60, equity is worth 54.756722 and the debt 45.243278, which is the risk-free 46.728047 less a put worth 1.484769. So a corporate bond is a risk-free bond plus a written put, and the lender is short volatility. The model reproduces the observed shape of real spread curves, rising for low gearing and falling for high, and it sends the one-month spread to zero, which is the credit spread puzzle.

Feynman test A bond cannot be worth more than the total of the payments it has left, so the closer it gets to paying them the less room there is for its price to move. Any formula that assumes the price can wander anywhere will eventually predict a price the bond could not reach. Separately, owning shares in a company that has borrowed money is like owning an option: if the assets end up worth more than the debt you keep the difference, and if they do not you walk away. Which means lending to that company is selling that option, and the interest you earn is the premium.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the boundedness failure is a rare thing in modelling: an assumption whose violation produces an answer that is not merely inaccurate but impossible. A two-sigma upper price of 129.32 on a bond that can be worth at most 112.00 is not a calibration problem. It is arithmetic telling you the model does not apply, and it is worth having one example of that kind in mind, because most model failures are quieter.

The second is that the Merton decomposition is the intellectual bridge between the equity and credit markets, and it changes how a lender should think about what they hold. A bond is not a loan with a risk attached; it is a risk-free loan plus a written option, and everything that follows about being short volatility, about the position deteriorating precisely when uncertainty rises, and about spread being an option premium received in instalments, follows from that identity rather than from intuition.

The third is the leverage effect, which is the most practically useful output of the model and the least discussed. The same 25 per cent asset volatility becomes 32.54 per cent equity volatility at low gearing and 53.17 at high, purely because equity is a levered claim. So observed equity volatility is a property of the business and the capital structure, and a rise in it is ambiguous between the assets becoming riskier and the firm having borrowed more. Anyone comparing volatilities across companies without adjusting for gearing is comparing two different things.

And a fourth. The credit spread puzzle is the clearest example in finance of a model that is right about shape and wrong about level, and of why two modelling traditions coexist. Merton gets the term structure of spreads right and the short end badly wrong; the reduced-form hazard rate approach of derivatives lecture 6 gets the short end right and has nothing to say about why. Neither replaces the other, and knowing which question each answers is the useful skill.

The assumption that fails outright

Black's formula assumes the underlying is lognormally distributed at expiry, and therefore unbounded above. A bond price is not unbounded. It cannot exceed the undiscounted sum of the cash flows still to come, and that sum falls as the bond shortens.

The Merton model: a company as a set of options on its own assets

The source gives E = V PHI(d1) - D exp(-rT) PHI(d2), which IS Black-Scholes with the firm's asset value V as the underlying and the face of its debt D as the strike. The economics is one sentence: AT MATURITY THE SHAREHOLDERS RECEIVE max(V - D, 0), because limited liability lets them walk away from a firm worth less than it owes. THAT IS A CALL OPTION ON THE ASSETS. THE REASON IS THAT DEFAULT CAN ARRIVE BY JUMP RATHER THAN BY DRIFT. Fraud, a lost lawsuit, a liquidity freeze, a regulator withdrawing a licence: these do not take a firm's asset value smoothly down through a barrier, they remove it. A PURE DIFFUSION HAS NO VOCABULARY FOR A SURPRISE, and the fixes all amount to adding one, whether as a jump process, an unobservable default barrier, or a reduced-form hazard rate. THE TWO TRADITIONS DISAGREE MOST AT THE SHORT END, the reduced-form one is closer to the market, and the structural one explains why the curve has the shape it has. Credit desks quote hazard rates and equity-based default models quote Merton, and neither is a substitute for the other.

The same business with the same 25 per cent asset volatility shows 32.54 per cent equity volatility when lightly geared and 53.17 when heavily geared, a span of more than twenty percentage points arising entirely from the capital structure. SO OBSERVED EQUITY VOLATILITY IS NOT A PROPERTY OF THE BUSINESS. It is a property of the business AND its gearing, which has three consequences worth stating. A RISE IN EQUITY VOLATILITY IS AMBIGUOUS. The assets may have become riskier, or the firm may simply have borrowed more, and the two have opposite implications for whether the business has deteriorated. COMPARING VOLATILITIES ACROSS COMPANIES WITHOUT ADJUSTING FOR GEARING compares two different quantities, which makes a naive volatility screen a gearing screen. AND EQUITY VOLATILITY RISES AS THE SHARE PRICE FALLS, mechanically, because a falling equity value raises V/E. That is one structural explanation for the leverage effect observed in equity option markets, where implied volatility rises as prices fall, and it requires no behavioural story at all. THE SOURCE GIVES THE FIRST EQUATION AND NOT THE SECOND, so it has half of a system that is only useful solved jointly. The joint solution is the engine behind commercial default-probability models built on equity prices, and it is the main reason the Merton model is used in practice at all.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to take is that model failures come in a hierarchy, and this module has now produced one of each grade. An interpolation choice gives several defensible answers. A volatility assumption gives a biased answer. A lognormal price for a bounded underlying gives an impossible answer. The third is the only one that cannot be recalibrated away, because the defect is in the shape of the assumed distribution rather than in its parameters, and it is the only one you can detect without market data by asking what the underlying's support actually is.

Ask what the largest value the underlying can take is, and whether the assumed distribution assigns probability beyond it. For a bond the answer is the undiscounted sum of the remaining cash flows. For a share it is unbounded, which is why Black-Scholes was fine there and the analogy travelled badly.

The second is that convexity produced two effects here that point in opposite directions, and confusing them is common. The mean price rises above the price at the mean yield, which is the convexity benefit everyone knows. The dispersion falls below the duration estimate, which is the same curvature seen from the second moment rather than the first. A trader who knows convexity is good for them and does not know it narrows their risk estimate will overstate the volatility of their own book by nine per cent at a 40 per cent yield volatility, which is a large error made by someone who understands the concept.

The third is the Merton identity, which is worth carrying beyond credit. A corporate bond is a risk-free bond plus a written put, and once that is internalised the entire behaviour of credit becomes predictable rather than empirical. Spreads widen when volatility rises, because a written option is worth more to its holder. Spreads widen more for the highly geared, because the option is nearer the money. Recovery rates fall in recessions, because the asset value at default is lower when the barrier is breached by a larger margin. None of those needs a separate theory.

And in every case the position is revalued against the holder exactly when volatility arrives, which is what makes the noticing matter.

The fourth is about the credit spread puzzle as an example of how to read a model that is wrong. Merton gets the shape of the spread curve right, including the non-obvious prediction that distressed curves invert, and gets the short-dated level badly wrong. The temptation is to conclude the model is bad. The better conclusion is that it is a model of one mechanism, gradual asset deterioration, and that the missing spread at the short end is a measurement of everything else: fraud, litigation, liquidity, and every other way a company fails suddenly. Read that way, the model's error is informative rather than embarrassing, and the size of the error is an estimate of how much default risk is not about the business gradually shrinking.

And the fifth. The leverage effect explains something that is usually attributed to sentiment. Equity implied volatility rises as prices fall, and one standard story is that investors panic. Merton gives a mechanical explanation requiring no psychology: a falling equity value raises V over E, so the same asset volatility shows up as a higher equity volatility. That does not prove the behavioural story wrong, but it does mean that anyone offering it has to first show the effect is larger than gearing alone would produce, and the arithmetic in step 4 says gearing alone produces a great deal of it.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. Duration and convexity, and the fact that a bond's price is a convex function of its yield, are from fixed income pricing and financial instruments. The discount factors and forward rates used throughout are from yield curves and bootstrapping. The change of numeraire, used here for the fourth time, and Black's model with its lognormality condition, are from pricing interest rate derivatives, and the annuity numeraire and the parity check pattern are from swap and swaption valuation. On the derivatives side, the cost-of-carry relation with income in the dividend-yield role is from forwards and futures, the writer's short volatility position is from options, the short-rate tree that replaces Black where the pull to par dominates and the four instruments that embed a written option are from CFDs and other OTC contracts, and the reduced-form hazard rate approach that this lecture contrasts with Merton is from credit derivatives. The seniority waterfall that determines what the put pays on is from government and corporate bonds.

Where this goes next. Lecture 6 prices currency forwards and commodity futures, which use the continuous-income form of the carry relation that is wrong for bonds and right there. Lecture 7 takes up hedging and the optimal hedge ratio, where the duration approximation tested here is the tool being used and its convexity error is the residual. Lecture 8's asset pricing taxonomy will distinguish absolute from relative models, and the Merton model is the cleanest example of an absolute one. Lecture 13 on credit analysis takes the structural and reduced-form approaches as two lenses on the same question and adds the accounting and judgemental layer that neither contains. The gearing-dependence of equity volatility is a portfolio construction issue whenever risk is estimated from equity returns, and the model risk of a wrong-support distribution belongs in risk measurement.

Where the bank sits opposite. The Merton model is the theoretical ancestor of the Basel A-IRB capital framework, whose single-factor asset value formulation is Merton with a common factor added, so the distance-to-default computed here is the same object as the standardised normal threshold in the capital formula. The gap between the model's short-dated spread and the observed one is why credit risk measurement estimates probability of default from realised default data rather than from asset value diffusion alone. A bank buying a callable or prepayable asset, or funding itself with a puttable instrument, is on one side of the written options catalogued in step 5, which is why the negative convexity of such a book against non-maturing deposits is the core of interest rate risk in the banking book. Bond option positions in the trading book sit under market risk, where the wrong-support failure identified in step 4 is precisely the kind of finding a model validation function exists to produce.

The arithmetic is exact and internally checked four ways: the modified duration agrees with a numerical differentiation to 3.7 times ten to the minus ten, call minus put equals the discounted forward less strike at every strike, the directly priced put reproduces the Merton debt value to 7.1 times ten to the minus fifteen, and the exact repricing integration is run at twenty thousand points.
