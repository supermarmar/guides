Credit analysis: the fourth step is listed last and is logically first

The source's four steps run purpose of issue, source of repayment, source of credit risk, issue terms. But the terms are what make the first three binding: a debt-funded share buyback takes 12.50 per cent off existing bondholders without the business changing at all, and only a covenant prevents it. The same lecture shows that expected loss explains 2.4 per cent of a AAA spread and that correlation redistributes securitisation losses between tranches while creating none.

Step 1

The 30-second version

This closes the module. Credit analysis asks a narrower question than equity valuation, namely whether a borrower will repay rather than what the business is worth, and the narrowness makes it more tractable and its failures more specific.

The source's methodology, to consider probability of default, severity and interest earned, is not a checklist. It is the expected loss identity, and the deal is worth doing when the spread exceeds expected loss by enough to pay for the capital held against unexpected loss. Note which lever is neglected: moving severity from 25 to 70 per cent multiplies expected loss by 2.8, and severity is a question about security and capital structure rather than about the borrower's health.

The purpose of issue is a credit signal and the source's list happens to be ordered by risk. The share buyback deserves the attention, because it is a direct transfer from creditors to shareholders that the Merton model prices exactly: a buyback funded by 30 of new debt against a firm worth 100 takes 4.7882 off existing bondholders, or 12.50 per cent of their position, with the business unchanged. That is why covenants exist, and it is why the source's fourth step is logically the first.

Two evaluation results close the lecture. A rating is ordinal, not a probability: expected loss explains 2.4 per cent of a AAA spread, 16.0 per cent of a BBB and 58.0 per cent of a CCC, with the remainder paying for unexpected loss, liquidity and a genuine risk premium. And in a securitisation, correlation redistributes loss between tranches and creates none: the pool's expected loss is 3.00 per cent at every correlation while the equity tranche falls from 82.23 to 29.29 per cent and the senior rises from 0.01 to 1.61.

Feynman test Lending money is a bet that you will be repaid, so the three things that matter are how likely you are not to be, how much you lose when that happens, and how much you are paid to take the chance. What people forget is that the borrower can change the first two after you have lent, by borrowing more and handing the proceeds to its owners. The paperwork that stops them doing so is worth more than the analysis that decided the loan was safe.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is the ordering. A credit analysis that prices today's balance sheet has priced a snapshot of something the borrower controls, and the arithmetic shows exactly how much of a lender's position can be removed by a decision the lender has no say over. Terms are not documentation to be handled after the credit decision; they are what makes the credit decision survive.

The second is that the gap between spread and expected loss is where most of the return lives, and it is routinely mislabelled. At investment grade the expected loss is a small fraction of the spread, which is often read as evidence that credit is cheap. It is not: the remainder pays for capital, illiquidity and the fact that credit losses arrive in bad states, which the pricing equation of lecture 8 says must earn a premium.

The third is that a securitisation cannot manufacture credit quality. The pool's expected loss is invariant to correlation, so any evaluation reporting the pool's loss has said nothing about the tranche being bought. Every rand of improvement in a senior tranche came from somewhere, and the somewhere is the equity tranche below it.

And a fourth. The mezzanine tranche's expected loss moves non-monotonically in correlation, peaking around 0.30 on the worked pool, which makes it the slice whose value depends most delicately on a parameter nobody observes. That is the structural reason mezzanine tranches were the ones that failed to behave as rated, and it is visible from the arithmetic without any history.

The detail

The methodology is the expected loss identity

The source: consider probability of default (creditworthiness), SEVERITY (exposure), and interest earned (return). Those are not three items on a checklist; they are the three factors of one product and one comparison. EXPECTED LOSS = probability of default x severity x exposure and the deal is worth doing if the SPREAD exceeds the expected loss by enough to pay for the CAPITAL held against the UNEXPECTED loss. On R100m of exposure: PD 0.20%, severity 45% -> expected loss 0.0900% a year = R 90,000 PD 0.75%, severity 45% -> expected loss 0.3375% = R 337,500 PD 2.00%, severity 45% -> expected loss 0.9000% = R 900,000 PD 0.75%, severity 25% -> expected loss 0.1875% = R 187,500 PD 0.75%, severity 70% -> expected loss 0.5250% = R 525,000 NOTE WHICH LEVER IS NEGLECTED. Holding the probability at 0.75 per cent, moving severity from 25 to 70 multiplies the expected loss by 2.8. Holding severity at 45, moving the probability from 0.20 to 2.00 per cent multiplies it by 10. Both matter and only one is usually modelled with care. THE SOURCE'S WORD "SEVERITY" IS BETTER THAN THE USUAL "LOSS GIVEN DEFAULT" and is worth preferring, because it names the thing directly: how bad is it WHEN it happens. That is a question about the SECURITY and the CAPITAL STRUCTURE rather than about the borrower's health, which means it is answered by the documents rather than by the accounts, and it is the half of the product a lender can most directly control.

No product failed, no customer left, no forecast was missed. The assets fell only because cash was handed out, and the claim on those assets was diluted by an equal-ranking new claim. THAT IS WHY COVENANTS EXIST, and it is why the source's step 4, issue terms, includes covenants alongside price and term. A LENDER WHO PRICES THE CREDIT CORRECTLY TODAY AND TAKES NO COVENANT HAS PRICED A COMPANY THE BORROWER IS FREE TO REPLACE. Note also that the transfer is convex in the amount borrowed: 10 of new debt costs the bondholders 0.9638 and 40 costs 7.5117, so quadrupling the buyback multiplies the damage by 7.8. Small releveragings are nearly free to the borrower and large ones are not, which is why covenant packages are usually written as incurrence tests on a ratio rather than as absolute caps.

Source of repayment, and the one that is different in kind

The source gives three: profit profile, sale of assets, refinancing. source repayment depends on fails when profit profile the business working the business stops working sale of assets an asset market existing everyone sells at once refinancing CREDIT MARKETS BEING OPEN credit markets close THE THIRD IS CATEGORICALLY DIFFERENT AND THE SOURCE LISTS IT AS ONE OF THREE. A company repaying from profit depends on ITSELF. Quantifying the roll risk. The company has not changed. The market has, and the covenant converts a market move into a default. AND THE TWO RISKS ARE CORRELATED IN THE WRONG DIRECTION. Spreads widen in exactly the conditions in which profits fall, so the refinancing option is least available when it is most needed. THAT IS THE WRONG-WAY RISK OF DERIVATIVES LECTURE 6 IN A DIFFERENT COSTUME, and it is why a maturity wall is analysed separately from leverage: a company can be comfortably geared and still fail on a date in the calendar.

Rating an entity: what a rating is and is not

A rating is an ORDINAL RANKING of creditworthiness, not a probability. Agencies publish realised default rates by rating, and those rates are the empirical content of the letter. THE REMAINDER IS NOT MISPRICING. It pays for four things the expected loss ignores: UNEXPECTED LOSS the capital held against the tail, which is what lecture 5's Merton put and the A-IRB capital formula both price LIQUIDITY a corporate bond cannot be sold at will, and least of all when it most needs selling TAX AND FRICTION corporate coupons are taxed differently from government coupons in some jurisdictions RISK PREMIUM defaults CLUSTER, so credit losses arrive in bad states, which by lecture 8's pricing relation MUST earn a premium THE LAST IS THE ONE PEOPLE FORGET AND IT IS THE LARGEST. By the stochastic discount factor relation, an asset that pays badly when the discount factor is high must earn a premium, and credit pays badly in recessions by construction. So the gap between spread and expected loss is not evidence that credit is cheap; it is what the pricing equation predicts. Expressed as a ratio of what an investor is paid to what they expect to lose: AAA 40.89x BBB 6.27x B 2.21x AA 18.16x BB 3.21x CCC 1.72x A 11.66x THE RATIO FALLS MONOTONICALLY DOWN THE SCALE, which is the useful reading: high yield is paid closer to its expected loss and therefore has less cushion for the loss being underestimated. A rating tells you the ORDER and not the PRICE, and the price tells you how much error the spread can absorb.

Evaluating a securitisation: the tranche nobody can rate

A pool of loans is sliced, and losses hit the equity tranche first, then mezzanine, then senior. The question an evaluator must answer is not how risky the pool is but HOW RISKY THIS SLICE IS, and the answer depends on DEFAULT CORRELATION, which is not observable. A pool with a 6 per cent default probability and 50 per cent severity, so an expected pool loss of 3.00 per cent, under a one-factor Gaussian copula: correlation equity 0-3% mezzanine 3-7% senior 7-100% WHOLE POOL 0.05 82.23% 13.03% 0.01% 3.00% 0.15 69.00% 18.66% 0.20% 3.00% 0.30 55.65% 19.90% 0.58% 3.00% 0.50 41.67% 18.47% 1.09% 3.00% 0.70 29.29% 15.68% 1.61% 3.00% READ THE COLUMNS AS DERIVATIVES WITH RESPECT TO CORRELATION: EQUITY is LONG correlation, falling from 82.23 to 29.29 per cent, because high correlation makes the outcome "almost no defaults at all" possible SENIOR is SHORT correlation, rising from 0.01 to 1.61 per cent, because only correlated defaults can ever reach it MEZZANINE moves NON-MONOTONICALLY, from 13.03 up to a peak of 19.90 near a correlation of 0.30 and back down to 15.68, so it is neither long nor short correlation and its value depends most delicately on a parameter nobody observes AND THE LAST COLUMN IS CONSTANT AT 3.00 PER CENT IN EVERY ROW. CORRELATION REDISTRIBUTES LOSS BETWEEN TRANCHES AND CREATES NONE. So A SECURITISATION CANNOT MANUFACTURE CREDIT QUALITY; it can only move it, and every rand of improvement in the senior tranche came out of the equity tranche beneath it. Any evaluation that reports the pool's expected loss has said nothing about the tranche being bought. THE PRACTICAL RULE FOR AN EVALUATOR is to ask what correlation the rating assumed and what the tranche is worth if that number is wrong by a third. For the senior tranche, moving correlation from 0.15 to 0.30 changes the expected loss by +191.6 per cent. THE MEZZANINE RESULT IS THE STRUCTURAL EXPLANATION FOR A HISTORICAL EVENT. Mezzanine tranches of structured credit were the ones that failed most spectacularly to behave as rated, and the arithmetic above shows why without needing the history: they sit at the point where the correlation derivative changes sign, so a rating agency's assumed correlation determines their loss more than any property of the underlying borrowers does.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to carry is that credit analysis has a boundary the analysis itself cannot see. Every ratio, rating and expected loss calculation describes the borrower as it is today, and the borrower can change what it is. The buyback arithmetic puts a number on that: 12.50 per cent of a bondholder's position, removed by a decision the lender has no vote on and no warning of. Only the documents constrain it, which means the most quantitative discipline in this module has its binding constraint in prose.

WHAT EACH PART OF A CREDIT FILE CAN AND CANNOT DO. component tells you cannot tell you accounting ratios the position today what it will be tomorrow a rating the ORDER of creditworthiness the price, or the recovery an expected loss the average outcome the tail, which sets capital a spread what you are paid what for: EL, capital, liquidity or premium THE COVENANTS what the borrower MAY NOT DO whether they will comply THE LAST ROW IS THE ONLY FORWARD-LOOKING ONE and it is the only one written in words rather than numbers. That is not a coincidence: a constraint on future behaviour cannot be expressed as a current measurement.

The second is the gap between spread and expected loss, which is where most of the return lives and which is routinely misread in both directions. Read as mispricing it invites the conclusion that investment grade credit is a free lunch, paid 40.89 times its expected loss at AAA. Read as pure risk premium it invites the conclusion that credit adds nothing. Neither is right: the gap pays for capital against the tail, for illiquidity, and for the fact that credit losses cluster in bad states, which the pricing relation of lecture 8 says must earn a premium. Knowing which portion is which decides whether a credit allocation is a diversifier or a levered equity position in disguise.

The third is the correlation result, which appears twice in this lecture in different clothes and is the single most transferable idea in structured credit. In a securitisation, correlation redistributes loss between tranches and creates none: the pool's expected loss is 3.00 per cent at every correlation. In a credit derivative, correlation between the reference and the seller destroys protection without changing either credit. In both cases the parameter is unobservable, in both cases it moves the answer enormously, and in both cases the party who ignores it believes they hold a safer asset than they do.

THE SAME UNOBSERVABLE, IN FOUR PLACES IN THIS COURSE. where what correlation does who is exposed a securitisation tranche moves loss between slices, creating none whoever bought the slice nearest the correlation kink a CDS with a correlated seller destroys protection: 98.00% at zero the protection buyer correlation, 60.43% at 0.90 a diversified loan book sets how much idiosyncratic risk the lender, through actually diversifies away concentration risk an equity portfolio sets the floor below which the investor, through diversification cannot go systematic risk FOUR SETTINGS, ONE PARAMETER, AND IT IS NEVER OBSERVED. IN EVERY CASE THE HONEST PRESENTATION IS A RANGE RATHER THAN A NUMBER, and in every case a range is what nobody wants to be given.

The fourth is the mezzanine finding, which is worth stating as a general principle rather than as a fact about tranches. The equity tranche is long correlation and the senior is short it, so somewhere between them the sensitivity changes sign, and whatever sits at that point has a value determined almost entirely by an assumption rather than by the underlying assets. That is a structural warning about any instrument constructed at the crossover of two opposing sensitivities: it will look stable in normal conditions precisely because its exposures offset, and it will be the least predictable thing in the book.

And the fifth, closing the module. Thirteen lectures have moved through three modes of valuation. Replication gave exact answers pinned by arbitrage, where the work was finding the assumption. Modelling gave estimates whose inputs could not be measured, where the work was bounding the error. Judgement gives an argument about one issuer, where the work is knowing what the argument cannot cover. THE CONSISTENT FINDING ACROSS ALL THREE IS THAT THE FORMULA IS THE EASY PART. In the first mode the difficulty was a condition nobody stated, in the second an input nobody could observe, and in the third a document nobody read. None of those is a mathematical difficulty, and all of them decide the answer.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The Merton model that prices the buyback transfer, and the reading of corporate debt as a risk-free bond less a written put, are from bond options and the Merton model. The credit triangle, the wrong-way risk arithmetic reused in step 4, the cheapest-to-deliver option and the ISDA credit event list are from credit derivatives. The tranche loss attachment and the one-factor copula are from securitisation and insurance-linked securities. The pricing relation that makes a clustered loss earn a premium is from the asset pricing models, and the four-level factor hierarchy the source's third step refers to is from fundamental analysis and accounting ratios, whose interest cover ratio is the covenant tested in step 4 here.

Where this goes next. This closes the valuation module. The credit exposure quantified here is one of the eight risks in risk measurement, where the distinction between expected and unexpected loss becomes the distinction between a provision and a capital charge. The decision to hold credit at all, and whether the spread above expected loss is compensation or a hidden equity exposure, is a portfolio construction question, and the concentration limits that follow from the correlation results belong there too. Credit as an asset class, with its seniority waterfall and its illiquidity, is government and corporate bonds and private debt.

Where the bank sits opposite. This lecture is the investor's version of what a bank does continuously, and the correspondences are exact. The expected loss identity is the A-IRB formula's first term, with probability of default, loss given default and exposure at default modelled separately for precisely the reason step 4 gives, that severity is set by security rather than by the borrower. The gap between spread and expected loss is what capital management allocates capital against, and the unexpected loss it pays for is the tail the capital formula computes. Covenant monitoring and the buyback transfer are why a bank's credit function reviews facilities rather than merely originating them, under credit risk measurement. The correlation parameter that redistributes tranche losses is the same asset correlation the capital formula fixes by regulation rather than letting banks estimate, and the concentration it fails to remove is credit concentration risk. Wrong-way risk on a protection seller is counterparty credit risk, and the refinancing wall of step 4 is the borrower-side mirror of the bank's own liquidity management problem.
