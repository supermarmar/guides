Asset pricing models: three categories that are one equation with three different guesses

The source lists general, absolute and relative pricing models as though they were three theories. They are three choices of the stochastic discount factor, and once that is seen, a single inequality tests all of them at once: the maximum Sharpe ratio a market offers is a lower bound on how volatile the discount factor must be. Applied to the consumption model that forces a risk aversion of at least 20, at which a person would give up 17.03 per cent of consumption to avoid a coin flip.

Step 1

The 30-second version

This lecture opens a different half of the module. Lectures 1 to 7 priced by replication, where the answer is pinned by an arbitrage and the interesting work is finding the assumption that pins it. From here nothing is pinned. Every number is an estimate, every model is a hypothesis, and the reader should adjust accordingly.

The source's taxonomy is general, absolute and relative pricing models, and it notes in passing that the general model's second equation is the stochastic discount factor. That remark is the whole lecture. Price equals the expected value of the discount factor multiplied by the payoff, and every model in the list is a different guess at that discount factor: a constant for risk neutrality, minus the market return for CAPM, a weighted sum of factors for a multi-factor model, marginal utility of consumption for the consumption model.

One rearrangement then does an enormous amount of work. Expected excess return equals minus the covariance with the discount factor divided by its mean, so an asset earns a premium if and only if it pays badly when the discount factor is high. Bounding the correlation at one gives the Hansen-Jagannathan bound: the Sharpe ratio cannot exceed the discount factor's coefficient of variation. That is a testable restriction on any model before a single parameter is estimated, and applied to the consumption model with 2 per cent consumption growth volatility and an equity Sharpe of 0.4 it demands a risk aversion of 20 against a plausible range of 1 to 5.

Two practical findings close the lecture. Factor models exist because a 500-asset covariance matrix needs 125,250 parameters against 3,015 for five factors, and worse, the sample matrix is singular until you have forty-one years of monthly data. And the source's phrase that a factor model "explains historical returns" hides the distinction that matters: explaining is about R-squared, pricing is about alpha, and a model can have a 0.95 R-squared with a 4 per cent alpha, making it an excellent risk model and a failed pricing model.

Feynman test Something is worth what you expect to get from it, weighted by how much you will need it when you get it. That weighting is one number per future state of the world, and every theory of what things are worth is a different guess about that number. There is a shortcut for testing any such guess: if a market offers good returns for the risk, the weighting must swing about a lot, and if your theory says it barely moves, your theory is wrong before you check anything else.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the taxonomy is unmemorable as a list and obvious as a structure. Three categories with five or six named models under them is exam material to be memorised and forgotten. One equation with a slot in it, and the models as competing fillings for the slot, is something you can reconstruct from scratch. That difference matters more in this half of the module than in the last, because there are more models coming.

The second is that the Hansen-Jagannathan bound is the most efficient piece of reasoning in asset pricing and it is almost never taught at this level. It rejects a model using nothing but the market's Sharpe ratio and the model's own implied volatility of the discount factor. No estimation, no regression, no data mining. Knowing that one inequality is worth more than knowing the names of six models.

The third is the explaining-versus-pricing distinction, which is where applied factor work most often goes wrong. A risk model and a pricing model are tested by different statistics, one of which can be measured well and one of which cannot. Detecting a 2 per cent alpha at 95 per cent confidence takes 24 years of data at a 5 per cent residual volatility, so the quantity that decides whether a pricing model works is essentially unmeasurable over any horizon a career allows.

And a fourth. The parameter count explains why the industry is organised the way it is. Factor models are not an intellectual preference over the full covariance matrix; the full matrix is arithmetically unavailable. Every risk system, every optimiser and every attribution report inherits that constraint, and understanding it explains why the same handful of factors appears everywhere.

Step 3

The mental model

One equation, one inequality that tests every filling of it, and two facts about what factor models can and cannot do.

Step 4

The detail

The one equation underneath the taxonomy

The source gives two equations for a general pricing model and notes that the second is the stochastic discount factor. THEY ARE THE SAME EQUATION: P(t) = E[ sum over s of m(t+s) x X(t+s) ] where m = f(data, parameters) Read it as a definition of what a pricing model IS. Every model in the source's taxonomy is a different guess at m, and nothing else about them differs: risk neutrality m = a constant no risk premium anywhere CAPM m = a - b x R(market) only market covariance priced a k-factor model m = a - sum of b_k x f_k k sources of priced risk consumption CAPM m = beta x (C'/C)^(-gamma) risk is bad consumption states Black-Scholes m implied by the replicating portfolio no view needed: it is relative THE SOURCE'S DIVISION INTO ABSOLUTE AND RELATIVE MODELS IS REAL AND WORTH KEEPING, and this framing sharpens it. AN ABSOLUTE MODEL SPECIFIES m FROM FUNDAMENTALS: consumption, macro factors, the market portfolio. A RELATIVE MODEL DOES NOT SPECIFY m AT ALL; it derives whatever m is consistent with the prices of other assets and prices the new asset against those. That is why Black-Scholes needs no view on the expected return of the share, and why the whole of lectures 1 to 7 could proceed without one. AND IT EXPLAINS WHY RELATIVE MODELS ARE MORE RELIABLE AND LESS AMBITIOUS. They inherit their credibility from the prices they are calibrated to and can say nothing about whether those prices are right. An absolute model attempts the harder question and, as the next subsection shows, fails it.

The relation every model must satisfy, and the inequality that follows

For an EXCESS return, which costs nothing today, the pricing equation gives 0 = E[m x R_e] = E[m] E[R_e] + Cov(m, R_e) and rearranging, E[R_e] = -Cov(m, R_e) / E[m] AN ASSET EARNS A PREMIUM IF AND ONLY IF IT PAYS BADLY WHEN m IS HIGH. Since m is high in the states you care most about, the premium is compensation for failing you when it matters. THAT IS THE WHOLE OF ASSET PRICING IN ONE LINE, and every competing model is an opinion about which states those are. Note what it does NOT say. It does not say that risky assets earn more than safe ones. An asset with a high variance but no covariance with m earns NOTHING for it, and an asset that pays well in bad states earns a NEGATIVE premium, which is what insurance is. Now write the covariance as a correlation times two standard deviations: E[R_e] / sigma(R_e) = -rho x sigma(m) / E[m] and since a correlation cannot exceed one in absolute value: THE HANSEN-JAGANNATHAN BOUND: Sharpe ratio <= sigma(m) / E[m] THE HIGHEST SHARPE RATIO AVAILABLE IN A MARKET IS A LOWER BOUND ON HOW VOLATILE THE DISCOUNT FACTOR MUST BE. That is a restriction on ANY model of m, testable before a single parameter is estimated, and it is the most efficient argument in the subject.

What the bound does to the consumption model

The consumption model sets m = beta x (C'/C)^(-gamma), so the log discount factor is a constant minus gamma times log consumption growth, and for small volatilities sigma(m) / E[m] is approximately gamma x sigma(consumption growth) which turns the bound into a direct requirement on risk aversion: gamma >= Sharpe ratio / sigma(consumption growth) Sharpe at 1% vol 2% vol 3% vol 5% vol 0.20 20.0 10.0 6.7 4.0 0.30 30.0 15.0 10.0 6.0 0.40 40.0 20.0 13.3 8.0 0.50 50.0 25.0 16.7 10.0 0.60 60.0 30.0 20.0 12.0 AGGREGATE CONSUMPTION GROWTH HAS A VOLATILITY OF ABOUT 2 PER CENT A YEAR AND EQUITY SHARPE RATIOS RUN AROUND 0.4, WHICH FORCES A RISK AVERSION OF AT LEAST 20. What does that mean behaviourally? Take a fifty-fifty gamble to gain or lose 20 per cent of consumption and ask what certain amount someone would accept instead: gamma certainty equivalent would pay this to avoid the gamble 1 0.9798 2.02% 2 0.9600 4.00% 5 0.9094 9.06% 10 0.8616 13.84% 20 0.8297 17.03% 30 0.8194 18.06% AT A RISK AVERSION OF 1 SOMEONE GIVES UP 2.02 PER CENT TO AVOID THE COIN FLIP. AT 20 THEY GIVE UP 17.03 PER CENT, which nobody observes. The range implied by other evidence is 1 to 5, and running the bound the other way shows how far short that leaves the model: gamma = 1 -> the model can explain a Sharpe ratio of 0.02 gamma = 5 -> 0.10 gamma = 20 -> 0.40 gamma = 50 -> 1.00 A RISK AVERSION OF 5 JUSTIFIES A SHARPE RATIO OF 0.10 AND MARKETS DELIVER FOUR TIMES THAT. THAT IS THE EQUITY PREMIUM PUZZLE. NOTE HOW IT WAS OBTAINED, because the method generalises. Not by fitting the model and finding a poor fit, which invites arguments about the sample and the estimator. By an INEQUALITY that any discount factor must satisfy, applied to a model's own implied volatility. The bound rejects the model without estimating anything, and it does so using two numbers a first-year student could look up.

THE FACTOR MODEL IS NOT A CONVENIENCE, IT IS THE ONLY ROUTE TO A USABLE MATRIX. The source says finding factors is difficult, which is true and understates the position. The choice is not between a factor model and the truth; it is between a factor model and nothing.

The distinction the source blurs: explaining versus pricing

The source says a multi-factor model "explains historical returns". It does, and that is NOT the same as pricing them. Writing the regression: R_i(t) = alpha_i + sum of beta_ik x f_k(t) + e_i(t) EXPLAINING is about the R-SQUARED: how much of the VARIANCE the factors capture PRICING is about the ALPHA: whether the average return is fully accounted for by the exposures THE TWO ARE INDEPENDENT and all four combinations occur: case R-squared annual alpha verdict high R-squared, no alpha 0.95 0.00% a good asset pricing model high R-squared, big alpha 0.95 4.00% great RISK model, failed PRICING model low R-squared, no alpha 0.20 0.00% prices correctly, forecasts risk badly low R-squared, big alpha 0.20 4.00% no use for anything THE SECOND ROW IS THE DANGEROUS ONE AND IT IS COMMON. A model capturing 95 per cent of return variance while leaving 4 per cent a year unexplained will size positions well and value them wrongly, and its high R-squared will be quoted as evidence that it works. AND THE TWO TESTS DIFFER ENORMOUSLY IN FEASIBILITY. R-squared is estimated precisely from a few years of data. Alpha is estimated terribly, because its standard error is the residual volatility divided by the square root of the number of years: residual volatility 5% a year years of data standard error of the estimated alpha 1 5.00% 3 2.89% 5 2.24% 10 1.58% 20 1.12% 50 0.71% TO DETECT A 2% ALPHA WITH 95% CONFIDENCE TAKES 24 YEARS OF DATA. SO THE QUANTITY THAT DECIDES WHETHER A PRICING MODEL WORKS IS THE ONE YOU CANNOT MEASURE OVER ANY USEFUL HORIZON, AND THE QUANTITY YOU CAN MEASURE PRECISELY DOES NOT TEST THE MODEL. That asymmetry is why factor debates are interminable, and why the Hansen-Jagannathan bound is valuable: it tests a model without needing to estimate an alpha at all.

The passive use: how many holdings does an index need?

The source says index tracking means finding assets with the same economic exposure, by synthetic or PARTIAL replication, and stops. The arithmetic of partial replication is exact for an equally weighted index tracked by randomly chosen holdings: tracking error = idiosyncratic volatility x sqrt( 1/n - 1/N ) With a 25 per cent idiosyncratic volatility and an index of 500 equally weighted stocks: holdings n tracking error 5 11.124% 10 7.826% 25 4.873% 50 3.354% 100 2.236% 200 1.369% 300 0.913% 400 0.559% 500 0.000% FOR n WELL BELOW N IT FALLS AS ONE OVER THE SQUARE ROOT OF THE HOLDINGS, so halving the tracking error takes roughly quadrupling the portfolio. NEAR FULL REPLICATION IT FALLS FASTER, because the second term starts to bite: 10 -> 40 holdings: 7.826% -> 3.791%, a factor of 2.06 25 -> 100 holdings: 4.873% -> 2.236%, a factor of 2.18 100 -> 400 holdings: 2.236% -> 0.559%, a factor of 4.00 So the rule of thumb is a rule for SMALL portfolios and understates the gain at the end. THE LAST FEW BASIS POINTS ARE STILL THE EXPENSIVE ONES, because going from 400 to 500 holdings removes the final 0.559 per cent and requires a quarter more lines to trade, rebalance, reconcile and settle, each with its own cost and operational risk. THAT TRADE-OFF IS THE WHOLE OF PARTIAL REPLICATION and the source names the technique without it. ONE ASSUMPTION MATTERS. Random selection is the WORST case. Choosing holdings to match the index's FACTOR EXPOSURES does much better for the same number of lines, which connects the source's two passive uses directly: INDEX TRACKING IS LIABILITY MATCHING WHERE THE LIABILITY HAPPENS TO BE AN INDEX. Both work by matching exposures rather than holdings, and both leave a residual that is exactly the basis risk of lecture 7.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to say is what changed at this lecture, because the module now demands a different kind of reading. Everything up to lecture 7 was priced by replication: build a portfolio that pays what the instrument pays, and the price follows with no view required. The answers were exact and the interesting work was locating the assumption that made them exact. From here the machinery is gone. A factor premium is an estimate, a risk aversion is an assumption, an expected return is a forecast, and no arbitrage stands behind any of it.

THE THREE MODES, AND HOW MUCH TO TRUST EACH. mode lectures what pins the answer free parameters how it fails REPLICATION 1 to 7 an arbitrage portfolio none the assumption behind the arbitrage MODELLING 8 to 10 a specification of m as many as the the specification model has factors is wrong JUDGEMENT 11 to 13 an argument about one unbounded the argument is issuer wrong THE FREE-PARAMETER TEST OF LECTURE 6 SEPARATES THEM CLEANLY. It is the same test throughout, and it is the only reliable guide to how much weight a number deserves. A replication price that is wrong is wrong because someone mis-stated a condition. A model price that is wrong may simply be a different opinion.

The second is that the stochastic discount factor is the most valuable organising idea in the subject and it is almost always introduced too late or not at all. Presented as a taxonomy, asset pricing is a list of models to memorise with no relationship between them. Presented as one equation with a slot, it is a single question, and the models become answers that can be compared on the same terms. The source is one sentence away from this and does not take the step.

The third is the Hansen-Jagannathan bound as a template for a kind of argument worth imitating. Instead of fitting a model and arguing about the fit, derive an inequality the model must satisfy whatever its parameters, then check it. That is far harder to dispute: there is no estimator to quarrel with and no sample to re-select. The same move appears elsewhere in this course. Put-call parity tests a pricing library without testing its calibration. The full-carry ceiling bounds a commodity future without modelling the convenience yield. In each case a bound does work that an estimate cannot.

BOUNDS THAT TEST WITHOUT ESTIMATING, MET IN THIS COURSE. bound what it tests needs no put-call parity a pricing library's arithmetic volatility cap minus floor = the swap option and linear pricing agree any model at all full carry ceiling a commodity future is not the convenience yield arbitrageable Hansen-Jagannathan any model of the discount factor the model's parameters a bond's undiscounted sum a lognormal price model's support a volatility EVERY ONE OF THESE IS CHEAPER THAN AN ESTIMATE AND HARDER TO ARGUE WITH. Building the habit of looking for the bound before building the model is most of what separates a careful quantitative worker from a productive one.

The fourth is the alpha measurement problem, which deserves to be stated as a general limit rather than a technical footnote. Twenty-four years to detect a two per cent alpha at a five per cent residual volatility means that most claims about whether a factor model prices correctly are, in the strict sense, untestable within a working lifetime. That does not make the claims worthless, but it does mean the debate cannot be settled by data alone, and it explains why factor research proliferates: with hundreds of candidate factors and short samples, something will always look significant.

And the fifth, on the two uses the source describes. Active use compares a required return against an expected one; passive use matches exposures rather than holdings. Those look like opposite activities and rest on the same insight, which is that an asset is a bundle of exposures rather than a name. Index tracking is liability matching with an index as the liability. Liability matching is index tracking with a liability as the index. And both leave a residual which is exactly the basis risk of lecture 7, measured by the same square root and just as easily overstated.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The free-parameter test that separates a replication argument from a model is from FX forwards and commodity futures, and the residual measured by a square root, which reappears here as tracking error, is from arbitrage pricing, hedging and the optimal hedge ratio. The relative pricing models named in the taxonomy are the whole of lectures 1 to 7: Black's model, the change of numeraire that makes it work, and the Merton model, which is the clearest example in the course of an absolute model, since it prices debt from a specification of the firm's assets rather than from other debt. Expectation, variance, covariance and regression are from statistics, and utility and risk aversion from economics.

Where this goes next. Lecture 9 takes the dividend discount model used in step 4 as a required-return comparator and treats it properly, including the growth assumption whose fragility step 4 exposed. Lecture 10 covers the empirical characteristics of asset prices, which is where the distributional assumptions behind every model in this taxonomy meet the data, and the source's own list of leptokurtosis, dependent increments and heteroscedasticity is a list of ways the standard specification of m fails. Lectures 11 to 13 move to the third mode, pricing by judgement about a single issuer. Beyond the module, the covariance matrix problem and the factor structure that solves it are the foundation of portfolio construction, the alpha measurement problem is the central difficulty of performance measurement, and factor exposures are how risk measurement aggregates a portfolio it cannot model asset by asset.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank faces the same covariance problem in its trading book and solves it the same way, which is why the standardised approach to market risk is built on prescribed risk factors and prescribed correlations rather than on an estimated matrix: the regulator has made the factor choice because the bank cannot estimate the alternative. The single-factor structure of the A-IRB capital formula is the same device applied to credit, reducing an unmanageable correlation matrix across every borrower to one asset correlation parameter, and the regulatory choice to fix that parameter rather than let banks estimate it is a direct consequence of the estimation problem in step 4. The alpha measurement problem is why credit risk models are validated on discrimination and calibration over short samples rather than on whether they price correctly, and the distinction between a good risk model and a good pricing model is exactly the distinction between a model that ranks borrowers well and one that sets the right spread.
