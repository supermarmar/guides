Fixed income pricing: a floating rate note has two durations, and they differ by a factor of sixteen

The source says a floating rate bond is worth par after a payment date. It is, and only if the issuer's credit spread has not moved since issue. A note paying Jibar plus 100 basis points into a market demanding 250 trades at 94.2225, because only the benchmark half of the coupon floats. The consequence is that the same instrument carries 0.2445 years of interest rate duration and 3.9909 years of spread duration, a distinction a fixed rate bond cannot make at all.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The clean price of a bond excludes interest accrued since the last coupon and the dirty price includes it. The source defines this correctly and the reason for the convention is worth stating: the dirty price sawtooths upward through every coupon period and drops by the full coupon on payment date, entirely mechanically, so quoting it would make two prices on different dates incomparable. The clean price strips the sawtooth out.

Accrued interest is not one number. On the same bond on the same date, actual over 365 gives 3.45822 per 100, actual over actual gives 3.44877 and thirty over 360 gives 3.43750. The clean price is identical under all three and the cash settled is not, by R2,072 on R10m nominal. And in South Africa the JSE's ten-day books-closed period makes accrued interest go negative, because a buyer inside the window will not receive the coming coupon and must be compensated rather than charged.

The source gives the fixed bond price as a sum of cash flows discounted at spot rates and separately introduces the gross redemption yield. Those are two different objects and it is worth being blunt about the difference. On an upward-sloping curve a five-year 9 per cent bond prices at 100.4870 and its redemption yield is 8.8752 per cent, which is neither the five-year spot rate of 9 per cent nor anything on the curve. Change the coupon and the yield changes: zero coupon gives 9.0000 per cent, 20 per cent coupon gives 8.7847. The gross redemption yield is a property of a bond, not of a maturity.

The floating rate results are correct and load-bearing, so they deserve a proof rather than an assertion. A note is worth par at every reset by backward induction, exactly, at any rate level. Between resets it is worth par plus the fixed coupon discounted to the reset, which is the source's formula. But both results assume the discount margin equals the quoted margin, and when it does not the note behaves like a five-year credit instrument.

Feynman test A bond's quoted price pretends the last coupon was paid yesterday, so that prices on different days can be compared; the buyer then pays the seller for the days since. A bond whose coupon resets to whatever rates are is always worth its face value on a reset day, because the payment and the discounting are the same number and cancel. But only the interest rate part resets. The extra bit the borrower pays for being risky was fixed at the start, so if the borrower gets riskier the note falls, exactly as an ordinary bond would.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is the one a portfolio manager meets most often. Floating rate notes are bought to reduce interest rate risk and they do: the rate duration falls from 3.8897 years to 0.2445, a cut of 93.7 per cent. What they do not reduce is credit risk, and the spread duration is 3.9909, marginally longer than the fixed bond's. An investor who reads "floating rate" as "low risk" has halved one exposure and kept the other whole, and in a credit sell-off will discover that the note fell almost exactly as far as the fixed bond would have.

The second is that the gross redemption yield is the most widely quoted and most widely misunderstood number in fixed income. It is an internal rate of return, which means it is a summary of a particular set of cash flows and cannot be transplanted onto another bond. Two bonds maturing on the same day with different coupons have different redemption yields on the same curve, so a curve drawn through redemption yields is not a term structure of interest rates. That is the defect bootstrapping exists to repair, and lecture 2 does the repair.

The third reason is practical and unglamorous. Day count conventions and books-closed periods are the kind of detail that never appears in a model and always appears in a settlement dispute. The R2,072 difference on a single R10m trade is trivial; the same error running unnoticed through a bond book's daily valuation is not, and it is the sort of thing that shows up as an unexplained profit and loss item rather than as an obvious mistake.

And a fourth. Every later lecture in this module discounts something. Getting the discounting conventions right here, once, is what allows lectures 2 to 7 to be about the economics rather than about the arithmetic of dates.

Step 4

The detail

Clean, dirty and accrued

The source's definition is that a clean fixed interest bond price "excludes accrued interest since the date of last coupon". That is exact. The dirty price, which is what actually settles, adds it back.

dirty price = clean price + accrued interest A bond whose CLEAN price does not move at all through a coupon period, 8.25% coupon: days since last coupon accrued dirty price 0 0.00000 100.00000 30 0.67808 100.67808 60 1.35616 101.35616 91 2.05685 102.05685 120 2.71233 102.71233 182 4.11370 104.11370 payment date 0.00000 100.00000 THE DIRTY PRICE RISES BY 4.11 POINTS AND THEN DROPS BY 4.11, and nothing about the bond changed. THAT is why the clean price is the quoted one: it removes a movement that is arithmetic rather than information, so a quote on 15 March and a quote on 15 August mean the same thing. The same logic explains a rule that otherwise looks arbitrary. When a portfolio's return is computed, the ACCRUAL is income and the CLEAN price change is capital. Using dirty prices would attribute the accrual to capital gain for most of the period and then book a large capital loss on the coupon date, which is wrong in both directions and nets to the right answer only over a complete period.

Three day count conventions, three answers

Actual/365 divides actual days by a fixed 365. Actual/actual takes the exact fraction of the actual coupon period, so it is the only one of the three that guarantees the accruals over a full year sum to exactly one coupon. And 30/360 pretends every month has 30 days, which was a computational convenience before computers and survives by inertia in much corporate documentation. WHAT MATTERS IS THAT THE CONVENTION IS PART OF THE INSTRUMENT, not part of the model. South African government bonds use actual/365. Getting it wrong does not produce an obviously silly price; it produces a small settlement difference that reconciles as unexplained profit and loss, which is precisely the kind of error that survives for years.

The South African books-closed period, and negative accrued interest

The buyer owns the bond from settlement but will not receive the coupon covering that ownership, so the buyer must be COMPENSATED for the interest between settlement and the coupon date. The dirty price is the clean price MINUS that rebate. Note the discontinuity: accrued jumps from +3.88767 on 19 September to -0.20342 on 21 September, a swing of 4.09 points, because entitlement to a whole coupon transfers. That is not a market movement and a system that treats it as one will report a spurious four-point loss on the bond twice a year. MOST TEXTBOOKS DO NOT COVER THIS because most textbooks are written for markets without a books-closed convention. It is worth knowing for an ASSA paper specifically, and it is worth knowing generally as an example of a rule that makes a quantity most people believe is always positive go negative.

Pricing off a curve, and what the redemption yield actually is

It is not the five-year spot rate of 9.00%, and it is not any other rate on the curve. It is a cash-flow-weighted average of the whole curve, sitting 12.5 basis points below the five-year spot because the earlier coupons are discounted at the lower short rates. AND IT MOVES WITH THE COUPON. Same curve, same maturity, five bonds: coupon price gross redemption yield 0% 64.9931 9.0000% 5% 84.7119 8.9226% 9% 100.4870 8.8752% 13% 116.2620 8.8368% 20% 143.8683 8.7847% ONLY THE ZERO-COUPON BOND'S YIELD EQUALS A SPOT RATE, and that is definitional rather than a coincidence: with one cash flow the internal rate of return and the discount rate for that maturity are the same object. Every coupon bond's yield is contaminated by the shape of the curve in front of it, and the contamination grows with the coupon because more weight sits in the early years. THE PRACTICAL CONSEQUENCE IS SEVERE. A yield curve drawn through the redemption yields of traded bonds is not a term structure of interest rates; it is a plot of five different internal rates of return that happen to share an axis. Two bonds maturing on the same day can sit 115 basis points apart on it, as the zero and the 20 per cent coupon do here, with no arbitrage available and nothing anomalous happening. That defect is the whole reason bootstrapping exists, and it is lecture 2's subject.

Proving the floating rate result the source asserts

The source states that a floating bond is worth par after a payment date and worth par plus the coupon discounted before one. Both are true and both are worth deriving, because the derivation is where the assumption lives.

THE BACKWARD INDUCTION. At the FINAL reset the note pays the redemption plus the coupon just fixed at the prevailing rate r over a period of length tau: V = (N + N x r x tau) / (1 + r x tau) = N x (1 + r x tau)/(1 + r x tau) = N FOR ANY r WHATSOEVER. The coupon and the discount rate are the same number, so they cancel identically rather than approximately. Now step back one period: the note is worth N at the next reset plus that period's coupon, discounted at that period's rate, which is the same expression again and therefore N again. Induct backwards to issue. verified numerically on a 5-year quarterly note: Jibar value at reset 2.0% 100.000000 5.0% 100.000000 8.0% 100.000000 12.0% 100.000000 20.0% 100.000000 EXACTLY PAR, to six decimals, at rate levels a decade apart. That is the check that the result is an identity and not an approximation. BETWEEN RESETS the coupon is already fixed, so only the discounting period shrinks: coupon fixed at 8.00%, R1.99452 per 100 payable in 91 days days into the quarter days to the reset discount period value 0 91 0.249315 100.000000 30 61 0.167123 100.648859 60 31 0.084932 101.306194 91 0 0.000000 101.994521 Which is the source's P = (N + c)(1 + i)^-t exactly. The note drifts up through the period and snaps back to par at each reset, a sawtooth of the same kind as the dirty price but arising from a different cause.

Where the result breaks, and why

THE INDUCTION ASSUMED THE DISCOUNT RATE EQUALS THE COUPON RATE. For a real note paying a benchmark plus a fixed spread, that holds only if the market demands exactly the spread the note pays. a 5-year quarterly note paying Jibar + 100 bp, Jibar flat at 8%: market requires price discount to par 0 bp 104.0879 +4.0879 50 bp 102.0195 +2.0195 100 bp 100.0000 0.0000 150 bp 98.0281 -1.9719 250 bp 94.2225 -5.7775 400 bp 88.8419 -11.1581 600 bp 82.2345 -17.7655 AT 100 BASIS POINTS THE NOTE IS WORTH EXACTLY PAR, because the QUOTED MARGIN it pays equals the DISCOUNT MARGIN the market demands. Everywhere else it is not. THE REASON IS ONE SENTENCE: ONLY THE BENCHMARK HALF OF THE COUPON FLOATS. A note paying Jibar + 100 bp resets the Jibar every quarter and never resets the 100. That 100 basis points was fixed at issue and is exactly as fixed as a fixed rate bond's coupon, so it is exposed to credit spread movement for the note's whole life. SO THE SOURCE'S "AFTER PAYMENT DATE P = N" IS CORRECT AND CONDITIONAL, and the condition it omits is the one that matters most in practice, because benchmark rates move for everybody while credit spreads move for one issuer. An exam answer that gives the formula should say "assuming the issuer's credit spread equals the note's quoted margin" and will be a better answer for six words.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The habit worth taking from this lecture is to ask, of any quoted number, what it is a property of. A clean price is a property of the bond. A dirty price is a property of the bond and the date. Accrued interest is a property of the bond, the date and a convention chosen by someone else. And a gross redemption yield is a property of one bond's particular cash flows, which is why it cannot be transplanted onto another bond or read as a market rate. Most fixed income confusion is a category error of this kind rather than an arithmetic mistake.

WHAT EACH QUOTED NUMBER IS A PROPERTY OF. quantity depends on safe to compare across clean price the bond and the market bonds, dates dirty price + the settlement date nothing, without adjustment accrued interest + a day count convention nothing, without stating it gross redemption yield + the bond's own coupon bonds with the SAME coupon and maturity, and nothing else spot rate a maturity only everything discount margin the issuer's credit only floaters of any coupon THE LAST TWO ROWS ARE THE USEFUL ONES, and it is not an accident. A spot rate and a discount margin are both constructed to strip out the instrument's own features, which is exactly what makes them comparable and exactly why they must be derived rather than observed. Lecture 2 derives the first. The second is what a credit desk quotes instead of a price.

The second is that the floating rate note is the cleanest demonstration in finance that "risk" is not one thing. The same instrument, valued the same way, has a 0.2445-year exposure to one risk factor and a 3.9909-year exposure to another. A fixed rate bond cannot make that distinction because its yield bundles them, and the bundling is so familiar that people forget it is happening. What the floater does is decompose a bond into its two components and let you hold one without the other, which is a genuine service and is mis-sold about as often as it is used well.

The third is that the source is right far more often than it is wrong, and the failures follow a pattern. Both of the gaps found here, the unstated credit assumption behind the par result and the silent leap between a curve-based price and a single redemption yield, are places where a correct formula is stated without the condition that makes it correct. That is not carelessness; it is what a summary is. The reading skill worth developing is to ask of every stated result what would have to be true for it to hold, and then to check whether anybody said so.

THE TWO GAPS, STATED AS THE CONDITIONS THAT WERE OMITTED. the source says the missing condition what happens without it "floating bond, after payment the discount margin equals the a note paying 100 bp into a date: P = N" quoted margin 250 bp market is worth 94.22 price = sum of cash flows the resulting single yield is a curve through redemption discounted at spot rates, NOT a rate on that curve and yields is not a term and separately, a GRY varies with the coupon structure; 115 bp of spurious spread at one maturity Both formulas are right. Both are unusable by someone who does not know what was assumed.

The fourth is about the unglamorous half. Day count conventions, books-closed periods and settlement dates are not intellectually interesting and they are where money is actually lost, because an error in them is small enough to survive scrutiny and systematic enough to accumulate. The R2,072 on a single trade would never be noticed. The same convention error applied consistently across a bond book's daily valuation for a year is a reconciliation problem nobody can source. A practitioner's respect for these details is not pedantry; it is a memory of having had to find one.

And the fifth, looking forward. Everything in the remaining twelve lectures discounts something, and the discounting conventions established here do not change. What changes is what is being discounted and how the rate is obtained: from a bootstrapped curve in lecture 2, from a no-arbitrage argument in lectures 3 to 7, from a model of risk and return in lectures 8 to 10, and from an analysis of a specific issuer in lectures 11 to 13. The arithmetic of dates is settled. The rest of the module is about the rate.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. Discounting, annuities and the internal rate of return are from financial mathematics, and duration and convexity as sensitivity measures are from financial instruments. On the asset class side, what a bond is, the seniority waterfall and the decomposition of a spread into expected loss and risk premium are from government and corporate bonds, and the South African money market benchmarks that a floating rate note resets against, including the transition from Jibar to ZARONIA, are from money market instruments. The par yield identity that reappears here as the coupon making a bond price at par is from swaps.

Where this goes next. Lecture 2 takes the defect identified in step 4, that a redemption yield is a property of a bond rather than a maturity, and repairs it by bootstrapping a spot curve from traded prices, then derives forward rates from it. Lectures 3 to 5 price interest rate and bond derivatives off that curve, and lecture 5's conversion from yield volatility to price volatility uses the duration relationship established here. Lecture 13 takes the discount margin of step 4 and asks what determines it, which is credit analysis. The spread duration computed here is the exposure that risk measurement has to capture separately from rate exposure, and the choice between fixed and floating in a portfolio built against a liability stream is portfolio construction.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank's loan book is overwhelmingly floating rate and its deposits reprice on different dates, so the mismatch between the two reset profiles is the core of interest rate risk in the banking book, measured through net interest income and economic value of equity. The observation that a floater has almost no rate duration and full spread duration is the same observation that makes a bank's own funding cost the binding constraint in financial management, and it is why funds transfer pricing separates the benchmark from the credit charge rather than quoting one blended rate. A bank issuing floating rate notes is on the other side of the discount margin computed in step 4, and the same margin drives its senior and subordinated issuance costs under capital management. The credit spread widening that moves a floater's price is the market-implied version of what credit risk measurement models directly.
