Hedging and the optimal hedge ratio: a correlation of 0.90 removes 81 per cent of the variance and 56 per cent of the risk

The hedge ratio formula minimises variance, and variance is not what a risk report shows. Because the square root is concave, a hedge that removes 81 per cent of the variance removes only 56.41 per cent of the standard deviation, and you need a correlation of 0.99499 to remove ninety per cent of it. The second finding runs the other way: the better the hedge, the more a sizing error costs, because there is less residual left for it to hide in.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The source gives the optimal hedge ratio as the correlation multiplied by the ratio of the two standard deviations, and calls it optimal without saying in what sense. It is the minimum variance hedge, which is a real answer to a narrow question, and the narrowness is where most of the useful content lives.

The derivation takes two lines and delivers two results. The first is the source's formula, h* = 0.680000 on the worked numbers. The second is what survives: the residual variance is the unhedged variance multiplied by one minus the correlation squared. So the variance falls by the correlation squared. The standard deviation falls by much less, and a risk report shows standard deviations. At a correlation of 0.90 the variance falls 81 per cent and the standard deviation 56.41. At a correlation of 0.70 the variance falls 49 per cent and the standard deviation 28.59, leaving more than seventy per cent of the volatility standing after a hedge most people would call reasonable.

The sensitivity result is the one I did not expect. The variance is quadratic in h, so it is flat near the optimum, and at a correlation of 0.85 a twenty per cent sizing error costs only 10.41 per cent extra variance. But the damage factor is the correlation squared divided by one minus the correlation squared, which explodes: at 0.99 the same twenty per cent error adds 197.01 per cent, almost tripling the residual. The better the hedge, the less forgiving it is, which inverts the natural intuition.

Three practical points the source omits entirely. Contracts are integers, so a R5m book leaves 7.00 per cent of its exposure unhedged to rounding while a R1bn book leaves 0.0250. A futures hedge should be tailed by the discount factor, worth 2 per cent at three months and 33 at five years. And the formula has no opinion on whether to hedge at all: over ten years a 3.5 per cent annual hedging cost is a certain 35 per cent given up to remove 72.25 per cent of a variance.

Feynman test If you cannot buy exactly the thing you own, you buy something similar and hope the two move together. How much of the similar thing depends on how closely they move and how jumpy each one is. What nobody tells you is that removing most of the squared risk removes much less of the risk you actually feel, and that the closer your substitute is to the real thing, the more careful you have to be about the amount, because there is nothing left to absorb a mistake.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that hedge effectiveness is routinely reported as R-squared, which is the correlation squared, and routinely read as though it were the fraction of risk removed. It is the fraction of variance removed, and the two differ by the square root. A committee told that a hedge is 81 per cent effective will not imagine that 43.59 per cent of the volatility remains, and the gap between those two numbers is where unpleasant surprises live.

The second is that the sensitivity result changes how a hedging programme should be governed. If the hedge is loose, the ratio can be estimated roughly and reviewed rarely. If the hedge is tight, small errors in the ratio dominate the residual, so the estimation deserves attention proportional to the quality of the hedge rather than inversely to it. That is the opposite of how effort is usually allocated.

The third is that the formula silently answers a question nobody asked. It takes as given that hedging is desirable and computes the size. Whether to hedge at all requires comparing a certain cost against an uncertain benefit, and lecture 6 established that a currency hedge costs the interest differential with certainty while the risk it removes has historically carried a positive expected return. A tool with no opinion on the prior question is frequently used as though it had one.

And a fourth. Basis risk is the residual this lecture measures, and it is the same quantity that has appeared under other names throughout the course: the CDS-cash basis in derivatives lecture 6, the cross-currency basis in lecture 6 of this module, the mismatch between a linear futures hedge and a concave FRA exposure in lecture 3. Every one of them is a hedge that does not quite fit, and this lecture supplies the arithmetic for how much that costs.

Step 3

The mental model

One minimisation, one result about what survives it, and one surprise about how carefully it must be done.

Step 4

The detail

Deriving the formula, and what "optimal" means

The source gives h = rho x sigma(dS) / sigma(dF) and calls it the optimal hedge ratio. OPTIMAL IN WHAT SENSE? Minimum variance, and the derivation is two lines. Hold one unit of the asset and short h units of the hedging instrument. The change in the hedged position's value is dV = dS - h dF so its variance is Var(dV) = sigma_S^2 - 2 h rho sigma_S sigma_F + h^2 sigma_F^2 Differentiate with respect to h and set to zero: -2 rho sigma_S sigma_F + 2 h sigma_F^2 = 0 -> h* = rho sigma_S / sigma_F and the second derivative is 2 sigma_F^2, which is positive, so it is a minimum rather than a maximum. That matters because the objective is now explicit: THE FORMULA MINIMISES VARIANCE AND NOTHING ELSE, which is the answer to give when someone asks why not simply hedge one-for-one. worked example: sigma(dS) = 0.032, sigma(dF) = 0.040, rho = 0.85 h* = 0.85 x 0.032 / 0.040 = 0.680000

THE GOVERNANCE CONSEQUENCE IS THE OPPOSITE OF USUAL PRACTICE. Effort spent estimating and re-estimating the hedge ratio should RISE with the quality of the hedge, not fall. A loose cross-hedge can be sized roughly and reviewed annually. A near-perfect hedge needs the ratio maintained, because that is where the ratio is doing all of the work.

What the formula cannot see

The minimum-variance hedge minimises variance. It says nothing about expected return, and the two are separate decisions the formula silently merges by answering only one. Suppose hedging costs 3.5 per cent a year in carry, as the currency hedge of lecture 6 did: horizon variance removed certain cost of the hedge 0.25 yr 72.25% 0.88% 1.00 yr 72.25% 3.50% 3.00 yr 72.25% 10.50% 5.00 yr 72.25% 17.50% 10.00 yr 72.25% 35.00% THE VARIANCE REMOVED DOES NOT GROW WITH THE HORIZON. THE CERTAIN COST DOES, LINEARLY. So the trade-off worsens with time, and over ten years a 3.5 per cent annual cost means giving up a certain 35 per cent to remove 72.25 per cent of a variance. Whether that is worth doing depends on the investor's objective, the horizon and whether the risk being removed carries a premium. Lecture 6 supplied the last part: the currency risk has historically carried a positive expected return, so the hedge gives up a premium as well as paying a cost. THE FORMULA ANSWERS ONE QUESTION WELL, WHICH IS HOW MUCH TO HEDGE GIVEN THAT YOU ARE HEDGING. IT DOES NOT ANSWER THE PRIOR QUESTION. A great deal of confusion in hedging programmes comes from using a tool that has no opinion on a question as though it had one, and the fix is simply to separate the two decisions explicitly: WHETHER TO HEDGE IS A MANDATE DECISION AND HOW MUCH IS AN ARITHMETIC ONE.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first habit is to check whether a reported statistic is in the units the decision is made in. Hedge effectiveness is reported as R-squared, which lives in variance, and decisions are made about volatility, drawdowns and capital, which live in standard deviations. The square root between them is not a rounding difference: it turns 81 per cent effective into 43.59 per cent of the risk remaining. The same mismatch appears wherever variance is the natural mathematical object and standard deviation the natural human one, which is most of quantitative finance.

THE SAME UNITS PROBLEM, IN FOUR PLACES. reported in variance experienced as the gap R-squared of a hedge residual volatility sqrt: 81% -> 43.59% left variance contribution a position's felt riskiness concave in the weight diversification benefit drawdown reduction two assets at rho 0.5 cut variance 25%, sd 13.40% tracking error VARIANCE tracking error quoted in sd for good reason THE LAST ROW IS THE ONE THE INDUSTRY GOT RIGHT. Tracking error is universally quoted as a standard deviation, in the units of the thing people care about. Hedge effectiveness is universally quoted as R-squared, in the units of the algebra. There is no reason for the inconsistency other than history.

The second is the sensitivity inversion, which is the most useful thing in the lecture and the least intuitive. A hedge at a correlation of 0.5 barely cares what h is; a hedge at 0.99 cares enormously. The reason is that damage from a sizing error is measured against the residual, and a good hedge has almost no residual to measure against. That reverses where estimation effort should go, and it also explains a pattern practitioners report without explaining: near-perfect hedges that behave badly. They behave badly because they were sized by an estimate that was good enough for a worse hedge.

The third is that the whole apparatus is silent on the question people actually want answered. The formula assumes hedging and computes the amount. That is a legitimate division of labour provided somebody makes the other decision explicitly, and in practice nobody does, because the formula's confident output makes the prior question feel already settled. The discipline is to write down the cost and the expected return of the risk being removed before opening the spreadsheet, and lecture 6 showed those two are large and pull in opposite directions for a currency hedge.

THE TWO DECISIONS, SEPARATED. question which discipline answers it what it needs SHOULD we hedge? mandate, objectives, liabilities the cost of the hedge and the premium being given up HOW MUCH? this formula two volatilities and a correlation the second is arithmetic and takes an afternoon. the first is judgement and is frequently never asked, because the second was answered. A HEDGE RATIO OF 0.68 IS NOT AN ARGUMENT FOR HEDGING. It is an answer conditional on a decision that a formula cannot make.

The fourth is about basis risk as a unifying idea rather than a topic. Every mismatch in this course reduces to the same arithmetic: a CDS spread against a bond spread, an overnight discount curve against a term projection curve, a covered parity forward against a market forward, a linear futures hedge against a concave FRA exposure, a jet fuel cargo against a Brent contract. In each case something almost right is used in place of something exactly right, and the residual is what this lecture measures. Recognising that they are one phenomenon is worth more than remembering five names for it.

And the fifth, closing the arc. Lectures 1 to 7 have built the no-arbitrage machinery from discounting conventions up to hedging, and the recurring finding has been that the arbitrage-free relations are exact and the practical relations are not. A bond prices exactly off a curve; the curve between nodes is a choice. A swap values exactly off two legs; which curves build them is a convention. A forward is pinned exactly by parity; enforcing the parity costs balance sheet. And a hedge ratio minimises variance exactly; the variance is not the risk and the ratio is estimated. THE MATHEMATICS IS EXACT AND THE APPLICATION IS APPROXIMATE, and the size of the approximation is what the second half of every one of these lectures has been about.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. Variance, covariance and correlation are from statistics, and the regression interpretation of the hedge ratio, where h* is the slope coefficient and rho squared the R-squared, is the same estimator. Basis risk was first quantified in forwards and futures, which also established the contract rounding problem that reappears here at scale. Daily settlement, which forces the tailing adjustment, is from derivative markets and produced the convexity adjustment in pricing interest rate derivatives. The distinction between a notional and a risk measure is from swap and swaption valuation, where the annuity rather than the notional is the quantity managed. The certain cost of a currency hedge and the premium it gives up are from FX forwards and commodity futures, which supplies the half of the hedging decision this formula cannot see, and the CDS-cash basis that is the credit market's version of the same residual is from credit derivatives.

Where this goes next. This closes the fixed income and no-arbitrage arc. Lectures 8 to 10 turn from pricing by arbitrage to pricing by a model of risk and return, where nothing is pinned down by a replication argument and every answer depends on assumptions of the kind this arc has been careful to isolate; the free-parameter test of lecture 6 is the cleanest way to see the difference. Lecture 8's taxonomy makes it explicit. Beyond the module, the residual measured here is the tracking error that performance measurement reports and the basis risk that risk measurement must capture, and the decision of whether to hedge at all, as opposed to how much, belongs to portfolio construction and to the mandate that governs it.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank hedging its own exposures faces every problem in this lecture and one more: hedge accounting. Under IFRS 9 a hedge must be shown to be effective before the accounting treatment follows the economics, and effectiveness is assessed on exactly the R-squared this lecture warns about, so a hedge can qualify for hedge accounting while leaving 43.59 per cent of the volatility in place. The residual after hedging is basis risk in the banking book, measured under interest rate risk in the banking book, and in the trading book it attracts capital under market risk, where the standardised approach charges explicitly for basis between closely related risk factors precisely because a hedge is never exact. The cross-hedging of a commodity or currency exposure taken as loan collateral is credit risk with a market risk residual, which is why collateral haircuts are set above the volatility of the collateral rather than at it.

Numbers used here. The optimal hedge ratio formula is from the F105 summary chapter 9, stated there as a result with no derivation and no statement of what is being optimised; the treatment follows Hull, Options, Futures and Other Derivatives, chapter 3.
