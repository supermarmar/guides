Yield curves and bootstrapping: three interpolations that calibrate identically and price differently

The source says a zero-coupon curve is built from bond prices with linear interpolation between values. Interpolation of what? Linear in spot rates, linear in discount factors and linear in log discount factors all reprice the original bonds to machine precision, and they disagree by R17,265 on an eighteen-month R10m cash flow and by 25 basis points on a three-and-a-half-year forward rate. The first of the three invents two downward steps in a forward curve the data says should rise.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Lecture 1 ended on a defect: the gross redemption yield is a property of a bond rather than of a maturity, so a curve drawn through redemption yields is not a term structure. Bootstrapping is the repair. Starting from the shortest bond and working outward, it solves for the discount factor at each maturity that reproduces the observed price, and the result is a set of spot rates each attaching to one date and one cash flow.

From five par bonds at 7.00, 7.80, 8.40, 8.80 and 9.00 per cent, the bootstrap gives spot rates of 7.0000, 7.8314, 8.4752, 8.9165 and 9.1360 per cent, and one-year forward rates of 7.0000, 8.6694, 9.7744, 10.2512 and 10.0183. All five bonds then reprice at exactly 100, which is the arbitrage-free condition the source refers to. On this rising curve forward exceeds spot exceeds par yield at every term, and on an inverted curve the ordering reverses exactly, so the ranking is a fact about the slope rather than about the measures.

The interpolation question is where the source is underspecified rather than wrong, and it matters more than it looks. All three natural choices agree at the nodes and therefore calibrate identically, so nothing in the fitting process reveals the difference. Everything priced off the curve between nodes differs: an eighteen-month cash flow by R17,265 per R10m, and the forward rate for the six months from 3.5 to 4.0 years by 25 basis points, worth R125,028 on R100m.

Two results close the loop. The par yield recomputed from the bootstrapped discount factors is (1 − DF(n)) divided by the annuity, which reproduces the input par yields to seventeen decimal places and is the same formula as the swap rate from derivatives lecture 4, so the par curve and the swap curve are one object. And when a maturity is missing, the bootstrap is far more forgiving than its recursive structure suggests: a 10.1 basis point error propagates to the next spot rate at 8.1 per cent of its size, but it corrupts the forward rates on either side of the gap by 30 and 34 basis points.

Feynman test A bond's yield mixes up rates for every date it pays money on, so you cannot read a rate for a single date off it. Bootstrapping unpicks the mixture: use the shortest bond to find the rate for its one date, then the next bond, which now has only one unknown left, and so on. What is left over is a rate for each date. You then have to guess the rates for the dates in between, and the honest thing to notice is that every sensible guess fits the bonds you started with equally well and gives different answers for everything else.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the curve is the single input everything else in this module consumes. Lectures 3 to 5 price interest rate derivatives, bond forwards and bond options off it. Lecture 7 hedges with it. A portfolio's duration, an insurer's liability discount rate and a bank's funds transfer price are all readings from it. An error in the curve is not one mispricing, it is every mispricing at once, which is why the construction deserves more attention than its arithmetic warrants.

The second is that interpolation is a real modelling decision disguised as a technicality. Nothing in the calibration diagnostics will ever flag it: all three methods reproduce every input price exactly, so the residuals are zero under all of them. The choice is invisible where you look and material where you do not, which is the worst combination a model assumption can have. Anyone who has inherited a curve library has inherited someone else's answer to this question, usually undocumented.

The third is that the difference between spot, par and forward rates is the most common source of confused conversation in fixed income. A treasurer quoting "the five-year rate" may mean the par yield of 9.00 per cent, the spot of 9.1360 or the forward of 10.0183, and on this curve those span more than a hundred basis points. Knowing which one a counterparty means, and which one a formula wants, is most of the skill.

And a fourth. The forward rate is a difference between neighbouring points on the curve, which means it magnifies whatever error is in those points. Both findings in step 4 make the same structural observation: the levels are robust and the differences between levels are not. That generalises well beyond curve building, and it is the reason a model that fits observed prices well can still produce nonsense hedges.

Lecture 1 showed the redemption yield varies with the coupon on a fixed curve, so it cannot be a term structure point. The par yield and the forward rate are both DERIVED from the spot curve, exactly and without extra information. Which sets up the logical order of everything that follows: observe prices, bootstrap spots, derive everything else. The source lists the four measures side by side as if they were alternatives. They are not alternatives; three of them are consequences of the first.

The bootstrap

Five annual-coupon bonds trading at par, so the coupon equals the par yield in each case: 1 year 7.00% 2 years 7.80% 3 years 8.40% 4 years 8.80% 5 years 9.00% A par bond satisfies c x [DF(1) + ... + DF(n)] + DF(n) = 1 and every DF before n is already known from the shorter bonds, so rearranging leaves one unknown: DF(n) = (1 - c x sum of the earlier DFs) / (1 + c) year 1: DF = (1 - 0.0700 x 0.000000) / 1.0700 = 0.934579 -> spot 7.0000% year 2: DF = (1 - 0.0780 x 0.934579) / 1.0780 = 0.860021 -> spot 7.8314% year 3: DF = (1 - 0.0840 x 1.794601) / 1.0840 = 0.783444 -> spot 8.4752% year 4: DF = (1 - 0.0880 x 2.578045) / 1.0880 = 0.710599 -> spot 8.9165% year 5: DF = (1 - 0.0900 x 3.288644) / 1.0900 = 0.645892 -> spot 9.1360% THE VERIFICATION IS THE POINT OF THE EXERCISE. Repricing each input bond off the bootstrapped discount factors gives 100.0000000000 in all five cases. That is what "arbitrage free" means operationally: the curve reproduces the prices it was built from, so no combination of the input bonds can be traded against the curve for a certain profit. NOTE WHAT THE BOOTSTRAP DOES NOT NEED. No model, no volatility, no view. It is pure arithmetic on observed prices, which is why it is the right first step and why anything model-based should be reconciled against it.

Forward rates, and an ordering that is about the slope

THE SPOT EXCEEDS THE PAR YIELD because a par bond pays coupons early, at the lower short rates, which drags its single-rate summary below the pure long rate. Same mechanism as lecture 1's finding that the redemption yield falls as the coupon rises. THE FORWARD EXCEEDS THE SPOT because a spot rate is a geometric average of every forward up to that date, and the average of a rising series always lies below its latest term. NOW INVERT THE CURVE and the whole ordering reverses: year par yield spot rate 1-year forward 1 11.0000% 11.0000% 11.0000% 2 10.2000% 10.1595% 9.3254% 3 9.6000% 9.5161% 8.2406% 4 9.2000% 9.0825% 7.7919% 5 9.0000% 8.8699% 8.0234% SO THE RANKING IS NOT A FACT ABOUT THE MEASURES, IT IS A FACT ABOUT THE SLOPE. Memorising "forwards are highest" is memorising a special case. The general statement is that the forward curve is the marginal quantity, the spot curve is its running average, and the par curve is the coupon-weighted version of the spot curve, so the three fan out in whichever direction the marginal rate is moving. Note the five-year forward of 8.0234 per cent against a five-year par yield of 9.00 per cent on the inverted curve. AN INVERTED CURVE IMPLIES FUTURE SHORT RATES BELOW ANYTHING CURRENTLY QUOTED, which is why an inversion is read as a cut expectation rather than as a term premium story. Whether the market is forecasting or demanding compensation is the ambiguity flagged in derivatives lecture 2 and again in lecture 6, and it is not resolvable from the curve.

Where the source is underspecified: interpolation of what?

The source says the zero-coupon curve is "constructed from prices of FI bonds, linear interpolation between values". Bootstrapping gives values at the maturities that trade, and a curve must price a cash flow on any date, so something has to fill the gaps. Three natural choices exist and the source does not say which.

What the undocumented choice costs

An eighteen-month zero-coupon cash flow of R10,000,000, which falls exactly between two bootstrap nodes: linear in spot rates R8,982,520.42 linear in discount factors R8,973,002.96 linear in log discount factors R8,965,255.64 SPREAD R17,264.77 (17.3 bp of the notional) And the forward rate for the six months from 3.5 to 4.0 years, on R100m: linear in spot rates 10.2129% linear in discount factors 10.2512% linear in log discount factors 10.0011% SPREAD 25.0 bp = R125,028 for the six-month period THE FORWARD IS THE WORSE CASE AND IT IS NOT A COINCIDENCE. A discount factor at 1.5 years depends on the interpolation once. A forward rate between 3.5 and 4.0 years depends on it twice, and takes a RATIO of the two, so the errors do not cancel and can compound. WHICH IS WHY THIS IS A MODELLING DECISION RATHER THAN A DETAIL. Every derivative priced in lectures 3 to 5 sits on a forward rate. A firm that changed its interpolation convention would revalue its FRA book by roughly 25 basis points of forward rate without a single market price moving, and no calibration report would show anything different.

The par yield is the swap rate

Reading the par yield back off the bootstrapped curve rather than observing it: par(n) = (1 - DF(n)) / [DF(1) + DF(2) + ... + DF(n)] term recomputed par yield input par yield difference 1 7.000000% 7.000000% 0.00e+00 2 7.800000% 7.800000% 2.78e-17 3 8.400000% 8.400000% 0.00e+00 4 8.800000% 8.800000% 1.39e-17 5 9.000000% 9.000000% 0.00e+00 IDENTICAL TO SEVENTEEN DECIMAL PLACES, which closes the loop: the par yields the curve was built from are recovered from the curve they generated. It is not new information, it is a consistency check, and a bootstrap that fails it has a bug. AND THAT FORMULA IS THE SWAP RATE OF DERIVATIVES LECTURE 4, CHARACTER FOR CHARACTER. The reason is structural rather than coincidental: a PAR BOND exchanges a fixed coupon stream for par at maturity, and costs par today a SWAP exchanges a fixed coupon stream for a floating leg, which lecture 1 of this module proved is worth par, and costs nothing today Same fixed leg, same thing received, so the same fixed rate makes each fair. THE PAR YIELD CURVE AND THE SWAP CURVE ARE ONE OBJECT constructed from different instruments, which is why they are quoted interchangeably and why a divergence between them is a genuine dislocation rather than a definitional artefact.

What happens when a maturity is missing

Suppose only the 1, 2, 4 and 5-year bonds trade. The four-year equation is c4 x [DF(1) + DF(2) + DF(3) + DF(4)] + DF(4) = 1 with TWO unknowns. One equation, two unknowns: undetermined. Something must be assumed. HOW BADLY DOES THE ASSUMPTION CONTAMINATE THE REST? The sensitivity is analytic: DF(4) = (1 - c4 x [DF1 + DF2 + DF3]) / (1 + c4) so d DF(4) / d DF(3) = -c4 / (1 + c4) = -0.0880 / 1.0880 = -0.0809 AN ERROR IN DF(3) REACHES DF(4) AT 8.1 PER CENT OF ITS SIZE, WITH THE SIGN FLIPPED. The recursion is heavily DAMPED rather than amplified, because the mis-stated discount factor enters the next equation only through the coupon annuity, weighted by the coupon rate of 8.80 per cent rather than by one. Numerically, assuming a linear spot between years 2 and 4: year assumed / implied spot true bootstrapped error 3 8.3740% 8.4752% -10.1 bp 4 8.9233% 8.9165% +0.7 bp 5 9.1416% 9.1360% +0.6 bp A 10.1 BASIS POINT ERROR AT THE GAP LEAVES LESS THAN A BASIS POINT ANYWHERE ELSE. That is the opposite of the usual warning about recursive methods, and it is worth stating plainly: THE BOOTSTRAP IS SELF-CORRECTING, because every subsequent bond price is an observed constraint that pulls the curve back onto the market. BUT LOOK WHERE THE DAMAGE ACTUALLY LANDS: period true forward with the gap assumed error 2 to 3 yr 9.7744% 9.4673% -30.7 bp 3 to 4 yr 10.2512% 10.5882% +33.7 bp THE SPOTS ARE ALMOST RIGHT AND THE FORWARDS ARE BADLY WRONG, in opposite directions, by roughly thirty times the spot error. Same structural lesson as the interpolation section: a forward is a DIFFERENCE between neighbouring points, so it magnifies exactly the errors that a comparison of levels conceals. ANYONE PRICING AN FRA OR A CAPLET OFF A BOOTSTRAPPED CURVE IS TRADING THE DERIVATIVE OF THE THING THAT WAS CALIBRATED, and the derivative is where the uncertainty lives.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The habit this lecture is really teaching is to distinguish what a model was fitted to from what it is used for. A bootstrapped curve is fitted to five bond prices and used to price a cash flow at eighteen months, a forward rate at three and a half years, and a caplet on a rate that never appears in any input. The fit is exact and says nothing whatever about those three uses, because the data does not constrain them. Every diagnostic you have will look perfect, and it will look equally perfect under three interpolations that disagree by R17,265.

WHAT THE INPUTS CONSTRAIN AND WHAT THEY DO NOT. quantity constrained by the input bonds? discount factors at 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 YES, uniquely the par yields at those maturities YES, they were the input a discount factor at 1.5 years NO, three answers spanning R17,265 per R10m a forward rate from 3.5 to 4.0 NO, three answers spanning 25 bp the instantaneous forward anywhere NO, and the three methods do not even agree on whether it rises or falls THE RULE THAT FOLLOWS: BEFORE TRUSTING A NUMBER FROM A CALIBRATED MODEL, ASK WHETHER ANY OBSERVED PRICE WOULD HAVE MOVED IF THAT NUMBER HAD BEEN DIFFERENT. If not, the number is the modeller's assumption wearing the market's clothes.

The second is that differences are always noisier than levels, and the curve makes this concrete twice over. The interpolation choice moves an eighteen-month discount factor by 17 basis points of notional and a three-and-a-half-year forward by 25 basis points of rate. A missing maturity moves the neighbouring spots by less than a basis point and the neighbouring forwards by thirty. In both cases the level is robust and the difference between levels is not, because differencing amplifies whatever error is present while averaging suppresses it. Anyone whose job involves hedges rather than valuations is working entirely in differences.

The third is that the bootstrap's recursive structure is much less dangerous than it sounds, and it is worth being precise about why. The intuition that "errors compound in a recursive method" is the right worry and the wrong conclusion here, because each step brings a fresh observed price. The damping factor is exactly minus the coupon rate over one plus the coupon rate, 8.1 per cent on this curve, so an error decays by an order of magnitude per step and flips sign. That is not luck; it is because a bond's price is dominated by its redemption payment, and the earlier discount factors enter only through the coupon annuity.

WHY THE DAMPING FACTOR HAS THE FORM IT DOES. DF(n) = [1 - c x (DF(1) + ... + DF(n-1))] / (1 + c) the earlier discount factors enter multiplied by c (a small number, here 8.8%) the unknown enters multiplied by (1 + c) (a number just above 1) so the transmission of any earlier error is c / (1 + c) coupon rate damping factor error surviving 3 steps 2% 1.96% 0.00075% 9% 8.26% 0.056% 15% 13.04% 0.222% 30% 23.08% 1.229% A HIGH-COUPON CURVE PROPAGATES ERROR MORE, because more of each bond's value sits in the coupon stream and therefore in the previously-solved discount factors. A zero-coupon curve propagates none at all, which is the degenerate case where every bond determines its own discount factor independently. THE BOOTSTRAP IS SAFEST EXACTLY WHERE IT IS LEAST NEEDED.

The fourth is a point about the source's presentation rather than its accuracy. It lists spot, redemption, par and forward rates as four items in a bulleted taxonomy, which invites a reader to memorise four definitions. The useful structure is a hierarchy: prices are observed, spot rates are extracted from prices, and par and forward rates are computed from spot rates by formula. Once that hierarchy is clear, the relationships between the four are derivable rather than memorable, and the ordering that reverses with the slope stops being a fact to remember and becomes a consequence to notice.

And the fifth. The identity between the par yield curve and the swap curve is the kind of result that looks like a coincidence and is a structural fact, and it is worth carrying because it connects two markets that are usually taught separately. A par bond and a swap deliver the same fixed leg against the same value, so they must fix the same rate. When government par yields and swap rates diverge, therefore, one of the two is not what it appears to be, and in practice the divergence is a measure of the difference between government credit and interbank credit rather than a curve-building error. That is the swap spread, and it is a traded instrument in its own right.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The defect this lecture repairs, that a gross redemption yield is a property of a bond rather than of a maturity, is from fixed income pricing, along with the discounting conventions used throughout and the proof that a floating leg is worth par, which the swap identity in step 4 relies on. The swap rate as the par yield, and both valuation routes for a swap, are from swaps. The warning that a forward rate is a market price rather than a forecast is from forwards and futures and recurs in credit derivatives as the bootstrapped hazard curve, which is the identical construction applied to default probabilities instead of interest rates. Discounting and internal rates of return are from financial mathematics.

Where this goes next. Lecture 3 prices FRAs and interest rate futures off the forward rates derived here, and the convexity adjustment it introduces is a second reason a futures rate and a forward rate differ, on top of the interpolation ambiguity found in step 4. Lecture 4 values swaps and swaptions using the same annuity that appears in the par yield formula. Lectures 5 and 6 discount bond and currency forwards off this curve. Lecture 7's optimal hedge ratio is a statement about differences rather than levels, which is why the amplification result in step 4 matters there specifically. The choice of curve on which to discount a liability stream is a portfolio construction decision with a much larger effect than most asset allocation choices, and the model risk created by an undocumented interpolation convention is one of the exposures risk measurement has to own.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank runs this construction daily and everything downstream consumes it: the discount curve for interest rate risk in the banking book, where economic value of equity is the present value of every future cash flow and therefore inherits every interpolation choice made here; the forward curve that sets the benchmark leg of funds transfer pricing, which is why an FTP rate can be argued about without either side being wrong; and the valuation curve for the trading book under market risk, where the interpolation convention is a recognised model risk requiring validation and, in some jurisdictions, a prudent valuation adjustment. The self-correcting property found in step 4 is also why a bank can bootstrap a usable curve from a thin government bond market, which matters in jurisdictions where few maturities trade.

Numbers used here. The four yield measures, the description of bootstrapping as recursive and starting with the shortest bond, the arbitrage-free requirement, and the statement that a zero-coupon curve is built from bond prices with linear interpolation are all from the F105 summary chapter 9. One gap in the source is addressed rather than corrected: it specifies linear interpolation without specifying of what, and step 4 shows the three natural readings calibrate identically and price differently. Everything numerical is invented for illustration: the par yields of 7.00 to 9.00 per cent, the inverted set of 11.00 to 9.00, the R10m and R100m notionals, and the removal of the three-year bond. The arithmetic is exact and internally checked three ways: every input bond reprices at 100.0000000000 off the bootstrapped factors, the par yields recomputed from those factors match the inputs to within 3 times ten to the minus seventeen, and all three interpolations agree with the bootstrapped discount factors at the nodes to machine precision.
