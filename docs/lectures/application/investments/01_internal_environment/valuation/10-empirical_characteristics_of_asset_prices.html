Empirical characteristics of asset prices: four facts that are really two, with magnitudes attached

The source lists fat tails, dependent increments, time-varying volatility and a weak inflation correlation as four separate characteristics. Time-varying volatility causes fat tails: a market that is normal at 12 per cent volatility nine days in ten and normal at 30 per cent otherwise has an unconditional kurtosis of 6.1999 with no fat-tailed ingredient anywhere. And each characteristic is given without a magnitude, which is the difference between knowing the list and being able to use it.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Every model in the last two lectures assumed something about the distribution of returns. This lecture is where those assumptions meet the data, and the source's four-item list is correct on all four counts. What it does not supply is a single magnitude, and without magnitudes a list of characteristics cannot tell anyone to change anything.

Leptokurtosis first. A five standard deviation daily move is a once-in-6,922-year event under a normal distribution and a once-in-4.6-year event under a t with six degrees of freedom, so the fat-tailed model makes it 1,511 times more likely. The October 1987 fall of about 22 per cent against a 1 per cent daily volatility is a 22 sigma move whose normal waiting time exceeds the age of the universe by a factor of ten to the ninety-four.

The source's remark that longer horizons look more normal is exactly right and exactly quantifiable: for independent increments the excess kurtosis of a sum divides by the number of periods, so a daily excess kurtosis of 6.0 becomes 0.0238 at a year. But that requires independence, which the source's own next two bullets deny. The four characteristics are in tension with one another, which is the most interesting thing about the list.

Dependent increments break the square root of time. The variance ratio converges to one plus the autocorrelation over one minus it, so a lag-one autocorrelation of minus 0.10 makes long-run volatility 90.45 per cent of the square-root figure. That is the entire time diversification argument and it is smaller than its reputation. And on the last characteristic, at a correlation of 0.15 equities remove 1.13 per cent of the volatility of an inflation-linked liability against 68.78 for an inflation-linked bond, a ratio of sixty to one.

Feynman test Markets are calm most of the time and wild occasionally. If you average the calm and the wild together you get a distribution with more extreme days than a bell curve allows, and people call that a fat tail, but nothing is fat: you have blurred two ordinary pictures into one strange one. The practical difference matters. A fat tail says be permanently more careful. Two regimes say be relaxed now and very careful later, and knowing which you are in is the whole job.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that every model in lectures 1 to 9 rests on one of these assumptions and none of them stated it as an empirical claim. Black's model assumed lognormality. The square root of time appeared in the convexity adjustment, in hedge tailing and in every volatility scaling. The dividend discount model assumed a constant discount rate. This lecture is where those get their error bars.

The second is the causal link the source's list conceals. Presenting fat tails and volatility clustering as two characteristics invites two separate fixes, when fitting a conditional volatility model reproduces the fat tails for free. A practitioner who fits a t-distribution to unconditional returns has described the symptom; one who models the volatility process has described the mechanism, and only the second knows whether today is calm or stressed.

The third is that the prescriptions differ, not just the descriptions. A fat-tailed unconditional model says risk is constant and large. A regime model says risk is small now and large later. On the worked numbers a 99 per cent one-day value at risk is R1,758,554 in the calm regime and R4,396,384 in the stressed one, and managing to the blended R2,171,654 is too cautious in one and far too relaxed in the other.

And a fourth. The inflation result is the one with the largest practical consequence for a South African fund. Equities are routinely held against inflation-linked liabilities on the argument that they are a real asset, and the arithmetic says that hedge removes about one per cent of the volatility. Lecture 7 supplied the formula; this lecture supplies the correlation that makes it damning.

The mental model

Four stated characteristics, one causal link between two of them, and a magnitude for each.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to take is that a list of stylised facts is not knowledge until each item carries a magnitude. The source's four characteristics are all correct and none of them is actionable, because "returns are fat-tailed" is compatible with abandoning Black-Scholes entirely and with adding a two per cent buffer, and only the number distinguishes them. That generalises well beyond this lecture: whenever a model assumption is described as violated, the useful next question is by how much, and the answer is often smaller than the tone of the criticism suggests and occasionally very much larger.

THE FOUR CHARACTERISTICS, RANKED BY HOW MUCH THEY SHOULD CHANGE YOUR BEHAVIOUR. characteristic magnitude change your behaviour? low inflation correlation removes 1.13% of the volatility YES: the hedge does not work leptokurtosis 5-sigma tail understated 1,511x YES, in the tail; the centre of the distribution is fine heteroscedasticity calm and stressed VaR differ by YES: a single number is wrong a factor of 2.5 in both regimes dependent increments 10-year volatility out by 9.55% MARGINALLY: real, small, and hard to estimate reliably THE LAST ROW IS THE ONE MOST OFTEN OVERSOLD. Time diversification is invoked to justify large equity allocations for long-horizon investors, and the arithmetic supports under a ten per cent reduction in long-horizon volatility at an autocorrelation nobody can measure precisely. It is a real effect and it is not a licence.

The second is the causal finding, which changes what to build rather than what to believe. Fat tails and volatility clustering are one phenomenon, so the modelling choice is not between a fat-tailed distribution and a conditional volatility model as two alternatives; it is between describing the symptom and describing the mechanism. Only the mechanism tells you where you are today, and knowing whether the market is in the 12 per cent regime or the 30 per cent regime is worth more than knowing that the blend has a kurtosis of 6.20.

The third is that the source's four items constrain one another and the source cannot say so. The central limit theorem delivers normality at long horizons if increments are independent; the second and third characteristics say they are not. A list format presents facts as additive when they are in tension, and the tension is where the content is. That is a general weakness of revision summaries and a general opportunity for anyone reading one carefully: look for the two bullets that cannot both be fully true.

WHERE THE FOUR CHARACTERISTICS PULL AGAINST EACH OTHER. claim A claim B the tension longer horizons look more normal increments are dependent the CLT needs independence longer horizons look more normal volatility varies over time the CLT needs identical distribution too returns are fat-tailed volatility varies over time B CAUSES A, so they are not two pieces of evidence equities are a long-run real asset returns are measured quarterly the hedge horizon exceeds the measurement horizon FOUR ITEMS, FOUR TENSIONS, AND NONE OF THEM VISIBLE IN A BULLETED LIST.

The fourth is the inflation result, which is the most consequential thing here for a South African institution and the least mathematical. A fund holding equities against inflation-linked liabilities on the grounds that equities are a real asset has, on a 0.15 correlation, hedged 1.13 per cent of the volatility. The instrument that does the job removes 68.78. The defence, that equities track inflation over decades, is true and irrelevant, because the fund is measured quarterly and pays monthly. Two true statements about different horizons are routinely used as though one supported the other.

And the fifth, closing the arc. Lectures 8, 9 and 10 have moved from models to their inputs to the data those inputs are drawn from, and the direction of travel has been consistently downward in confidence. A stochastic discount factor is a clean idea; the models that specify it fail a bound derived from two lookup numbers. A dividend discount model is exact arithmetic; its output moves by a factor of two on a percentage point. And the distribution everything assumes is wrong in four documented ways, two of which are the same way. THE HONEST SUMMARY OF THIS HALF OF THE MODULE IS THAT THE MACHINERY IS SOUND AND THE INPUTS ARE NOT, which is exactly the opposite of the first half, where the inputs were market prices and the interesting question was what the machinery assumed.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. Moments, kurtosis, autocorrelation and mixture distributions are from statistics. The hedging arithmetic used in step 4 to price the inflation correlation, where variance removed is the correlation squared and volatility removed is one minus the square root of one minus it, is from arbitrage pricing, hedging and the optimal hedge ratio. The lognormality assumption this lecture tests is Black's, from pricing interest rate derivatives, where it was found to fail outright at negative rates and is here found to fail in the tail as well; the square root of time appears there as the convexity adjustment and in bond options and the Merton model as the growing tail that crosses the bond's ceiling. The constant discount rate and constant growth of the dividend discount model are the assumptions the heteroscedasticity result contradicts. The empirical failure of a diffusion to generate short-dated credit spreads, met as the credit spread puzzle, is the same fat-tail problem in the credit market.

Where this goes next. Lectures 11 to 13 move to the third mode of valuation, judgement about a single issuer, where these distributional facts matter less because the analysis is not statistical. Beyond the module, the regime result is the case for conditional volatility modelling in risk measurement, where value at risk is computed daily and the choice between an unconditional and a conditional estimate is the largest single methodological decision. The inflation result is a portfolio construction conclusion: an inflation-linked liability needs an inflation-linked asset, and no equity allocation substitutes for one. And the dependence of realised volatility on the measurement horizon is why performance measurement must state the frequency of the returns it reports.

Where the bank sits opposite. Every finding here is a supervisory issue. The tail understatement is why the Basel framework moved market risk capital from value at risk to expected shortfall, which averages over the tail rather than reading a single quantile from a distribution whose shape is wrong there. The regime finding is why banks run stressed calibrations alongside current ones rather than a single blended history, and why a stress testing framework is a separate discipline from value at risk rather than an extension of it. The square-root-of-time scaling is embedded in the liquidity horizons of the market risk framework, and the dependence result says those scalings are approximations even before any modelling choice is made. And the mismatch between the horizon at which a hedge works and the horizon at which risk is measured is the core of interest rate risk in the banking book, where economic value of equity and net interest income measure the same exposure over different horizons and can disagree in sign.

Numbers used here. The four characteristics, leptokurtosis with a peak at the mean and fat tails becoming more normal over longer horizons, dependent increments with non-linear variance growth and long-run mean reversion, heteroscedastic returns with volatility rising in recessions, and a small correlation between equity growth and inflation making equities not a real asset, are all from the F105 summary chapter 9, and all four are correct as stated. Two things the source does not supply are added in step 4: a magnitude for each, and the causal relationship by which time-varying volatility generates the fat tails listed separately. The approximate fall of 22 per cent on 19 October 1987 is a matter of record; the 1 per cent daily volatility it is scaled against is a round figure chosen for the illustration. Everything else numerical is invented for illustration: the daily excess kurtosis of 6.0, the autocorrelations from minus 0.20 to plus 0.20, the 12 and 30 per cent volatility regimes, the R100m portfolio, the 18 per cent annual volatility, and the four assumed correlations with inflation, of which the 0.15 for equities is at the optimistic end of the short-horizon evidence rather than a measured figure for any particular market. The arithmetic is exact: the t tail probabilities are computed by numerical integration of the standardised density, the mixture kurtosis is closed form and verified against the degenerate case where the two volatilities are equal and the kurtosis returns exactly 3, and the 22 sigma probability is computed in logarithms because it underflows double precision.
