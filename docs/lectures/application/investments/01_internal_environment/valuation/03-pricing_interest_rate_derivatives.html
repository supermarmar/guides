Pricing interest rate derivatives: the conditions of use the formulas do not carry

The source states the FRA, futures, cap and floor formulas correctly and says nothing about when they may be used. Two omissions are expensive. The convexity adjustment separating a futures rate from a forward rate reaches 47.5 basis points at eight years and costs R1.23m in present value across a ten-year strip. And Black's model, applied where both the forward rate and the strike are negative, does not fail: it returns a negative option premium and carries on.

Valuation 3 of 13 Reading time, 60 minutes Sources: F105 summary chapter 9; Hull, Options, Futures and Other Derivatives, chapters 28 to 29
Step 1

The 30-second version

This module's index flags this lecture as a gap, and the reason is precise: the source lists five interest rate derivative formulas without a derivation or a condition of use between them. The formulas are right. What is missing is everything that tells you whether they apply, and in two cases the omission is worth more than the formula.

The forward rate agreement is the easy one. On a curve with continuously compounded zeros of 8.00 and 8.15 per cent at one and 1.25 years, the three-month forward is 8.8464 per cent as a simple rate, and an FRA struck there is worth exactly zero, which is what defines a forward rate. Struck at 8.50 per cent on R100m it is worth R78,213 to the borrower.

The convexity adjustment is where the source states a result and skips both its derivation and its sign. A rate future settles daily and its quoted price is 100 minus the rate, so a long the price is short the rate and is disadvantaged in both directions: losses are funded at the new higher rate and gains reinvested at the new lower one. The futures rate must therefore be set above the forward rate. At an absolute short-rate volatility of 1.2 per cent the gap is 0.90 basis points at one year and 47.52 at eight, growing with the product of the two maturities, so it very nearly quadruples every time the maturity doubles. Across a forty-contract strip hedging a ten-year swap it is worth R1,226,912 in present value.

Black's model for caps and floors rests on three assumptions the source does not state, and two of them fail routinely. Lognormality of the forward rate broke for the euro, franc, krone, krona and yen from 2014, and the failure mode is worse than a crash: where forward and strike are both negative the formula completes and returns a negative premium. Constant volatility fails always, and pricing the same cap on a humped caplet volatility curve rather than a flat 20 per cent moves it by 15.12 per cent.

Feynman test Two contracts can promise the same rate and still be worth different amounts, because one pays up daily and the other waits. The one that pays daily hurts you at exactly the wrong moments, so it has to offer a slightly better rate, and the gap grows roughly with the square of how far away it is. Separately, the standard formula for an interest rate option assumes rates cannot be negative. When they became negative the formula did not break loudly; it quietly produced prices below zero, which is the kind of failure nobody notices until someone trades on it.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that a formula without its conditions of use is a trap rather than a tool. Every result in this lecture is stated correctly by the source and every one of them has a domain outside which it silently misleads. That is not a criticism of a revision summary, which is doing its job by being short. It is a statement about what has to be added before the formula can be used, and about what an examiner is likely to probe, since the conditions are exactly the part a candidate who has memorised the formula will not have.

The second is that the convexity adjustment is the cleanest example in the syllabus of a difference between two instruments that look identical. A futures strip and a series of FRAs cover the same periods at the same rates, and treating one as a hedge for the other without adjustment builds in an error that grows geometrically with maturity. Twenty-four basis points of average error on a rate quoted to a quarter of a basis point is ninety-six ticks, which is not a rounding difference.

The third is that the negative rate episode is the most instructive model failure of the last two decades and it is still poorly taught. Rates went below zero across five currencies and stayed there for years, and an entire market's quoting convention had to be rebuilt. The lesson is not that Black's model is bad; it is that an assumption which had been treated as a harmless technical convenience turned out to be a binding constraint, and that the profession had no plan for it.

And a fourth, following on from lecture 2. That lecture found the interpolation choice invisible in calibration and material in pricing. This one finds the volatility assumption invisible in an at-the-money quote and material in the wings, and the lognormality assumption invisible until rates cross zero. The pattern is now three for three, and it is the reason model validation exists as a discipline separate from model building.

The convexity adjustment: derivation of the sign, and the magnitude

The source states that the continuous futures rate equals the continuous forward rate plus half sigma squared times T1 times T2, and stops. Neither the direction nor the size is discussed, and both matter.

WHY THE FUTURES RATE MUST BE HIGHER, in three steps. 1. A futures position is settled DAILY. A forward is settled ONCE, at maturity. So the futures holder's cash flows arrive spread through the contract's life rather than in a lump at the end. 2. For a rate future the quoted price is 100 minus the rate, so BEING LONG THE PRICE IS BEING SHORT THE RATE. Now trace both directions: rates RISE -> the futures price falls -> the long LOSES -> and must FUND that loss at the new, HIGHER rate rates FALL -> the futures price rises -> the long GAINS -> and can only REINVEST that gain at the new, LOWER rate THE LONG IS DISADVANTAGED IN BOTH DIRECTIONS. This is not a probabilistic argument about which way rates go; it is a statement that the timing of the cash flows is systematically unhelpful whichever way they go. 3. To compensate, the futures price must be set BELOW the corresponding forward price. Since price is 100 minus rate, a lower price is a HIGHER RATE. THEREFORE futures rate = forward rate + a positive adjustment. THE MAGNITUDE, at an absolute (normal) short-rate volatility of 1.2 per cent: T1 T2 adjustment in 25 bp ticks 0.25 yr 0.50 0.09 bp 0.00 1.00 yr 1.25 0.90 bp 0.04 2.00 yr 2.25 3.24 bp 0.13 4.00 yr 4.25 12.24 bp 0.49 6.00 yr 6.25 27.00 bp 1.08 8.00 yr 8.25 47.52 bp 1.90 10.00 yr 10.25 73.80 bp 2.95 AT THE FRONT OF THE CURVE IT IS GENUINELY NEGLIGIBLE, under a tenth of a tick inside a year. At eight years it is 47.52 basis points, nearly two ticks, and impossible to ignore. THE GROWTH RATE IS THE PART WORTH INTERNALISING. Because the adjustment scales with the PRODUCT T1 x T2, and T2 is only a quarter longer than T1, it grows almost exactly as the square of maturity: doubling 1 year to 2 multiplies the adjustment by 3.60 doubling 2 years to 4 multiplies it by 3.78 doubling 4 years to 8 multiplies it by 3.88 doubling 8 years to 16 multiplies it by 3.94 APPROACHING FOUR FROM BELOW. So a habit formed at the short end, where the adjustment is correctly ignored, becomes a geometrically growing error when carried to the long end. AND IT SCALES WITH THE SQUARE OF VOLATILITY, which makes the volatility assumption load-bearing rather than decorative: sigma 0.6% -> 8-year adjustment 11.88 bp sigma 1.2% -> 8-year adjustment 47.52 bp sigma 2.4% -> 8-year adjustment 190.08 bp A factor of four in volatility is a factor of sixteen in the adjustment. Anyone quoting a convexity-adjusted rate to a quarter of a basis point is asserting a volatility they have almost certainly not defended to that precision.

Black's model, and the three conditions the source omits

The source gives the caplet as N x tau x DF x [Rf x PHI(d1) - Rk x PHI(d2)] and the floorlet as its mirror. Both are right. The derivation requires three things: (a) THE FORWARD RATE IS LOGNORMALLY DISTRIBUTED at the caplet's expiry (b) ITS VOLATILITY IS CONSTANT over the caplet's life (c) EXPECTATIONS ARE TAKEN UNDER THE MEASURE in which the T2-maturity zero-coupon bond is the numeraire Condition (c) is the technical one and it holds by construction: choosing that numeraire is precisely what makes the forward rate a martingale and lets the discount factor come outside the expectation as a simple multiplier. It is why the formula looks like Black-Scholes with a discount factor bolted on the front, and it is the part most worth knowing and least likely to cause trouble. Conditions (a) and (b) are the ones that fail. FIRST, THE RELATION THAT NEEDS NONE OF THEM. On a flat 9 per cent continuously compounded curve, every quarterly forward is 9.1020 per cent. Where constant volatility fails, which is always

Assumption (b) says the forward rate's volatility is constant. A real caplet volatility curve humps at one to three years and decays afterwards, because short rates are pinned by policy, medium rates carry the most policy uncertainty, and long rates are anchored by expectations. Pricing the same five-year cap at a 9.25 per cent strike two ways: flat 20 per cent volatility everywhere R3,668,164.89 a humped caplet volatility curve R4,222,821.65 DIFFERENCE R 554,656.76 (+15.12%) FIFTEEN PER CENT, ON THE SAME CONTRACT, FROM AN ASSUMPTION THE FORMULA DOES NOT MENTION. WHICH IS WHY THE MARKET QUOTES A SEPARATE FLAT VOLATILITY FOR EACH CAP MATURITY. A quoted cap volatility is not a property of the underlying rate; it is the single number that reprices THAT cap given the true term structure of caplet volatilities. Calibrating a flat volatility to the humped curve at four maturities: 1-year cap -> flat quoted volatility 17.2937% 2-year cap -> flat quoted volatility 21.5441% 3-year cap -> flat quoted volatility 23.3643% 5-year cap -> flat quoted volatility 22.8542% THE FLAT VOLATILITY RISES AND THEN FALLS, because it is an average of the hump over a lengthening window. TWO FLAT VOLATILITIES AT DIFFERENT MATURITIES ARE AVERAGES OF DIFFERENT THINGS AND CANNOT BE COMPARED. A trader saying "three-year caps are richer than one-years, 23.4 against 17.3" has said nothing about relative value; they have described the shape of the caplet curve. STRIPPING is the operation that inverts this: recovering the caplet volatility curve from a set of quoted flat cap volatilities, so that a caplet can be priced consistently with every cap in the market. It is the volatility analogue of lecture 2's bootstrap, with the same structure and the same weakness, namely that the answer between quoted maturities depends on an interpolation nobody documents.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to carry is a taxonomy of how a model fails, because this lecture contains one of each kind and they demand different defences. Black's model with a negative forward and a negative strike fails silently, returning a number that is wrong. Black's model with mixed signs fails loudly, raising an error. The constant volatility assumption fails quietly but consistently, by fifteen per cent in the same direction every time. And the convexity adjustment is not a failure at all but an omission, correct arithmetic applied to the wrong instrument. Only one of those four is caught by testing.

FOUR WAYS TO BE WRONG, AND WHAT CATCHES EACH. failure mode example here what catches it LOUD Black with mixed-sign F and K any test case, immediately SILENT AND WRONG Black with F and K both < 0 nothing, unless someone checks that a premium is non-negative SILENT AND BIASED flat vol against a humped calibration to market prices, caplet curve, +15.12% if market prices exist OMISSION using a futures rate where a only knowing the instruments forward rate belongs, 47.5 bp differ in the first place THE ORDER IS BY DANGER, NOT BY SIZE. The loud failure is harmless because it announces itself. The 47.5 basis point omission is the largest number on the page and is fixable by anyone who knows to look. THE SILENT NEGATIVE PREMIUM IS THE DANGEROUS ONE, because the only defence is a sanity check somebody has to have thought to write.

The second is that the convexity adjustment is a specific instance of a general principle: whenever two instruments differ only in the timing of their cash flows, and the cash flows are correlated with the rate at which they are financed, the two cannot carry the same price. Daily settlement against terminal settlement is the version here. Margined against unmargined is the version in derivatives lecture 1. Collateralised against uncollateralised is the version that produced the multi-curve framework after 2008, where a single reference rate stopped being sufficient. In each case the wedge is second order in a small quantity and grows with maturity, and in each case it was ignored until it became too large to ignore.

The third is a point about the negative rate episode that generalises well beyond it. Lognormality was chosen for interest rates by analogy with equities, where a price genuinely cannot go below zero, and the analogy was never examined because it cost nothing to accept. It cost nothing for thirty years and then it cost an entire market's quoting convention. The lesson is not to distrust lognormality; it is that an assumption inherited by analogy carries the analogy's domain of validity with it, and nobody checks the domain of a convention.

THE ASSUMPTION AND WHAT IT WAS BORROWED FROM. equities a share price cannot be negative, because limited liability caps the loss at the investment. lognormal is a STRUCTURAL fact. interest rates a rate can be negative, because a lender may rationally accept a small certain loss over the cost of holding physical cash, and a central bank may set the policy rate below zero deliberately. lognormal was an ANALOGY, and the barrier at zero was never a real barrier. WHAT MADE IT SURVIVE was that the two are indistinguishable while rates are comfortably positive. A model assumption that only bites in a region you have never visited is indistinguishable from a correct one until you visit it.

The fourth is about the quoting convention itself, which repays attention because it encodes what the market thinks is stable. Rates were quoted in lognormal volatilities while everyone believed the zero barrier, and moved to normal volatilities once it fell. Equity options are still quoted in lognormal volatilities, and will be while limited liability holds. Commodities sit between the two, quoted lognormally but with negative prices having actually occurred in oil futures in April 2020. A quoting convention is a claim about the shape of the distribution, and reading it that way tells you what a market believes about its own tails.

And the fifth, closing the loop with lecture 2. That lecture found an interpolation choice invisible in calibration and worth R17,265. This one finds a volatility surface assumption invisible at the money and worth fifteen per cent, and a distributional assumption invisible above zero and worth an unbounded amount below it. Three lectures, three assumptions that were undetectable exactly where anyone would have looked for them. That is not a coincidence about fixed income; it is the definition of a modelling assumption, which is a statement the data cannot contradict. The discipline that follows is to enumerate them explicitly, price the range they span, and report the range rather than the point.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The forward rates this lecture prices against are bootstrapped in yield curves and bootstrapping, which also established that a forward is a difference between curve points and therefore amplifies whatever error the levels contain, a warning that compounds with everything here. The discounting conventions and the proof that a floating leg is worth par are from fixed income pricing. On the derivatives side, the FRA settlement convention and the proof that its payoff is concave are from forwards and futures, which is what makes the convexity adjustment necessary rather than optional; the daily settlement machinery and the margin argument are from derivative markets; the asymmetry between an option's holder and its writer is from options; and the cap as a strip of caplets, the parity relation verified in step 4, and the flat volatility caution are from swaptions, caps, floors and collars, where the simplification was flagged and is here quantified at 15.12 per cent.

Where this goes next. Lecture 4 values swaps and swaptions, using the same annuity and the same parity structure verified here. Lecture 5 takes Black's model from a rate to a bond price, which needs the conversion from yield volatility to price volatility, and applies it to the Merton model where a firm's debt becomes a short put. Lecture 7's optimal hedge ratio is the formal answer to the question this lecture raises informally, namely how to hedge one instrument with another that is not quite the same. The model risk enumerated in step 5 is an exposure in its own right for risk measurement, and the choice of volatility surface used to value a liability hedge is a portfolio construction decision.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank hedging its banking book with futures rather than FRAs carries exactly the 47.5 basis point wedge computed here, and the mismatch between a linear hedge and a concave exposure is one of the basis risks that interest rate risk in the banking book has to measure rather than assume away. The trading book that warehouses caps and swaptions sits under market risk, where the volatility surface is itself a risk factor attracting capital, and where the failure modes in step 5 are the subject of formal model validation and, for illiquid surfaces, a prudent valuation adjustment. A bank selling a cap to a corporate borrower is short a strip of options priced off assumptions the client cannot see, which makes the conditions of use a conduct matter as well as a pricing one. The wider point that instruments differing only in cash flow timing must differ in price is the same argument that produced multi-curve discounting after 2008, covered in the international regulatory framework alongside the clearing mandate.
