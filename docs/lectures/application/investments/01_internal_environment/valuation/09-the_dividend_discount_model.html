The dividend discount model: an exact formula that transmits every error in its inputs

Step 1

The 30-second version

The source gives the dividend discount model as the present value of future dividends, with the note that the simplified version assumes growth is below the discount rate. Both halves are right and the second is doing far more work than its phrasing suggests.

That condition is the convergence condition of an infinite geometric sum. Above it the series diverges, and the closed form quietly returns a negative number that looks like a valuation. A dividend discount model reporting a negative value has not discovered a worthless company; it has been handed an impossible input.

The model's defining property is its elasticity. Differentiating gives a sensitivity of value to growth equal to the growth rate divided by the gap between the required return and growth. At 2 per cent growth against a 12 per cent required return that elasticity is 0.20 and the model is docile. At 10 per cent growth it is 5.00, and one extra percentage point of growth doubles the value. The formula is exact and it transmits input error rather than damping it.

Three findings follow. In a two-stage model with a five-year explicit forecast, 75.97 per cent of the value sits in the terminal value, so the part everyone argues about is the minority of the answer. Growth is worth nothing unless return on equity exceeds the required return: at ROE equal to r the value is 83.33 whatever the retention ratio. And the source's definition, taken literally, values a company that returns all its cash by buyback at zero, which the total payout correction repairs, raising the worked company from 5,000 to 10,000.

Feynman test A share is worth the cash it will hand you, discounted. If that cash grows at a steady rate for ever, the sum collapses to next year's payment divided by the gap between what you demand and what it grows at. The whole difficulty is that gap: it is the difference between two guesses, so when it is small, tiny changes in either guess move the answer enormously. And growing is only worth something if the money kept back earns more than you could have earned with it yourself.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this is the workhorse of equity valuation and its failure mode is not obvious. It fails by being confidently precise. Every input is plausible, the arithmetic is exact, and the answer can be out by a factor of two because one assumption moved by a percentage point. Knowing the elasticity in advance turns that from a surprise into a stated uncertainty.

The second is that the terminal value share exposes a misallocation of effort that is nearly universal. Analysts build five-year forecasts line by line and choose a terminal growth rate in a meeting, when the forecast determines 24.03 per cent of the answer and the meeting determines 75.97. Effort should follow influence, and it usually follows familiarity instead.

The third is the growth result, which contradicts an intuition almost everybody holds. Growth is not a virtue. A company retaining everything at a return below its cost of equity destroys value while its earnings rise, and a model fed that growth rate will report a high valuation. Value comes from the spread between return on equity and the required return, and growth simply multiplies whatever that spread is, including when it is negative.

And a fourth. Inverting the model is more useful than running it. A price implies a growth rate, and a growth rate can be tested against a constraint the model cannot see: a company growing faster than the economy eventually becomes the economy. That converts an unfalsifiable valuation into a checkable claim.

NOTE THE CONTRAST WITH THE FIRST HALF OF THIS MODULE. A bond price is a sum of discounted certain cash flows and is well conditioned: a one per cent error in the yield moves the price by roughly the duration. Here a one per cent error in growth can move the price by a hundred per cent. Same discounting, radically different conditioning, because a perpetuity's denominator is a difference.

The correction the source's definition needs

The source says the model discounts future DIVIDENDS. Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first habit is to compute a model's elasticity before trusting its output. Every valuation is a function of inputs, and the useful question is not whether the arithmetic is right but whether the function amplifies or damps. A bond price damps: a one per cent yield error moves the price by roughly the duration. A perpetuity amplifies without limit as growth approaches the discount rate. Same discounting, opposite conditioning, and the difference is visible in one derivative that takes a line to compute.

CONDITIONING, ACROSS THIS MODULE. valuation sensitivity to a 1% input error conditioning a five-year bond price about 4% for a 1pp yield move WELL conditioned a swap's mark-to-market linear in the rate, via the DV01 WELL conditioned a caplet at the money roughly proportional to volatility WELL conditioned Gordon at g = 2%, r = 12% +11.11% for +1pp of growth acceptable Gordon at g = 10%, r = 12% +100.00% for +1pp of growth BADLY conditioned Gordon at g = 11%, r = 12% infinite for +1pp unusable THE FIRST THREE ARE THE REPLICATION HALF OF THIS MODULE AND THE LAST THREE ARE THIS LECTURE. That is not a coincidence: a replication price is anchored to observable prices, and a perpetuity is anchored to nothing but two forecasts and their difference.

The second is that effort should follow influence and almost never does. Three quarters of a two-stage valuation sits in the terminal value, and terminal values are set by convention in a meeting while explicit forecasts are built line by line over weeks. The correct allocation is the reverse, and the reason it does not happen is that the explicit forecast feels like analysis while the terminal assumption feels like an admission of ignorance. It is an admission of ignorance, and it is also three quarters of the answer.

The third is the growth result, which is worth stating as flatly as possible because it contradicts a nearly universal intuition. GROWTH IS NOT A VIRTUE. A company that retains earnings and reinvests below its cost of equity destroys value while its reported earnings rise, and every growth-based valuation fed that growth rate will call it cheap. What creates value is the spread between the return on reinvested capital and the required return; growth is a multiplier on that spread, and multiplying a negative number by a larger factor makes it worse.

THE SAME POINT IN FOUR SETTINGS, ALL MET IN THIS COURSE. setting what looks like value what is actually value equity valuation earnings growth ROE minus the required return a bank's loan book asset growth spread minus expected loss minus the cost of capital an insurer's new business premium growth the new business margin a fund management business assets under management growth fee minus the cost to serve IN EVERY CASE THE VISIBLE QUANTITY GROWS AND THE VALUABLE QUANTITY IS A SPREAD. A bank growing its book at 20 per cent while lending below its risk-adjusted cost is the same arithmetic as the 8 per cent ROE company above, and it fails the same way: slowly, while looking successful.

The fourth is the inversion, which converts an unfalsifiable exercise into a checkable one. Running a dividend discount model forwards produces a number that cannot be tested against anything. Running it backwards produces the market's implied growth rate, which CAN be tested, against nominal GDP growth, against the company's return on equity, against its history. The general form is worth carrying: when a model has too many free inputs to validate, invert it and validate the implied input instead. That is what the credit triangle did in derivatives lecture 6 and what the implied volatility surface does for options.

And the fifth. The buyback correction started as a definitional pedantry and ended as an asset allocation conclusion. Taken literally, the source's definition understates the worked company by half, and applied to a whole market it turns an implied equity risk premium of positive 150 basis points into negative 50. Definitions that look harmless in a formula have a habit of surfacing as strategic conclusions, and the discipline is to test every definition against the case it was not written for. A firm that pays no dividends is exactly that case.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. Discounting and geometric series are from financial mathematics. The required return this model discounts at is produced by the factor models of asset pricing model taxonomy and multi-factor models, which also used this model as its step 2 comparator and flagged the fragility of its step 3 price comparison; that fragility is derived properly here. The technique of inverting a model to recover an implied input, then testing the input rather than the output, is the credit triangle of credit derivatives applied to equity. On the asset class side, what a share is and what dividends and buybacks represent are from listed equity, and the equity risk premium computed in step 4 is the quantity the asset class framework uses to rank asset classes.

Where this goes next. Lecture 10 covers the empirical characteristics of asset prices, which is where the constant-growth and constant-discount-rate assumptions of this model meet the evidence that returns are leptokurtic, autocorrelated and heteroscedastic. Lecture 11 on fundamental analysis takes the inputs this model treats as given, earnings, retention and return on equity, and asks how to forecast them from accounts, so it is this lecture's missing first half. Lecture 13 uses the same discounting apparatus for credit rather than equity. Beyond the module, the implied equity return from step 4 is the central input to strategic asset allocation in portfolio construction, and the elasticity result is why a liability valuation's discount rate is regulated rather than left to judgement.

Where the bank sits opposite. The spread-not-growth result is the whole of bank value creation. A bank growing its loan book while lending below its risk-adjusted cost of capital is the eight per cent return on equity company of step 4, and the arithmetic is identical: see the business model for how a bank earns its spread and capital management for the return on equity that has to clear the hurdle. The dividend discount model is also how a bank's own equity is valued, and the retention ratio in step 4 is the same quantity a bank negotiates with its supervisor when setting a dividend policy against its capital plan, since retained earnings are the cheapest source of common equity tier 1. The terminal value problem reappears in IFRS 9 impairment modelling, where lifetime expected credit loss on a long-dated exposure is dominated by assumptions about the far future that no forecast reaches.
