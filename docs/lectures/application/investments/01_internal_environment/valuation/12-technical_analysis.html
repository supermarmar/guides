Technical analysis: a testable claim, an internal contradiction, and one method that survives

The source lists "high profit chance" as an advantage and "high loss's chance" as a disadvantage. Those are not two findings, they are one description, and the description is high variance, which a fair coin delivers by betting bigger. What decides the question is the cost hurdle the source lists third of three: a weekly rule at a half per cent round trip must earn 26.00 per cent a year gross to match doing nothing.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The source defines technical analysis unusually well: estimating the future price from past prices, volumes or yields. Naming the information set exactly is what makes the claim testable, and it puts technical analysis in a different category from everything else in this module. It is not a philosophy of valuation. It is a direct test of weak-form market efficiency, and the two statements are the same statement.

The source's own advantage and disadvantage lists contain a contradiction. "High profit chance" and "high loss's chance" appear on opposite sides of the ledger as though they offset. They do not offset; they are the same fact. A strategy with no edge whatsoever can be given both simultaneously by staking more, which raises the chance of a 20 per cent gain and the chance of a 20 per cent loss together while leaving the expected return at exactly zero.

What actually decides the question is the item listed third of three disadvantages. A rule trading N times a year at a round-trip cost c needs N times c of gross return simply to break even, so a weekly rule at 0.50 per cent costs 26.00 per cent a year. With symmetric two per cent wins and losses the break-even hit rate is 62.50 per cent rather than 50, and five percentage points of hit rate is worth 10.40 per cent a year.

And the evidence cannot settle it either, because of selection. Testing 100 rules that have no edge at all produces a best t-statistic of about 2.37 and an 89.99 per cent chance that one clears the two-sigma bar. A single moving-average crossover family with plausible parameter ranges contains 8,526 distinct rules, whose best pure-noise t-statistic is 3.698. The source's third method, relative strength, is the exception: cross-sectional momentum has genuine support, and it appeared as a factor in lecture 8.

Feynman test If you try enough patterns on enough charts, one of them will have worked, and it will look convincing because you did not count the ones that did not. Meanwhile every time you act on a pattern you pay to buy and pay to sell, so a rule that fires weekly has to be right about three times in five just to stand still. The one exception is dull: shares that have gone up recently tend to keep going up a bit, across hundreds of shares at once, and even that barely survives the dealing costs.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this is the only claim in the module that is decidable by evidence. A dividend discount model cannot be refuted by data, because its inputs are forecasts. A factor model can be tested but needs 24 years to detect a 2 per cent alpha. Technical analysis names its information set precisely, which makes it falsifiable, and a falsifiable claim in finance is rare enough to be worth handling carefully rather than dismissively.

The second is that the cost arithmetic generalises far beyond charting. Any active strategy faces the same hurdle, and the hurdle scales with turnover rather than with conviction. That is the reason low-turnover active management can add value where high-turnover active management usually cannot, and it applies to a fundamental analyst who trades weekly exactly as it applies to a chartist.

The third is the selection problem, which is the single most useful statistical idea for reading any backtest, marketing document or research paper. The expected best result from a hundred worthless rules looks significant, and nobody reports how many rules they tried. Knowing the size of that effect changes how much a reported t-statistic is worth, and the answer is usually much less than its face value.

And a fourth. Being fair about momentum matters more than being sceptical about chartism. Lumping all three methods together as discredited would be wrong, and the source lumps them together as equivalent, which is wrong in the other direction. The interesting position is between the two: one method has survived out-of-sample testing across markets and decades, and even that one is marginal after costs.

Step 4

The detail

What the claim is, stated precisely

The source: estimate the future price from PAST PRICES, VOLUMES OR YIELDS, in contrast to fundamental value. That is an unusually clean definition and it is what makes the claim testable, because it names its information set exactly. A market is WEAK-FORM EFFICIENT if prices already reflect all information contained in past prices. So: technical analysis works if and only if markets are NOT weak-form efficient THOSE ARE THE SAME STATEMENT. Technical analysis is not an alternative philosophy of valuation; it is a hypothesis about one specific information set, and it is the only one of the three analytical approaches in this module that can be settled by evidence rather than by argument. CONTRAST WITH FUNDAMENTAL ANALYSIS, which uses public information beyond prices and therefore tests SEMI-STRONG efficiency. Note the asymmetry between the two: if weak-form efficiency fails then semi-strong efficiency fails too, since past prices are public information. SO TECHNICAL ANALYSIS WORKING WOULD IMPLY FUNDAMENTAL ANALYSIS CAN WORK, BUT NOT THE REVERSE. The technical claim is the stronger one and therefore the easier to refute.

The contradiction in the source's own list

advantages: data easy to collect, quick to execute, timing decision helped, HIGH PROFIT CHANCE disadvantages: distracts from long-term value, HIGH LOSS'S CHANCE, active strategy costs A HIGH CHANCE OF PROFIT AND A HIGH CHANCE OF LOSS ARE NOT TWO FINDINGS. THEY ARE ONE DESCRIPTION, AND THE DESCRIPTION IS HIGH VARIANCE. To see that this carries no information about skill, take a strategy with no edge at all, a fair coin over 100 bets, and vary only the stake: stake per bet P(up at least 20%) P(down at least 20%) expected return 0.5% 0.00% 0.00% 0.00% 1.0% 2.28% 2.28% 0.00% 2.0% 15.87% 15.87% 0.00% 4.0% 30.85% 30.85% 0.00% 8.0% 40.13% 40.13% 0.00% BOTH COLUMNS RISE TOGETHER AND THE EXPECTED RETURN NEVER MOVES. So a strategy can be given a high profit chance and a high loss chance simultaneously by doing nothing more sophisticated than betting bigger, WHICH IS AVAILABLE TO ANYONE AND IS NOT AN ANALYTICAL TECHNIQUE. The source places the two on opposite sides of a ledger as though they offset. They do not offset, because they are the same fact, and splitting one fact across two columns makes a high-variance strategy look balanced. THE TEST TO APPLY TO ANY SUCH PAIR: if both items could be produced by changing the position size alone, neither is a statement about the method.

The cost hurdle, which decides almost everything

The source lists "active strategy costs" as one disadvantage among three. It is not one among three; it is the binding constraint, and it is arithmetic rather than judgement. A rule trading N times a year at a round-trip cost of c needs N times c of GROSS return every year merely to break even against a portfolio that does nothing. round-trip cost frequency 0.20% 0.35% 0.50% 0.80% 1.50% quarterly (4) 0.80% 1.40% 2.00% 3.20% 6.00% monthly (12) 2.40% 4.20% 6.00% 9.60% 18.00% fortnightly (26) 5.20% 9.10% 13.00% 20.80% 39.00% weekly (52) 10.40% 18.20% 26.00% 41.60% 78.00% twice weekly(120) 24.00% 42.00% 60.00% 96.00% 180.00% daily (250) 50.00% 87.50% 125.00% 200.00% 375.00% A WEEKLY RULE AT A HALF PER CENT ROUND TRIP MUST GENERATE 26.00 PER CENT A YEAR GROSS TO MATCH DOING NOTHING. Against a long-run equity return of perhaps 12 per cent, the rule must more than triple the market's own return before the investor sees a single rand. AND THE SOURCE LISTS "QUICK TO EXECUTE" AS AN ADVANTAGE. The arithmetic turns that advantage into the disadvantage: the easier a rule is to act on, the more often it is acted on, and the cost scales linearly with the acting. The two entries are not independent items on a list; one is the mechanism by which the other operates. NOTE WHAT FREQUENCY CANNOT DO. At a 0.50 per cent round trip the required edge PER TRADE is 0.500 per cent whether the rule fires four times a year or 250 times, because every trade must beat its own cost. Frequency cannot help a rule clear the hurdle; it can only multiply the hurdle by the number of attempts.

The break-even hit rate

A rule with an average win W, an average loss L, a hit rate p and a round-trip cost c breaks even when p x W - (1 - p) x L = c. The cost moves the bar by twelve and a half percentage points, and a rule that is right three times in five loses money. Note the fifth and sixth rows. A rule with small frequent losses and large rare wins breaks even at a hit rate of 30 to 37 per cent, which is why trend-following systems are built to be wrong most of the time. THE BREAK-EVEN HIT RATE IS NOT A PROPERTY OF SKILL; IT IS A PROPERTY OF THE PAYOFF SHAPE, and quoting a hit rate without the payoff shape says nothing. The sensitivity is severe. So the whole question is whether a chart pattern moves the hit rate by a few percentage points, which is a small effect measured in noisy data, and that is precisely where the next section becomes decisive.

Why a backtest of a trading rule cannot be believed

The source says mechanical trading rules remove the subjective element. THEY REMOVE IT FROM THE EXECUTION AND NOT FROM THE SELECTION, and selection is where the problem lies: somebody chose which rule to run, usually after looking at the data. Take M rules with no edge whatsoever, each producing a t-statistic that is a standard normal. At a thousand rules the best looks like 3.12 and even the three-sigma bar is cleared 74.10 per cent of the time. The probabilities are one-sided, which is the generous reading for the rule. AND NOBODY TESTS ONE RULE. A single moving-average crossover has two free parameters. Counting fast windows from 2 to 50 against slow windows up to 200 gives 8,526 DISTINCT RULES FROM ONE IDEA, whose expected best pure-noise t-statistic is 3.698. So one family of rules, honestly backtested with no dishonesty anywhere, WILL PRODUCE A RESULT THAT LOOKS OVERWHELMINGLY SIGNIFICANT EVEN IF PRICES ARE A PURE RANDOM WALK. The correction is to divide the significance level by the number of rules tried: conventional 5% bar t = 1.960 the same bar after 100 rules t = 3.481 the same bar after 1,000 rules t = 4.056 the same bar after 8,526 rules t = 4.531 ALMOST NOBODY APPLIES THAT CORRECTION, because almost nobody records how many rules they tried, and a rule discovered by looking at a chart has an unrecorded and unbounded number of predecessors. THAT IS THE DEEPEST PROBLEM WITH THE WHOLE APPROACH and it is statistical rather than financial.

Where the source's third method differs from the first two

The source lists three methods as equals. Support, resistance, and what a random walk looks like

The source: a line of support gives a sell signal if the price falls below it, and a line of resistance gives a buy signal if the price rises above it. NOTICE THE STRUCTURE OF THAT CLAIM. The rule fires only when the line is BROKEN, so the pattern is defined by its own failure. While the price respects the line, the chartist is right that it is a line; when it breaks, the chartist is right that a signal has been given. THERE IS NO OUTCOME THAT COUNTS AGAINST THE PATTERN, which places the claim outside the reach of evidence unless the rule is fixed in advance. And a random walk generates apparent support and resistance for free, because a driftless walk revisits its recent extremes often. Over n steps the expected number of NEW running maxima is approximately the harmonic sum, which grows only as the logarithm of n: 20 steps -> about 3.60 new highs, roughly one every 5.6 steps 60 steps -> about 4.68 new highs, roughly one every 12.8 steps 120 steps -> about 5.37 new highs, roughly one every 22.4 steps 250 steps -> about 6.10 new highs, roughly one every 41.0 steps 1,000 steps -> about 7.49 new highs, roughly one every 133.6 steps A PRICE SERIES WITH NO MEMORY WHATSOEVER SETS A NEW HIGH ROUGHLY ONCE EVERY 41 DAYS OVER A TRADING YEAR and spends the rest of the time below its recent peak. THAT IS EXACTLY THE VISUAL SIGNATURE OF A RESISTANCE LEVEL. The pattern is what randomness looks like, and reading it as information is reading the absence of information. Note the logarithmic growth. Doubling the observation period from 500 to 1,000 days adds under one new high, so the LONGER a chart is, the MORE it appears that a ceiling is holding, purely because new records become rarer with time under no assumption at all.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first habit is to ask what would count as evidence against a claim before assessing the claim. Support and resistance fail that test as the source states them, because the pattern is defined by its own breach: while the line holds it is a line, and when it breaks a signal has been given. Nothing counts against it. A mechanical rule passes the test, which is a real improvement, and it then faces two arithmetic obstacles that a chart pattern never has to confront because it was never precise enough to confront them.

THREE GRADES OF CLAIM, IN ORDER OF HOW MUCH THEY CAN BE ARGUED WITH. claim can it be refuted? what refutes it "support levels matter" NO as stated nothing; the rule fires on the breach "buy when the 20-day crosses YES a backtest, subject to selection the 50-day" "recent winners outperform, YES, and it has out-of-sample tests in other markets across many names" been tested and later periods MOVING DOWN THE LIST IS MOVING FROM RHETORIC TO SCIENCE, and the source presents all three as methods of equal standing. THE PRECISION OF A CLAIM AND ITS TESTABILITY ARE THE SAME PROPERTY, which is why the vaguest technique is also the one that has never been refuted.

The second is the cost hurdle, which generalises to every active decision anybody makes. It scales with turnover and not with conviction, so a manager who is genuinely skilled but trades weekly gives back 26.00 per cent a year at a half per cent round trip before anything else happens. That is why low-turnover active management can work where high-turnover active management usually cannot, and it applies to a fundamental analyst who churns exactly as it applies to a chartist. Conviction is free; acting on it is not.

The third is the selection problem, which is the most transferable thing in this lecture. A hundred worthless rules produce a best t-statistic of 2.37 and a near-certainty of clearing the conventional bar. Nobody reports the denominator. Whenever a result is presented as significant, the question is not how large the statistic is but how many statistics were computed, and the honest answer is usually unknown even to the person presenting it. THE APPROPRIATE DISCOUNT TO ANY REPORTED BACKTEST IS LARGE AND CANNOT BE CALCULATED, which is a harder position to sit with than either belief or dismissal.

THE SELECTION PROBLEM WHEREVER IT APPEARS IN THIS COURSE. setting the unreported denominator a trading rule backtest how many rules were tried a factor discovery how many candidate factors were screened a manager with a strong record how many managers started, and how many closed a stress scenario that "worked" how many scenarios were considered a model that fits history how many specifications were estimated IN EVERY CASE THE VISIBLE RESULT IS THE MAXIMUM OF AN UNKNOWN NUMBER OF DRAWS, and the maximum of many draws from a distribution centred on nothing still looks like something. The defence is not statistical sophistication; it is asking for the denominator and noticing that it is never supplied.

The fourth is about being fair to momentum, which matters more than being severe about chartism. Cross-sectional momentum has survived out-of-sample testing across markets and decades, and it appeared in this course as one of four factors in lecture 8's worked model. Dismissing all three of the source's methods together would be a worse error than the source's own error of treating them as equals. And the same cost arithmetic that disposes of chartism also constrains momentum: at four times turnover and an 80 basis point round trip, an 8 per cent gross premium becomes 1.60 per cent net. A real effect whose size is comparable to the cost of harvesting it is a real effect that most implementations will not capture.

And the fifth, on what a list format does to a set of claims. Every one of the source's seven points is individually defensible. Two of them are the same point placed on opposite sides of a ledger so that they appear to offset. One of them settles the question and is listed third of three disadvantages. And one advantage is the mechanism by which the decisive disadvantage operates. THE LIST IS NOT WRONG; WHAT IT DESTROYS IS THE WEIGHTING, and in this case the weighting is the entire content. That is the third time in this module that a bulleted list has hidden a relationship between its own items, after the pricing model taxonomy and the empirical characteristics, and it is worth treating as a general property of the format rather than as three coincidences.

Connections

What this assumes. Momentum as one of four factors in a worked multi-factor model, and the alpha measurement problem which is the selection problem's close relative, are from the asset pricing model taxonomy. The random walk whose running maxima generate apparent resistance is the independent-increments case of the empirical characteristics of asset prices, which also found that the source's own bullets can be in tension with one another. Fundamental analysis, the approach this one is contrasted with, is the previous lecture, where a list format concealed a redundancy in exactly the same way. Normal tail probabilities and the distribution of a maximum are from statistics.

Where this goes next. Lecture 13 closes the module with credit analysis, which returns to fundamentals but asks whether a company will repay rather than what its equity is worth. Beyond the module, the cost hurdle is the central arithmetic of portfolio management, where the active-versus-passive decision is settled by turnover more often than by skill, and the selection problem is the reason performance measurement has to account for survivorship and for the number of managers who started rather than the number who remain. The transaction costs themselves, and the market microstructure that generates them, belong to the same portfolio management module.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank's trading desk is on the other side of every technical trade and earns the round-trip cost that constitutes the hurdle, which is why market risk distinguishes client flow income from proprietary risk taking: the first is a fee for providing liquidity and the second is a directional bet. The selection problem is the reason model validation functions insist on out-of-time and out-of-sample testing for credit risk models, since a scorecard fitted by trying many variable combinations has exactly the inflated in-sample performance computed in step 4. And a bank marketing an actively traded product to retail clients faces the conduct question that the cost table makes concrete, which sits with the market conduct regulation covered in the external environment.
