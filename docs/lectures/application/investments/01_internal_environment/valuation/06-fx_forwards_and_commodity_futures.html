FX forwards and commodity futures: one formula with no free parameters and one with a free parameter

A currency forward has three observable inputs and no unknowns, so it is an arbitrage and a deviation of one rand cent is worth R5,648.65 on R10m. A commodity future has three observable inputs and a convenience yield nobody can observe, so the same-looking formula is an identity: name any futures price and it returns the yield that reconciles it. The two halves of this lecture look symmetrical and are not, and the asymmetry decides which curve carries information.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The source gives two carry relations, one for currencies and one for commodities, in two lines. They look like the same formula with different inputs. They are not the same kind of statement at all, and the difference is the most useful thing in the lecture.

Covered interest parity prices a currency forward from the spot rate and two interest rates, all three observable, leaving nothing to estimate. At a spot of 18.50 rand per dollar with rand rates at 8.25 per cent and dollar rates at 4.50, the one-year forward is 19.163876, a discount of 66.39 rand cents. Every input is a market price, so the relation is enforceable: at a quoted forward of 19.30 the round trip earns R76,891.89 on R10m with no risk taken.

Two things the source does not mention. The formula's direction depends entirely on the quote convention, and applying it to an inverted quote gives 17.8591 instead of 19.1639, an error of 6.81 per cent pointing the wrong way. And the relation stopped holding after 2008: persistent deviations known as the cross-currency basis have run at tens of basis points for years, because exploiting the arbitrage consumes balance sheet that the leverage ratio charges for.

The commodity relation is a different animal. The source's linear form is a one-period approximation, accurate to 0.0111 per cent at three months and out by 13.8824 per cent at ten years. More importantly the convenience yield is a residual rather than an input: given any observed futures price the formula returns whatever yield reconciles it, so it decomposes rather than predicts. And the arbitrage bound runs one way only. Contango cannot exceed full carry, because you can buy and store, but backwardation is unbounded, because you cannot short a commodity nobody will lend you.

Feynman test A currency forward is not a forecast, it is a financing calculation: two interest rates and today's rate tell you the only forward price at which nobody can make free money. A commodity future looks like the same sum, but it contains a term for how useful it is to actually have the stuff in a warehouse, and nobody can measure that. So you can always explain the price after the fact and never predict it. And you can only force the price down by buying and storing, never up by selling what you do not have, which is why these curves can invert as far as they like.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is the distinction between a relation with no free parameters and a relation with one. Nothing in the algebra of the two formulas signals it, and everything about how they should be used follows from it. A currency forward can be checked against the market and traded when it disagrees. A commodity futures price cannot be checked against anything, because the free parameter absorbs any discrepancy. Learning to count the free parameters before trusting a formula is worth more than either formula.

The second is the roll. A long commodity futures position must sell the expiring contract and buy the next, and in contango the next is dearer. At a 10 per cent annual contango a decade of rolling loses 65.13 per cent before the spot price has moved at all. That is the arithmetic behind the long-run underperformance of passive commodity index products, and it explains an outcome usually attributed to bad luck or bad timing.

The third is that hedging a currency exposure is an active decision rather than a neutral one. Covered parity says the hedge costs the interest differential with certainty. Uncovered parity says that is exactly what you would have lost anyway, so hedging is free in expectation. The empirical evidence rejects uncovered parity, so the hedge has an expected cost, and a fund that hedges is taking a position just as surely as one that does not.

And a fourth, continuing the module's pattern. Lecture 4 found the multi-curve break, where a valuation identity stopped holding because collateral practice changed. This lecture finds the covered parity break, where an arbitrage stopped holding because capital rules changed. Both have the same shape: a relation enforced by someone's willingness to trade it survives only while trading it remains worthwhile.

That sanity check costs nothing and catches the error every time, which is why it is worth carrying instead of the formula.

Where the arbitrage stopped being enforced

Covered parity held to within a basis point or two before 2008. Since then it has not. Persistent deviations, quoted as the CROSS-CURRENCY BASIS, have run at tens of basis points for years in the major pairs, and they widen predictably at quarter and year ends. THE ARBITRAGE IS UNCHANGED. What changed is that exploiting it consumes balance sheet. The leverage ratio charges capital against the gross position regardless of its risk, so a trade earning 20 basis points on a fully hedged book may not clear a bank's internal return hurdle even though it carries no market risk at all. For a South African fund hedging offshore assets that is a permanent drag attributable entirely to a relation the textbook calls an arbitrage, and it compounds. THE LESSON GENERALISES. AN ARBITRAGE RELATION HOLDS ONLY WHILE SOMEBODY IS BOTH ABLE AND WILLING TO ENFORCE IT. Before 2008 both held. After 2008 the ability was constrained by capital rules and the relation became an approximation with a persistent sign. That is exactly the shape of the multi-curve break in lecture 4, and it has the same cause: a regulatory change altering who can hold what.

The version that never held

COVERED parity is an arbitrage: lock the forward and no risk remains. UNCOVERED parity is a FORECAST: it says the expected future spot equals the forward, so borrowing in the low-rate currency and lending in the high-rate one earns nothing on average. the two differ in exactly one respect, whether the currency risk is HEDGED one is enforced by arbitrage; the other cannot be, because you cannot trade an expectation AND IT FAILS EMPIRICALLY. Regressing the realised spot change on the forward premium should give a slope of one if uncovered parity holds. The estimated slope across the major currencies is typically near zero or NEGATIVE, meaning the high-rate currency has tended to appreciate rather than depreciate. That is the FORWARD PREMIUM PUZZLE and the carry trade is the strategy that harvests it. WHAT A RAND-BASED INVESTOR SHOULD TAKE FROM THAT is uncomfortable and important. Hedging currency exposure costs the interest differential with certainty, 3.5885 per cent a year on these numbers, and buys away a risk whose expected return has historically not been zero. SO THE DECISION TO HEDGE IS AN ACTIVE POSITION, NOT A NEUTRAL ONE. A fund that hedges has sold the carry; a fund that does not has bought it. Neither is the default, and asset classes lecture 9 made the same argument about currency overlays from the other direction.

The asymmetry that lets backwardation persist

There IS an arbitrage bound on commodity futures, and it runs in only one direction. THE UPPER BOUND. If F exceeds S x exp((r + u) T), buy the physical, store it, sell the future and deliver. That is a certain profit, so the market cannot be in contango by more than FULL CARRY: full carry ceiling at one year = 1,000 x exp(0.08 + 0.03) = 1,116.2781 equivalently the convenience yield cannot be NEGATIVE, and y = 0 gives exactly that price NO LOWER BOUND EXISTS. The reverse trade requires SHORTING the physical commodity, which means borrowing it from someone who holds it. Contango is capped by an arbitrage anyone with a warehouse can execute. Backwardation is capped by nothing. Which is why crude, electricity and agricultural curves can invert violently and stay inverted, and why A COMMODITY CURVE IS AN INVENTORY SIGNAL RATHER THAN A FORECAST: deep backwardation says the physical is scarce now, not that prices are expected to fall. IT ALSO EXPLAINS WHY THE TWO HALVES OF THIS LECTURE DIFFER SO MUCH. A currency can be shorted freely, because borrowing it is simply borrowing money, so the parity bound is two-sided and tight. A commodity cannot, so its bound is one-sided and loose, and the looseness is where the free parameter lives.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first habit is to count the free parameters before trusting a formula, and this lecture is the cleanest illustration available. Two relations, one line each, both containing a spot price and a financing rate. One has nothing left to estimate and is therefore enforceable, checkable and tradeable. The other contains a term nobody can observe and is therefore unfalsifiable. The algebra gives no hint of the difference, and every practical consequence follows from it.

THE TEST, APPLIED ACROSS THIS MODULE. relation free parameters what it is FX forward, covered parity 0 an arbitrage: trade the deviation bond forward from carry 0 an arbitrage swap value from a curve 0 an arbitrage, given the curve a bootstrapped curve BETWEEN nodes 1 (implicit) a modelling choice, worth R17,265 a caplet under Black 1 (vol) a model, calibrated to quotes a commodity future 1 (y) a decomposition, never contradicted the Merton model 2 (V, sigma) unobservable, must be INVERTED READ THE LAST THREE ROWS TOGETHER. A model with a free parameter can be useful in two very different ways: calibrated to other quoted instruments, as a caplet volatility is, or inverted from something else observable, as Merton's asset value is. A commodity future's convenience yield is NEITHER by default, which is why the theory of storage exists: it supplies the missing model of y from inventory data, and without it the formula is only bookkeeping.

The second is that "arbitrage" is a claim about people rather than about mathematics. Covered parity is derived without a single assumption about behaviour, and it still stopped holding, because the derivation quietly assumed that someone with access to both money markets would put the trade on. After 2008 the leverage ratio charged capital against the gross position irrespective of its risk, and twenty basis points on a fully hedged book stopped clearing internal hurdles. The arbitrage remained; the arbitrageur left. That is worth remembering whenever a relation is described as enforced by arbitrage, because the enforcement mechanism is a balance sheet.

The third is the shorting asymmetry, which explains far more than commodity curves. Any bound that requires selling something you do not own is weaker than the bound that requires buying something you do. A currency can be shorted by borrowing money, so parity is two-sided and tight. A commodity cannot, so contango is capped and backwardation is not. The same asymmetry appears in equity markets where short-sale constraints let overpricing persist longer than underpricing, in credit where the cheapest-to-deliver squeeze of derivatives lecture 6 arose from a shortage of deliverable bonds, and in housing where there is no way to short at all.

WHERE ELSE THE SAME ASYMMETRY BINDS. market can you short it easily? consequence currencies YES, borrow the money parity is tight in both directions government bonds YES, via repo forwards are pinned by carry commodities NO, needs a physical lender contango capped, backwardation unbounded single equities PARTLY, subject to borrow overpricing persists longer than under houses NO no arbitrage bound at all private assets NO valuations can drift from fundamentals THE RULE: WHERE SHORTING IS HARD, MISPRICING IS ONE-SIDED AND PERSISTENT. That is not a market inefficiency to be exploited so much as a structural feature to be expected, and it tells you in advance which direction the persistent error will run.

The fourth is about the roll, which deserves the emphasis it rarely gets. An investor buying a commodity index believes they are buying exposure to the spot price. What they actually own is spot plus roll, and at a 10 per cent contango the roll costs 65.13 per cent over a decade. The general point is that a derivative gives you exposure to the thing plus the cost of maintaining the exposure, and the second term is invisible in the thesis and dominant in the outcome. The same structure appeared in derivatives lecture 7, where a contract for difference's financing crossed its tax saving in 8.5 days.

And the fifth. Three separate lectures have now said a forward price is not a forecast, about interest rates, exchange rates and commodities. The reason is identical each time and worth stating once properly: a forward price is determined by an arbitrage between holding the asset and holding cash, so it is fully occupied by financing considerations and has no room left to express a view. When the forward and the expectation do differ, as they must for a risk premium to exist, that difference is a risk premium and not a forecast error, and the forward premium puzzle is the measurement of how large it has been in currencies.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The cost-of-carry relation and the argument that a forward price is a financing calculation rather than a forecast are from forwards and futures, which also introduced covered and uncovered parity in their contract form. The distinction between a relation with observable inputs and one requiring a model is the theme of pricing interest rate derivatives and yield curves and bootstrapping, whose interpolation finding is the same free-parameter test applied to a curve. The break in a relation caused by a regulatory change is the multi-curve story in swap and swaption valuation. The contrast between the continuous-income and discrete-income forms of carry, wrong for bonds and right here, is from bond options and the Merton model. On the asset class side, the commodity roll and the case for and against commodities in a portfolio are from commodities and currency, and the deliverable squeeze that is the credit market's version of the shorting asymmetry is from credit derivatives.

Where this goes next. Lecture 7 takes up hedging and the optimal hedge ratio, which is the formal answer to what a hedger should do when the instrument available does not match the exposure, and the cross-currency basis found here is exactly such a mismatch. Lecture 8's model taxonomy distinguishes relative from absolute pricing, and the free-parameter test in step 5 is the sharpest way to tell them apart. The hedging decision framed as an active position belongs to portfolio construction, where a currency overlay is a deliberate allocation rather than a risk control, and the basis risk that remains after any hedge is one of the exposures in risk measurement.

Where the bank sits opposite. The bank quoting the forward earns the cross-currency basis, and its inability to arbitrage the basis away is a direct consequence of the leverage ratio in capital management, which charges capital on gross exposure regardless of risk: the clearest case in the syllabus of a capital rule changing a market price. Funding a foreign currency asset book through the swap market, and the basis that funding pays, is a financial management and liquidity management question, since a bank relying on FX swaps for foreign currency funding has a rollover exposure that the liquidity coverage ratio treats explicitly. Commodity and FX positions in the trading book sit under market risk, where the convenience yield's unobservability makes commodity forward curves a recognised model risk, and lending against commodity inventory turns the same asymmetry into credit risk, because the collateral's value depends on a curve that can invert without warning.

Numbers used here. The covered interest parity formula and the commodity futures relation with its convenience yield are from the F105 summary chapter 9, and the treatment follows Hull, Options, Futures and Other Derivatives, chapter 5. Four things the source does not state are supplied in step 4: the formula's dependence on the quote convention, the persistent post-2008 failure of covered parity known as the cross-currency basis, the fact that its commodity form is a single-period linearisation, and the one-directional nature of the arbitrage bound that lets backwardation run unbounded. The failure of uncovered interest parity in regression tests and the resulting forward premium puzzle are established empirical findings. The arithmetic is exact and internally checked: the round trip returns exactly R10,825,000.00 by both routes at the parity forward, the annualised forward premium is 3.5885 per cent at all six tenors as construction requires, and the full carry ceiling corresponds exactly to a zero convenience yield.
