Fundamental analysis: two adjacent formulas, identical presentation, and only one means what it says

The source gives operating and financial leverage side by side with the same gloss, that a high ratio means profit is sensitive to the driver. Operating leverage is the exact elasticity, agreeing with a direct perturbation to four parts in ten thousand billion. Financial leverage as interest over profit before interest and tax is not an elasticity at all: on the worked company it reads 0.4000 where the true sensitivity is 1.6667, and the error is U-shaped in gearing with no region where it is usable.

Step 1

The 30-second version

This lecture opens the third and last mode of valuation in the module. Lectures 1 to 7 priced by replication and lectures 8 to 10 priced by a model of risk and return. Fundamental analysis prices by judgement about one issuer, and its tools are accounting ratios rather than distributions. The tools look simple, which is why the errors in them survive.

The central finding is a mismatch the source's layout conceals. Operating leverage, defined as contribution over profit before interest and tax, is exactly the elasticity of operating profit to sales: the formula gives 2.6667 and a direct one per cent perturbation gives 2.6667, agreeing to four parts in ten thousand billion. Financial leverage, defined as interest over profit before interest and tax, is not the elasticity of anything. It reads 0.4000 where the measured sensitivity of profit after tax to operating profit is 1.6667.

The relationship between the two is exact and unflattering. Writing the source's ratio as s, the true degree of financial leverage is one over one minus s, so the discrepancy is U-shaped: 21.05 times at low gearing, 4.00 times at its best, 11.11 times at high gearing. There is no region in which the source's version approximates the sensitivity, and it is worst exactly where an analyst would assume gearing can be ignored.

Two further findings. The source's three combination ratios are not three pieces of information: dividend yield multiplied by the price-earnings ratio multiplied by dividend cover equals exactly one, always, for any company at any price. And a price-earnings ratio of 15 is consistent with implied growth anywhere from 4.00 to 12.00 per cent depending on payout and required return, so comparing ratios across companies compares bundles rather than valuations.

Feynman test If a shop's costs are mostly rent, a small drop in takings hurts a lot, because the rent is still due. If it has also borrowed, the interest is still due too, and the two effects multiply rather than add. A ratio that tells you how big the interest bill is relative to profit is useful, and it is not the same thing as how much profit moves when trading moves. Those two numbers can differ by a factor of twenty, and they are usually printed next to each other with the same explanation underneath.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that accounting ratios are the most widely used analytical tools in finance and the least scrutinised, precisely because they look like arithmetic rather than modelling. Nobody checks whether a ratio measures what its name claims, and this lecture checks two adjacent ones and finds that one does and one does not. That is not a criticism of the source so much as a demonstration of what a check costs, which is one perturbation.

The second is that the two leverages compound, and the source never multiplies them. A business with high fixed costs and high debt has a combined leverage of 13.3333 on the worked scenarios, meaning a ten per cent fall in sales takes 133 per cent off profit after tax, turning a profit into a loss. That is the arithmetic of sudden corporate failure, and neither half of it is visible from either ratio alone.

The third is that "relative value", which the source defines as comparing a ratio against competitors, is only meaningful when the things being compared are alike in the dimensions the ratio does not show. A price-earnings ratio bundles payout, required return and growth into one number, so two companies on the same ratio can be priced for completely different futures, and a company on a lower ratio can be priced for more growth than its more expensive peer once the other two inputs are supplied.

And a fourth. The identity between dividend yield, price-earnings and cover is worth knowing not because anyone will be caught out by it but because it is a template. When a source lists ratios as a menu, ask which are functions of the others, because a dashboard showing three numbers that satisfy an identity is showing two numbers and a decoration.

WHICH IS THE HONEST DESCRIPTION OF RELATIVE VALUE: it is not a shortcut around a valuation model, it is a valuation model with two of its three inputs hidden.

Two ratio definitions in the source that need care

Balance sheet: assets 1,400, liabilities 700, equity 700, of which intangibles 90; current assets 480, current liabilities 300; debt 500. THE SOURCE'S RETURN ON CAPITAL = PBIT / ASSETS: 150.00 / 1,400 = 10.7143% The more usual definition divides by CAPITAL EMPLOYED, being assets less current liabilities, on the reasoning that trade payables and accruals are not capital anybody supplied: 150.00 / 1,100 = 13.6364% The source's version is lower by 2.9221 percentage points, or 21.43 per cent relatively, AND THE GAP WIDENS WITH THE SIZE OF THE TRADE PAYABLES. So it penalises exactly those businesses that fund themselves cheaply through their suppliers, which is a real competitive advantage being scored as a weakness. THE SOURCE'S RETURN ON EQUITY = PROFIT AFTER INTEREST / (ASSETS - LIABILITIES): profit after interest but BEFORE tax 90.00 / 700 = 12.8571% profit after interest AND tax 65.70 / 700 = 9.3857% a gap of 3.4714 percentage points "PROFIT AFTER INTEREST" IS AMBIGUOUS AND THE TWO READINGS DIFFER BY THE TAX RATE. The source lists four levels of fundamental factor: global economy, local economy, industry, firm. IT ALSO EXPLAINS THE DIVISION OF LABOUR IN AN INVESTMENT HOUSE: strategists forecast the levels that cannot be diversified and analysts cover the level that can, and the second job only pays if the analyst is genuinely better than average at it, whereas the first pays for everyone.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first habit is to test whether a ratio measures what its name claims, and the test costs one perturbation. Move the driver by one per cent, measure the response, compare. Operating leverage passes to four parts in ten thousand billion; financial leverage fails by up to twenty-one times. Nothing about the two formulas signals which is which, and no amount of reading the definitions carefully would reveal it, because both definitions are perfectly sensible quantities. Only the test separates them.

THE TEST, GENERALISED. a ratio claims to measure test it by passes here? sensitivity of Y to X perturb X by 1%, measure Y operating leverage YES financial leverage NO a level or a burden no test needed; it is a the source's financial definition leverage, read correctly a rate of return check the numerator and the source's ROC and ROE denominator cover the same both need care claimants and the same period THE MIDDLE ROW MATTERS AS MUCH AS THE FIRST. The source's financial leverage is a perfectly good ratio wrongly labelled, not a wrong ratio. Interest over operating profit is the reciprocal of interest cover and tells you how close a company is to being unable to service its debt. THE DEFECT IS IN THE GLOSS, NOT THE ARITHMETIC, which is the harder kind of error to find because the number itself is correct.

The second is that the two leverages multiply and nobody writes them down together. A company chooses its cost structure and its capital structure, and each choice separately looks moderate while their product does not: on the worked scenarios a business with high fixed costs and high debt has a combined leverage of 13.3333, so a ten per cent revenue fall wipes out more than all of its profit. The individual ratios would both have looked survivable. Sudden corporate failure is almost always a compounding of two moderate exposures rather than one extreme one.

The third is the identity, which is worth carrying as a template rather than as a fact. Dividend yield times price-earnings times cover is exactly one, so a dashboard showing all three shows two numbers and a decoration. The general move is to ask, of any set of ratios presented as a menu, which are functions of the others. Almost every ratio pack contains redundancy, and the redundancy matters because it makes weak evidence look corroborated: three ratios all pointing the same way are impressive unless two of them had no choice.

WHERE REDUNDANCY HIDES IN A RATIO PACK. ratios quoted together the hidden relationship dividend yield, PE, cover their product is exactly 1 operating margin, asset turnover, ROC is the product of the first two return on capital debt/equity, debt/(debt+equity), all three are monotone functions of one another capital cover current ratio, quick ratio differ only by inventory FOUR SETS, AND IN EVERY ONE A READER SEEING THREE AGREEING SIGNALS IS SEEING FEWER THAN THREE. The discipline is to count the independent quantities before counting the confirmations.

The fourth is what relative value actually is. The source presents it as an alternative to intrinsic value, as though comparing multiples avoided the need for a model. It does not: a price-earnings ratio is Gordon's model with payout, required return and growth compressed into one number, so comparing two multiples compares two bundles. The demonstration that A on a PE of 12 is priced for two percentage points more growth than B on 18 is not a trick; it is what happens whenever the hidden inputs differ, and they always differ. RELATIVE VALUE IS INTRINSIC VALUE WITH TWO INPUTS SUPPRESSED, and suppressing them does not make them irrelevant, it makes them unexamined.

And the fifth, on the factor hierarchy. Read as a variance decomposition, the source's four levels say that more than half of a company's earnings variability comes from above the company, and that only the firm-specific part can be diversified away. Both halves have organisational consequences: analyst effort concentrated on the company is effort spent on the 45 per cent that a portfolio of twenty-five holdings has already removed, while the 55 per cent that cannot be removed is forecast by whoever happens to be doing macro. THE PART YOU CANNOT DIVERSIFY IS THE PART THAT MUST BE FORECAST, and the part you can diversify is where individual skill has to be exceptional to be worth anything.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The Gordon model that generates the price-earnings identity in step 4, and the result that value comes from the spread between return on equity and the required return rather than from growth, are from the dividend discount model; the price-to-net-asset-value observation at the end of step 4 is that same condition read off the balance sheet. The required return that a relative comparison silently assumes is the output of the asset pricing models, and the variance decomposition in step 4 is the factor structure of that lecture applied to earnings rather than returns, with the same conclusion that only the idiosyncratic part diversifies. The equity as a call on the firm's assets, which is what makes leverage magnify equity returns, is from bond options and the Merton model, where the same gearing effect appeared as the leverage multiplier on equity volatility. Accounting statements themselves are from financial management.

Where this goes next. Lecture 12 covers technical analysis, which the source presents as the alternative to the fundamental approach and which rests on a claim about prices rather than about companies. Lecture 13 takes the same accounting material to a different question, namely whether a company will repay rather than what its equity is worth, which is why the source's credit rating methodology sits in the same chapter. Beyond the module, the redundancy point applies directly to performance measurement, where several risk-adjusted measures are monotone transformations of one another, and the combined leverage result is the equity analyst's version of the exposure question that risk measurement asks of a portfolio.

Where the bank sits opposite. Every ratio here appears in a credit file, and the leverage finding is the one that matters most: a lender assessing whether a borrower can survive a downturn needs the elasticity, not the burden, and the two differ by up to twenty-one times. The credit risk function's rating models use interest cover and gearing as inputs precisely because they proxy for the combined leverage computed in step 4, and the A-IRB probability of default models that consume those ratios inherit whatever the ratios do and do not measure. A bank analysing its own equity faces the same ratios from the other side, where capital management targets a return on equity that this lecture shows is ambiguous between pre- and post-tax readings, and where a bank's own operating leverage, being the ratio of its cost base to its income, is the quantity a cost-to-income ratio approximates. The variance decomposition is also the structure behind credit concentration risk: a loan book diversifies idiosyncratic borrower risk and cannot diversify the industry and economy levels above it.
