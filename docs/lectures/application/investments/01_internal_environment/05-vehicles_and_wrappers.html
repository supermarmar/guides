Lectures / Investments / Internal environment

The wrapper is worth 3.0809 per cent a year, which is more than any manager reliably delivers

One unit of income, one fund, one gross return: inside a direct holding it becomes 2.7970 over twenty-five years and inside a pension wrapper 5.9723. That gap is 3.0809 per cent a year, available with certainty rather than in expectation, and it is larger than any published estimate of reliable active management. Along the way: the crossover marginal rate above which a life policy beats a direct holding is exactly the policy's internal rate plus its fee divided by the gross return, matching a numerical search to 4.4e-16, and for a zero-rate investor the pension wrapper's advantage is minus 1.6469 per cent.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The F105 summary has no vehicles section. Its only reference is one clause in the taxation chapter listing "the tax-efficiency of the vehicle" among seven factors affecting post-tax returns. The legal forms of collective vehicles, the ETF arbitrage band and the fee cascade are built in the collective schemes and ETFs lecture, so this one is about the other half: the tax and regulatory wrapper that sits around whatever fund is chosen.

Put the same fund inside four wrappers with the gross return identical in every case. Over twenty-five years one unit of income becomes 2.7970 in a direct holding taxed at 40 per cent as it goes, 3.0416 in a life policy taxed at 30 per cent internally, 5.2404 in a tax-free account and 5.9723 in a pension wrapper with relief in and tax out. A ratio of 2.14 between best and worst, from a decision that has nothing to do with the manager or the strategy.

Expressed annually that gap is 3.0809 per cent a year for twenty-five years. No published study of active management finds a reliable edge that size, and the wrapper choice is available with certainty rather than in expectation. So the wrapper decision is larger than the manager decision, and it is usually taken with less analysis.

But there is no generally best wrapper, because the ranking depends on the investor's own marginal rate. At zero the direct holding wins at 5.4274; at 18 and 30 per cent the tax-free account wins; at 40 and 45 the pension wrapper wins. A zero-rate investor is actively harmed by a life policy, which taxes inside the fund at a rate that investor would not otherwise pay.

The life-policy question has an exact answer. A wrapper taxed internally charges one rate to every holder, so the crossover marginal rate is where the net annual returns are equal, and that gives internal rate + fee / gross return. Here 0.3000 plus 0.0035 over 0.0700, which is 35.0000 per cent, matching a numerical search to 4.4e-16 and not moving with the holding period at all. So the naive rule "use the wrapper if your rate exceeds the wrapper's rate" is wrong by the width of the fee divided by the return, and the fee's effect is divided by the gross return rather than added to the rate, which makes the whole decision asset-class dependent.

All three wrappers earn their fees for a 40 per cent taxpayer, with break-evens from 0.7000 to 3.4405 per cent against charges of 0.15 to 0.35, and the pension wrapper has the most headroom at 3.1905 points. For a zero-rate investor the tax-free account's break-even fee is 0.000000 per cent, so any charge at all is pure loss. And the lock-up turns out not to be the binding objection: the pension wrapper's 3.0809 per cent advantage exceeds the lock-up cost even at a 50 per cent chance of needing the money early, though that conclusion holds only because the lock-up is priced as a yield give-up rather than as an inability to pay.

Step 2

Why this matters

This is the largest single decision in personal and institutional investment that receives the least analysis. Section 4.1's 3.0809 per cent a year exceeds the entire delivery-chain cost from lecture 1, exceeds any reliable manager edge, and is knowable in advance with certainty. An adviser who spends a week on fund selection and a sentence on wrapper choice has allocated effort in inverse proportion to the size of the effect, and the same is true of any analysis of investment performance that does not state which wrapper the money sat in.

It matters for institutional work too, because the wrapper decides which regulatory regime applies before any mandate is written. Lecture 4 treated Regulation 28 as a given, and it applies because the money sits in a pension wrapper: the same investor holding the same fund directly faces no such limit. So the sequence runs wrapper, then regime, then mandate, then manager, and reasoning in the opposite direction produces recommendations that turn out to be prohibited.

The crossover identity in section 4.2 matters because it is the kind of result that changes practice. Most wrapper comparisons are done with spreadsheets on particular assumptions, and the identity shows the answer depends on exactly three numbers: the wrapper's internal rate, its fee and the gross return. It does not depend on the horizon at all. That means the advice can be given robustly rather than as the output of a projection whose assumptions the client cannot check.

And the zero-rate cases in sections 4.1, 4.3 and 4.4 matter professionally. A non-taxpayer inside a life policy pays a tax they would not otherwise pay; a non-taxpayer inside a tax-free account pays a fee for a benefit worth nothing; a non-taxpayer inside a pension wrapper accepts a lock-up for a negative advantage. All three are common arrangements and all three are wrong for that client, and identifying them requires only asking a question about tax status before recommending a product.

Step 3

The mental model

A wrapper does not change what the fund holds. It changes when tax is paid, at whose rate, what can be held, and when the money can be taken out. Those four things are the whole subject.

Pick a fund. Now put it in a box. The box does not touch the shares inside it, but it changes four things about your relationship with them.

Whose tax rate applies. If you hold the fund directly, the tax is yours at your rate. Inside a life policy the insurer pays tax on the fund at the insurer's rate, and you pay nothing more. If your rate is higher than the insurer's, the box helps. If it is lower, the box is charging you a tax you would not have paid.

When the tax falls. A pension box lets you put money in before tax and takes tax out at the end. That means more money compounds for longer, which lecture 2 showed is worth a third more wealth over forty years even at an unchanged rate.

What you may hold. The box comes with rules. A pension box in South Africa cannot go beyond 45 per cent offshore or 75 per cent in shares. Hold the fund directly and nobody restricts you at all.

When you may leave. A pension box locks the door until you retire. That is what it charges for the tax break, and it is the only cost here that is not a percentage.

Now the arithmetic that makes this crisp. A box that taxes inside itself at 30 per cent and charges 0.35 per cent a year, on a fund returning 7 per cent, is exactly as good as holding directly at a personal rate of 35 per cent. Not 30. The extra five points is the fee, divided by the return. And notice what that means: if the fund returns 3 per cent instead of 7, the same fee is worth twelve points of tax rate rather than five, so the box that was clearly worth it on shares may not be worth it on bonds.

Three of the four things a box changes are costs and one is a benefit. The benefit is usually bigger. But it is zero for someone who pays no tax, and then all you have left are the three costs.

Formally, if the gross return is g, the wrapper's internal tax rate t_w and its fee f, then the net annual return inside the wrapper is g(1 - t_w) - f and outside it is g(1 - t_i) for an investor at rate t_i. Setting those equal gives the crossover t_i = t_w + f / g, which is independent of the horizon because both quantities act on the annual return.

4.2 The crossover rate, and why it is an identity

A wrapper taxed inside the fund charges one rate to every holder. That is a good deal for an investor whose own rate is higher and a bad one for an investor whose rate is lower, and the crossover is exact rather than approximate.

   the investor's   direct holding   the life policy   which is better   by how much
   marginal rate    outcome          outcome
                10%           4.6061            3.0416   direct                1.5645
                20%           3.9048            3.0416   direct                0.8632
                30%           3.3066            3.0416   direct                0.2651
                40%           2.7970            3.0416   the policy            0.2446
                45%           2.5714            3.0416   the policy            0.4702

The direct holding still wins at a 30 per cent rate, which equals the policy's own internal rate.

The policy's advantage rises with the investor's marginal rate, and it is still negative at a 30 per cent rate even though that equals the policy's own internal rate. Solving for equality gives a crossover of 35.0000 per cent, five points above the internal rate, and the five points are the fee. An investor taxed at exactly the policy's internal rate is worse off inside it, because they pay the same tax and also the 0.35 per cent wrapper charge. The naive rule "use the wrapper if your rate exceeds the wrapper's rate" is therefore wrong by the width of the fee.

The crossover does not move with the horizon at all, and the reason is an identity. The two routes are equal when their net annual returns are equal, so the crossover is the internal rate plus the fee divided by the gross return: 0.3000 + 0.0035 / 0.0700 = 0.3500, matching a numerical search to 4.4e-16. That is worth memorising, because it means the wrapper decision can be made from three numbers without a projection, and a client can check it.

The division by the gross return carries a consequence nothing else in the material mentions. At a 3 per cent gross return the same 0.35 per cent fee moves the crossover by 11.6667 points rather than 5.0000, so a wrapper clearly worth it on equities may not be worth it on bonds. The wrapper decision is asset-class dependent, and it becomes less attractive precisely in the low-return assets where the fee is a larger fraction of the return, which is the same effect lecture 1 found for the delivery chain as a whole.

4.3 When a wrapper's fee eats its own tax break

Every wrapper charges something and saves something. The number a wrapper should be judged against is not other wrappers' fees but the fee at which its own tax break is exactly consumed.

Break-even fees for a 40 per cent taxpayer

   the wrapper           break-even fee a year   the fee   headroom   worth it?
                         against a direct         charged
                         holding for a 40% payer
   a life policy                         0.7000%      0.35%    0.3500 pp   yes
   a pension wrapper                     3.4405%      0.25%    3.1905 pp   yes
   a tax-free account                    2.8000%      0.15%    2.6500 pp   yes

All three earn their fees. The pension wrapper has the most headroom at 3.1905 points, not the tax-free account.

All three wrappers are worth their fees for a 40 per cent taxpayer, with break-evens from 0.7000 to 3.4405 per cent against charges of 0.15 to 0.35. The pension wrapper has the most headroom at 3.1905 points, which is the combined effect of the entry relief and the internal exemption. The life policy has the least at 0.3500, so its case is the most fragile: a policy charging 0.70 per cent rather than 0.35 would be worthless even to a 40 per cent payer.

The headroom is not a licence, though, because the break-even is computed for a 40 per cent payer. For a zero-rate investor the tax-free account saves nothing at all, so its break-even fee is 0.000000 per cent and any charge is pure loss. The same fee that is comfortably justified for one investor is a deadweight for another, which is why a wrapper recommendation is advice rather than a product selection, and why a single "competitive charges" comparison across providers answers the wrong question.

4.4 What the lock-up costs

A pension wrapper buys its tax treatment with an access restriction. Price it, because it is the only cost in this lecture that is not a percentage, and it is the objection most often raised.

   the money early     happens          a year          annual tax advantage    worth it?
                     0%             2.00%          0.0000%                  3.0809%   yes
                     5%             2.00%          0.1000%                  3.0809%   yes

The tax advantage dominates even at a 50 per cent chance of needing the money early.

The pension wrapper's advantage over a direct holding is 3.0809 per cent a year for a 40 per cent taxpayer, and the lock-up costs 1.0000 per cent a year even at a 50 per cent chance of needing the money early. So the tax break dominates the illiquidity at these magnitudes, which is not what the argument usually assumes.

That conclusion holds only because the lock-up is priced as a yield give-up, and the caveat matters more than the result. An investor who simply cannot meet a necessary expense suffers a loss no annual rate describes, and that is the real argument for an emergency reserve held outside the wrapper rather than for a smaller pension contribution. The two are frequently confused, and the arithmetic here separates them: the correct response to illiquidity risk is a liquid buffer sized to the need, not a permanent reduction in the tax-advantaged allocation.

For a zero-rate investor the pension wrapper's advantage is minus 1.6469 per cent: there is no relief to claim, there is still tax on the way out, and there is still a fee. So the lock-up is being accepted in exchange for a negative return advantage. That is the case of a non-taxpayer contributing to a pension, which is common, usually wrong, and identifiable from a single question about tax status. At an 18 per cent rate the advantage is positive at 0.3182 per cent but still smaller than the lock-up cost, so the crossover for a pension wrapper sits between 18 and 30 per cent rather than at zero.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Three things a practitioner does with wrappers that the arithmetic alone does not produce.

Establish the marginal rate before discussing any product, and establish it for the future as well as the present. Section 4.1's ranking turns entirely on that number, and section 4.4 shows a pension wrapper being actively harmful to a non-taxpayer. But the harder version of the question is the one the arithmetic cannot answer: the relief is claimed at today's rate and the exit tax paid at a rate decades away, so a pension wrapper is a bet that your rate falls. For a young person on a low income the bet usually runs the wrong way, and for a high earner near retirement it usually runs the right way. That is the opposite of how contribution advice is typically given, which starts from age and contribution capacity rather than from the direction of the rate over the working life.

Use the identity rather than a projection, and quote it. Section 4.2 gives the crossover as three numbers with no horizon in it. That is a rare thing in financial advice: a result the client can verify on the back of an envelope and that does not depend on a return assumption in the way most projections do. It also fails in a specific and statable way, since it assumes both the fee and the tax act proportionally on the annual return, so a fixed cash charge or a tax with an annual allowance breaks it. Knowing where a clean result stops being clean is more useful than the result.

Treat the wrapper and the asset allocation as one decision, not two in sequence. Section 4.5 shows the prudential limits arriving with the wrapper, and section 4.2 shows the wrapper's value depending on the gross return of what goes inside it. Those two together mean the optimal arrangement is often to split: hold the low-return, high-income assets inside the wrapper where the tax saving is largest per unit of return, and hold the growth assets outside where no limit binds and the deferral would have happened anyway. That is asset location rather than asset allocation, it is worth a substantial fraction of section 4.1's 3.0809 per cent, and almost nobody does it because the two decisions sit with different people.

On the summary, the observation to record is an omission rather than an error. It lists "the tax-efficiency of the vehicle" as one of seven factors affecting post-tax returns and develops none of them, and this lecture shows that single clause to be worth 3.0809 per cent a year, larger than any other item in the whole investments syllabus that an investor can control with certainty. A list that gives it equal weight with "the investor's financial position" is not wrong about the contents and is badly wrong about the proportions.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The legal forms of collective vehicles, the ETF arbitrage band, the closed-ended discount and the fee cascade are built in collective schemes and ETFs, which this lecture deliberately does not repeat. The tax deferral arithmetic is derived in objectives, liabilities and constraints, which found deferral worth 33.63 per cent over forty years at an unchanged rate, and the compounding of annual charges is in the business model, which found the same low-return effect on the delivery chain. The prudential limits that arrive with a pension wrapper are quantified in mandates and the statement of investment principles, and the governance that chooses between wrappers is in governance, trustees and delegation. The manager edge the wrapper advantage is compared against is bounded in active versus passive and measuring portfolio performance.

Where this goes next. Lecture 6 closes the overview sequence by turning to the issuer's side, covering the front, middle and back office split, long-term capital budgeting, short-term working capital, and mergers and divestitures. That is where the assets everything in this track buys are actually created, and it is the one overview lecture that looks outward rather than inward. Beyond the overview lectures, the external environment covers the regulators named in section 4.5, since each wrapper's supervisor is a separate body with a separate mandate, and the tax authorities whose rates drive every result here.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank is the provider of most wrappers and the arithmetic of section 4.3 is its product economics: the break-even fee is the ceiling on what it can charge before the wrapper stops being worth buying, and it differs by client tax status, which is why the same product is mis-sold to some customers and correctly sold to others. That conduct exposure is treated in operational risk. Section 4.2's identity is also a pricing constraint on the bank rather than merely a fact for the investor, since a fee above the break-even makes the product unsellable to its own target market. And a life wrapper is a genuine insurance liability on the provider's balance sheet, so the mismatch framing from lecture 1 applies to it: the provider sells a policy and buys assets, and the capital it holds against the gap is set out in market risk.

Numbers used here. The summary's only relevant material is a single clause in chapter 2 listing "the tax-efficiency of the vehicle" among seven factors affecting post-tax returns, alongside tax systems, asset rules, investor rules, the investor's financial position and offshore holdings; and one clause in chapter 3 on a trust's purposes. Both are transcribed from the ASSA F105 summary in the user's notes. The prudential limits in section 4.5 are external and verifiable, being Regulation 28 of the South African Pension Funds Act as amended February 2022. One observation is recorded against the summary, and it is an omission of proportion rather than an error of fact: it lists the tax-efficiency of the vehicle as one factor among seven and develops none of them, when that single clause is worth 3.0809 per cent a year, larger than any other item in the syllabus that an investor can control with certainty. Twenty-one claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions, all twenty-one passed, and none can pass without the computation. Three claims were flagged as unfalsifiable by a vacuity check and rewritten against computed variables, and one claim broke when a table label was shortened and was repaired.
