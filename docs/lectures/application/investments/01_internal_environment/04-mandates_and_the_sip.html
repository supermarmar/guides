Lectures / Investments / Internal environment

Six of Regulation 28's seven limits leave double-digit headroom, and the one that binds is the smallest

A conventional balanced fund sits 13.0 points below the 75 per cent equity cap and 10.0 below the 45 per cent offshore cap, and touches only the 2.5 per cent other-excluded bucket. So the headline caps that attract all the argument constrain nothing, and the small buckets nobody discusses are what actually shape behaviour. Along the way: the caps interact, since every point of offshore equity removes exactly a point of local equity capacity, so a rule written to limit foreign exposure ends up limiting domestic equity, and a self-investment holding is exactly twice as damaging as an equal holding in an unrelated firm.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Lecture 3 covered who decides. This lecture covers the document that records the decision. The F105 summary gives a statement of investment principles that is "less prescriptive", a mandate with seven instructions, a separate list of seven explicit mandate terms, and four localisation requirements, all sitting on top of whatever prudential limits the jurisdiction imposes. Assembling and pricing that stack produces four findings.

Start with the prudential limits, since a mandate inherits them before it adds anything. A conventional balanced fund sits 13.0 points below the 75 per cent equity cap and 10.0 below the 45 per cent offshore cap, and of Regulation 28's seven limits only the 2.5 per cent other-excluded bucket comes within five points. So the headline caps constrain nothing for a conventional fund, and the limits that actually shape behaviour are the small buckets, because a fund wanting a 4 per cent allocation to something in the 2.5 per cent bucket is stopped and a fund wanting 70 per cent equity is not. The same seven limits bind a young growth fund on both headline caps and a mature matching fund on neither, so prudential limits constrain long-horizon funds and not short-horizon ones, which inverts where the prudential concern usually sits.

The limits also interact, which the list format conceals. An offshore equity holding uses both the 45 per cent foreign allowance and the 75 per cent equity allowance, so every point of offshore equity removes exactly a point of local equity capacity. A fund at the full 45 per cent offshore in equity has only 30 per cent of local equity left against 75 if it holds nothing offshore. The consequence is unintended: a rule written to limit foreign exposure ends up limiting domestic equity, and the only way to restore local equity is to go offshore in bonds instead, which is a currency decision taken for a regulatory reason.

Third, the departure limit, which is the only one of the summary's seven instructions that governs the manager's discretion rather than what may be held. It turns out to be either free or proportionally costly with nothing in between: a limit above the manager's intended position size costs nothing, and the information ratio is identical at 4.0 and 10.0 per cent limits, while a 0.5 per cent limit delivers 25.00 per cent of the unconstrained ratio. So the only question is whether the limit exceeds the intended bet, which is a question about the manager rather than about prudence. Uncomfortably, the same 2.0 per cent clause is free for a manager at an information coefficient of 0.02 and binding for one at 0.25, so it constrains the better manager and not the worse one.

Fourth, the self-investment prohibition, which the summary lists as one of seven instructions with no more weight than a counterparty limit. It deserves more, because it is the only one addressing a double exposure. A fund loses its sponsor's covenant whether or not it holds sponsor shares, and self-investment adds an asset loss on the same event. A 20 per cent sponsor holding is therefore exactly twice as damaging as a 20 per cent holding in an unrelated firm, with a correlation of one by construction that no diversification calculation can improve. That is why the prohibition is absolute rather than a percentage.

And one observation on the localisation requirements. Three of the four protect the beneficiary or the regulator. The first, a forced holding of government bonds, protects the government: at a two point yield give-up a 30 per cent forced holding costs 0.6000 per cent a year, compounding to 12.7093 over twenty years, which is the same order as the entire asset management fee from lecture 1, transferred to the state and disclosed nowhere.

Step 2

Why this matters

The mandate is where every earlier lecture becomes enforceable. Lecture 2's objectives, lecture 3's governance decisions and the techniques of the whole portfolio management module are all abstractions until somebody writes them into a contract with a manager, and what is not in the mandate does not happen. That makes the drafting a substantive investment activity rather than a legal formality, and section 4.3's result is the clearest case: the departure limit alone determines how much of a manager's skill can reach the portfolio, and it is frequently set by copying the previous mandate.

The finding in section 4.1 matters because it redirects effort. Enormous energy goes into debating prudential caps, and for a conventional fund those caps are not the constraint. The binding items are the small buckets and, far more often, the fund's own self-imposed limits: its departure ranges, its single-name caps, its exclusion lists. A trustee body that spends its time lobbying for a higher offshore allowance while running a 1.0 per cent departure limit has misidentified what is restricting it by an order of magnitude.

The interaction result in section 4.2 matters for a practical reason that generalises well beyond Regulation 28. Limits written as independent clauses are almost never independent, because assets belong to several categories at once, and the feasible set is therefore a polygon rather than a list of ceilings. A mandate can be internally infeasible without any single clause looking wrong, which is a drafting failure that only shows up when the manager tries to implement it. Checking feasibility before signing is a five-minute exercise that is rarely done.

Section 4.4 matters most for the actuary specifically. Self-investment is the one exposure where the asset and the support for the asset are the same credit, so it defeats the entire apparatus of diversification and correlation that the portfolio construction module builds. An actuary assessing a scheme's covenant and its asset allocation separately will miss it entirely, and the doubling is large enough to change a funding conclusion.

Step 3

The mental model

A mandate is a list of things the manager may not do, plus a definition of what success means. The first list is prudential and the second is contractual, and confusing them produces documents that constrain without directing.

You hire someone to invest your money. You cannot watch them, and you cannot judge them for years. So you write down two kinds of thing.

The first kind is what they may not do: no more than this much in one company, nothing in our own employer, no more than that far from the benchmark, nothing on this list. Every one of these is a fence. Fences do not tell anybody where to go.

The second kind is what they are trying to achieve: this benchmark, over this period, judged this often, with this much freedom. That is a direction. A mandate with only fences produces a manager who stays inside them and has no idea what you want.

Now the thing that catches people. The regulator has already built some fences, and they are usually a long way away. If the rules say no more than 75 per cent in shares and you want 62, that fence is not touching you. Arguing about it changes nothing. The fence that is touching you is nearly always one you built yourself, and it is nearly always the one about how far from the benchmark the manager may stray, because that one is set at a level somebody guessed at.

And fences interact in a way lists hide. If one rule says no more than 45 per cent abroad and another says no more than 75 per cent in shares, then a share bought abroad uses up both allowances at once. Buy 45 per cent of foreign shares and you have 30 per cent of domestic shares left, not 75. Nobody wrote that rule and it is what the rules say.

Finally, one fence is different in kind from the rest. Every other limit stops you concentrating. The ban on owning your own employer's shares stops something worse: if the employer fails, you lose the shares and you lose the employer who was going to top up the fund. Same event, two losses, and no amount of clever portfolio construction separates them.

Formally, if the manager's information coefficient is IC and the underlying volatility sigma, the intended position size is IC x sigma, and a departure limit d achieves a fraction min(1, d / (IC x sigma)) of the unconstrained information ratio. That is why the cost function has a kink at d = IC x sigma and is flat above it.

The detail

4.1 Which prudential limits actually bind

A mandate inherits the prudential limits before it adds its own. Regulation 28 was amended in February 2022, replacing a 30 per cent offshore limit plus a 10 per cent Africa allowance with a single 45 per cent aggregate, alongside caps on equity, property, private equity, commodities, hedge funds and other excluded assets. Set a conventional balanced fund against all seven.

One of seven comes within five points, and it is the smallest bucket. The equity and offshore caps have 13.0 and 10.0 points of headroom.

Only one of the seven limits sits within five points of this fund's holding, and it is the 2.5 per cent other-excluded-assets bucket. The headline caps on equity and foreign assets both have double-digit headroom. So the prudential limits mostly do not constrain a conventional fund, and the ones that do are the small buckets nobody argues about rather than the headline caps that attract all the attention. A fund wanting a 4 per cent allocation to something in the 2.5 per cent bucket is stopped; a fund wanting 70 per cent equity is not.

The same seven limits against three funds

   the fund                 wants equity   wants offshore   which limits bind?
   a mature matching fund              20%                10%   none
   a balanced fund                     62%                35%   none
   a young growth fund                 85%                50%   equity, offshore

Both headline caps bind the growth fund and neither binds the matching fund.

The region in which the limits matter is worth stating because it runs against expectation. The same seven caps bind the young growth fund on both headline items and bind the mature matching fund on nothing. Prudential limits therefore constrain funds with long horizons and high risk capacity, and leave mature funds untouched, which inverts where the prudential concern normally sits: the fund closest to paying out is the one the rules do not restrict.

4.2 The limits interact, so the feasible region is smaller than the list

The summary lists mandate instructions as separate items and Regulation 28 lists its caps as separate percentages. They are not independent, because the same asset sits in more than one bucket. An offshore equity holding consumes both the 45 per cent foreign allowance and the 75 per cent equity allowance.

Filling the 45 per cent offshore allowance four ways

   offshore   offshore   foreign total   local equity   equity total   the 45% is
   equity     bonds      against 45%     available      against 75%    used by
          0%         45%              45%              75%            75%   bonds only
         15%         30%              45%              60%            75%   both
         30%         15%              45%              45%            75%   both
         45%          0%              45%              30%            75%   equity only

The offshore allowance is fully used in every row. The local equity capacity falls from 75 to 30 per cent depending only on how it is used.

Every point of offshore equity removes exactly a point of local equity capacity, because the 75 per cent cap counts both. A fund at the full 45 per cent offshore in equity has only 30 per cent of local equity available, against 75 if it takes its offshore exposure entirely in bonds. So a rule written to limit foreign exposure ends up limiting domestic equity, which is not what anyone intended, and a fund can restore its local equity only by taking its offshore allowance in bonds instead. That is a currency and asset class decision made for a regulatory reason rather than an investment one.

The feasible frontier

   total equity wanted   maximum offshore equity   is the combination feasible?
                     50%                        45%   yes
                     65%                        45%   yes
                     75%                        45%   yes
                     85%                        45%   NO, the equity cap is breached

A fund wanting 85 per cent total equity is infeasible whatever its offshore split.

The two caps together describe a polygon rather than two independent lines. A fund wanting 85 per cent total equity is infeasible regardless of how it splits it, and a fund wanting 75 per cent can place at most 45 of that offshore. A mandate written as two separate clauses can be internally infeasible without any clause looking wrong, which is the practical reason to check feasibility before signing rather than discovering it when the manager reports a breach.

4.3 What a departure limit costs the manager

The summary's first mandate instruction is a departure limit, the permitted deviation from benchmark weights. It is the term that determines how much of the manager's skill reaches the portfolio, and it is usually set by intuition. Take a manager with an information coefficient of 0.10 on positions whose underlying volatility is 20 per cent, with 25 independent bets available.

The information ratio at five departure limits

   departure limit   the intended bet   how much of the   information   as a % of the
   per position      size               bet survives      ratio         unconstrained
                 0.5%             2.0000%             25.00%        0.1250            25.00%
                 1.0%             2.0000%             50.00%        0.2500            50.00%
                 2.0%             2.0000%            100.00%        0.5000           100.00%
                 4.0%             2.0000%            100.00%        0.5000           100.00%
                10.0%             2.0000%            100.00%        0.5000           100.00%

Identical at 2.0, 4.0 and 10.0 per cent. The cost is proportional below the intended bet size and exactly zero above it.

The intended bet size is 2.0000 per cent per position. A departure limit above that costs nothing at all, and the information ratio is identical at 4.0 and 10.0 per cent. Below it the cost is proportional: a 0.5 per cent limit delivers 25.00 per cent of the unconstrained ratio. So a departure limit is either free or proportionally costly, with nothing in between, and the only question worth asking is whether it exceeds the manager's intended position size. That is a question about the manager's process, which is why the summary is right that trustees should consult the managers before setting risk parameters.

The same 2.0 per cent clause against four managers

   the manager's IC   intended bet size   is a 2.0% departure limit binding?
                 0.02             0.4000%   no, it is free
                 0.05             1.0000%   no, it is free
                 0.12             2.4000%   YES
                 0.25             5.0000%   YES

Free for the weakest manager and binding for the strongest, because the intended bet scales with the information coefficient.

A 2.0 per cent limit is free for a manager at an information coefficient of 0.02, whose intended bet is 0.4000 per cent, and binding for one at 0.25, whose bet is 5.0000. The same mandate clause constrains the better manager and not the worse one, which is an uncomfortable property for a control described as prudential. The implication is not that departure limits are wrong but that a single figure applied across a manager panel is doing something different to each of them, and the mandate should state the limit relative to the manager's own process rather than as a universal number.

4.4 The self-investment prohibition, and why it is different in kind

The summary lists a self-investment prohibition among seven mandate instructions, giving it no more weight than a counterparty limit. It deserves more, because every other limit addresses concentration and this one addresses a double exposure. Take a fund of 100 whose sponsor's covenant is worth 20 to it.

                   20%           20.0000         20.0000      40.0000            40.00%

The covenant is lost in every row. Self-investment adds an asset loss on the same event.

The fund loses the 20.0000 covenant whether or not it holds sponsor shares, because the sponsor's failure destroys both. Self-investment adds an asset loss on that same event, taking the total from 20.00 to 40.00 per cent of the fund at a 20 per cent holding. The correlation is exactly one by construction, which no diversification calculation can improve, and this is the only exposure in the whole framework where the asset and the support for the asset fail together with certainty rather than with a probability.

That is why the prohibition is absolute in most jurisdictions rather than a percentage cap. The summary is right to list it separately and understates the reason: it is not a large concentration, it is a different kind of exposure.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Three things a practitioner does with a mandate that a careful reading alone does not produce.

Compute the distance to every limit before discussing any of them. Section 4.1's finding generalises: in almost every mandate, most constraints are slack and one or two bind, and the binding ones are rarely the ones under discussion. The five-minute exercise is to tabulate the fund's current position against every limit in the document and sort by headroom. What emerges is usually surprising, and it converts a debate about principles into a list of two items. It also identifies the clauses that can be deleted, which matters because every clause in a mandate is something a manager must monitor and a trustee must review, and slack clauses consume that attention without protecting anything.

Test the mandate for feasibility, not just for prudence. Section 4.2 shows that limits written as separate clauses interact through assets that occupy several categories, and the result is a feasible region rather than a set of ceilings. The practitioner's check is to ask the manager to state the portfolio it would hold absent all constraints and then to test that portfolio against the whole clause set jointly. If the answer is infeasible, the mandate is asking for something impossible and one clause must give. Discovering that at drafting is free; discovering it through a breach report is not.

Read the departure limit as the mandate's most consequential number and set it with the manager rather than for them. Section 4.3's kink means a limit set slightly too low costs proportionally and a limit set generously costs nothing, so the asymmetry of the error is strongly one-sided: erring high is free and erring low is expensive. The correct procedure is to ask the manager what position size its process produces, set the limit above that, and control the total risk through the tracking error budget rather than through the per-position cap. That gets the prudential protection without the proportional cost, and it is a different document from the one most funds have.

On the summary, two things are recorded. Its seven mandate instructions treat the self-investment prohibition as one concentration control among several, when section 4.4 shows it addresses a double exposure with a correlation of one that no other clause resembles. And its four localisation requirements are presented as a homogeneous prudential list when one of them, the forced holding of government bonds, protects the state at the members' expense and costs 12.7093 per cent of a twenty-year outcome at plausible magnitudes. Neither is an error of fact. Both are places where a flat list hides a distinction that changes what a practitioner should do.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The governance that produces this document is in governance, trustees and delegation, whose time-scale and evaluation-frequency findings are two of the seven explicit mandate terms, and the objectives the mandate encodes are in objectives, liabilities and constraints. The fee the forced government holding is compared against is from the business model. The information ratio and position sizing in section 4.3 come from active versus passive, the offshore limit is applied in currency hedging, the counterparty limits govern the instruments priced in using swaps, and the ESG limits are the exclusions quantified in the governance lecture. The benchmark selection the mandate must specify is in benchmarks and comparators.

Where this goes next. Lecture 5 covers vehicles and wrappers, which determines which prudential regime and which tax treatment apply before any mandate is written, so the choice of vehicle precedes the choice of clause. Section 4.1's finding carries into it directly, since a different vehicle faces a different limit set and the binding constraint changes with the wrapper rather than with the strategy. Lecture 6 turns to the issuer's side and the board decisions that create the assets a mandate permits. Beyond the overview lectures, the external environment covers the regulators who set the prudential limits and the market infrastructure the counterparty limits protect against.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank faces the same structure with the constraints binding much harder, since its capital and liquidity ratios are calibrated to sit close to actual balance sheets rather than far from them, and the interaction problem of section 4.2 is the central difficulty of the Basel framework, where the same asset consumes risk-weighted capital, leverage exposure and liquidity buffers simultaneously. That interaction is set out in capital management and liquidity risk. The bank is also the counterparty the exposure limits are written against, and the self-investment problem of section 4.4 has a direct banking analogue in a bank holding its own or another bank's capital instruments, which is why regulatory deductions exist and is treated in credit concentration risk.

Two observations are recorded against the summary, with the examinable position stated alongside each. Second, its four localisation requirements are presented as a homogeneous prudential list when one of them protects the state rather than the beneficiary, costing 0.6000 per cent a year and 12.7093 over twenty years at a 30 per cent forced holding and a two point yield give-up. Everything numerical beyond the Regulation 28 limits is invented for illustration: a balanced fund holding 62 per cent equity and 35 per cent offshore against matching and growth funds at 20 and 85 per cent equity, offshore splits from zero to 45 per cent, an information coefficient from 0.02 to 0.25 on a 20 per cent underlying volatility with 25 available positions, departure limits from 0.5 to 10.0 per cent, a fund of 100 with a sponsor covenant worth 20 and self-investment from zero to 20 per cent, and a two point yield give-up on forced holdings from zero to 30 per cent. Two claims were flagged as unfalsifiable by a vacuity check because they compared literal constants, and were rewritten to reference the computed headroom and cap values. A crash from unescaped percentage signs in a format string was fixed, five literal double-percent signs in unformatted table headers were corrected, and two labelling defects were repaired: a floating-point tie was reporting a departure limit exactly equal to the intended bet as binding, and a column of zeroes was carrying no information. Four figures written into the prose from hand arithmetic were also wrong and were corrected against the script, since the twenty-year compounded cost is 12.7093 per cent rather than the 12.7159 I had calculated. Every headline finding states the region in which it holds: which limits bind depends entirely on where the fund wants to sit, the cap interaction bites only when the fund is near both caps at once, the departure limit's cost is zero above the intended bet size and proportional below it, and the self-investment doubling holds at the covenant value assumed and scales with it.
