Credit derivatives: a spread pins down a product, not a probability

A credit default swap spread determines the hazard rate multiplied by the loss given default and nothing else, so the same 250 basis point quote is consistent with a five-year default probability of 14.5 per cent or 46.5 per cent depending on a recovery assumption nobody observes. That indeterminacy is the reason credit desks quote spreads and risk models report probabilities, and the two are not interchangeable. Two of the source's credit events are also not credit events.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A credit default swap pays the protection buyer if a defined credit event befalls a reference entity, in exchange for a regular premium. The source describes it as an option to sell the underlying if an event occurs, with a reference bond, a premium paid by the holder, and recovery possible. Most of that is right and the word "option" is not: the holder has no discretion at all, because the payoff is triggered by an event rather than elected. That is why the instrument is called a swap, and the source itself lists it among swaps elsewhere.

The pricing rests on one relation. To first order the spread equals the hazard rate times one minus recovery, so a quoted spread determines the product and nothing else. At 250 basis points the implied five-year default probability is 14.5 per cent if recovery is 20 per cent and 46.5 per cent if it is 80: a factor of 3.2 from an assumption. Anyone converting spreads into probabilities without stating the recovery has produced a number with no content.

Valuation is two legs and one number. The premium leg is the spread times the risky annuity, which is the sum of survival-weighted discount factors: 3.522579 on the worked curve against a risk-free annuity of 3.958357, so 89.0 per cent. The protection leg is the discounted expected loss. And the mark-to-market of an existing position is a single line: the spread change times the risky annuity times notional, which is R35,226 per basis point on R100m.

Two structural features then explain most of what looks anomalous. The cheapest-to-deliver option means the buyer delivers the worst bond, worth 7.7 points of notional on the worked capital structure, so CDS protection is worth more than any single bond's credit risk. And wrong-way risk means protection from a correlated seller is worth much less than it appears: at a 0.90 default correlation only 60.4 per cent of the protection works.

Feynman test You pay someone a few hundred basis points a year and they promise to make you whole if a particular company fails. The price tells you the market's view of how much you would lose, which is how likely failure is multiplied by how bad it would be, and it cannot tell you which of the two is doing the work. And the promise is only as good as the person making it, so protection bought from someone who fails in the same storm is not protection.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the recovery-default indeterminacy is the single most important fact about credit markets and it is routinely elided. A CDS curve is an observable market price and a probability of default is a modelled quantity, and the bridge between them requires an assumption about recovery that changes the answer by a factor of three. Any statement of the form "the market implies a 20 per cent chance of default" has smuggled in a recovery assumption, and the honest version names it.

The second reason is that the risky annuity does for credit what duration does for rates. A credit desk's entire position risk is expressed in it, the mark-to-market of any position is a spread change times it, and it is systematically below the risk-free annuity because the premium stream might stop. Understanding that one quantity is most of understanding how credit positions are managed.

The third reason is that wrong-way risk is the mechanism behind the largest credit derivative failures on record, and it is computable. Protection on structured credit bought in size from a small number of sellers whose own solvency depended on the same structured credit performing is the 2008 monoline story in one sentence, and the arithmetic in step 4 shows how little such protection is worth before anything goes wrong.

And a fourth reason about reading a source critically. The summary's list of credit events includes a rating downgrade and fraud, and neither is a credit event under standard documentation. That matters practically: a fund that believes a downgrade triggers its protection has misunderstood what it bought, and downgrades happen far more often than the events that do trigger.

Step 4

The detail

What a credit default swap is, and two corrections to the source's description

The source describes a CDS as an "option to sell underlying if event occurs within term, no payment provided if no event occurs, reference bond, regular premium paid by CDS holder, removes credit risk of underlying, recovery possible". Four of those five clauses are exactly right. The premium is paid by the buyer, no payment is made absent an event, there is a reference obligation, and recovery reduces the payout.

CORRECTION 1: A CDS IS NOT AN OPTION. Lecture 3 established the defining property of an option: the holder has a RIGHT and may choose whether to exercise. In a credit default swap the holder has no choice at all. The credit event triggers the payoff, and if no event occurs there is nothing to elect. That is why the instrument is called a SWAP: a stream of premiums exchanged for a contingent payment. The source itself lists "credit default swaps" among its swap types elsewhere, describing them as a "fixed payment provided if event occurs for regular premiums", which is right. So the two passages disagree, and the swaps passage is the correct one. The distinction is not pedantic. It determines the margin treatment from lecture 3: an option's writer posts margin because the holder has already paid everything it can lose, whereas in a CDS BOTH parties have future obligations (the buyer owes premiums, the seller owes a contingent payment), so both sides carry exposure and both are collateralised. Reading a CDS as an option gets the collateral logic backwards. CORRECTION 2: A RATING DOWNGRADE IS NOT A CREDIT EVENT. NOR IS FRAUD. The source's list is: missing payment, temporary suspension, cancellation of outstanding payments, cross-default, debt restructuring, RATING DOWNGRADE, FRAUD, bankruptcy. The standard credit events under the 2014 ISDA Credit Derivatives Definitions are: Bankruptcy the source has this Failure to Pay the source's "missing payment" Obligation Acceleration related to the source's "cross-default" Obligation Default related to the source's "cross-default" Repudiation / Moratorium the source's "temporary suspension" and "cancel outstanding payments" are close to this Restructuring the source has this Governmental Intervention added in 2014, for financial reference entities A RATING DOWNGRADE IS NOT AMONG THEM AND IS EXPLICITLY NOT A TRIGGER. Neither is fraud as such, though fraud that causes a bankruptcy would trigger through bankruptcy. Why it matters: downgrades are far more frequent than defaults. A holder who believes a downgrade triggers its protection has bought something quite different from what it thinks, and will discover the difference at the worst moment. There ARE instruments that pay on a downgrade, which is what a credit spread option or a ratings-linked structure is for, and they are separate products with separate prices.

The credit triangle, and the recovery-default indeterminacy

To first order: spread = hazard rate x (1 - recovery) So a quoted spread determines the PRODUCT of the two and nothing else. That is why a credit desk quotes SPREADS and a risk model reports PROBABILITIES, and the two are not interchangeable. Any statement of the form "the market implies an X per cent chance of default" has embedded a recovery assumption, and the honest version names it. AND IT RUNS THE OTHER WAY TOO. At a fixed default probability the fair spread scales with the loss given default: hazard rate 3.00%, recovery 20% -> fair spread 240 bp hazard rate 3.00%, recovery 40% -> fair spread 180 bp hazard rate 3.00%, recovery 60% -> fair spread 120 bp hazard rate 3.00%, recovery 80% -> fair spread 60 bp Which is the same identity that asset classes lecture 4 used for the seniority waterfall: one default event, three tranches, three loss given defaults, three fair spreads. The CDS market prices the same quantity the A-IRB expected loss calculation estimates, and the difference is only that one is observed and the other modelled. A PRACTICAL NOTE FROM THE MODELLING SIDE. Market convention assumes a fixed recovery, often 40 per cent for senior unsecured corporates, precisely so that spreads can be compared. That convention makes the quotes consistent and does not make the recovery right, and a portfolio whose true recoveries differ from the convention has a systematically biased implied default probability in a direction the convention conceals.

That is what an upward-sloping credit curve means: either the market expects the credit to deteriorate, or it demands more compensation for the uncertainty of the distant future. The spread alone cannot distinguish those, which is the same ambiguity asset classes lecture 4 identified in breakeven inflation and lecture 2 in forward rates. AND AN INVERTED CREDIT CURVE IS WORTH RECOGNISING because it says something specific. A distressed name often has a HIGHER short spread than long, because if it survives the next two years it has probably restructured and is safer. So inversion is a near-term-crisis signal rather than a curve anomaly. A useful diagnostic falls out of this. If a bootstrap produces NEGATIVE forward hazard rates, the arithmetic is telling you the inputs are inconsistent, and the usual culprit is the recovery assumption: a distressed name's spread can exceed what any hazard rate can generate at an assumed 40 per cent recovery, which means the market is pricing a lower recovery. The model failing is the model reporting information.

Settlement, and why neither of the source's two methods survived alone

The source gives both methods correctly: CASH: the seller pays face less recovery, determined by a valuation PHYSICAL: the buyer delivers the bond and receives face PHYSICAL SETTLEMENT HAS AN ARITHMETIC PROBLEM. The notional of CDS written on a name is not limited by the bonds outstanding: bonds outstanding CDS notional written ratio consequence R2,000,000,000 R 2,000,000,000 1.0x fine R2,000,000,000 R 6,000,000,000 3.0x a squeeze R2,000,000,000 R10,000,000,000 5.0x a severe squeeze R2,000,000,000 R30,000,000,000 15.0x the mechanism fails At five times the deliverable supply, protection buyers compete to buy a bond that has just defaulted, which pushes its price UP and therefore reduces the payout on every contract. The instrument designed to pay face less recovery pays less, precisely because so many people bought it. The protection is self-defeating in proportion to its own popularity. WHICH IS WHY THE MARKET MOVED TO AN AUCTION. A single auction establishes one recovery price for all contracts on the name, the great majority settle in cash against it, and physical delivery is available only in a size the auction itself determines. That removes the squeeze by removing the requirement to find a bond. THE SOURCE PREDATES THAT DEVELOPMENT and describes the two original methods accurately. Neither is wrong; both have been subsumed into a third mechanism that exists precisely because of the arithmetic above. An examination answer should describe both and know that the auction is what actually happens.

The cheapest-to-deliver option

Under physical settlement the BUYER chooses which deliverable obligation to hand over, so it delivers the cheapest. That choice is an option and it has a value. deliverable obligation post-default price payout if delivered senior 2027 42 58 senior 2031 37 63 subordinated 2029 28 72 <-- delivered The buyer delivers the subordinated bond at 28 and receives 72. Had the contract specified a single named bond at the average price of 35.7, the payout would have been 64.3. So the cheapest-to-deliver option is worth 7.7 points of notional, or 11.9 per cent of the protection's value. TWO CONSEQUENCES FOLLOW AND BOTH MATTER. A CDS SPREAD IS NOT DIRECTLY COMPARABLE TO A SINGLE BOND'S SPREAD, because the CDS contains an option the bond does not. That is one source of the basis in the next subsection, and it is a systematic one: it always makes the CDS spread wider than the bond's, never narrower. AND THE OPTION IS WORTH MORE WHEN THE CAPITAL STRUCTURE IS DEEPER AND MORE DISPERSED. A name with subordinated debt outstanding, or with bonds of widely differing maturity and coupon, offers the buyer more to choose from. So the CDS-to-bond basis should be wider for a complex capital structure than for a single senior issue, which is a testable prediction and a reason not to treat the basis as a constant. Note that this interacts with the seniority arithmetic of asset classes lecture 4. There, one default produced three different recoveries across three tranches. Here, the CDS buyer gets to pick which of those three recoveries applies to their contract, which means the protection references the WORST tranche in the structure regardless of what the buyer actually holds.

The CDS-cash basis, and why it is not an arbitrage

Buy the bond and buy protection and you should be left holding a risk-free asset, so the bond's credit spread and the CDS spread should be equal. They are not. the bond trades at a 230 bp spread and the CDS at 250 bp basis = CDS - bond = +20 bp FIVE FRICTIONS EXPLAIN IT, and none of them is a free lunch: FUNDING the bond must be financed at your OWN rate, not the risk-free rate. a 20 basis point funding disadvantage consumes a 20 basis point basis entirely COUNTERPARTY the protection seller may default first, which is the next subsection and can be a large adjustment DELIVERABLE the CDS references a basket and the bond is one instrument, so the cheapest-to-deliver option is in the CDS and not in the bond DEFINITIONS a restructuring can trigger the CDS without impairing the particular bond held, or impair the bond without triggering COLLATERAL the CDS position must be collateralised as it moves, which is a funding cost that varies with the mark and is largest when the trade is most profitable SO A BASIS OF A FEW TENS OF BASIS POINTS IS A FAIR PRICE FOR FIVE REAL FRICTIONS. A NEGATIVE basis, where the CDS trades below the bond spread, is the configuration that historically attracted levered capital, and the failures of that trade were funding failures rather than credit failures: the position was right about the credit and the financing was withdrawn before it paid off. That is worth stating plainly because it generalises. A relative-value trade whose thesis is correct can still fail on the leverage, which is exactly the mechanism asset classes lecture 13 identified in market-neutral strategies: the position is small because the two legs are alike, the leverage is large because the position is small, and the financing is the thing that breaks.

Wrong-way risk: protection from a correlated seller

Buying protection is only useful if the seller survives to pay. If the seller's default is correlated with the reference entity's, the protection fails precisely when it is needed. AT A 0.90 CORRELATION ONLY 60.43 PER CENT. So buying protection on a South African bank from another South African bank, in a crisis that would affect both, purchases considerably less than the notional suggests. AND THE ADJUSTMENT IS THE QUANTITY A BANK CALLS CVA. The protection's fair value must be reduced by the expected loss from the seller's default in exactly those states where the reference has also defaulted. Which is why protection from a weak, correlated seller trades at a LOWER spread than the same protection from a strong uncorrelated one: the market prices the wrong-way risk, and a buyer comparing spreads without adjusting for the seller has mispriced the trade in their own favour on paper. THIS IS THE MECHANISM BEHIND THE 2008 MONOLINE FAILURES IN ONE SENTENCE: protection on structured credit had been bought in size from a small number of sellers whose own solvency depended on the same structured credit performing. The correlation was not 0.90 by coincidence; it was 0.90 by construction, because the seller's entire business was writing that protection. THE PRACTICAL DISCIPLINE THAT FOLLOWS is to ask, before buying any protection, what would have to be true for the seller to fail, and whether that overlaps with what would have to be true for the reference to fail. If the answer is that they are the same conditions, the protection is decoration. Collateralisation and central clearing from lecture 1 are the structural remedies, and both work by removing the reliance on the seller's solvency rather than by improving it.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing an experienced credit person carries is that a spread is a price and a probability of default is an opinion. That sounds like a slogan and it is an operational rule: whenever someone hands you an implied default probability derived from a CDS spread, the first question is what recovery they assumed and the second is what the answer would be at half that recovery. The factor of 3.2 in step 4 is the size of the disagreement available for free, and it is larger than almost any modelling refinement anyone will argue about in the same meeting.

The second is that the risky annuity carries the same weight in credit that duration carries in rates, and for the same structural reason. Both convert a change in a quoted yield-like quantity into money in one multiplication, both are first-order approximations with a convexity correction, and both are the number a desk actually manages. Where they differ is instructive.

On the worked curve the risky annuity is 3.522579 against a risk-free 3.958357, so 89.0 per cent. Push the hazard rate to 40 per cent and it collapses. The position stops responding at exactly the point the credit story is most dramatic, which surprises people every cycle.

The third is the pattern this lecture shares with almost everything before it: a contract's stated terms are not its economics. The cheapest-to-deliver option means a CDS is not protection on the bond you hold but protection referencing the worst bond in the capital structure, worth 11.9 per cent more. The deliverable squeeze means physical settlement pays less the more popular it becomes. Wrong-way risk means protection from a correlated seller delivers 60.4 per cent of what its notional claims. In each case reading the contract literally produces one number and reading it economically produces another, and the second is the one that pays.

The fourth is what to make of the source's two errors, because the specific mistakes carry a general lesson. Calling a CDS an option and listing a rating downgrade as a credit event are not careless slips; both are natural intuitions. A CDS feels optional because it pays only sometimes, and a downgrade feels like a credit event because it is bad credit news. Both intuitions fail for the same reason.

THE TRIGGERS ARE DEFINED LEGALLY, NOT ECONOMICALLY. the economic intuition what the contract says "the credit got worse, so I should be a downgrade is NOT a trigger; the credit must paid" FAIL in one of seven defined ways "it pays only sometimes, so it must there is no election; the event triggers the be optional" payoff whether the holder wants it or not "I hold this bond, so I am hedged" the reference is a BASKET and the buyer delivers the cheapest member of it "a restructuring hurt me, so I claim" whether it triggers depends on which restructuring clause was selected at trade date Each of those gaps was negotiated by people who knew exactly which ambiguity they were closing. Which is why credit derivative documentation is long, and why "WHAT DOES THE CONTRACT ACTUALLY SAY" is not a lawyer's question but a pricing one. The 2014 definitions exist because each earlier version was tested by a real default and found wanting somewhere.

And the fifth is the one worth carrying furthest. The monoline failures of 2008 were not a failure of credit analysis; the analysts had the reference credits roughly right. They were a failure to notice that the protection had been concentrated in sellers whose solvency was a function of the same exposures. The arithmetic in step 4 is available before the event and not only after it, and the question it answers is answerable in a sentence: what would have to be true for my counterparty to fail, and is it the same thing that would make me claim? An institution that asks that of every hedge it holds has done most of the work CVA formalises.

Where the arithmetic here is deliberately simple Three simplifications run through this lecture and each matters in practice. Annual premium steps rather than quarterly accrual produce the R186,043 gap between the premium and protection legs, which is discretisation and not mispricing. A constant recovery assumption is imposed where the market convention imposes it, and the whole of step 4's first subsection is about why that convention conceals rather than solves the problem. And the Gaussian copula used for wrong-way risk is the same model whose tail behaviour securitisation lecture 10 criticised, so the 60.43 per cent figure should be read as an illustration of the mechanism rather than a calibrated number: a heavier-tailed dependence structure gives a worse answer, not a better one.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The counterparty problem, collateral and central clearing are from derivative markets, and this lecture supplies the calculation that justifies them: at a 0.90 correlation only 60.43 per cent of uncollateralised protection works. The property that defines an option, namely that the holder chooses, is from options, and it is the test that shows a CDS is a swap despite the source's phrasing. The architecture of two legs valued separately is from swaps, with a contingent payment replacing the floating leg and a survival-weighted annuity replacing the risk-free one. The bootstrap that turns a quoted curve into forwards is from swaptions, caps and floors and forwards and futures, and it carries the same warning: the rise from 2.00 to 6.50 per cent is a price, not a forecast. Spread as compensation for expected loss and the seniority waterfall that the cheapest-to-deliver option exploits are from government and corporate bonds, the tranche loss attachment behind credit-linked notes is from securitisation and insurance-linked securities, and the observation that a relative-value trade can be right about its thesis and still fail on its financing is from hedge funds.

Where this goes next. Lecture 7 covers contracts for difference and the remaining over-the-counter contracts, closing the module. Treating a credit spread as a component of a required return, and the reason it cannot be decomposed into a default expectation without naming a recovery, belong to valuation. The wrong-way risk arithmetic is the core of counterparty exposure measurement in risk measurement, and the observation that protection concentrated in few sellers is undiversified however many reference names it covers belongs in portfolio construction, alongside the same argument about reinsurance concentration.

Where the bank sits opposite. The credit triangle is the same object as the A-IRB expected loss calculation, one observed and one modelled, so a bank whose modelled figures are inconsistent with the CDS market on its own name has learned something: see A-IRB capital for the modelled side and credit risk measurement for the framing. The wrong-way risk computed in step 4 is exactly what the counterparty credit risk framework sets capital against, and CVA is its accounting counterpart. A bank buying protection to hedge a large single exposure is managing credit concentration risk and should note that it has swapped a concentration in the reference name for a concentration in the protection seller. The trading book that warehouses these positions sits under market risk, and the loss provisioning that a triggered event forces is IFRS 9. Note that the source attributes additional capital on over-the-counter trades to IFRS 9; IFRS 9 governs the accounting for expected credit losses, while capital is set by the Basel counterparty credit risk framework, and the two should not be conflated.
