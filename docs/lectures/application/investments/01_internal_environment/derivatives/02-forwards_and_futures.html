Forwards and futures: one arbitrage, four markets, and three ways a hedge falls short

The forward price is not a forecast. It is whatever makes buying spot with borrowed money break even against selling forward, and the single formula behind that produced covered interest parity, the commodity curve and the bond forward in earlier lectures. What is worth the effort here is the gap between a forward and a future: the FRA is concave in the rate, the futures contract is exactly linear, and daily margining makes the futures rate exceed the forward rate by 74 basis points at ten years.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A forward is, in the source's words, non-standardised and tailored, on a specific underlying, at a set date and a set price, with no premium paid. A future is the same economic contract standardised and exchange-traded. The two differ in three respects that matter: the future is margined daily, the future's terms are fixed by the exchange rather than negotiated, and the future can be closed out rather than delivered. Everything in this lecture follows from those three.

The price of both is an arbitrage result. Buy spot with borrowed money, collect whatever the asset yields, pay whatever storing it costs, deliver at maturity: the forward price that makes that break even is F = S·e^((r−q+u)T), and it is the only price that can persist. That one formula gave covered interest parity when q is the foreign interest rate, the commodity curve when u is storage and q the convenience yield, and a bond forward below spot whenever the coupon yield exceeds the financing rate.

Two things then separate the future from the forward. A forward rate agreement settles at the start of the period it covers, so the interest difference must be discounted: on R100m at an agreed 8 per cent against a realised 10 per cent over 91 days, the undiscounted difference is R498,630 and the payment is R486,501. That discounting makes the FRA concave in the realised rate. The interest rate future, by the source's own formula, is worth exactly R25 per basis point at every rate, which makes it exactly linear. So a futures hedge of an FRA exposure is over-hedged whichever way rates move.

And daily margining drives a wedge between the two prices. A short futures position receives cash when rates rise and reinvests it at the higher rate, so the margining flow is systematically favourable and the futures rate must exceed the forward rate. The standard adjustment is half sigma squared times T₁T₂, which at a 120 basis point rate volatility is 0.45 basis points at one year and 73.8 basis points at ten. A curve bootstrapped from long-dated futures without the correction is wrong by that much.

Feynman test Agreeing today to buy something next year does not require a view about next year. It requires knowing what it costs to buy the thing now, hold it, and borrow the money, because anyone who can do that will undercut a price that says otherwise. The only interesting differences between a private agreement and an exchange contract are that the exchange one settles up every night and comes in fixed sizes, and both of those cost the hedger something measurable.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the cost-of-carry relation is the most reused single formula in the module and it has now appeared four times under different names. Seeing it as one relation with four parameterisations is worth more than four memorised special cases, and it means a new underlying can be priced by asking only what the asset yields and what it costs to hold.

The second reason is that the convexity difference between an FRA and an interest rate future is the kind of detail that separates a working knowledge from a nominal one. Both instruments hedge the same rate. One is concave and one is linear, so a hedge that matches at the current rate diverges as the rate moves, always in the same direction. A hedger who sizes the hedge on the slope alone is over-hedged in both directions and does not know it.

The third reason is that the futures-forward convexity adjustment is not a curiosity. At the long end it is 74 basis points, which is large enough to move a bootstrapped discount curve materially and therefore every valuation derived from it. The source states the result and gives a formula; the arithmetic shows when it can be ignored, which is at the front of the curve, and when it cannot.

And a fourth reason about hedging honestly. The source names three sources of basis risk and the minimum-variance formula turns them into a number: at a 0.95 correlation between the hedged exposure and the contract, 31.2 per cent of the risk survives a perfectly executed hedge. A fourth source the source omits is contract rounding, which is negligible for a large hedger and can leave a small one 67 per cent mis-hedged in either direction.

Why the futures rate exceeds the forward rate

The source states the result and gives a formula. The mechanism is daily margining, from lecture 1. A short interest rate futures position gains when rates rise. Those gains are paid in cash immediately and reinvested at the new, higher rate. Losses when rates fall must be funded immediately, at the new, lower rate. So the margining flow is systematically favourable to the short, and the futures rate must be HIGHER than the forward rate to compensate the other side. forward rate = futures rate - (1/2) sigma^2 T1 T2 where sigma is the annual standard deviation of the change in the short rate in absolute terms, T1 the futures contract's maturity and T2 the maturity of the rate underlying it. AND THE SOURCE'S SECOND REMARK IS THE GENERAL CASE. It says that if the asset price is negatively correlated with interest rates, the futures price is BELOW the unmargined forward price. Same mechanism, opposite sign: margin gains arrive when rates are falling and are reinvested at a lower rate, which is unfavourable, so the futures price must be lower. THE SIGN OF THE ADJUSTMENT IS THE SIGN OF THE CORRELATION between the underlying and the financing rate, and the rate-futures case is simply the one where that correlation is minus one by construction.

Delivery, close-out, and why the basis must converge

At delivery the futures price and the spot price must be equal, because the contract IS the spot asset at that moment. The basis therefore converges to zero by arbitrage, and that convergence is the reason the contract hedges anything at all. So the basis decomposes: basis = a CARRY component, predictable from r, q and time remaining + a RANDOM component, from supply and demand in the contract itself A HEDGER WHO HOLDS TO DELIVERY BEARS ONLY THE CARRY and faces no basis risk at all, because convergence is certain. One who closes out early bears both components. So the source's "uncertain date of sale/purchase" and "closing out required" are the same risk described twice, and the practical remedy follows directly: choose a contract whose delivery date is just AFTER the hedge horizon rather than just before, so the position is closed with the basis as small as it can be made. AND CHECK THE OPEN INTEREST BEFORE COMMITTING TO THAT CHOICE, using lecture 1's ratio. The far contract may have the right maturity and almost no open interest, in which case the execution cost and the rounding of using it exceed the basis risk of using a nearer one. The two lectures' arithmetic has to be applied together, and neither answer is right alone.

The grandmaster's perspective

A forward and a future are the same trade with the cash flows rearranged in time

Both contracts fix a price today for a transaction later. The economic difference is entirely about when money moves, and every distinction in this lecture follows from that single rearrangement. Setting them side by side makes the pattern explicit.

What the source gets right, and one thing worth stating more carefully

Its contract taxonomy is exact and its list of what a futures specification fixes (size, underlying details, currency, settlement price, delivery date, tick size) is the complete list. Its identification of the futures-forward difference, and of the sign condition through the correlation with interest rates, is a genuinely sophisticated point that many treatments omit, and it is stated correctly. Its three sources of basis risk are the right three.

One thing needs stating more carefully than the summary does. It describes forwards as having "no premium paid" and lists that alongside the same feature for futures, which is correct and can mislead. No premium is paid because the contract is struck at the forward price, which makes its initial value zero by construction. That is not the same as the contract being free: it has a value that becomes non-zero the moment the market moves, and for a forward that value is an unsecured claim on a counterparty. An option, by contrast, has a positive initial value and therefore a premium. So "no premium" is a statement about where the strike was set, not about the contract being costless, and a candidate who reads it the second way will not understand why forwards create credit exposure at all.

The other item worth flagging is one the source gets exactly right and phrases briefly: that a futures position can be closed out rather than delivered, and that open interest falls as delivery approaches. Put together with this lecture's convergence arithmetic, those two facts explain why almost nobody delivers and why the contract still works: the threat of delivery is what forces convergence, and convergence is what makes the contract hedge. The mechanism nobody uses is the one that makes the instrument function.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The margining mechanics, the potential future exposure figures compared in step 5, the contract specification list and open interest are all from derivative markets, and the convexity adjustment in step 4 is that lecture's daily settlement seen from the pricing side. The cost-of-carry relation has appeared three times before: as covered interest parity and the commodity curve in commodities and currency, and as the bond forward in government and corporate bonds, which also supplied the bootstrapping that step 4 warns must be corrected. The arbitrage band is the structure established in collective schemes and ETFs, and the rounding problem is the divisibility arithmetic from property and infrastructure.

Where this goes next. Lecture 3 covers options, where the payoff is asymmetric and the premium is genuinely a premium rather than a consequence of where the strike was set. Lecture 4 covers swaps, which are a strip of the forward rate agreements priced here. Lecture 5's caps and floors are strips of options, and lecture 6's credit derivatives replace the market variable with a credit event. The pricing of all of them, including the curve construction this lecture warns about, is valuation, and their use in a portfolio, including the hedge ratio arithmetic, is portfolio management. The basis risk quantified here is one of the eight risks catalogued in risk measurement.

Where the bank runs the same arithmetic. A bank's interest rate futures and forward rate agreements sit in its trading book under market risk, where the convexity of the FRA against the linearity of the future is a basis risk with a capital charge. The unmargined forward's growing exposure is measured in counterparty credit risk, where the supervisor prescribes the add-on this lecture derived from a volatility assumption. A bank hedging its own structural rate position with futures faces exactly the basis and rounding problems in step 4, in interest rate risk, and the discount curve the convexity adjustment corrects is the one used throughout financial management.
