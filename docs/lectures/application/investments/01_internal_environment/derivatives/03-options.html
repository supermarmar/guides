Options: the asymmetry that explains the margin, the ladder and the premium

The source's sharpest line about options is that the writer posts margin and the buyer does not, and the reason is exact: the buyer's entire possible loss was paid at inception, so there is nothing left to default on. That one asymmetry also orders the four exercise styles into a price ladder, makes the writer's margin necessarily dynamic because the loss is convex, and decides which side of an option trade earns the equity risk premium and which side pays it.

Step 1

The 30-second version

An option gives the holder a right and imposes on the writer an obligation, and the source's description is complete: standardised or tailored, on a specific underlying, at a set date and a set price, with a premium paid. That last item is the difference from lecture 2's forward, where no premium changed hands because the contract was struck at the forward price. Here the payoff is asymmetric, so the contract has value at inception and somebody must pay for it.

Everything else follows from the asymmetry. The source notes that the writer pays initial and variation margin and the buyer pays none, and the reason is that the buyer's maximum loss is the premium, already paid, leaving no future obligation to secure. A short call's loss is unbounded and a short put's is bounded only by the strike, so both need margin. That is a cleaner rule than the one governing futures, where the exposure is symmetric and both sides post.

The four exercise styles the source lists form a price ladder rather than a taxonomy. At a 100 strike, one year, 8 per cent rates and 25 per cent volatility, the European call is worth R13.8922 and the Asian call about R10.2029, because averaging cuts the effective volatility to sigma over root three. The American call on a non-dividend payer equals the European exactly; the American put is strictly worth more, and the whole of its extra value is interest on the strike received early, capped at R7.6884.

And the writer's margin must be dynamic because the loss is convex. A three standard deviation move costs a short call 3.40 times its one standard deviation loss where a linear position would lose exactly 3.0 times. That single fact is why option margining uses scenario grids rather than a volatility number, and it is the one place where option margining differs structurally from futures margining rather than in its parameters.

Feynman test A forward is a promise both ways, so both parties can let each other down and both must post collateral. An option is a promise one way: the buyer has already paid everything they can lose, so nobody needs security from them, and the writer has promised something open-ended and must. Every technical feature of options is that sentence worked out.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the margin asymmetry is the clearest single illustration in the module of a general principle: collateral secures obligations, not positions. A buyer with an enormous unrealised gain posts nothing, because they owe nothing. A writer with a small unrealised loss posts a great deal, because what it might owe is open-ended. Getting that right makes the whole of derivatives credit exposure intuitive rather than memorised.

The second reason is that put-call parity is the most useful relation in the module and it contains no volatility. It holds whatever the pricing model, at every strike simultaneously, which makes it the first thing to check on any option quote and the bridge back to lecture 2: a long call plus a short put at the same strike is a forward, so the option market can manufacture the forward and the two markets must price consistently.

The third reason is that the exercise styles are examinable and the reasoning behind their ordering is more valuable than the ordering itself. The American call equalling the European on a non-dividend payer, and the American put's premium being entirely an interest rate effect that vanishes at a zero rate, are both results a candidate can derive rather than recall, and both are frequently got wrong.

And a fourth reason about option writing as a strategy. On the worked figures, writing a call loses R2.8576 in expectation and writing a put gains R1.2235, because the premium was computed at the risk-free drift and the underlying is expected to drift faster. Selling index puts earns the equity risk premium in a levered, negatively skewed form, which is the same payoff shape as the carry trade and the range accrual from earlier lectures. Three instruments, one shape.

So margin sized on a normal move understates the tail, and the understatement grows with the size of the move: 8.8 per cent too low at two standard deviations, 13.3 per cent at three, 15.8 per cent at four. WHICH IS WHY OPTION MARGINING USES SCENARIO GRIDS rather than a single volatility number. The clearing house revalues the whole position at a set of price and volatility shocks and takes the worst outcome, which captures the convexity directly instead of approximating it. That is the one respect in which option margining differs structurally from futures margining rather than merely in its parameters, and lecture 1's formula does not apply. NOTE ALSO THE SECOND ROW OF THE DELTA TABLE. A short call's delta rises as the position loses money, so the writer's exposure to the next move is largest when the last move has already hurt. That is the same procyclical shape as the variation margin problem in lecture 1 and the private equity capital call problem in asset classes lecture 7: the obligation grows precisely when it is hardest to meet.

Is writing options profitable? The answer has a sign

The premium is the RISK-NEUTRAL expected payoff, discounted. Under a real-world drift the expected payoff differs, and the difference is systematic. Real-world drift 12 per cent against a risk-free 8 per cent, an equity risk premium of 4: position premium real-world expected payoff expected profit to the WRITER short call 13.8922 16.7497 -2.8576 short put 6.2038 4.9803 +1.2235 WRITING A CALL LOSES IN EXPECTATION AND WRITING A PUT GAINS. The reason is the equity risk premium: the underlying is expected to drift up faster than the risk-free rate, which helps the call holder and hurts the put holder, and the premium was struck as though the drift were the risk-free rate. So the call writer is SHORT the equity risk premium and the put writer is LONG it. Neither position is free money and neither is a mispricing: both are correctly priced under the risk-neutral measure, which is the only measure under which they can be hedged. THE SHAPE IS ONE THIS COURSE HAS MET TWICE ALREADY. Selling index puts earns the equity risk premium in a levered, negatively skewed form: small frequent gains, rare large losses, a flattering Sharpe ratio, and a tail that arrives when everything else has also gone wrong. Asset classes lecture 9 described the carry trade in those exact terms and lecture 13's range accrual too. THREE DIFFERENT INSTRUMENTS, ONE PAYOFF SHAPE, and a Sharpe ratio is the wrong statistic for all three. AND THE COVERED CALL IS THE OPPOSITE TRADE, worth writing out because it is so widely misunderstood as an income strategy: final price 70 85 100 115 130 160 stock alone -30.00 -15.00 +0.00 +15.00 +30.00 +60.00 covered call -16.11 -1.11 +13.89 +13.89 +13.89 +13.89 difference +13.89 +13.89 +13.89 -1.11 -16.11 -46.11 The premium is kept below the strike and the entire upside is surrendered above it. So a covered call reduces variance and reduces the expected return, because it gives away the equity risk premium on the upside in exchange for a certain premium. It is a variance trade and it is bought for its distribution rather than its mean, which is a defensible reason and not the reason it is usually sold.

The grandmaster's perspective

Three instruments in this course have had the same payoff shape

Selling an index put, the carry trade of asset classes lecture 9, and the range accrual of asset classes lecture 13 are three unrelated instruments in three different markets. They have the same distribution, and recognising that is worth more than any of the three individually.

Somebody must bear that risk and being paid to do so is a legitimate business, which is what an insurance company is. The error is not writing the option; it is writing the option and describing the resulting return series as low risk because its measured volatility is low.

What the option's premium buys that a forward's does not

feature FORWARD (lecture 2) OPTION obligation BOTH parties are obliged ONE party is obliged initial value zero, by choice of strike positive, hence the premium premium none, because value is zero the value at inception payoff LINEAR in the underlying KINKED at the strike margin both sides the writer only maximum loss to the buyer unbounded the premium the hedge it provides exact, both directions one-sided: protection without giving up the favourable move what it costs nothing upfront, everything the premium, and nothing more if the market moves against THE FOURTH ROW IS THE ECONOMIC DIFFERENCE AND THE REST FOLLOW FROM IT. A linear payoff can be replicated by holding the underlying with borrowed money, which is why a forward has no premium: it is the carry trade of lecture 2 written as a contract. A kinked payoff cannot be replicated by any static position, which is why it costs money and why its value depends on volatility while a forward's does not. AND THAT IS THE PRACTICAL CHOICE A HEDGER FACES. A forward removes both tails at no upfront cost. An option removes one tail for a fee. Which is right depends on whether the favourable outcome is worth keeping, which is a question about the liability rather than about the market: a fund that must meet a fixed obligation gains nothing from a favourable move and should use the forward, and one whose obligation is open-ended should consider paying for the option.

What the source gets right, and one thing it leaves implicit

Its options entry is short and unusually well chosen. The right-against-obligation distinction, the premium, the four exercise styles, the observation that the trading process matches futures, and the margin asymmetry are the five things that matter, and the margin asymmetry in particular is a point many longer treatments state without explaining. Its note that the contract specification is the same as a future's plus a strike price and a premium is exactly right and is the efficient way to remember it.

What it leaves implicit is why the exercise styles exist at all, and the answer is worth stating because it makes the ladder memorable. Each style is a different answer to the question "when may the holder choose?" and each answer has a purpose. A European option is the cheapest and the easiest to value, so it is the default. An American option suits a holder who may need the underlying at an unpredictable time, and costs more for exactly that flexibility. A Bermudan option matches a schedule of possible needs, such as a bond's call dates. And an Asian option suits a holder whose exposure is itself an average, such as a company hedging a year of monthly purchases, in which case the Asian option is not merely cheaper but a better hedge, because it matches the exposure's shape.

That last point deserves emphasis because the arithmetic makes the Asian option look like a cost-saving compromise and it is often the opposite. A treasurer hedging twelve monthly currency payments with a single European option at the year-end rate has hedged the wrong thing: their exposure is the average rate, not the final rate. The Asian option is cheaper and matches, which is the only case in this module where the cheaper instrument is also the better hedge, and it is a direct application of lecture 2's basis risk arithmetic to the choice of payoff rather than to the choice of contract month.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The margin mechanics, the distinction between initial and variation margin, and the clearing house's role are from derivative markets, whose margin formula step 4 shows does not apply to options. The forward that put-call parity manufactures, and the basis risk arithmetic that step 5 applies to the choice of payoff, are from forwards and futures. The negatively skewed payoff shape recurs from commodities and currency and hedge funds, where the same diagnostic applied. The embedded option in a convertible is government and corporate bonds, and the option inside a capital-guaranteed note is structured products, where the participation rate was that option's price.

Where this goes next. Lecture 4 covers swaps, which are strips of the forward rate agreements from lecture 2. Lecture 5 covers swaptions, caps, floors and collars, which are strips of the options priced here, and where put-call parity reappears as the relation between a cap, a floor and a swap. Lecture 6's credit derivatives replace the market variable with a credit event but keep the option structure. The Greeks and the hedging of an option book belong to valuation, and the use of options to reshape a portfolio's distribution is portfolio management.

Where the bank is on the other side. A bank writing options runs the convexity in step 4 as a book rather than a position, hedged dynamically, and the residual sits in the trading book under market risk, where the non-linearity is precisely why a delta-based capital measure was replaced by one capturing curvature. The scenario grids in step 4 are the same technique a bank applies to its own book. An option sold to a client creates a receivable whose exposure is measured in counterparty credit risk, and the asymmetry means the bank has counterparty exposure to a writer and none to a buyer, which is the same rule as step 4 read from the other direction.
