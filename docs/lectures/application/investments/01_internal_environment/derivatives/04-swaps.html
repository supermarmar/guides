Swaps: the swap rate is the par yield, and thirteen variants change four things

A fixed-for-floating swap is worth zero at inception and a floating leg is worth par, so the fixed rate is whatever coupon prices a bond at par. That is the par yield computed in an earlier lecture, arrived at from a different market: 9.6039 per cent on the same curve, to four decimal places. The rest of the lecture is what happens after inception, where a swap's credit exposure turns out to be humped rather than growing, and a currency swap's is sixteen times larger at nine years.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A swap is, in the source's words, a series of payments traded on a prearranged formula on set dates. The vanilla case exchanges fixed interest for floating with no principal exchanged, and the source is precise about why a currency swap differs: there the principal is exchanged at the start and the end, at the spot rate. That single asymmetry accounts for most of the practical difference between the two instruments.

The pricing follows from one observation. A swap must be worth zero at inception, or nobody would enter it, and a floating leg is worth par because a floating note reprices at every reset. So the fixed rate is the coupon that prices a bond at par: the par yield. On the curve bootstrapped in asset classes lecture 4, the three-year swap rate is 0.242446 divided by 2.524459, which is 9.6039 per cent, identical to the par yield computed there. The swap rate is not a new quantity.

After inception the source offers two valuation routes and does not choose between them: as the difference between a fixed and a floating bond, or as a strip of forward rate agreements. On a R100m three-year swap struck at par, after a 150 basis point rise, both routes give a receive-fixed value of minus R1,539,192, agreeing exactly. They are the same calculation rearranged, and a receive-fixed swap is a long bond position with all of lecture 4's duration arithmetic attached.

And the credit exposure behaves unlike anything in lecture 1. Rate uncertainty grows with the square root of time while the remaining payments shrink, so an interest rate swap's exposure is humped, peaking at 9.25 per cent of notional about four years into a ten-year swap. A currency swap's does not amortise at all, because the principal exchange is at risk to the very end: 45.0 per cent against 2.8 per cent at nine years, a factor of sixteen.

Feynman test Two parties agree to swap one stream of payments for another. Neither hands over any capital, so the only question is what fixed rate makes the swap worth nothing today, and the answer is the rate a bond would need to trade at par. Everything afterwards is bookkeeping, except for one thing: how much you could lose if the other side disappears, and that turns out to peak in the middle rather than at the end.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the swap rate being the par yield collapses two apparently separate markets into one. A swap curve and a government curve are the same object measured against different credits, which is why they are quoted as a spread and why that spread is a meaningful number rather than an accounting artefact. A candidate who can derive the swap rate from a bootstrapped curve does not need to learn swap pricing separately.

The second reason is that swaps are the largest over-the-counter market and therefore the main subject of lecture 1's clearing mandate. Understanding why their exposure is humped, and why a currency swap's is not, explains the regulatory treatment rather than merely reciting it: the two instruments have genuinely different exposure profiles and are collateralised differently for that reason.

The third reason is that the thirteen variants the source lists are not thirteen things to memorise. Each changes exactly one of four features: the notional profile, the term, the reference on the floating leg, or the currency. That four-way split places a variant the source does not list, which is the same method asset classes lecture 14 applied to investment innovations.

And a fourth reason for an actuary specifically. Two of the source's variants, the longevity swap and the inflation swap, are the instruments through which a pension fund transfers its two largest non-market risks. The inflation swap's fixed leg is the breakeven inflation rate from asset classes lecture 4, with the same three contaminations, so the instrument is cleaner than a pair of bonds and the inference from it is exactly as contaminated.

Valuing a swap after inception: two routes, one answer

Route A is faster to compute and route B shows where the value comes from, so both have a use. THE SIGN IS THE USEFUL PART. Rates ROSE and the RECEIVE-FIXED position LOST money, because it is now receiving a below-market fixed rate. A receive-fixed swap is a long bond position in disguise, so everything in asset classes lecture 4 about duration applies to it directly: its sensitivity is the duration of the fixed leg, and a fund using swaps to extend duration is buying a bond without buying a bond.

Why no principal in an interest rate swap and principal in a currency swap

The source states both facts and not the reason, which is one sentence. in a SINGLE-currency swap the two principals are IDENTICAL, so exchanging them would be handing each other the same amount on the same day. They cancel and are dropped. in a CROSS-currency swap they are in DIFFERENT currencies and do not cancel, so they must be exchanged, at the spot rate, at the start and again at the end. R1,800,000,000 against $100,000,000 at a spot of 18.00, paying 9 per cent in rands and receiving 5 per cent in dollars over five years: time ZAR leg USD leg 0 +R1,800,000,000 -$100,000,000 1 -R 162,000,000 +$ 5,000,000 2 -R 162,000,000 +$ 5,000,000 3 -R 162,000,000 +$ 5,000,000 4 -R 162,000,000 +$ 5,000,000 5 -R1,962,000,000 +$ 105,000,000 SO A CURRENCY SWAP IS A PAIR OF BONDS: short a rand bond, long a dollar bond, with the value being the difference converted at the prevailing rate. The principal exchange is not optional decoration; without it the two legs are not bonds and the instrument does not replicate anything. AND THAT IS WHY IT IS THE NATURAL INSTRUMENT FOR A BORROWER. A South African company that can issue cheaply in dollars but needs rands issues the dollar bond and swaps it, ending with rand liabilities and a dollar bond it never has to think about. The principal exchange at inception funds the purchase and the one at maturity repays it, which is the whole point.

THIS IS THE ONLY INSTRUMENT IN THE MODULE WITH THIS SHAPE, and it matters because lecture 1's square-root rule cannot be applied to it. A swap's peak exposure is smaller than a forward's terminal exposure of the same term and volatility, and it arrives at a different time, so both the size and the timing of the collateral requirement differ.

A currency swap's exposure never amortises, and is far larger

The principal exchange at maturity is at risk until the final day and does not amortise. So the exposure keeps growing with the square root of time throughout. Two causes, and they are separable. The FX volatility is fifteen times the rate volatility in absolute terms, which accounts for the level. And the absence of amortisation accounts for the shape. Even at equal volatilities the currency swap would have the worse profile, because the exposure would still grow rather than hump. WHICH EXPLAINS A MARKET FACT WORTH KNOWING: cross-currency swaps are collateralised more aggressively than interest rate swaps and attract more capital, and it is not because they are more exotic. The exposure profile is genuinely different in shape as well as in level, so the same collateral schedule applied to both would be adequate for one and not the other.

An inflation swap's fixed rate is the breakeven

A zero-coupon inflation swap pays realised inflation against a fixed rate, and must be worth zero at inception. So the fixed rate is whatever makes the two legs equal, which is the breakeven inflation rate from asset classes lecture 4: nominal 10.50%, index-linked real 4.50% -> breakeven 5.7416% so the inflation swap's fixed leg trades at 5.7416% AND THE SAME THREE CONTAMINATIONS APPLY, unchanged: risk premium 0 bp, ILB illiquidity 0 bp -> implied expected inflation 5.7416% risk premium 40 bp, ILB illiquidity 30 bp -> 5.6416% risk premium 60 bp, ILB illiquidity 10 bp -> 5.2416% SO THE INSTRUMENT IMPROVED AND THE INFERENCE DID NOT. An inflation swap is a cleaner way to TRADE the breakeven than buying one bond and selling another, because it isolates the exposure in a single contract with no duration, no credit and no relative liquidity to worry about. It is exactly as contaminated as an ESTIMATE of expected inflation, because the contamination is in the market's pricing rather than in the instrument's construction. THAT DISTINCTION IS WORTH GENERALISING. A cleaner instrument gives a cleaner exposure and not a cleaner inference, and confusing the two is how a trading desk's convenience becomes an economist's estimate. FOR A PENSION FUND THE PRACTICAL POINT IS DIFFERENT AGAIN. An inflation swap hedges inflation-linked liabilities without requiring the fund to own index-linked bonds, which matters because the index-linked market is usually too small to supply the demand. So the swap is a capacity solution as much as a hedging one, and its counterparty exposure is the price of that capacity.

TWO OF THEM NEED A SECOND LOOK. THE PUTTABLE AND EXTENDABLE SWAPS ARE OPTIONS, so step 1's zero-value argument does not price them. Whoever holds the right has been given something, so they must pay for it in the rate: a puttable swap's fixed rate is worse for the holder than a vanilla swap's. That is lecture 3's asymmetry, and it is the convertible bond's mechanism from asset classes lecture 4 in a different instrument: the premium is embedded in the coupon rather than paid across. THE TOTAL RETURN SWAP IS SYNTHETIC OWNERSHIP, priced in asset classes lecture 14. At a 40 basis point financing spread against a 30 basis point round trip and 3 basis points of custody, the break-even holding period is 0.81 years: the swap is cheaper for short holdings and the physical asset for long ones. And it substitutes counterparty risk for ownership, which is the degradation that lecture identified in every wrapper.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

A swap is the instrument for changing what you already have

Every other contract in this module acquires or disposes of an exposure. A swap does something narrower and more useful: it changes the character of an exposure already held, without moving the underlying asset or liability. That is why swaps are the largest derivative market by notional and why they appear in almost every institutional balance sheet.

the problem the swap that solves it what does NOT move a fund holds fixed-rate bonds and receive floating, pay fixed the bonds wants floating exposure a company can borrow cheaply in a currency swap the bond it issued dollars and needs rands a pension fund has inflation-linked an inflation swap the asset portfolio liabilities and cannot buy enough index-linked bonds a pension fund bears longevity risk a longevity swap everything else it has no wish to hold an investor wants an index's return a total return swap no shares are bought for six months without buying it a borrower has floating-rate debt and pay fixed, receive floating the loan agreement wants certainty of payments THE COMMON FEATURE IS THAT THE UNDERLYING TRANSACTION IS UNTOUCHED. The bonds stay, the loan stays, the liability stays, and a separate contract adjusts the cash flows. That separation is the whole economic value of the instrument and it has two consequences worth naming. FIRST, IT IS CHEAPER THAN THE ALTERNATIVE. Selling the bonds and buying floating ones incurs the round-trip cost of lecture 2, and the swap incurs a spread instead. For a temporary change the swap wins on exactly the arithmetic lecture 2 used for synthetic against physical exposure. SECOND, IT LEAVES TWO EXPOSURES WHERE THERE WAS ONE. The fund now holds bonds AND a swap, so it has the original market risk, offset, plus a new counterparty risk that did not exist before, with the humped profile from step 4. The net market exposure fell and the number of things that can go wrong rose, which is the trade every derivative in this module makes and this one makes most often.

What the source gets right, and two things worth adding

Its thirteen-variant list is more complete than most treatments and includes instruments an actuary genuinely meets: the longevity swap, the inflation swap and the total return swap. Its identification of the two valuation routes, as a bond difference and as a strip of forward rate agreements, is exactly the pair a practitioner uses, and offering both without choosing is the right editorial decision because each has its use. Its note that a zero-coupon swap hedges better than a par swap is correct and non-obvious: a par swap's single rate is a blend across the curve, so it hedges a blend, whereas separately traded payments hedge each point.

Two things are worth adding to it. The first is the reason for the principal exchange, which the source states as a fact twice without connecting the two statements: the principals cancel in one currency and do not in two. The second, and more consequential, is that the two instruments have different exposure shapes and not merely different sizes. Nothing in the source suggests that an interest rate swap's credit exposure peaks in the middle and returns to zero, and that fact governs how the instrument is collateralised, capitalised and limited.

One phrase in the source is worth reading carefully rather than correcting. It says an interest rate swap uses the "forward floating rate from previous CF used", which is a compressed way of stating the market convention that each floating payment is set at the start of its period and paid at the end. That convention is why the first floating payment of a swap is already known at inception, and it is the same convention that made the forward rate agreement in lecture 2 settle at the start of its period and require discounting. Same convention, two instruments, and the source states it once in each place without linking them.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The bootstrapped curve, the par yield that turns out to be the swap rate, the duration arithmetic behind a receive-fixed position, and the breakeven inflation decomposition are all from government and corporate bonds. The forward rate agreements that a swap is a strip of, and the settlement convention that makes the first floating payment known at inception, are from forwards and futures. The potential future exposure that step 4 shows does not apply here is from derivative markets, and the reference rate transition that has emptied most of the credit out of a swap spread is from money market instruments. The embedded-option pricing of a puttable swap is options, and the synthetic ownership break-even is new ways of investing in old asset classes.

Where this goes next. Lecture 5 covers swaptions, caps, floors and collars, which are options on the instrument priced here, and where put-call parity reappears as the relation between a cap, a floor and a swap. Lecture 6 covers credit default swaps, which the source lists among swap types and which replace the market variable with a credit event. Lecture 7 covers contracts for difference. The curve construction underlying all of it is valuation, and the use of swaps to match a liability's duration and inflation profile is portfolio management and portfolio construction.

Where the bank runs the other side. A bank is the counterparty to almost every swap in this lecture, so the humped exposure profile in step 4 is exactly what the standardised approach in counterparty credit risk is calibrated to reproduce, and the currency swap's non-amortising profile is why that framework treats the two asset classes separately. A bank using swaps to manage its own structural rate position is doing so under interest rate risk, where a receive-fixed swap extends duration exactly as step 4 describes. The swap spread's components, including the regulatory demand for government paper, connect to liquidity risk, and the trading book's swap positions to market risk.
