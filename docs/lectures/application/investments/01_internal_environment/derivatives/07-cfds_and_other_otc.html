Contracts for difference and embedded bond options: a one-off saving against a running cost

The source calls a contract for difference cost-efficient. It is, for 8.5 days. The structural saving is a one-off 0.25 per cent of transfer tax and the financing is a running 10.75 per cent charged on the full exposure, so the crossover against simply buying the share arrives inside a fortnight. The same lecture closes the module with the embedded bond options the source lists but does not price, where a call worth 46.9 basis points of yield turns a bond's convexity from plus 20 to minus 10.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A contract for difference pays the buyer the rise in an asset's value and the seller the fall, with no transfer of the asset. The source's description is exactly right and its payoff is linear and symmetric, which makes it a forward rather than anything more exotic. What the source's five listed features hide is the arithmetic: at 10 per cent margin and a 50 per cent close-out rule the position is liquidated on a 5 per cent adverse move, which a 30 per cent volatility share touches with 56.4 per cent probability inside a month.

The cost claim deserves the same treatment. The CFD's genuine structural advantage in South Africa is that no security changes hands, so no securities transfer tax is due: 0.25 per cent of R1m, or R2,500. The financing runs at the benchmark plus a spread on the whole R1m, R294.52 a day. The break-even is 8.5 days, or 11.8 once the dividend adjustment is netted off. Beyond a fortnight the CFD is the more expensive way to hold the same exposure, and at a year it costs 8.163 per cent against 1.050 per cent.

Two source corrections follow. The summary says over-the-counter contracts carry "no margin, collateralising required", then says of CFDs that "margins apply". Both cannot be true, and since 2016 neither is: the BCBS-IOSCO framework for non-centrally cleared derivatives phased in mandatory variation and initial margin, so uncleared over-the-counter positions are margined by rule.

The module closes on the fixed income derivatives the source lists without pricing. On a monthly tree calibrated to a flat 9 per cent curve, a five-year 9 per cent bond callable at a declining schedule prices at R981.96 against R1,000 straight, so the issuer's call is worth R18.04, or 46.9 basis points of yield. The puttable equivalent gives up 49.8. And the callable's effective convexity is about minus 10 against the straight bond's plus 20, which is the property that makes a callable a different animal rather than a slightly cheaper bond.

Feynman test You can get the ups and downs of a share without owning it, by agreeing with someone that whoever is on the wrong side pays the difference. You put down a tenth of the money, so a small move against you takes all of it. And because you did not put up the rest, you are charged interest on it every day, which is fine for a week and ruinous for a year. Separately, a bond that the borrower may repay early is worth less than one they cannot, and the discount is the price of an option you have written without being asked whether you wanted to.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that "cost-efficient" is the kind of claim that only means anything once a holding period is attached, and attaching one reverses it. A one-off saving against a running cost always has a crossover, and the whole art of reading a product description is noticing which costs are stocks and which are flows. The 8.5-day figure is not a criticism of contracts for difference; it is a statement of what they are for, which is short-horizon exposure, and it condemns them only for the use retail marketing most often suggests.

The second is that leverage does not change a view, it changes whether you are still holding the position when the view pays. A 56.4 per cent chance of being closed out inside a month applies to a share you may be entirely right about. That gap between being right and being paid is the single most underappreciated feature of a margined product, and it is computable in advance from two numbers the provider publishes: the margin percentage and the close-out rule.

The third is that embedded bond options are where the option machinery of lectures 3 and 5 stops being a separate product and becomes a feature of an ordinary security. Anyone holding corporate or agency debt is likely short a call whether they thought about it or not, and the 46.9 basis points of extra yield is the compensation. Treating that pickup as free money, without noticing the negative convexity that comes with it, is a common and expensive mistake in a rate-cutting cycle.

And a fourth reason of a different kind. This lecture closes a module that began with the exchange-traded and over-the-counter distinction, and it is worth noticing that the distinction has substantially eroded. Central clearing was mandated, uncleared margin was mandated, and retail leverage was capped. Four of the five features the source lists for over-the-counter contracts have been changed by rule since the summary was written, which is a lesson about which parts of a syllabus decay fastest.

Step 3

The mental model

Two halves. A margined forward on the left, an embedded option on the right, and a cost or a convexity that the product description omits in both cases.

That places it in the forward family from lecture 2, and it is closer to a forward than to a future: it is bilateral, tailored, and not exchange-traded. The source's phrase "non-standardised future" points at the right family with the wrong noun, since a future is standardised by definition. WHAT IT IS ACTUALLY EQUIVALENT TO is a total return swap on a single share with a floating funding leg, which is why the financing appears explicitly rather than being buried in a forward price. Lecture 2 showed a forward price of S x e^((r-q)T), where the funding is embedded in the strike. A CFD unbundles the same thing: you trade at spot and pay the funding daily. The economics are identical and the presentation is not, and the presentation is why the cost gets overlooked.

THE ASYMMETRY IS THE POINT. Being closed out is terminal for the trade and the subsequent recovery accrues to nobody. So the distribution of outcomes on a levered position is not the distribution of the share's returns scaled by ten; it is that distribution with its left tail truncated into a realised loss and its right tail intact only for paths that never touched the barrier. Leverage does not scale a view, it applies a path-dependent filter to it. Two corrections on margin, one internal and one factual

THE SOURCE SAYS BOTH OF THESE: of OTC contracts generally: "tailored instruments, higher dealing costs, lower liquidity, higher credit risk, NO MARGIN, collateralising required, no quoted market values" of CFDs specifically: "non-standardised future, tailored (OTC), MARGINS APPLY, leveraged, cost-efficient" A CFD IS AN OTC CONTRACT, SO THE TWO LISTS CANNOT BOTH BE RIGHT. The CFD line is the accurate one, and the general line reflects a market that no longer exists. THE FACTUAL CORRECTION. The BCBS-IOSCO framework for margin requirements on non-centrally cleared derivatives phased in variation margin and initial margin obligations from 2016 onwards, implemented in each major jurisdiction. Uncleared OTC derivatives are now margined by rule, with initial margin held in segregated form and variation margin exchanged against daily moves. The distinction between "collateralised" and "margined" that the source's phrase "no margin, collateralising required" is reaching for has largely dissolved. WHY THE ERROR IS INSTRUCTIVE RATHER THAN TRIVIAL. Lecture 1 argued that margin is what turns a credit exposure into an operational one, and that the exchange-traded advantage was margin rather than standardisation as such. If uncleared OTC contracts are margined too, then the remaining advantage of clearing is the mutualised default fund and the multilateral netting, NOT the margin. That is a sharper statement of lecture 1's conclusion than lecture 1 made, and it is only visible once this error is corrected.

Effective convexity here is a second difference of tree prices with a handful of discrete exercise dates, so the figure is stable to about a unit rather than to a decimal: bumping by 25, 50, 100 and 200 basis points gives callable convexities between -9.1 and -10.6. The sign and the order of magnitude are the results; the second decimal is not. Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first habit worth taking from this lecture is to sort every cost into a stock or a flow before comparing anything. The contract for difference saves a one-off 0.25 per cent and charges a running 10.75 per cent, and once those are labelled the 8.5-day crossover follows in one division. The same test disposes of a great many product comparisons: a platform fee against a dealing spread, an upfront commission against a trail, a swap's execution cost against its collateral funding. The arithmetic is trivial and the labelling is where the work is, because marketing material is written precisely to leave the flow implicit.

THE GENERAL FORM. break-even holding period = one-off saving / daily running cost product pair saving running cost crossover CFD vs owning the share STT 0.25% financing 10.75% 8.5 days CFD vs owning, after dividends STT 0.25% net 7.75% 11.8 days Any product whose advantage is a stock and whose disadvantage is a flow has one of these, and the number is usually shorter than the holding period the buyer has in mind. THAT MISMATCH IS THE PRODUCT, not a flaw in it. A CFD is an excellent instrument for a two-day position and a poor one for a two-year position, and a description that omits the horizon is not wrong so much as unfinished.

The second is that a margined position has two distinct ways of losing and only one of them is about being wrong. The 56.4 per cent one-month close-out probability is computed with zero drift, on a share the holder may have analysed correctly, and it says that leverage converts a view into a path-dependent bet on that view. This is the same structural point that the swap exposure profile made in lecture 4 and that the negative-basis trade made in lecture 6: the thesis and the financing are separate risks, and the financing usually resolves first.

The third is that embedded options are the most widely held derivatives in the world and almost nobody thinks of themselves as holding them. Every callable corporate bond, every mortgage with a prepayment right, every insurance contract with a surrender option is the same structure: a counterparty holds a right that they will exercise exactly when it hurts. The 46.9 basis points of extra yield is not a free spread, it is an option premium received in instalments, and the negative convexity is the position it funds. An actuary reading this should recognise the surrender option immediately, because a policyholder who lapses when rates rise is doing precisely what a bond issuer who calls when rates fall is doing.

THE SAME STRUCTURE UNDER FOUR NAMES. instrument who holds the option they exercise when the holder is callable bond the issuer rates FALL short a call puttable bond the investor rates RISE long a put prepayable mortgage the borrower rates FALL short a call surrenderable policy the policyholder rates RISE, or need cash short a put IN THREE OF THE FOUR THE OPTION IS HELD BY THE PARTY WHO DID NOT PAY FOR IT EXPLICITLY, and in all four the compensation arrives as a spread rather than a premium. The consequence is that the position is short volatility, and it is revalued against the holder exactly when volatility arrives. That is why the volatility table in step 4 matters more than the price: a 46.9 basis point pickup at 20 per cent volatility becomes a 103.5 basis point liability at 40 per cent, and nothing about the bond changed.

The fourth is about the module as a whole. It opened on the exchange-traded and over-the-counter distinction, and it closes having found that four of the five features the source attributes to over-the-counter contracts have been changed by regulation: margin is now mandatory on uncleared derivatives, clearing is mandatory for standardised ones, trade reporting and daily valuation are obligations rather than conveniences, and retail leverage is capped. What survives is the tailoring, which is the one feature that could never be legislated away because it is the reason the market exists. If there is a single lesson in that, it is that the durable part of any market structure is the economic function and the perishable part is the plumbing.

And the fifth is the one this module has repeated in seven different costumes. In every lecture the contract's stated terms produced one number and the contract's economics produced another: the convexity adjustment in futures, the concavity of FRA settlement, the writer's asymmetric margin in options, the humped exposure profile in swaps, the constraint on when a zero-cost collar exists, the cheapest-to-deliver option in credit, and here the running financing cost and the negative convexity. The pattern is not a coincidence of these seven contracts. It is what a derivative is: an instrument whose description is a payoff and whose economics are everything else.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The exchange-traded versus over-the-counter distinction, the argument that margin converts a credit exposure into an operational one, and the collective action problem that forced the clearing mandate are all from derivative markets, and step 4 sharpens that lecture's conclusion once the source's margin error is corrected. The cost of carry relation, with the bond's coupon replacing the dividend yield, is from forwards and futures. Convexity, the asymmetry between an option's holder and its writer, and the reason a written option is a short volatility position are from options. The exposure profile argument that a thesis and its financing are separate risks is from swaps and reappears in credit derivatives as the negative-basis trade. And the machinery for valuing an option on a rate, which this lecture applies to an option embedded in a bond rather than sold separately, is from swaptions, caps, floors and collars. On the asset class side, the duration and convexity being modified here are from government and corporate bonds, and the leverage-and-financing failure mode is the one hedge funds identified.

Where this goes next. This closes the derivatives module. Calibrating a rate tree properly, choosing between lognormal and normal short-rate dynamics, and building a volatility surface rather than assuming a flat 20 per cent all belong to valuation, which also takes up option-adjusted spread as the correct way to compare a callable bond with a straight one. Using derivatives to change a portfolio's exposure without trading the underlying is portfolio management, and the negative convexity of a callable portfolio against a liability stream with positive convexity is a portfolio construction problem before it is a trading one. The gearing and gap risk quantified in step 4 are two of the exposures risk measurement has to capture, and the conduct question raised by selling leverage to retail clients sits with market conduct regulation in the external environment.

Where the bank sits opposite. The bank writing the CFD is the party earning the financing spread, which is a funding and balance sheet pricing decision before it is a trading one, and the position it warehouses sits in the trading book under market risk. Gap risk on a levered retail book is a genuine credit exposure to clients who may owe more than they deposited, which is counterparty credit risk at the retail end, and mis-selling leverage is operational risk in its conduct form. On the bond side, a bank issuing callable debt is buying the option that the investor sells, which is why callable instruments feature in capital management, and the negative convexity of a callable and prepayable asset book against non-maturing deposits is the core of interest rate risk in the banking book. The uncleared margin rules referenced in step 4 sit within the international regulatory framework.
