Swaptions, caps and floors: an option on a portfolio is not a portfolio of options

A cap is a strip of independent options, one per period. A swaption is a single option covering all the periods at once. So a swaption can never be worth more than the corresponding cap, and on the worked curve it costs 75.9 per cent as much. That distinction decides which instrument a hedger should buy, and the source's own relation, cap minus floor equals swap, turns out to be put-call parity for the third time in this module.

Derivatives 5 of 7 Reading time, 50 minutes Sources: F105 summary chapters 8 and 9; Black (1976)
Step 1

The 30-second version

The source describes an interest rate cap as a portfolio of call options on an interest rate, a floor as a portfolio of puts, a collar as a long cap and a short floor at different strikes, and a swaption as an option on a swap. It then states a relation worth taking seriously: the value of a swap equals the value of a cap less the value of a floor at the same strike. That is put-call parity in interest rate clothing.

Two details in the mechanics matter more than they look. A five-year annual cap contains four caplets, not five, because the first period's rate is already known at inception and there is nothing optional about it. And each caplet expires at the start of the period it covers and pays at the end, which is the settlement convention from lecture 2 appearing again. Get either wrong and the parity relation appears to fail.

The parity holds exactly once the first period is accounted for. On the worked curve, a cap at 9 per cent is worth R4,490,624 and the floor R2,398,036, a difference of R2,092,588, and the five-year pay-fixed swap is worth R1,166,662. The gap is exactly the first year's known payment of minus R925,926. And at the forward swap rate over the covered periods, 9.7057 per cent, the cap and the floor are worth precisely the same: R3,501,194 each.

The deep point is the one the source's definition conceals. A cap is a portfolio of options, exercised period by period; a swaption is an option on a portfolio, exercised once. By Jensen's inequality the second can never exceed the first, and on the worked figures a one-into-four payer swaption at a 9 per cent strike costs R3,410,275 against the cap's R4,490,624: 75.9 per cent. The gap is the value of being able to decide period by period, and it tells a hedger which instrument matches their exposure.

Feynman test Suppose you want protection against high interest rates over five years. You can buy five separate insurance policies, one for each year, and claim on whichever years go badly. Or you can buy one policy that pays only if the five years taken together go badly. The second is cheaper, because it cannot pay you for a bad year that a good year cancels out. Which you should buy depends entirely on whether your problem arrives year by year or all at once.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the cap-against-swaption distinction is the one genuinely subtle idea in interest rate derivatives, and it is the difference between an instrument that matches an exposure and one that merely correlates with it. A treasurer hedging quarterly interest payments has a period-by-period exposure and needs the cap. A fund deciding whether to enter a swap at a future date has a single decision and needs the swaption. Buying the wrong one either overpays by a quarter or leaves periods unhedged.

The second reason is that put-call parity now appears for the third time in three lectures, in three markets, with the same structure: a long call and a short put at the same strike equal the underlying forward. In equity options it was C minus P equals S minus the discounted strike; here it is cap minus floor equals swap; and for swaptions it is payer minus receiver equals the forward-starting swap. One relation, and recognising it saves learning three.

The third reason is that the zero-cost collar is the instrument most often sold to corporate borrowers, and the arithmetic contains a constraint that is not obvious. A zero-cost collar requires the floor strike to sit below the cap strike, and that is only possible when the cap strike is above the forward swap rate. Below it, the cap costs more than any floor beneath it can fund, so the structure does not exist. A collar quoted at a cap strike below the forward swap rate is not a collar.

And a fourth reason about how to compare hedges. On the borrower's table in step 4, buying a cap is never the cheapest outcome at any single interest rate. That is not a finding against caps; it is what insurance always looks like when compared at a point. The premium is certain and the benefit is not, so a point comparison is structurally unable to favour it, and any hedging decision made from such a table is being made on the wrong evidence.

A swaption is an option on a portfolio

The source calls a swaption an "option on swaps, holder has the right to trade cashflows starting on set date". That is exactly right, and it conceals the point. A CAP is a PORTFOLIO OF OPTIONS. Each caplet is exercised independently, so the holder collects on every period whose rate exceeded the strike, regardless of the others. A SWAPTION is an OPTION ON A PORTFOLIO. One decision at expiry covers all the payments, so the holder collects only if the swap AS A WHOLE is worth having. An option on a portfolio can never be worth more than a portfolio of options on its components, because the single decision cannot select the good periods and discard the bad ones. That is Jensen's inequality and it gives a hard bound: SWAPTION <= CAP, with equality only if the forward rates move together perfectly. A one-into-four PAYER swaption, the right in one year to pay fixed on a four-year swap: forward swap rate = (df1 - df5)/(df2 + df3 + df4 + df5) = (0.925926 - 0.638136)/2.965154 = 9.7057% strike swaption value the four caplets (the cap) swaption / cap 8.50% R4,391,547 R5,330,590 82.4% 9.00% R3,410,275 R4,490,624 75.9% 9.50% R2,586,076 R3,766,958 68.7% 10.00% R1,916,694 R3,151,435 60.8% THE BOUND HOLDS AT EVERY STRIKE, and the gap widens as the strike rises: 17.6 per cent at a strike 120 basis points below the forward, and 39.2 per cent at 29 basis points above it. That is intuitive once seen: the further out of the money, the more the cap's value depends on individual periods happening to breach the strike, which is exactly what the swaption cannot capture. WHICH DETERMINES WHICH INSTRUMENT A HEDGER SHOULD BUY: an exposure that exists in EVERY period, each mattering separately -> the CAP a DECISION about whether to enter a swap at a future date -> the SWAPTION Buying a cap to hedge a swaption exposure overpays by the gap above. Buying a swaption to hedge a period-by-period exposure leaves the periods unhedged in the case that matters most: when the average is acceptable and one period is not.

A point comparison charges the cap's premium in every scenario and credits its benefit in only some, so it is structurally unable to favour it. Anything that is better in all five is not a hedge, it is a forecast.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

One inequality, applied everywhere

The swaption-against-cap result is a specific case of something more general, and once it is stated in the general form it turns up repeatedly. An option on an average, or on a sum, or on a portfolio, is worth less than the corresponding collection of individual options, because a single decision cannot cherry-pick.

the pair which is cheaper why, in one line a SWAPTION against a CAP the swaption, at one decision covers all periods, so 75.9% here good periods offset bad ones an ASIAN option against a EUROPEAN the Asian, at 73.4% averaging the underlying removes (lecture 3) at a 25% volatility variance before the payoff applies a BASKET option against a basket the basket option the basket's volatility is below the of options average of its components' unless they are perfectly correlated a TRANCHE of a pool against the the senior tranche the pool's losses offset within it, individual credits (asset classes 10) which is what subordination sells ALL FOUR ARE THE SAME INEQUALITY. Aggregation removes variance, and an option's value rises with variance, so aggregating before applying the payoff always reduces the price. The size of the reduction is governed by one parameter in every case: the CORRELATION between the components. At perfect correlation there is nothing to offset and the two sides are equal; the lower the correlation, the larger the gap. AND THAT GIVES A UNIFIED WAY TO READ THE FOUR PRICES. A swaption at 75.9 per cent of a cap is telling you what the market believes about the correlation between forward rates along the curve, in the same way that asset classes lecture 10's senior tranche price was telling you what it believed about default correlation. The instrument is a correlation quote wearing a different name, which is why the desks that trade both call them correlation products. THE PRACTICAL TEST FOLLOWS. When choosing between an aggregated option and a strip of individual ones, ask whether the exposure itself aggregates. If the liability is an average, buy the option on the average and save the difference. If the liability arrives period by period, the saving is not a saving: it is a gap in the hedge.

What the source gets right, and one thing it does not say

Its definitions are precise and economical. Describing a cap as a portfolio of calls on a rate, or equivalently as puts on a zero-coupon bond, is the correct dual statement and the second half is the more useful one for anybody valuing it in a bond-based model. Its statement of the parity relation, and its observation that a collar arises when the strikes differ, are both exactly right. And its inclusion of the swap-as-cap-minus-floor identity in the valuation chapter rather than the instrument chapter is a sensible editorial choice, because it belongs with the pricing.

What it does not say is that a swaption and a cap are not substitutes. The source lists them adjacently as two instruments for managing interest rate exposure without indicating that one is bounded by the other or that the choice between them is determined by the shape of the exposure rather than by preference. That omission is the single most consequential gap in its derivatives coverage, because it is the error a practitioner would actually make.

A caution about the flat volatility used throughout Every calculation here uses a single 20 per cent volatility for every caplet, which makes the arithmetic transparent and is not how the market works. Caplet volatilities have a term structure that is typically humped, peaking somewhere in the first two years and declining thereafter, so a flat number will overprice some caplets and underprice others. The cap's total is therefore approximately right and its composition is not, which matters if the position is to be hedged caplet by caplet rather than held. The direction of the error cannot be signed without the actual volatility surface, which is why market practice quotes a separate flat volatility for each cap maturity rather than one for all of them.

Where the money actually goes in a collar

the collar is sold as costless and it is not free, which is a distinction worth making precisely rather than rhetorically. what the borrower RECEIVES protection above Kc = 11.00% what the borrower PAYS the whole benefit of rates below Kf = 8.8546% the upfront cost zero the expected cost the difference in expected payoff between the two legs, which is zero BY CONSTRUCTION under the risk-neutral measure and NOT zero under any other THE LAST LINE IS THE ONE THAT MATTERS AND IT IS LECTURE 3'S POINT AGAIN. The two legs have equal risk-neutral value, which is what "zero cost" means. Under a real-world measure with any drift in rates at all, one leg is worth more than the other, and which one depends on the drift's direction. So a borrower entering a zero-cost collar in a market where forward rates are above spot, as on the worked curve, is selling a floor that is more likely to be struck than the cap is, judged by today's spot rate. That is not a mistake and it is not free money for the bank either: it is the forward curve doing what it does, and the borrower has taken a view on it whether or not they intended to. THE HONEST SUMMARY: a zero-cost collar costs nothing today and commits the borrower to a position on the forward curve. If they believe the forwards, it is fair. If they believe rates will stay at spot, they have sold something cheaply. Asset classes lecture 9 made exactly this argument about currency hedging, where the forward embeds the interest differential and hedging is mean-neutral only if uncovered parity holds. Same structure, different market, third appearance.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The swap rate as the par yield, the two valuation routes and the forward swap rate are from swaps. Put-call parity in its equity form, the asymmetry between buyer and writer, and the Asian option's variance reduction are from options, where the aggregation inequality in step 5 first appeared without being named. The settlement convention that makes a caplet expire at the start of its period and pay at the end is from forwards and futures. The correlation dependence that the swaption-to-cap ratio expresses is the tranching arithmetic of securitisation and insurance-linked securities, and the forward-curve argument in step 5 is the currency hedging argument from commodities and currency.

Where this goes next. Lecture 6 covers credit derivatives, where the source lists the credit default swap among swap types and where the option structure recurs with a credit event replacing the market variable. Lecture 7 covers contracts for difference, closing the module. Black's model, the volatility term structure that the caution in step 5 flags, and the calibration of a caplet surface belong to valuation. The use of caps and swaptions to shape a liability-driven portfolio's exposure is portfolio construction, and the basis risk that remains after any of these hedges is one of the eight risks in risk measurement.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank selling a cap to a borrower is short a strip of options and must hedge the convexity that options quantified, in its trading book under market risk, where the volatility surface is itself a risk factor with a capital charge. A bank buying caps to hedge its own structural position is managing interest rate risk, and the asymmetry means it has counterparty exposure to a cap seller and none to a cap buyer, which is counterparty credit risk reading lecture 3's rule from the other side. Selling a collar to a corporate client that does not understand the forward-curve position it embeds is a conduct matter for the FSCA.

Numbers used here. The definitions of a cap as a portfolio of calls on a rate or puts on a zero-coupon bond, a floor as the converse, a collar as a long cap and short floor at differing strikes, a swaption as an option on a swap, and the parity relation that a swap equals a cap less a floor are all from the F105 summary chapters 8 and 9. Black's 1976 model is standard, as is the Jensen bound between an option on a portfolio and a portfolio of options. Everything else is invented for illustration: the five-point zero curve and every forward derived from it, the R100m notional, the 20 per cent flat Black volatility, and every strike. The flat volatility is a simplification flagged in step 5; real caplet volatilities have a term structure and market practice quotes a separate flat volatility per cap maturity. The arithmetic on all of these figures is exact and none is a market observation.
