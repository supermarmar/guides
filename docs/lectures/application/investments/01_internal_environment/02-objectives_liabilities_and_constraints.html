Step 1

The 30-second version

Lecture 1 described the chain that carries the money. This lecture describes what the money is for, which is the specification every later module optimises against. The F105 summary spreads this material across four chapters and never assembles it, so the first job is to put objectives, liabilities and constraints in one place and price them.

Start with the constraint that turns out to be largest. The summary says post-tax returns are maximised by minimising the tax liability or deferring it, and treats those as comparable options. Paying 18 per cent capital gains tax annually turns 1 into 9.3234 over forty years at a 7 per cent gross return; paying the identical rate once at the end turns it into 12.4591, 33.63 per cent more. Nothing was avoided and no rate was negotiated. More striking, deferring at 18 per cent beats realising annually at 9, so over a long horizon deferral is worth more than halving the rate, and it requires only not selling.

The advantage is nothing at a one-year horizon and rises to 81.01 per cent for a highly taxed investor in a high-return asset, a factor of 10.9 across the plausible range. That makes turnover a tax decision before it is an investment one, and it is the same finding the transition management lecture reached when capital gains tax turned out to be the largest single cost of changing a portfolio.

Then a genuine contradiction inside the summary. It lists three components of a clear objective, the third being "not affected by what others do", and separately lists relative performance risk, the risk of under-performing competitors, whose suggested controls are index tracking and commercial matching. A fund that matches its liabilities and ignores its peers carries 16.8760 per cent tracking error against them; a fund that tracks its peers carries none and has abandoned the first objective. There is no strategy satisfying both, and the summary's control for the risk is the direct negation of the objective.

On liabilities, the summary's rule is "asset parameters equal liability parameters" without saying which parameters. Four matter, and they are not comparable in size: mismatching currency costs 15.0000 per cent at one standard deviation and mismatching certainty costs 4.4721, a factor of 3.4. More important, three of the four have matching instruments and the fourth does not. Longevity has no traded asset whose payoff depends on how long members live, so it is an unhedgeable residual rather than a mismatch to be closed.

The liquidity section produces the result most likely to be got wrong under exam pressure. The summary's LRE is zero when the fund is immunised, and with assets of 105 against liabilities of 100 that happens at an asset duration of 11.4286 against a liability duration of 12. A fund in surplus needs a shorter asset duration, because it has more money to multiply by it. The instinct that a long liability needs a long asset is right only at exactly full funding.

Finally, tax makes "the value of the asset" incomplete without naming the holder. The same ten-year 8 per cent bond is worth 114.7202 to a tax-exempt pension fund and 109.9298 to a higher-rate individual, a spread of 4.36 per cent of the lowest price with nothing about the bond changing. So a mispricing claim needs a marginal holder attached, which is the coupon effect the bond anomaly lecture found to be a tax result arriving here from the investor's side.

Step 2

Why this matters

Every optimisation in this track needs an objective function, and this lecture supplies it. The portfolio construction module maximises return for a given risk; the performance measurement module compares outcomes against a benchmark; the portfolio management module chooses techniques. All three are meaningless until somebody has specified what the fund is trying to achieve, and the specification is not "maximise return". It is to meet a particular set of payments, in a particular currency, at particular dates, with a particular tolerance for failing, subject to constraints on what may be held and what may be taxed.

The tax arithmetic in section 4.1 matters because it changes the ranking of strategies rather than their scores. An active strategy with high turnover is competing against a passive one with a handicap of 33.63 per cent of terminal wealth for a taxed investor, and the handicap does not appear in any gross return comparison. Every active manager's track record for a taxable client is therefore overstated by an amount that depends on the client rather than the manager, which is why after-tax performance measurement is a separate discipline and why the same fund is a good buy for a pension scheme and a poor one for an individual.

Section 4.4's immunisation result matters because it is the point where actuarial technique diverges from investment intuition. A trustee body told that its liabilities have a twelve-year duration will ask its manager for twelve-year assets, and if the scheme is in surplus that is the wrong answer. Getting it right requires thinking in money duration rather than duration, which is a small technical step and a large practical one, and it is the reason liability-driven investment mandates are written in terms of hedge ratios rather than durations.

The contradiction identified in section 4.2 matters most of all, because it is not a drafting error in the summary: it is a real and unresolved tension in how institutional funds are governed. Trustees are told to ignore their peers and are measured against them. Understanding that the second is a risk to the trustees rather than to the beneficiaries is what allows an adviser to say so, and saying so is often the most valuable thing an adviser does.

Step 3

The mental model

The liabilities set the target, the constraints remove options, and the objective is what remains. Reversing that order, which is what "maximise return subject to constraints" does, produces a different answer.

Suppose you must pay someone 100 in twenty years' time. What should you buy?

The tempting answer is "whatever returns the most", and it is wrong, because you do not need the most. You need 100 in twenty years, and anything beyond that is a bonus while anything short of it is a failure. Those are not symmetrical, so the thing to minimise is not volatility but the chance of arriving short.

Which means the right asset is defined by the liability and not by the market. If the payment is fixed at 100, buy a twenty-year bond paying 100 at maturity and you cannot fail. If the payment is 100 in today's money, that bond is now risky, because inflation could double the requirement, and the safe asset is an index-linked bond instead. Same payment, different definition, completely different answer.

Now add the four things that can differ between what you owe and what you hold. When you owe it, in what currency, whether it is fixed or linked to something, and whether you know the amount at all. The first three you can fix by buying the right instrument. The fourth you usually cannot: if you owe a pension for as long as somebody lives, no asset in the market pays out according to how long they live.

And then the constraints, which do not change what you want but do change what you can have. Tax means you keep less of what you earn, and the timing of the tax matters enormously: a tax paid at the end leaves your money compounding in the meantime, a tax paid annually does not. Regulation says some assets are forbidden. Liquidity says you must be able to pay next month even if the twenty-year plan is perfect. And the peer group says, quietly, that you will be judged against what everybody else did, which is not a constraint anyone wrote down and is the one that most often decides the answer.

Formally, the after-tax accumulation of a gain taxed annually at rate t is (1 + g(1-t))^n, and of the same gain taxed once at the end it is 1 + ((1+g)^n - 1)(1-t). The two are equal at n = 1 and diverge thereafter, which is section 4.1. Immunisation requires the money durations to match, D_A x PV_A = D_L x PV_L, which is section 4.4 and reduces to matching durations only when the fund is exactly fully funded.

Step 4

The detail

4.1 Deferral is worth more than halving the rate

The summary offers two routes to a better post-tax return, minimising the liability or deferring it, and presents them as alternatives of similar weight. Price the second: take a 7 per cent gross return and 18 per cent capital gains tax, and compare paying annually with paying once at the end on the whole accumulated gain.

The same rate on the same gain, paid at two different times

   years   realise the gain   defer to the end   the deferral's   as a % of the
           every year         and pay once       advantage        realising route
       1             1.0574             1.0574           0.0000              0.00%
       5             1.3219             1.3301           0.0082              0.62%
      10             1.7474             1.7931           0.0457              2.61%
      20             3.0534             3.3531           0.2997              9.82%
      40             9.3234            12.4591           3.1357             33.63%

Identical at one year, because the tax is paid at the same moment either way. Worth 33.63 per cent more over forty.

At a one-year horizon deferral is worth exactly nothing, which is the right sanity check: the tax falls at the same moment either way. Over forty years it is worth 33.63 per cent more terminal wealth at the same rate on the same gain. Nothing was avoided; the timing alone did it, and what the investor kept was the return on money that would otherwise have gone to the revenue authority each year.

The comparison that reframes the summary's sentence is this: deferring at 18 per cent ends at 12.4591 against 11.8241 for realising annually at 9 per cent. So over a long horizon, deferral is worth more than halving the tax rate. Minimising requires a lower rate, which in practice means a different asset, a different vehicle or a different jurisdiction. Deferring requires only not selling. The two are not options of similar weight and a complete answer says which is cheaper to obtain.

How the deferral advantage depends on the rate and the return

   tax rate   gross return   deferral's advantage over forty years
         10%              4%                                   7.43%
         10%              7%                                  17.89%
         18%              4%                                  13.22%
         18%              7%                                  33.63%
         28%              4%                                  20.02%
         28%              7%                                  54.75%
         40%              4%                                  27.04%
         40%              7%                                  81.01%

From 7.43 per cent for a lightly taxed investor in a low-return asset to 81.01 for a highly taxed one in a high-return asset, a factor of 10.9.

The region matters for advice. The advantage ranges over a factor of 10.9 across plausible parameters, so the strength of the deferral argument depends entirely on the client. For a tax-exempt pension fund it is zero and turnover is free; for a higher-rate individual holding growth assets it is the dominant consideration in the whole portfolio decision. Turnover is therefore a tax decision before it is an investment one, which is the same conclusion the transition management lecture reached from the cost side when capital gains tax proved to be 3.6000 per cent of a 5.0000 per cent transition bill.

4.2 The objective and the risk list contradict each other

The summary gives three components of a clear objective: meet liabilities, hold an attitude to risk and return, and be "not affected by what others do". It separately lists relative performance risk, defined as the risk of under-performing competitors, and gives as its controls "minimise index tracking and commercial matching", which means tracking others. Both positions cannot be held, and the size of the conflict is computable.

Three strategies against a peer group at 16 per cent volatility

   the fund's strategy          volatility   correlation   tracking error   is the
                                             with peers    against peers    objective met?
   match the liabilities             12.00%          0.30          16.8760%   liabilities yes
   a compromise, half each           14.00%          0.70          11.7644%   neither fully
   track the peer group              16.00%          1.00           0.0000%   peers yes

Matching the liabilities carries 16.8760 per cent tracking error against peers. Tracking peers carries none and abandons the first objective.

A fund that matches its liabilities and ignores its peers carries 16.8760 per cent tracking error against them. A fund that tracks its peers carries none and has stopped matching its liabilities. The compromise achieves neither fully, at 11.7644 per cent. There is no strategy that satisfies both statements, so the summary lists an objective and a risk that are the same quantity with opposite signs, and its control for the risk is the negation of the objective.

The examinable position is both lists as the summary gives them, since both appear in the syllabus material. The complete answer adds the resolution: relative performance risk is a risk to the trustees and to the manager rather than to the beneficiary. A beneficiary is harmed by a fund that cannot pay them, not by a fund that paid them while a neighbouring fund did better. That is exactly the career-risk mechanism the market behaviour lecture identified behind herding, and naming it is what allows the tension to be discussed rather than silently resolved in favour of the peer group.

When the conflict is real, by how similar the peers' liabilities are

   how similar the peers'   correlation   tracking error   is the conflict
   liabilities are          with peers    against peers    real?
   identical liabilities           0.98           4.8662%   barely
   broadly similar                 0.80           9.6333%   yes
   quite different                 0.50          14.4222%   yes
   unrelated                       0.10          19.0158%   yes

With near-identical liabilities the tracking error falls to 4.8662 per cent and the conflict nearly disappears.

The tension dissolves in one case: where the peer group's liabilities resemble the fund's own. At a 0.98 correlation the tracking error is 4.8662 per cent and tracking the peers is nearly the same act as matching the liabilities. So peer comparison is defensible for a homogeneous set of funds and indefensible for a heterogeneous one, which is precisely the validity criterion the benchmarks lecture applied when the peer median failed on six of seven counts. A defined benefit scheme comparing itself with other defined benefit schemes of similar maturity is doing something reasonable; the same scheme comparing itself with a balanced managed sector average is not.

4.3 The four liability parameters, and the one that cannot be matched

The summary's matching rule is "asset parameters equal liability parameters" and it does not say which parameters. Four matter, and each has a measurable consequence when it is wrong.

Four mismatches and the error each produces

   the parameter       the mismatch                                             error at one
                                                                                standard deviation
   term                a 20-year liability met with a 5-year asset                     7.7460%
   currency            a rand liability met with a dollar asset                       15.0000%
   inflation linkage   an inflation-linked liability met with a nominal asset          6.7082%
   certainty           a longevity-exposed liability met with any asset                4.4721%

A factor of 3.4 between the largest and smallest. "Match the parameters" is a ranked instruction rather than a flat one.

The four mismatches are not comparable in size. Currency is the largest here at 15.0000 per cent and certainty the smallest at 4.4721, a factor of 3.4, and the order depends on the particular liability rather than being a general fact. So the instruction to match parameters is a ranked one, and a fund with limited capacity to hedge should close the largest gap first.

The more important distinction is between three parameters and the fourth. Term is matched by a long bond, currency by a forward or swap, and inflation linkage by an index-linked bond or an inflation swap, all of which the derivative lectures priced. Longevity has no traded asset whose payoff depends on how long the members live, so the fourth parameter is not a mismatch to be closed but an unhedgeable residual. The summary's rule is achievable for three parameters and impossible for the fourth, and a complete answer separates the mismatch that can be closed from the risk that must be retained, reinsured or transferred through a buy-in.

4.4 The liquidity gap and the LRE, and why a fund in surplus goes shorter

The summary gives two liquidity measures, a level and a sensitivity. Take liquid assets of 30 against volatile liabilities of 22, so the liquidity gap is 8.0000, positive and therefore good on the summary's six-month criterion. Now the second measure, which is the derivative of the basic gap with respect to the interest rate.

The LRE with assets of 105 against liabilities of 100

   asset duration   liability duration   basic gap   LRE      the summary's verdict
             4.0000               12.0000       5.0000    7.8000   positive, so not flagged
             8.0000               12.0000       5.0000    3.6000   positive, so not flagged
            11.4286               12.0000       5.0000   -0.0000   no risk: immunised
            16.0000               12.0000       5.0000  -4.8000   HIGH RISK

The LRE is zero at an asset duration of 11.4286 against a liability duration of 12, not at 12.

The LRE is zero at an asset duration of 11.4286 rather than 12. The reason is that the match required is of money durations, asset value times duration against liability value times duration, and since the assets exceed the liabilities by 5.0000 the assets need a shorter duration to produce the same product. A fund in surplus must hold a shorter asset duration than its liability duration to be immunised, which is the opposite of the instinct that a long liability needs a long asset. That instinct is correct only at exactly full funding.

The two measures can disagree

   the situation                       liquidity gap   LRE       what each measure says
   short assets, long liabilities             8.0000    7.8000   gap fine, LRE fine
   long assets, long liabilities              8.0000   -4.8000   gap fine, LRE FLAGS IT
   the gap measures a SIX-MONTH horizon and the LRE measures the whole balance sheet's
   sensitivity, so a fund can pass one and fail the other

A positive 8.0000 gap and a negative 4.8000 LRE at the same time, because they answer different questions.

The two measures answer different questions and can disagree, which is why the summary gives both. The gap asks whether the next six months of outflows are covered by assets that can be sold; the LRE asks whether a rate movement damages the balance sheet as a whole. A fund can pass one and fail the other, and a fund holding long assets against long liabilities in surplus is exactly the case that passes the gap test and fails the sensitivity test.

4.5 The same asset is worth different amounts to different investors

The summary lists five sources of investor difference: expectations, risk, objectives, regulation and tax. The last is computable, and it means "the value of the asset" is an incomplete phrase. Price a ten-year 8 per cent coupon bond redeeming at 100, at a 6 per cent gross required yield, for four different holders.

One bond, four investors

   the investor                     income tax   CGT     the price it can pay
                                                          for the same bond
   a pension fund, tax exempt                0%       0%               114.7202
   a life insurer                           28%      18%               111.4958
   a higher-rate individual                 40%      18%               109.9298
   an offshore fund, coupons taxed          15%       0%               113.0634

A spread of 4.7904, or 4.36 per cent of the lowest price, with nothing about the bond changing.

The same bond is worth between 109.9298 and 114.7202 depending only on who holds it, a spread of 4.36 per cent of the lowest price. The tax-exempt investor can pay the most, which is why pension funds are the natural holders of high-coupon bonds and taxed investors the natural holders of low-coupon ones. That is the coupon effect the bond anomaly lecture found to be a tax result rather than a mispricing, arriving here from the investor's side rather than the instrument's.

The consequence for analysis is direct: an apparent anomaly visible to one investor may be correctly priced for another, so a mispricing claim is incomplete without naming the marginal holder. A bond that looks cheap to a pension fund may be fairly priced to the taxed investors who actually set its price, and the fund's opportunity is real but is a tax arbitrage rather than a market inefficiency. Those are different claims with different durability, since a tax advantage persists and an inefficiency does not.

4.6 The six tax types, and why only one of them can be deferred

The summary lists six tax types. Section 4.1's result attaches to only one of them, which is worth making explicit because it determines the shape of asset a taxed investor should prefer.

The summary's tax types and their timing

   the summary's tax type   what it reaches                  can it be deferred?
   income tax               coupons, dividends, rent          no: payable immediately
   capital gains tax        the gain, on sale                 YES: payable on sale only
   corporate tax            the company's profit before it    no: paid by the issuer
                            reaches the investor
   total return tax         income and gains together, so     partly: needs a valuation
                            valuations are needed at sale     at sale
   stamp duty               the purchase itself               no: paid at purchase
   withholding tax          offshore income at source         no, but it can be
                                                              RECLAIMED under a treaty

One of the six is deferrable, which is why the deferral result attaches to capital gains tax specifically rather than to tax in general.

Only capital gains tax is deferrable by holding on. An investor cannot defer income tax by not selling, so a high-income asset is taxed whether or not it is traded and a growth asset is not. That turns the income-against-growth split into a tax decision even between assets with identical total returns.

Two assets returning 7.00 per cent in total, split differently

   the asset            income   capital   tax paid each   tax deferred   after-tax over
                        yield    growth    year at 40%     at 18%         forty years
   a high-income asset    6.00%     1.00%          2.4000%        0.1800%         5.7648
   a growth asset         1.00%     6.00%          0.4000%        1.0800%        10.9431

Identical total returns. The growth asset ends 89.82 per cent ahead purely from which tax applies and when.

Both assets return 7.00 per cent in total and the growth one ends at 10.9431 against 5.7648, 89.82 per cent more, entirely from the split between income and growth and therefore from which tax applies and when. That is the summary's phrase "investor rules" made quantitative, and it explains a preference that otherwise looks like a style bias: a highly taxed individual should hold growth assets and a tax-exempt fund should hold the income ones, and both are behaving correctly.

It also explains why corporate tax appears in the summary's list at all, which is otherwise puzzling since the investor does not pay it. The three systems the summary names, classical, split rate and imputation, determine whether the company's profit is taxed once or twice before reaching the shareholder, and therefore whether an investor prefers dividends or buybacks. That is a tax constraint operating on the company's payout policy and reaching the investor indirectly, which is why it belongs on a list of investor constraints despite being levied elsewhere.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Three things a practitioner does differently once objectives and constraints are taken seriously, and the first is a question of sequence.

Write the liabilities down before looking at any asset, and write down which parameters you cannot match. The temptation in every actual engagement is to start from the current portfolio and ask what to change, which anchors the answer to whatever happens to be held. Starting from the liability produces a different and better question: what would I hold if I were starting today, and what stops me? Section 4.3's separation matters here, because the honest answer usually includes an unhedgeable residual, and stating that residual explicitly is more useful than an asset allocation that implicitly pretends it away. A scheme that knows it is carrying unhedged longevity risk of a stated size can decide whether to reinsure it. A scheme that has never separated it out cannot.

Ask who the client is for tax purposes before assessing any strategy, because the same strategy has different values to different clients. Section 4.1's factor of 10.9 across plausible parameters is larger than the range of manager skill in any published study. That means the tax status of the holder is a more powerful determinant of net outcome than manager selection, and it is knowable in advance with certainty rather than estimated with error. A practitioner who spends a week on manager selection and a paragraph on tax wrapper choice has allocated their effort backwards, and section 4.6's income-against-growth result is the concrete version of the same point.

Name the peer-group constraint out loud, because it is the one nobody writes into the mandate and it decides the answer anyway. Section 4.2 shows the conflict is genuine and unresolvable within the summary's own framing. In practice it resolves in favour of the peer group, because trustees are individuals with reputations and the beneficiary is an abstraction. The professional response is not to pretend otherwise but to make the trade-off visible: to state what tracking error against peers a liability-matched strategy would carry, to state that this is a risk to the trustees rather than to the members, and to let the trustees decide with that on the table. That is a governance intervention rather than an investment one, and it is frequently worth more than any asset allocation change.

On the summary, two things are recorded. The contradiction in section 4.2 is real rather than a slip, since both statements appear in the syllabus material and the suggested control for the risk is the negation of the objective. And the matching rule in section 4.3 is stated as though all parameters were equivalent and closeable, when they differ in size by a factor of three and one of them has no matching instrument at all. Neither of these is a factual error. Both are places where the summary's compression removes the distinction that makes the material usable.

Connections

What this assumes. The chain that delivers the money to these objectives is in the business model, which found the annual charges compounding to 52.08 per cent of a forty-year outcome, and the compounding arithmetic used here is the same. The immunisation condition is derived in bond passive management and liability-driven investment, and the instruments that match the first three liability parameters are priced in using swaps and currency hedging. The coupon effect that section 4.5 reaches from the investor's side is in bond active management, anomaly switching, the peer-group benchmark objection is in benchmarks and comparators, and the career-risk mechanism behind it is in behavioural finance, market behaviour. The tax charge on a transition is quantified in transition management and overlays.

Where this goes next. Lecture 3 covers the governance that sets these objectives, where the trust deed and the trustee's fiduciary duty determine who decides what the fund is for, and section 4.2's conflict becomes a live governance question rather than an analytical one. Lecture 4 turns the objectives into a mandate and a statement of investment principles, where the constraints named here are written down as instructions, including the localisation and prudential limits. Lecture 5 on vehicles and wrappers is largely a tax lecture in disguise, since section 4.1's deferral result is what makes the choice of wrapper worth as much as the choice of asset. Where the bank sits opposite. The bank runs the same four-parameter matching problem on a far shorter timescale, and its version of section 4.4's LRE is the interest rate gap analysis in interest rate risk in the banking book, where economic value of equity is the money-duration mismatch measured against capital rather than against a surplus. The summary's own liquidity gap is the banking measure imported directly, and its regulatory form is the liquidity coverage ratio in liquidity risk, which differs mainly in prescribing the stress rather than leaving it out. And the bank is the counterparty for three of the four matching instruments, so the fund's decision to close a mismatch is the bank's decision to open one, capitalised through market risk.

The three components of a clear objective, the liability matching rule, the definitions of actuarial and market risk, the five investor differences, the two routes to a better post-tax return with their six tax-specific and seven other factors, the six tax types with their sub-classifications, the liquidity gap and LRE definitions, and relative performance risk with its controls are all transcribed from the ASSA F105 summary in the user's notes, assembled from four separate chapters. Two findings are recorded against it, with the examinable position stated alongside each. First, the summary lists "not affected by what others do" as a component of a clear objective and separately treats under-performing competitors as a risk whose controls are index tracking and commercial matching, which is the direct negation of the objective; both lists are examinable as given, and the resolution is that relative performance risk is a risk to the trustees and manager rather than to the beneficiary. Second, its matching rule treats all parameters as equivalent and closeable when they differ in size by a factor of 3.4 and one of them, longevity, has no matching instrument at all, so a complete answer separates the closeable mismatch from the retained residual. Everything numerical is invented for illustration: a 7 per cent gross return with tax rates from 10 to 40 per cent over horizons from one to forty years, gross returns of 4 and 7 per cent, a fund at 12 per cent volatility against peers at 16 with correlations from 0.10 to 1.00, mismatch volatilities of 1.50 to 15.00 per cent over terms of one to twenty years, liquid assets of 30 against volatile liabilities of 22, assets of 105 against liabilities of 100 with durations from 4 to 16 years, a ten-year 8 per cent bond at a 6 per cent gross required yield priced for four investors taxed from zero to 40 per cent on income and zero to 18 on gains, and two assets each returning 7.00 per cent split as 6 and 1 per cent either way. Every headline finding states the region in which it holds: the deferral advantage is zero at a one-year horizon and rises with the horizon, the tax rate and the gross return; the peer-group conflict is real only where the peers' liabilities differ from the fund's own; the ranking of the four mismatches depends on the particular liability; and the immunising asset duration equals the liability duration only at exactly full funding.
