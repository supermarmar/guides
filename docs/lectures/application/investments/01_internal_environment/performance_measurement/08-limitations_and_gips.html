Step 1

The 30-second version

The summary lists seven disadvantages of performance measurement, and every one of them has now been quantified somewhere in this module. This lecture collects them as the examinable spine, then adds the calculation that only becomes possible once all seven are in hand: what happens when the defensible choices are combined rather than considered one at a time.

Take one fund, one year, and four choices each earlier lecture established as legitimate: time-weighted or money-weighted return, one of three comparators, raw or beta-adjusted excess, and tracking error on the standard deviation or the root mean square. That is twenty-four combinations. The excess return runs from −2.7420 to +2.0000 per cent, the information ratio from −0.8065 to +0.5882, and ten combinations say the manager beat the comparator while fourteen say they missed. The axis that moves the verdict most is the one that looks most innocuous: the return definition, at 0.7059 of information ratio.

Then "past is not future", derived rather than asserted. With every manager equally skilled, the share of top-quartile managers who repeat is 24.76 per cent at zero skill and 25.30 per cent at an information ratio of 1.00, because the quartile is defined within the group. Persistence appears only when skill varies: at a dispersion of 1.00 the repeat rate reaches 63.33 per cent. A persistence study measures how much managers differ, not how good they are.

The most useful new result concerns dismissal. Lecture 4 showed that proving an information ratio of 0.50 takes 10.82 years against a three-year review, which rules out one justification without settling the decision. Doing it properly as a Bayesian calculation: after a six per cent three-year shortfall the posterior probability of skill falls from 20.00 to 7.51 per cent, and the annual gain from switching is 0.2499 per cent. The break-even record is +0.2135 per cent over three years, so a manager must have been roughly flat or better merely to be worth keeping. The threshold is a transition-cost calculation, not a significance test.

And GIPS, which is absent from the summary. It closes four of eight choices, narrows two and leaves two open, cutting the verdict range from 1.3947 to 0.6888, a reduction of 50.61 per cent. The two it leaves open are the comparator's appropriateness and the attribution convention, which lectures 5 and 6 found to matter most. That is not an oversight: a standard can require a disclosure and cannot require a judgement to be correct.

Feynman test Seven standard complaints about measuring fund performance, and they all turn out to be true and measurable. But the real problem only appears when you stop taking them one at a time: there are two dozen entirely respectable ways to work out whether one manager had a good year, and they do not agree about whether the answer is yes. Managers who do well one period repeat about a quarter of the time, which is exactly what pure luck produces. The industry standard for reporting fixes about half of the disagreement, and what it leaves untouched is the half that matters. And the one thing the numbers do support is firing a manager, though for a reason nobody gives: because moving the money is cheap, not because three years proved anything.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the seven disadvantages are examinable and the summary gives them as a list to memorise. Objective (n)(v) asks a candidate to discuss the limitations, and a list recited is worth less than a list where each item comes with a magnitude. The table in step 4 supplies one number per disadvantage.

The second is that combining the choices is qualitatively different from considering them separately. Each of the four axes was defended in its own lecture as legitimate, so no single step in the chain is criticisable, and yet the chain admits both verdicts. That is not a statement about imprecision, it is a statement about the reporting process being underdetermined by the data.

The third is that the persistence result cuts against the most common inference in manager selection. A quarter of top-quartile managers repeating is what zero skill produces, so an observed repeat rate near a quarter is evidence of nothing, and a study reporting persistence is reporting dispersion of skill rather than its presence.

And the fourth is that the dismissal calculation reaches a conclusion the rest of the module does not: measurement does support a decision here, just not the decision people think it supports. The threshold is set by transition cost and expected holding period, both observable, rather than by a significance level that cannot be reached in the time available.

The mental model

Four choices compounding into twenty-four answers, persistence that is pure arithmetic, a dismissal rule that is a cost calculation, and a standard that closes half of it.

"Past is not future", derived rather than asserted

The summary's first disadvantage is that past performance does not predict future performance. That is usually offered as an empirical observation. It is partly an arithmetic necessity, and separating the two parts is what makes the disadvantage useful. Take 200 managers, give every one of them the SAME genuine information ratio, and measure how many of the first period's top quartile repeat in the second: every manager's genuine IR share of top-quartile managers who repeat (pure chance would give 25.00%) 0.00 24.76% 0.50 25.23% 1.00 25.30% A QUARTER REPEAT AT EVERY SKILL LEVEL. Making every manager brilliant does not raise the repeat rate, because the quartile is defined WITHIN the group: if all 200 have an information ratio of 1.00, which ranking of them survives is still decided entirely by noise. THAT IS LECTURE 5's PEER-MEDIAN FINDING IN ANOTHER FORM. A relative measure is blind to the level. PERSISTENCE APPEARS ONLY WHEN SKILL VARIES ACROSS MANAGERS: dispersion of genuine IR share of top-quartile managers who repeat 0.00 24.74% 0.25 31.58% 0.50 44.37% 1.00 63.33% THE REPEAT RATE RISES MONOTONICALLY FROM 24.74 TO 63.33 PER CENT AS THE DISPERSION WIDENS. So a persistence study measures HOW MUCH MANAGERS DIFFER, not how good any of them is. WHICH REFRAMES THE EMPIRICAL LITERATURE. The finding that persistence is weak is often read as "active management does not work". It supports a narrower conclusion: THE DISPERSION OF GENUINE SKILL IS SMALL RELATIVE TO THE NOISE, which is compatible with every manager being modestly skilled and with none of them being. The data cannot separate those two, and a repeat rate near a quarter is exactly what both produce.

Is a three-year dismissal ever rational?

Lecture 4 established that proving an information ratio of 0.50 at 95 per cent confidence takes 10.82 years while reviews run on three. THAT RULES OUT ONE JUSTIFICATION FOR A DISMISSAL AND DOES NOT SETTLE THE DECISION, and conflating the two is the most common misuse of that figure. Do it as a decision. Suppose 20 per cent of managers have genuine skill, worth an information ratio of 0.50, the annual tracking error is 4.00 per cent, and a three-year record is observed. A six per cent shortfall moves the posterior from 20.00 to 7.51 per cent, which is a material update. IT IS NOT CONCLUSIVE, AND THOSE ARE DIFFERENT THINGS. NOTE THE EXACT RESULT IN THE MIDDLE OF THAT TABLE. The posterior returns to precisely the 20.00 per cent prior at a cumulative active return of 3.00 per cent, which is exactly HALF the 6.00 per cent a skilled manager is expected to deliver over three years. That is not a coincidence: it is the point at which the skilled and unskilled likelihoods are equal.
NOW THE DECISION ITSELF. Replacing the manager gives a fresh draw from the prior, so the expected future active return is the prior times the skilled information ratio times the tracking error, less the cost of transitioning the portfolio. A manager who was exactly flat should be dismissed, because a flat three years is mild evidence against skill and the transition is cheap. SO THE ANSWER TO THE QUESTION IS YES, AND FOR A REASON NOBODY GIVES. THE THRESHOLD IS A TRANSITION COST CALCULATION AND NOT A SIGNIFICANCE TEST. Everything in it is observable: the transition cost, the expected holding period, and the prior share of skilled managers. None of it requires establishing that this particular manager is or is not skilled, which is the thing that takes 10.82 years. AND THE SENSITIVITY IS WHERE THE JUDGEMENT SITS. Raise the transition cost or shorten the expected holding period and the threshold moves down towards dismissal being unjustifiable; raise the prior share of skilled managers and switching becomes more attractive because the replacement is better. THE DEBATE THAT MATTERS IS ABOUT THOSE THREE NUMBERS AND NOT ABOUT WHETHER THREE YEARS IS LONG ENOUGH.

Every fee-paying discretionary portfolio must be included in a composite, which removes the ability to present the best accounts and quietly omit the rest, and that is the defect lecture 4's hundred-manager table measured from the outside. AND THE TWO LEFT OPEN ARE THE TWO THAT MATTER MOST. GIPS requires a benchmark to be DESCRIBED and does not require it to be APPROPRIATE, so a peer group meeting one of seven validity criteria is compliant. It does not prescribe an attribution method at all, so the convention that assigns 76.19 per cent of an excess return in lecture 6 remains the firm's choice. THAT IS NOT AN OVERSIGHT. A STANDARD CAN REQUIRE A DISCLOSURE AND CANNOT REQUIRE A JUDGEMENT TO BE CORRECT. Requiring appropriateness would mean the standard adjudicating whether a given benchmark suits a given mandate, which is a matter of investment judgement rather than of reporting practice.

PARTLY: the split is a convention was the risk worth taking? DEPENDS which risk you mean should we replace this manager? YES, as a transition-cost calculation rather than a significance test THE TWO UNQUALIFIED YES ANSWERS ARE BOTH ABOUT THE FUND AND ITS ASSETS. EVERY QUALIFIED OR NEGATIVE ANSWER IS ABOUT A MANAGER. That is the module's conclusion and it is the sentence lecture 1 ended on: the right measure is the one whose drivers the subject controls. A fund controls its funding plan and its asset allocation, and both are measurable exactly. A manager controls a small fraction of the outcome, 3.32 per cent of the variance by lecture 5's calculation, and the measurement industry is built to attribute far more than that to them. AND THE LAST ROW IS THE PRACTICAL PAYOFF. The one manager decision the arithmetic does support is the replacement decision, and it supports it through observable costs rather than through a judgement about skill. THAT IS THE USEFUL FORM OF SCEPTICISM: not that measurement tells you nothing, but that it answers a different question from the one being asked, and the substitute question is often the one you needed.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to carry is that the module's finding is not imprecision, it is underdetermination. Twenty-four defensible combinations, ten saying the manager won and fourteen saying they lost, with no criticisable step anywhere in the chain. A measurement that admits both verdicts is not a noisy estimate of the truth; IT IS A PROCESS WHOSE OUTPUT IS PARTLY DECIDED BY THE ANALYST, and the honest response is to fix the conventions in advance and in writing rather than to refine any one of them.

THE PRACTICAL FORM OF THAT, WHICH BELONGS IN A MANDATE RATHER THAN A REPORT. fix in advance the return definition 0.7059 of IR rides on it the comparator 0.3818 the risk adjustment 0.3653 the tracking error definition 0.1409 the attribution convention 76.19% of one excess return FIXING THEM COSTS NOTHING AND REMOVES THE DEGREE OF FREEDOM ENTIRELY. what cannot be fixed in advance is whether the comparator was APT, which is why that judgement has to be revisited rather than delegated to a standard.

The second is the persistence result, which is more useful stated as a question than as a fact. A quarter of top-quartile managers repeat whatever the group's shared skill, and the repeat rate rises to 63.33 per cent only as skill DISPERSION widens. SO THE QUESTION TO ASK OF ANY PERSISTENCE STUDY IS NOT "DID THE WINNERS REPEAT" BUT "HOW WIDELY DID GENUINE SKILL VARY IN THIS POPULATION", and the weak persistence found in the literature supports a narrower conclusion than it is usually given: the dispersion of skill is small relative to the noise, which is compatible with every manager being modestly skilled and with none of them being.

The third is the dismissal calculation, which is the one place in this module where measurement supports a decision cleanly. The break-even record is +0.2135 per cent over three years, and the whole calculation runs on three observable numbers: the transition cost, the expected holding period and the prior share of skilled managers. NONE OF IT REQUIRES ESTABLISHING WHETHER THIS PARTICULAR MANAGER IS SKILLED, which is the thing that takes 10.82 years. Lecture 4's figure rules out one justification for a dismissal; it does not rule out the dismissal, and treating it as though it does is the most common misuse of the result.

WHERE THE ARGUMENT ABOUT A DISMISSAL SHOULD ACTUALLY HAPPEN. NOT "is three years long enough to prove anything?" no, and it does not need to be BUT what does the transition cost? how long would we expect to hold the replacement? what share of available managers have genuine skill? ALL THREE ARE OBSERVABLE OR AT LEAST ARGUABLE FROM EVIDENCE. the significance question is neither, and it is the one that gets debated.

And the fourth, on GIPS, which deserves a fairer reading than the module's scepticism might suggest. It closes four of eight choices and halves the range of verdicts, from 1.3947 to 0.6888 of information ratio, and it closes precisely the choices that are matters of DEFINITION while leaving those that are matters of JUDGEMENT. That is the correct division of labour. A STANDARD CAN REQUIRE A DISCLOSURE AND CANNOT REQUIRE A JUDGEMENT TO BE CORRECT, and a standard that tried would be adjudicating investment decisions rather than reporting practice. The composite requirement in particular is the least discussed and most important clause in it, because it removes the ability to present the good accounts and omit the rest, which is the defect lecture 4 measured from the outside.

And a closing word on the module as a whole, since this is where it ends. Eight lectures have produced the same structural finding from eight directions: a return definition that depends on who chose the dates, a valuation conditional on an unobservable rate, a company measure whose sign is undetermined at short horizons, a decade-long skill inference against a three-year cycle, a comparator satisfying one of seven criteria, an attribution split that is a convention, seven risk measures that rank the same portfolios differently, and twenty-four defensible verdicts on one year. EVERY ONE IS A DISCRETIONARY CHOICE PRESENTED AS A CALCULATION. The useful conclusion is not that performance measurement is worthless but that it answers a different question from the one being asked, and the substitute question, whether the fund is on track and whether moving the money is worth its cost, is usually the one that needed answering.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. Every axis in the twenty-four-combination calculation comes from an earlier lecture in this module: the return definition from rates of return, the three comparators from benchmarks and comparators, and both the risk adjustment and the two tracking-error definitions from risk-adjusted measures and the information ratio. The 10.82-year inference and the manager-behaviour figures are from measuring portfolio performance, the attribution convention from attribution analysis, and the discount-rate conditionality in the second row of the closing table from individual asset performance. The Bayesian updating is standard, and the transition cost it trades against is the execution arithmetic from liquidity risk.

Where this goes next. This completes the performance measurement module and with it the investments internal environment's eighth and last module. What remains in the track is the thirteen-lecture portfolio management module, the six overview lectures on the institution and its governance, and the external environment. The findings here bear directly on the mandate lectures in the overview, since the practical response to underdetermination is to fix the conventions in the mandate rather than to refine the measurement, and on portfolio management, where the active-versus-passive decision is usually argued from persistence evidence that section B shows to be weaker than it appears.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank meets the underdetermination problem as model risk, and it has a formal apparatus for it that the investment industry does not: an independent validation function whose job is to establish that a measurement was constructed defensibly, documented in credit risk. The requirement to fix conventions in advance is the model documentation standard, and the requirement that a choice be justified rather than merely disclosed is where supervisory expectations go further than GIPS. The dismissal calculation also has a direct banking form in the decision to rebuild or retain a model: the same trade of an observable rebuild cost against an updated posterior on the model's quality, which is the monitoring trigger framework rather than a significance test on the latest backtest.
