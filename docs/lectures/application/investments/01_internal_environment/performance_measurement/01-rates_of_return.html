A manager 9.43 percentage points better can be ranked below one who is worse

The money-weighted return rewards whoever chose the cash flow dates, and that is the client. Searching for the boundary: an edge of up to 4.40 percentage points per sub-period, 9.43 over the year, can be erased entirely by the client's choice of contribution date. The time-weighted return is identical across every timing. Only one of the three measures can compare managers, and the exception is private equity, where the manager chooses the dates.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Every later lecture in this module depends on a return figure, and there are three of them. The money-weighted return is the internal rate of return on the actual cash flows and answers what the investor earned. The time-weighted return chains the sub-period returns and answers what the manager achieved per unit invested. The linked internal rate approximates the second without needing a valuation at every flow.

The difference is not academic. Take a manager who returns 20 per cent in the first half of a year and minus 10 in the second, and move only the date on which the client contributes. The time-weighted return is 8.0000 per cent in every case. The money-weighted return ranges from minus 1.3318 to 8.0000, a spread of 9.33 percentage points, and the manager did exactly the same thing throughout.

The stronger statement is that the money-weighted return can reverse a ranking. Searching for the boundary, a manager better in both sub-periods by up to 4.40 percentage points each, which is 9.4336 points over the year, is ranked below a worse manager once the first gets the worst contribution date and the second gets the best. That is why only the time-weighted return can compare managers.

Two exceptions matter. The two measures agree exactly when there are no cash flows, and when the return is constant throughout, both verified to machine precision. And the whole argument inverts for private equity, where the manager controls the drawdown dates: the same sub-period returns give a money-weighted return of 3.7816 per cent for a late call and minus 13.1782 for one at the top, a difference of 16.96 points that is genuine skill and that the time-weighted return discards.

Feynman test Two ways to say how a fund did. One asks what happened to the money, which depends on when the money arrived, and the client chose that. The other asks how each pound did while it was there, which is what the manager controls. Use the first to judge a manager and you can rank someone nine points better below someone worse, purely because their client paid in at an awkward moment. The exception is a fund where the manager decides when to call the money, because then the timing is the skill.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this lecture fixes the definitions the rest of the module uses. Every attribution, every risk-adjusted ratio and every benchmark comparison in lectures 2 to 8 starts from a return figure, and if that figure is money-weighted then every conclusion drawn from it is contaminated by cash flow timing the manager did not choose.

The second is that the magnitude is large enough to matter in practice rather than only in principle. A 9.33 percentage point spread from timing alone, on a fund whose true return was 8.00, is not a refinement. And it scales: at a flow of 300 per cent of opening value with volatile sub-periods the spread reaches 30.76 points, at which the money-weighted figure carries almost no information about the manager.

The third is that the reversal result sets a bound worth knowing. Any genuine edge below 4.40 percentage points per sub-period can be erased by timing, and most manager selection decisions turn on edges far smaller than that. So a comparison run on money-weighted figures is not merely noisy, it is capable of producing the wrong answer for a manager with a real and substantial advantage.

And a fourth. The rule is not that the time-weighted return is better. It is that the right measure is the one whose drivers the person being judged controls, which is why private equity reports internal rates of return and why a pension fund's own funding assessment uses money-weighted figures. Getting the rule right rather than the conclusion right is what makes it transferable.

The linked internal rate

The time-weighted return requires a valuation at every cash flow date. Before daily valuation that was impossible, so the LINKED INTERNAL RATE computes an internal rate over each sub-period and chains them. Its error depends on how much return dispersion is hidden inside a sub-period. BUT READ THE 50-DAY ROW, WHICH IS WORSE THAN ANNUAL. THE CONVERGENCE IS NOT MONOTONE. The error depends on WHICH returns happen to fall inside the sub-period containing the flow, not only on how long that sub-period is. A fifty-day window containing a sharp move produces a larger distortion than a whole year whose moves partly offset each other. THAT IS WORTH KNOWING BECAUSE THE INTUITIVE ASSUMPTION IS WRONG. More frequent valuation does not reliably reduce the linked internal rate's error; it reduces it in expectation and can increase it in any particular year. Only valuing at the flow date itself removes the error by construction, and at that point the measure has become the time-weighted return. CONFIRMING THE MECHANISM: with no cash flow at all, the linked internal rate equals the time-weighted return exactly at every frequency tested, to within 2.1 times ten to the minus fifteen, because each sub-period's internal rate is then simply its return.

IN PRIVATE EQUITY THE MANAGER DOES CHOOSE THEM, through capital calls and distributions, so the objection dissolves and the measure becomes the correct one. Demonstrate it on identical sub-period returns of plus 40 per cent then minus 25: capital called LATE (0.98y) TWRR 5.0000% MWRR 3.7816% capital called AT THE TOP (0.50y) TWRR 5.0000% MWRR -13.1782% THE TIME-WEIGHTED RETURN IS IDENTICAL AND THE MONEY-WEIGHTED RETURN DIFFERS BY 16.9598 PERCENTAGE POINTS. If the manager chose the timing, that difference is skill, and the time-weighted return discards every bit of it. A private equity manager who calls capital only when opportunities are good is doing the thing the fund exists to do, and reporting a time-weighted return would conceal it. SO THE RULE IS NOT "USE THE TIME-WEIGHTED RETURN". IT IS: USE THE MEASURE WHOSE DRIVERS THE PERSON BEING JUDGED CONTROLS. Stated that way it generalises, and it explains both halves of the table rather than the five rows that happen to favour one answer. TWO PRACTICAL CONSEQUENCES FOLLOW. FIRST, a fund's trustees need both figures for different purposes, and they are not alternatives: the money-weighted return tells them whether the fund is on track, and the time-weighted return tells them whether the manager is doing well. A fund can be behind on the first while the manager is ahead on the second, and the right response to each is different. SECOND, the GIPS standards require time-weighted returns for composites precisely because comparability is the whole purpose of a composite, and permit money-weighted returns for private market strategies precisely because of the exception above.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to carry is the boundary rather than the example. Any manager whose genuine edge is below 4.40 percentage points per sub-period can be ranked below a worse one by the money-weighted return, purely on the client's choice of contribution date. That is 9.4336 points over the year, and manager selection decisions are almost never made on edges that large. SO THE FAILURE IS NOT CONFINED TO CONTRIVED CASES; IT COVERS THE ENTIRE RANGE IN WHICH REAL DECISIONS ARE TAKEN.

WHY SEARCHING FOR THE BOUNDARY BEATS CONSTRUCTING AN EXAMPLE. an example shows the reversal is POSSIBLE the boundary shows the reversal covers every edge below 4.40% per half-year MY FIRST ATTEMPT AT THIS SECTION USED A SINGLE EXAMPLE AND FAILED, because I picked timings too weak to overcome the skill gap I had chosen. searching for the largest edge that still reverses avoids having to guess and produces the stronger statement.

The second is that the two measures agree in exactly two cases, and knowing them is what makes the divergence predictable. No cash flows, and a constant rate. Both verified to zero. Everything else diverges, and the size is the product of the flow relative to the fund and the dispersion of returns within the period: 0.36 percentage points at a ten per cent flow with mild returns, 30.76 at a 300 per cent flow with volatile ones. A GROWING SCHEME OR A FUND IN ITS LAUNCH PHASE SITS AT THE WRONG END OF THAT TABLE, which is exactly when the manager is most likely to be assessed.

The third is that the linked internal rate does not converge monotonically, which contradicts the natural assumption. A fifty-day valuation interval gave a larger error than an annual one here, because the error depends on which returns fall inside the sub-period containing the flow rather than on the length of that sub-period. MORE FREQUENT VALUATION IMPROVES THE APPROXIMATION IN EXPECTATION AND NOT IN ANY PARTICULAR YEAR, and only valuing at the flow date removes the error by construction, at which point the measure has become the time-weighted return.

THE THREE MEASURES, IN ONE SENTENCE EACH. MWRR correct about the money, contaminated by whoever chose the dates TWRR correct about the manager, silent about whether the fund is on track LIRR an artefact of the era before daily valuation, and not reliably monotone THE FIRST TWO ARE BOTH NEEDED AND ARE NOT ALTERNATIVES. the third is now of historical interest except where valuations remain infrequent, which is unlisted assets.

And the fourth, which is the transferable part. The rule is not that the time-weighted return is better. It is that the right measure is the one whose drivers the person being judged controls, and the private equity case proves it: identical time-weighted returns and a 16.96 point spread in the money-weighted figure, which is skill when the manager chose the dates. Stated as a conclusion, "use the time-weighted return" explains five rows of the table and contradicts the sixth. STATED AS A RULE ABOUT CONTROL, IT EXPLAINS ALL SIX, and it also explains why a scheme's own funding assessment is money-weighted while its manager's record is not.

And a closing note on where this module is going. Every lecture from here on begins with a return figure, so the choice made in this lecture propagates through all of them. Two problems already measured elsewhere bear on whether the rest of the module can do what it claims: the benchmark problem from portfolio construction lecture 4, where a proxy 98.88 per cent as efficient as the true one manufactures 0.97 per cent of spurious alpha, and the survivorship bias from risk measurement lecture 7, where culling the worst thirty per cent of funds lifts the peer median by 79 per cent of the edge being sought. NEITHER IS A PROBLEM WITH THE RETURN DEFINITION, AND BOTH SURVIVE GETTING IT RIGHT.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The internal rate of return and the discounting behind it are from fixed income pricing, and the geometric chaining of returns from strategic asset allocation, where the same compounding produces the time-diversification result. The observation that a measure answers a specific question and that choosing a measure is choosing a question is the pattern established in market risk. The two problems named at the end of step 5 are the benchmark inefficiency measured in the capital market line and CAPM and the survivorship bias measured in relative performance and basis risk. Cash flow timing as a source of divergence is the same mechanism as the drift in strategic asset allocation, seen through the return rather than the weights.

Where this goes next. Lecture 2 turns to individual assets, where the question is what a single holding contributed rather than what the fund returned. Lecture 3 covers company-level performance, economic value added and risk-adjusted return on capital. Lectures 4 to 7 build the portfolio-level machinery: why performance is measured at all and what it does to behaviour, the three benchmark choices, attribution into sector and stock selection, and the risk-adjusted ratios. Lecture 8 collects the limitations, and it is only meaningful once the number of discretionary choices in lectures 2 to 7 has become visible. Every one of those lectures starts from a return figure defined here.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank faces the same distinction in a different vocabulary. Return on equity is money-weighted in effect, since it divides earnings by the capital actually deployed, and it therefore rewards or punishes the timing of capital raising, which the business unit does not control. Risk-adjusted return on capital, treated in capital management, is the attempt to construct the equivalent of a time-weighted measure by charging each unit for the capital it uses rather than the capital the group happened to hold. And the private equity exception has a direct banking analogue in the treasury function, which does control the timing of funding and is therefore correctly judged on the money-weighted outcome.

Numbers used here. The three return definitions are from chapter 10 of the ASSA F105 summary in the user's notes, and the GIPS treatment of time-weighted returns for composites and money-weighted returns for private market strategies follows the Global Investment Performance Standards. Everything numerical is invented for illustration: an opening value of 100, contributions from 10 to 300 per cent of it, sub-period returns from plus 40 to minus 25 per cent, and a simulated year of 250 daily returns at a 10 per cent drift and 18 per cent volatility with a seeded generator. Internal rates are solved by bisection to 400 iterations. Thirteen claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions and every table was emitted by the computation and pasted without retyping. Four claims failed on the first run. The ranking-reversal example used flow timings too weak to overcome the skill gap I had chosen, and was replaced by a search for the boundary, which is the stronger result now reported. The linked internal rate calculation used a valuation frequency that did not divide the trading year exactly, dropping two days and producing a spurious error; correcting it revealed that the convergence is genuinely non-monotone, which is reported in step 4 rather than smoothed over. And the private equity comparison used two flow dates that both sat near the endpoints where the measures agree, so it demonstrated nothing until one was moved to the mid-point.
