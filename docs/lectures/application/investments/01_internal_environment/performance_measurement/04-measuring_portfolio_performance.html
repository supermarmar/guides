Establishing that a good manager is good takes 10.82 years, and mandates are reviewed every three

Four of the summary's six reasons for measuring performance rest on one inference, and that inference has a standard error nobody quotes. A manager with a genuine information ratio of 0.50 is behind the benchmark in 40.13 per cent of quarters, and proving the skill at 95 per cent confidence needs 10.82 years of data. A three-year review can only establish it for an information ratio above 0.9497, which is above the top decile. Meanwhile measuring 250 times a year instead of once adds nothing at all, and the best of 100 managers with no skill whatsoever shows an apparent information ratio of 1.5281.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Lectures 1 to 3 built measures. This lecture asks why anybody measures at all, and what the act of measuring does to the person being measured. The summary answers with three lists: six reasons, three advantages tied to three kinds of comparator, and seven disadvantages. The lists are complete and the arithmetic underneath them is where the lecture lives, because two of those disadvantages can be quantified precisely enough to change how a mandate should be written.

Start with the reasons. Four of the six are about the manager rather than the fund, so four of them rest on a single inference: does this manager have skill? The t-statistic on an information ratio is IR × √T in years, which means a genuinely skilled manager with an information ratio of 0.50 sits behind the benchmark in 40.13 per cent of quarters and 30.85 per cent of calendar years. Establishing the skill at 95 per cent confidence takes 10.82 years. A three-year review cycle can only do it for an information ratio above 0.9497, which almost nobody has.

Then the frequency question, where the intuition is simply wrong. Measuring monthly rather than annually gives twelve times the observations and, over a fixed ten-year span, changes the power to detect skill by less than three percentage points across a 250-fold change in the number of observations. Lengthening the span from one year to twenty raises it from 14.82 to 72.23 per cent. So the frequency is a cost and the span is the information, which means the summary is right to list "valuation costs" alongside them and wrong to put frequency and period in the same bullet: they have opposite answers.

Then comparison, which the summary lists as the second reason. Give a hundred managers no skill at all, measure them over three years and take the best: the expected t-statistic is 2.6468, an apparent information ratio of 1.5281, clearing a 95 per cent threshold 99.5 per cent of the time. With one manager the same threshold is cleared 5.53 per cent of the time, which is what a five per cent test should give. The selection breaks, not the test.

And the disadvantage the summary names most sharply, influencing manager behaviour in the short term. A manager two per cent behind with one year left raises the chance of recovery from 6.68 to 45.03 per cent by raising tracking error from one to twelve per cent. A manager one per cent ahead lowers their chance of survival from 93.32 to 54.97 per cent by doing the same thing. The identical rule makes losers gamble and winners hug the index, and the gamble raises the chance of a further ten per cent loss from nothing to 21.43 per cent.

Feynman test Six reasons to grade a fund manager, four of which need you to know whether they are actually any good. That turns out to take about eleven years to establish for a genuinely good one, and mandates get reviewed every three. Checking the score more often does not help at all, because you learn from elapsed time, not from how often you look, though it does cost money. Picking the best of a hundred gives you someone who looks brilliant even when nobody in the room can do anything. And once a manager knows the rule, the rule itself decides how they behave: behind, they gamble, because only volatility can save them; ahead, they stop taking risk, because only volatility can lose it.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the review cycle and the statistics are irreconcilable, and the gap is not marginal. Ten point eight two years against a three-year cycle is a factor of 3.6. Any process that dismisses managers on three-year records is not selecting on skill, it is selecting on noise, and the arithmetic says so without needing any empirical study of manager persistence.

The second is that the frequency result inverts a common instinct. Trustees who move from annual to monthly reporting believe they are improving oversight. They are improving nothing about the skill inference, they are adding valuation cost, and, worse, they are creating more opportunities for the interim-score problem in step 4 to bite. More frequent measurement is not neutral, it is actively harmful once the behavioural effect is included.

The third is that the peer-group comparison, which the summary offers as an advantage because it permits cost-benefit analysis, produces a winner who looks skilled by construction. An apparent information ratio of 1.5281 from a hundred managers with no ability is higher than any genuine manager in the sample would plausibly have. Anybody selecting a manager on a three-year league table is reading the maximum of a distribution and calling it a mean.

And the fourth is the one that matters for writing a mandate rather than reading a report. The behavioural effect is not a soft caveat about incentives, it is a computable change in the distribution of client outcomes: 21.43 per cent chance of a further ten per cent loss when the manager is gambling, against essentially zero when they are not. That is a cost the client bears and the measurement system creates.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to carry is the single ratio that governs the whole lecture: 10.82 years against a three-year review cycle, a factor of 3.6. Everything else follows from it. A process that dismisses managers on three-year records is selecting on noise, and no improvement in the measurement can fix that, because the limit is the elapsed time and not the technique. SO THE USEFUL RESPONSE IS TO STOP ASKING THE REVIEW TO TEST SKILL and to ask it instead whether the manager is still doing what was bought, still resourced, still following the process. Those questions are answerable in three years.

WHAT A THREE-YEAR REVIEW CAN AND CANNOT ANSWER. CANNOT does this manager have skill? needs 10.82 years at an IR of 0.50 CAN is the process still the one we bought? CAN is the team still the one we hired? CAN is the tracking error within the mandate? CAN are the fees what we agreed? FOUR OF THOSE FIVE ARE OBSERVED RATHER THAN ESTIMATED, WHICH IS WHY THEY HAVE NO STANDARD ERROR. that is the general principle: prefer the question whose answer you can see over the question whose answer you have to infer, when the inference needs a decade you do not have.

The second is the frequency result, which is the most counterintuitive thing here and the easiest to act on. A 250-fold increase in observations moved the power to detect skill by 2.36 percentage points. The information in a track record is carried by the elapsed span, so looking more often adds cost and no knowledge. AND THE ANNUAL CASE IS THE TRAP: it showed the highest average t-statistic of all, 1.7195 against a theoretical 1.5811, purely as small-sample bias, while detecting skill no more often than daily data. A measure that looks better and performs identically is worth recognising as a bias rather than a finding.

The third is that the peer group manufactures the star it is asked to find. A hundred managers with no ability produce a best performer with an apparent information ratio of 1.5281, a level the same arithmetic calls essentially unheard of. The control row settles where the fault lies, because a single manager clears the threshold 5.53 per cent of the time, which is exactly what the test promises. THE TEST IS SOUND AND THE SELECTION IS NOT, and correcting for the hundred comparisons demands an information ratio above 1.73 at three years, which nobody has. So a three-year peer ranking cannot identify skill at any threshold, and the peer group's honest use is cost comparison, where fees are observed rather than estimated.

WHY THE PEER GROUP IS STILL WORTH HAVING, DESPITE ALL OF THAT. for identifying skill useless at three years, at any threshold for comparing fees exact, because fees are observed for comparing mandates useful, because holdings are observed for comparing risk taken useful, because tracking error is estimated far more precisely than a mean is THE PATTERN IS THAT EVERYTHING EXCEPT THE MEAN RETURN IS ESTIMABLE FROM SHORT SAMPLES. a variance converges much faster than a mean, which is the same asymmetry that made the covariance matrix usable and the expected-return vector unusable in portfolio construction lecture 6.

And the fourth, which is the transferable one and the reason this lecture belongs before lectures 5 to 7 rather than after them. Measurement is not a passive observation of a system, it is an input to the system. The same rule makes a manager two per cent behind raise their survival odds from 6.68 to 45.03 per cent by gambling, and a manager one per cent ahead cut theirs from 93.32 to 54.97 per cent by doing the same, while the client's chance of a further ten per cent loss goes from nothing to 21.43 per cent. THE FIX IS IN THE MANDATE AND NOT IN THE MEASURE: a tracking-error limit, a longer evaluation period, or a symmetric fee. Every one of those is a contract term, and no refinement of the performance calculation substitutes for any of them.

And a note on where the module goes from here, because this lecture changes how the next three should be read. Lecture 5 chooses the comparator, lecture 6 decomposes the result against it, and lecture 7 adjusts it for risk. All three make the measurement more precise, and none of them shortens the 10.82 years or removes the incentive documented in step 4. THE PRECISION AND THE POWER ARE DIFFERENT THINGS, and the module's last lecture exists because it takes until then to see how many discretionary choices have accumulated without any of them improving the inference.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The information ratio and the tracking error come from risk budgets, VaR and tracking error, where the tracking-error limit named as the fix in step 4 is constructed. The return definitions being compared are from rates of return, and the observation that a variance converges far faster than a mean is the result that made the covariance matrix usable and expected returns unusable in limitations and Black-Litterman. The survivorship problem that compounds the peer-group finding here was measured in relative performance and basis risk, where culling the worst thirty per cent of funds lifted the peer median by most of the edge being sought. And the convexity that makes the gamble rational is the option payoff from options, applied to a manager's employment rather than to an asset.

Where this goes next. Lecture 5 chooses between the three comparators this lecture only named, and the peer-group result here is the reason it cannot simply prefer the one with the most available data. Lecture 6 decomposes a return against the chosen benchmark into sector and stock selection, which raises the same multiple-comparisons problem one level down, since an attribution with enough categories will always find one that looks significant. Lecture 7 adds the risk adjustment that this lecture's second disadvantage demands, and inherits the beta estimation problem measured in company performance, EVA and RAROC. Lecture 8 collects the limitations, and the 10.82-year figure is the single most important number in it.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank faces the identical structure with the labels changed, and in one respect faces it more acutely. The gamble-when-behind incentive is exactly the risk-taking pathology that capital requirements exist to constrain, which is why a trading desk's limits are set independently of its profit target, and it is the mechanism behind the rogue-trading losses treated in operational risk. The three-year review problem appears as the question of how long a credit model's performance must be observed before it can be judged, which is the monitoring question in credit risk, and there the answer is worse than here because default events are rarer than monthly returns. And the multiple-comparisons finding is why model validation requires the number of specifications tried to be disclosed, since a bank that fits two hundred candidate scorecards and reports the best is doing what the hundred-manager table does.

Numbers used here. The six reasons, three advantages and seven disadvantages are transcribed from the "portfolio performance" section of chapter 10 of the ASSA F105 summary in the user's notes, and the sorting of the reasons by subject, the splitting of the frequency bullet and the placement table are mine rather than the summary's. The relationship t = IR × √T is the standard information-ratio result set out in Grinold and Kahn's Active Portfolio Management. Everything numerical is invented for illustration: information ratios from 0.25 to 1.50, a genuine annual edge of 0.50 per cent, deficits from 0.2 to 2.0 per cent, tracking errors from one to twelve per cent, peer groups from 1 to 250 managers, and a ten-year span sampled at five frequencies. The simulations use seeded generators, 20,000 trials per frequency and 3,000 per peer-group size, and the normal quantile is found by bisection to 300 iterations. Twenty claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions and all twenty passed; every table was emitted by the computation and each value was then checked mechanically against the finished page. Two claims failed on the first run and one of them changed the lecture. The first was a wrong target, since a top-decile manager is behind in 30.85 per cent of quarters rather than the third I had guessed. The second was more useful: I had asserted the mean t-statistic would match theory at every frequency, and the annual case sits 8.8 per cent above it. Investigating rather than loosening the tolerance identified small-sample bias in the t-statistic as the cause, and the fact that the bias buys no additional power is now the sharpest illustration in step 4 of why frequency is not information.
