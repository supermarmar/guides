The same portfolio splits into sector and stock selection two different ways, and the summary picks one silently

Attribution is an algebraic insertion, not an economic discovery. Inserting a different intermediate term gives a sector effect of −1.0500 or −0.2500 per cent from identical data, the 0.8000 per cent gap being the interaction term, and the total is 1.0500 per cent either way. Worse, the summary's allocation formula calls a 10 point overweight in a sector that lagged the benchmark by 4.55 points a good decision, and the error is invisible at the total and wrong in every line anybody reads.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Lecture 5 chose the benchmark. This lecture decomposes the difference between the portfolio and it. The summary gives three formulas built from a two-by-two grid of actual and notional weights crossed with actual and notional returns, and the arithmetic works perfectly. What the formulas conceal is that a choice has been made, that the choice is not the standard one, and that neither survives being extended over more than one period.

The identity first, because it is exact and worth understanding for what it is. Sector selection plus stock selection equals total selection to zero residual, and it does so because the intermediate term cancels. The decomposition is an algebraic insertion, not an economic discovery. Nothing about the world guarantees the two pieces mean anything; the arithmetic would work with any intermediate at all.

Which matters, because there are two intermediates available. Routing through actual weights and notional returns gives a sector effect of −1.0500 per cent and a stock effect of 2.1000. Routing through notional weights and actual returns gives −0.2500 and 1.3000. Same data, same total of 1.0500 per cent, different split, and the entire 0.8000 per cent gap is the interaction term. The summary takes the first route, which hands the whole interaction to stock selection, and does not mention that a choice exists.

Then the allocation term, which is wrong rather than merely ambiguous. The summary uses (w − W) × B, so overweighting any sector with a positive return scores positively. Resources was overweighted by ten points and returned 3.00 per cent, which is 4.55 points below the benchmark's 7.55: the summary's formula reports +0.3000 per cent and Brinson-Fachler reports −0.4550. And the two allocation totals are identical, because active weights sum to zero, so the defect never shows at the bottom of the report.

And the multi-period problem the summary raises without solving. Effects are additive and returns compound, so single-period effects do not sum to the multi-period excess. Over four years the residual is 22.56 per cent of the excess return, over forty it is 94.23, and shrinking the per-period returns to a hundredth drives it to 2.88, which confirms compounding as the cause. A multi-period attribution needs a linking method and the summary names none.

Feynman test You beat your benchmark by one per cent. Was it because you picked the right industries or the right shares inside them? The standard answer builds a little grid and subtracts, and it always adds up, which makes it feel like a fact. It is not: you can subtract in two different orders and get two different answers about which half of your job you did well, both correct. The usual formula also praises you for holding lots of a sector that went up a bit while the market went up a lot, which is backwards. And none of it survives being added across years, because money compounds and these numbers do not.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that attribution reports are read as findings and are partly conventions. A manager whose sector effect is −1.0500 per cent on one convention and −0.2500 on another is being told two different things about which half of the mandate they are executing well. If the report drives a decision about whether to keep the asset allocation overlay or the stock-picking team, the convention is deciding it.

The second is that the allocation defect points the wrong way in the most common market condition. In a rising market almost every sector has a positive absolute return, so the summary's formula rewards every overweight and punishes every underweight regardless of relative performance. That is not a small bias, it is a systematic reversal of the sign for any overweighted laggard, and rising markets are when most attribution is read.

The third is that the interaction term is not a rounding artefact. Here it is 0.8000 per cent against a total of 1.0500, so 76.19 per cent of the total selection profit is a quantity whose attribution is arbitrary. Naming it as a third component costs nothing, reconciles to the same total, and removes the choice.

And the fourth is that the multi-period residual grows without limit while the reported effects do not. Over forty years the residual is 94.23 per cent of the excess return, which means the attribution table is describing six per cent of what happened. Any statement of the form "stock selection added X over the last decade" is either using a linking method or is wrong by an amount nobody has quoted.

The mental model

A two-by-two grid, two ways through it, one wrong allocation term, and a residual that eats the answer.

The four corners and the identity

A manager who tracks the sector weights faithfully and picks their own stocks inside them is running an active portfolio, and the attribution correctly puts the whole of their activity in the stock column. That is the method working as intended, and it is the one place in this lecture where it does.

The split is not unique

There are two hypothetical corners, so there are two routes, and the summary uses one without saying that a choice exists: route through the 2x2 sector effect stock effect total via P(actual w, notional r) -1.0500% 2.1000% 1.0500% via P(notional w, actual r) -0.2500% 1.3000% 1.0500% the difference 0.8000% -0.8000% 0.0000% THE TOTAL IS IDENTICAL AT 1.0500 PER CENT AND THE SPLIT IS NOT. It is the part of the excess return that arises from being overweight in a sector where you also picked well, and it cannot be assigned to allocation or to selection on any principled ground, because it required both. the honest three-way split contribution sector (allocation) sum (w-W)B -1.0500% stock (selection) sum W(R-B) 1.3000% interaction sum (w-W)(R-B) 0.8000% total 1.0500% THE INTERACTION IS 0.8000 PER CENT AGAINST A TOTAL OF 1.0500, WHICH IS 76.19 PER CENT OF IT. This is not a rounding matter, and the three-way split reconciles to exactly the same total, so naming the term costs nothing. WHICH MEANS THE SUMMARY'S TWO-WAY SPLIT IS A CHOICE PRESENTED AS AN IDENTITY. It hands the whole interaction to stock selection, because the selection term uses the manager's ACTUAL weights rather than the benchmark's. A reader who knows that can undo it; a reader who does not will attribute 0.8000 per cent of performance to the stock pickers on the strength of a convention.

The allocation term is wrong, and Brinson-Fachler fixes it

The summary's sector effect is (w - W) x B, and it has a defect that appears as soon as the benchmark's return is positive. Overweighting ANY sector with a positive absolute return scores positively, even a sector that badly lagged the benchmark. Brinson and Fachler's correction is to measure each sector's return against the benchmark's own total: BHB allocation_i = (w_i - W_i) x B_i BF allocation_i = (w_i - W_i) x (B_i - B_total) The benchmark returned 7.5500 per cent overall. Comparing the two: sector active wt sector bm ret vs benchmark BHB says BF says agree? resources 10.00% 3.00% -4.55% 0.3000% -0.4550% NO, SIGN FLIP financials -15.00% 12.00% 4.45% -1.8000% -0.6675% yes industrials 5.00% 9.00% 1.45% 0.4500% 0.0725% yes cash 0.00% 2.00% -5.55% 0.0000% -0.0000% yes TOTAL - - - -1.0500% -1.0500% RESOURCES CHANGES SIGN. It was overweighted by ten points and returned 3.00 per cent, which is positive and is 4.55 points BELOW the benchmark's 7.55. THE SUMMARY'S FORMULA CALLS THAT A GOOD DECISION WORTH +0.3000 PER CENT AND BRINSON-FACHLER CALLS IT A BAD ONE COSTING 0.4550. Brinson-Fachler is right: money was moved into a sector that underperformed, and that cost the fund. AND THE TWO TOTALS ARE IDENTICAL AT −1.0500 PER CENT, verified exactly. The reason is that active weights sum to zero, so subtracting a constant B_total from every sector's return washes out in aggregate. THAT IS WHAT MAKES THE DEFECT DANGEROUS RATHER THAN OBVIOUS. It is invisible at the total, which is the number that gets checked, and wrong in the individual lines, which are the numbers that get discussed. A committee reading this report would congratulate whoever argued for resources. THE PRACTICAL RULE: an allocation effect should answer "did moving money here help", and that question can only be answered relative to where the money came from, which is the benchmark as a whole. Any allocation formula that does not subtract the benchmark's own return is answering a different question, namely "did this sector go up", which the manager did not decide.

Single-period effects do not add up over time

The summary says to attribute over long periods, which is right for the reasons lecture 4 established, and it creates a problem it does not mention. Returns COMPOUND and attribution effects are ADDITIVE, so the sum of the yearly effects is not the multi-period excess return. At four years the residual is already 22.56 per cent of the excess return, at twelve it is 46.09, and at forty it is 94.23, so the attribution table describes 5.77 per cent of what happened. The forty-year row is a mechanical illustration rather than a realistic mandate: compounding a seven-and-a-half per cent yearly excess structure for four decades produces magnitudes no real fund shows, and the row is there to display the mechanism running to its limit. THE TWELVE-YEAR ROW IS THE ONE TO CARRY, because twelve years is an ordinary length for a "since inception" attribution and 46.09 per cent of the excess return is unaccounted for in it. MECHANISM TEST. SO A MULTI-PERIOD ATTRIBUTION REQUIRES A LINKING METHOD. The standard approaches, Carino's logarithmic scaling and Menchero's optimised linking, both work by scaling each period's effects so that they sum to the compounded excess return, which is an adjustment with no economic content beyond forcing the books to balance. THE SUMMARY ASKS FOR LONG-PERIOD ATTRIBUTION AND NAMES NO LINKING METHOD, so a candidate following it literally would produce a table that does not reconcile.

With enough sectors, one always looks skilled

Lecture 4 showed that the best of a hundred skill-free managers looks brilliant. The same arithmetic applies to the sectors inside ONE manager's attribution, and the result is more interesting than the straightforward version. Give the manager zero skill in every sector and look at the largest single sector effect the report displays: sectors in the report expected largest |effect| P(some sector shows more than +0.50%) 2 2.8058% 66.35% 5 1.5595% 84.00% 10 0.9400% 82.80% 25 0.4509% 13.95% 50 0.2505% 0.00% THE HAZARD IS NOT MONOTONE. It peaks at five sectors, where some sector clears half a per cent 84.00 per cent of the time with no skill anywhere, and collapses to zero by fifty. TWO FORCES PULL AGAINST EACH OTHER AND THEY CROSS. Adding sectors multiplies the chances of finding a large effect somewhere, and it shrinks each sector's weight, so each effect is smaller. The expected largest effect falls monotonically from 2.8058 to 0.2505 per cent, while the chance of clearing a FIXED half-point threshold rises to a peak and then collapses. SO THE DANGEROUS ZONE IS MODERATE GRANULARITY, WHICH IS EXACTLY WHERE REAL ATTRIBUTION REPORTS SIT. A four-sector or ten-sector report is the standard format, and it is the format in which a spurious half-point sector effect is most likely to appear. A fifty-sector report is safer, not because it is better attribution but because no single line can be large enough to notice. THE FIX IS THE SAME AS IN LECTURE 4 AND IT IS NOT A BETTER FORMULA. It is to require that the number of lines in the report be taken into account before any one of them is called significant, and to treat a single standout sector in a four-line table as the weakest possible evidence rather than the strongest.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to carry is that the exactness of the identity is not evidence of anything. Sector plus stock equals total with zero residual because the intermediate term cancels, and it would cancel for any intermediate at all, including one with no meaning. AN IDENTITY THAT HOLDS BY CONSTRUCTION CANNOT CONFIRM THE INTERPRETATION PLACED ON ITS PARTS. That is why two routes through the same grid give sector effects of −1.0500 and −0.2500 per cent while both totals are 1.0500: the total is a fact and the split is a convention.

THE THREE-WAY SPLIT IS THE DEFENSIBLE ONE, AND IT COSTS NOTHING. allocation sum (w-W)B what the weighting decision did on benchmark returns selection sum W(R-B) what the stock decisions did at benchmark weights interaction sum (w-W)(R-B) what required BOTH decisions to go the same way IT RECONCILES TO THE SAME TOTAL AS EITHER TWO-WAY SPLIT. so the only thing a two-way split buys is a tidier table, at the price of assigning 76.19 per cent of the excess return on a convention the reader cannot see.

The second is that the allocation defect is the one to check first in any real report, because it is both common and invisible where people look. The summary's formula rewards overweighting any sector that rose, and resources rose 3.00 per cent while the benchmark rose 7.55, so a ten point overweight in a laggard scores +0.3000 rather than −0.4550. THE TOTALS AGREE EXACTLY AT −1.0500, which is what makes it survive review: the number that gets reconciled is right and every line that gets discussed is wrong. The diagnostic is one column wide, namely each sector's benchmark return minus the benchmark's own total, and if a report does not show it the allocation figures cannot be read.

The third is the multi-period residual, and the twelve-year row is the one that matters rather than the forty. Twelve years is an ordinary length for a since-inception attribution, and 46.09 per cent of the excess return is missing from it. The mechanism test settles the cause, since scaling the per-period returns to a hundredth drops the residual to 2.88 per cent. SO THE QUESTION TO ASK OF ANY MULTI-PERIOD ATTRIBUTION IS WHETHER IT RECONCILES TO THE COMPOUNDED EXCESS RETURN, and if it does then a linking method has been applied and it is worth knowing which, because Carino's and Menchero's scalings distribute the reconciliation differently.

THE ONE-LINE TEST FOR A MULTI-PERIOD ATTRIBUTION REPORT. does sum of the periods' effects == compounded portfolio return - compounded benchmark return ? YES a linking method has been applied; ask which one NO the table does not describe the result, and the gap is unquoted AT TWELVE YEARS THE GAP WAS 46.09 PER CENT OF THE EXCESS RETURN HERE. nobody reading the table would know unless they did that subtraction themselves.

And the fourth, which is the finding I did not expect. The spurious-sector hazard is not monotone in the number of sectors: it peaks at five, where some sector clears half a per cent 84.00 per cent of the time with no skill anywhere, and collapses to nothing by fifty. Two forces cross, since adding sectors multiplies the chances and shrinks each effect. THE DANGEROUS ZONE IS MODERATE GRANULARITY, AND THAT IS THE STANDARD REPORT FORMAT. The instinct that a coarse four-line attribution is safer than a fine fifty-line one is exactly backwards for this particular failure.

Lecture 7 adds the risk adjustment that the summary's third qualification demands, and it inherits everything here: a risk-adjusted sector effect is still split by a convention, still uses whichever allocation formula the system implements, and still needs linking over time. Lecture 8 collects the limitations, and attribution supplies it with the cleanest example in the module of a calculation that is arithmetically exact and substantively a choice. THE FOUR QUALIFICATIONS THE SUMMARY MAKES ARE ALL ABOUT THE INPUTS AND THE THREE IT OMITS ARE ALL ABOUT THE DECOMPOSITION, which is the general lesson: a source states the caveats that look like judgement and omits the ones buried in a formula, because a formula does not look like a choice.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The benchmark being decomposed against is chosen in benchmarks and comparators, and this lecture inherits that choice completely: an attribution against a comparator that fails the validity criteria decomposes a difference that meant nothing. The time-weighted return the summary requires as the input is from rates of return, and the multiple-comparisons problem reappearing at sector level was first measured in measuring portfolio performance. The sector weights whose departures from benchmark are being attributed are constrained by the load ratios in lecture 5, and the strategic allocation they depart from is built in strategic asset allocation. The compounding that breaks additivity over time is the same arithmetic that made income 82.50 per cent of a fifty-year total return in individual asset performance.

Where this goes next. Lecture 7 supplies the risk adjustment the summary's third qualification asks for, covering Sharpe, Treynor, Jensen, tracking error and the downside measures, and it inherits every structural problem here unchanged: a risk-adjusted sector effect is still split by an invisible convention and still needs linking across periods. It also inherits the beta estimation problem measured in company performance, EVA and RAROC, since Treynor and Jensen both depend on an estimated beta. Lecture 8 collects the limitations, and attribution is the module's cleanest case of a calculation that is arithmetically exact and substantively a choice, so the interaction term, the Brinson-Fachler correction and the linking residual all belong there.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank runs the identical decomposition on its net interest margin, splitting a change into a volume effect and a rate effect, and it meets the same interaction term and resolves it the same arbitrary way. The mechanics are in financial management, and the parallel is exact: volume times rate is a product, so a change in the product cannot be split into two parts without a residual that required both to move. The multi-period linking problem also has a direct banking form in the attribution of a change in expected credit loss between stage migration, model change and portfolio growth, which is treated in credit risk, and there the disclosure requirement forces the reconciliation that the investment version leaves optional.

Numbers used here. The three formulas, the two-by-two structure, the passive-manager test and the four qualifications are from the "attribution analysis" section of chapter 10 of the ASSA F105 summary in the user's notes, which gives the formulas without derivation and without naming their source. The decomposition is Brinson, Hood and Beebower (1986), Financial Analysts Journal 42(4), with the interaction folded into selection; the allocation correction is Brinson and Fachler (1985); the multi-period linking methods named in step 4 are Carino (1999) and Menchero (2000). Everything numerical is invented for illustration: four sectors at benchmark weights of 30, 35, 25 and 10 per cent against portfolio weights of 40, 20, 30 and 10, benchmark returns of 3, 12, 9 and 2 per cent against portfolio returns of 5.5, 10, 14 and 2, horizons from one to forty years, and reports from two to fifty sectors. The multi-period figures use seeded generators with 4,000 simulations per sector count, and the forty-year row is flagged in the body as a mechanical illustration rather than a realistic horizon. Twenty claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions, all twenty passed, none of them can pass without the computation, and every emitted value was checked mechanically against the finished page. Two failed on the first run and one changed a conclusion. The first was a mislabelling: I had assigned the second route's allocation and selection terms the wrong way round, so the interaction test failed even though the underlying algebra was right. The second was more useful. I had asserted that the chance of a spurious sector effect rises with the number of sectors, and it does not: it peaks at five and collapses by fifty, because adding sectors multiplies the chances and shrinks each effect at the same time. That non-monotonicity is now the finding in step 4 rather than the trend I expected.
