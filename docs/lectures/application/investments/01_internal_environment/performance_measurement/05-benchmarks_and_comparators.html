Lectures / Investments / Internal environment / Performance measurement

A peer group satisfies one of the seven things a benchmark has to be

Scored against the standard validity criteria, the notional benchmark meets all seven, a published index meets six, and a peer group meets one. The summary offers them in the reverse order. Along the way: a mandate with a 25 per cent load ratio states a 75 per cent equity ceiling the manager cannot reach, the true ceiling being 70; a four-for-one share split cuts a company's weight in a price-weighted index by 33.20 points without changing anything economic; and measuring against a notional benchmark discards 96.68 per cent of what actually happened to the fund.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Lecture 4 established that measurement is an input to the system rather than an observation of it. This lecture chooses what to measure against. The summary names three comparators, a published index, a competitor peer group and a notional benchmark, and gives each one advantage. Scoring them instead against what a benchmark actually has to be settles the choice much more sharply than the summary's ordering suggests.

The standard validity criteria are that a benchmark be unambiguous, investable, measurable, appropriate, reflective of current investment opinion, specified in advance and accountable. Against those, the notional benchmark meets all seven, a published index meets six, failing only appropriateness, and a peer group meets one. The summary presents the published index first on the ground that its data is readily available, and availability is not one of the criteria at all.

The peer group's decisive defect is that it is not investable: no portfolio delivers the median manager's return, so "beat the median" is an objective no strategy can be built to meet. Worse, if every manager in the group is equally skilled then each has exactly a 50 per cent chance of beating the median, whatever that shared skill is. A group of excellent managers and a group of useless ones produce identical distributions of peer ranking. And culling the worst thirty per cent lifts the surviving median by 1.9075 per cent, which is 190.75 per cent of a one per cent genuine edge.

The notional benchmark earns its seven out of seven by being the fund's own strategic asset allocation held passively, and that is exactly what makes it silent about the fund. Simulating a three-sleeve fund, the return relative to the benchmark carries 3.32 per cent of the total variance and the benchmark absorbs 96.68 per cent. So it is the right comparator for the manager and the wrong one for the fund, which needs its liabilities instead. That is lecture 1's conclusion arriving again in different clothing.

Then the constraint vocabulary, where the arithmetic produces something a mandate drafter would want to know. A 25 per cent load ratio gives the 60 per cent equity sleeve 30 points of freedom and the five per cent cash sleeve 2.50 points, a ratio of twelve to one, so a proportional limit concentrates discretion where the fund is already most exposed. And because every overweight must be funded, the stated 75 per cent equity ceiling is unreachable: the other three sleeves can supply only ten points of underweight against the fifteen equity is nominally allowed, so the true ceiling is 70 per cent.

Feynman test Three things you could compare a fund against. Other funds, which sounds fair and is useless, because you cannot buy the average of your competitors and because if everyone is equally good half of them still lose. A published index, which you can buy but which was not built for your fund and can be built so badly that a share split changes a company's importance in it. Or a made-up portfolio that is exactly your own plan held without any trading, which is the only fair test of the manager and tells you nothing about whether the plan was any good, because it contains the plan.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the peer group is the most widely used comparator in practice and the least defensible in principle. One criterion out of seven is not a close call. And the coin-toss result is what makes it hopeless rather than merely imperfect: a measure that returns fifty per cent for every manager regardless of the group's shared skill is not a noisy estimate of skill, it is not measuring skill at all.

The second is that the notional benchmark's greatest strength and its greatest weakness are the same property. It strips out the asset allocation, which is what makes it a fair test of the manager, and the asset allocation was 96.68 per cent of the variance. A trustee reading only benchmark-relative reports is reading about the three per cent and not the ninety-seven.

The third is the load ratio result, which is a live drafting error rather than a theoretical curiosity. A mandate that states a 75 per cent equity limit the manager cannot reach is either misleading the trustees about the risk they have authorised or misleading the manager about the freedom they have been given. The fix is to state limits as load differences in percentage points, or to check the box for feasibility before signing it.

And the fourth is that index construction is not a technicality safely delegated to the index provider. A price-weighted index reweights its constituents in response to share splits, which carry no information whatever. The summary is right that the Dow Jones is unsuitable and gives the wrong reason, and the right reason is worth having because it generalises: any weighting scheme whose weights respond to corporate actions rather than to economic size fails both unambiguity and investability at once.

Step 4

The detail

Three comparators, seven criteria

The summary offers three comparators and one advantage each: a published index because its data is readily available, a competitor peer group because it permits cost-benefit analysis, and a notional benchmark because it checks the mandate against the fund's own objectives. Those are three different claims about three different things rather than three advantages of one thing. A sharper test is to ask what a benchmark has to BE. The standard set of criteria, from Bailey, Richards and Tierney, is that it must be UNAMBIGUOUS in its constituents and weights, INVESTABLE so that holding it is an option, MEASURABLE at reasonable frequency, APPROPRIATE to the manager's style and the fund's objectives, REFLECTIVE of current investment opinion, SPECIFIED IN ADVANCE, and ACCOUNTABLE in the sense that the manager accepts it. Applying them: criterion published index peer group notional benchmark unambiguous yes NO yes investable yes NO yes measurable yes yes yes appropriate NO NO yes reflects opinion yes NO yes specified ahead yes NO yes accountable yes NO yes criteria met 6 1 7 THE PEER GROUP MEETS ONE CRITERION OF SEVEN AND THE NOTIONAL BENCHMARK MEETS ALL SEVEN. The peer group is measurable and nothing else: its constituents are not known in advance, its composition changes as funds open and close, you cannot hold it, it was not chosen to suit the manager, and no manager agreed to it. AND THE SUMMARY PRESENTS THEM IN THE REVERSE ORDER, leading with the published index because its data is readily available. AVAILABILITY IS NOT ONE OF THE SEVEN CRITERIA. It is a property of the measurement process rather than of the benchmark, and it is the reason the least valid comparators are the most widely used.

What a notional benchmark measures, and what it hides

The notional benchmark is the fund's own strategic asset allocation held passively: the sleeve weights the trustees chose, each filled with an index. That is what earns it seven out of seven, and it has a consequence the summary does not draw. MEASURING AGAINST IT REMOVES THE ASSET ALLOCATION DECISION FROM THE ASSESSMENT ENTIRELY, because the benchmark contains it. Quantify what that discards. Simulate a fund of 60 per cent equity, 30 per cent bonds and ten per cent cash, with a three per cent active risk inside each sleeve, 200,000 draws: quantity standard deviation share of total variance total return of the fund 11.1535% 100.00% return relative to the notional benchmark 2.0323% 3.32% the market movement the benchmark absorbs 10.9668% 96.68% THE RELATIVE RETURN CARRIES 3.32 PER CENT OF THE TOTAL VARIANCE. THE BENCHMARK ABSORBS 96.68 PER CENT. SO THE NOTIONAL BENCHMARK IS THE RIGHT COMPARATOR FOR THE MANAGER AND THE WRONG ONE FOR THE FUND. It is appropriate precisely BECAUSE it strips out the decision the manager did not make, and that same property makes it silent on the decision that dominated the outcome. A fund can track its notional benchmark to the basis point and still fail every objective it has, because THE BENCHMARK CONTAINS NO OBJECTIVE. MAKE THAT CONCRETE RATHER THAN LEAVING IT AS AN OBSERVATION. Strip the active return out of the simulation entirely, so the fund holds the benchmark exactly, and set the return the liabilities actually need at 6.50 per cent: the benchmark's expected return 5.6000% the return the liabilities require 6.5000% share of outcomes in which a PERFECTLY tracking fund falls short 53.3300% A FUND THAT TRACKS ITS BENCHMARK PERFECTLY MISSES ITS OBJECTIVE 53.33 PER CENT OF THE TIME, and the tracking error has been removed, so none of that shortfall is the manager's doing. It is a property of the benchmark, which was built to describe the asset allocation and not to meet the liabilities. WHICH IS WHY THERE ARE THREE COMPARATORS AND NOT ONE, AND WHY THE FUND'S OWN COMPARATOR IS ITS LIABILITIES. That is lecture 1's conclusion arriving in different clothing: the right measure is the one whose drivers the person being judged controls, so the manager gets the notional benchmark, the trustees get the liabilities, and neither figure substitutes for the other.

The published index, and why price weighting disqualifies one

The summary calls the Dow Jones "30 shares, unweighted arithmetic, unsuitable as benchmark". THE VERDICT IS RIGHT AND THE REASON IS MISSTATED, and the correct reason is more useful than the stated one. The Dow is PRICE-WEIGHTED, which is not the same as unweighted. Each company's weight is its share price divided by the sum of the constituents' share prices, so the weights are determined by an arbitrary number rather than by economic size. Every other company's weight rises to fill the gap. THE VALUE-WEIGHTED INDEX IS COMPLETELY UNCHANGED, because price times shares is invariant to a split. SO THE DISQUALIFYING PROPERTY IS NOT THAT THE DOW IS "UNWEIGHTED". IT IS THAT ITS WEIGHTS RESPOND TO CORPORATE ACTIONS THAT CARRY NO INFORMATION. That breaks unambiguity, since the weights depend on decisions unrelated to value, and investability, since tracking it requires trading on every split for no economic reason. AND THE PRINCIPLE GENERALISES BEYOND THE DOW: any weighting scheme whose weights respond to corporate actions rather than to economic size fails both criteria at once, which is the test to apply to any index offered as a benchmark. THE SUMMARY MAKES THE SAME KIND OF POINT ABOUT PROPERTY INDICES, calling them subjective and saying they show short-term movements, and there the objection is different and also correct: property valuations are appraisals rather than transactions, so the index is smoothed and lags. THAT BREAKS MEASURABILITY RATHER THAN UNAMBIGUITY, and the two failures need different remedies.

Active money positions, and the beta they omit

The summary's last benchmark measure is the ACTIVE MONEY POSITION, the portfolio weight less the benchmark weight for each asset. It correctly notes that these SUM TO ZERO and give an INCOMPLETE PICTURE because they carry NO BETA. That warning is worth the arithmetic. Take the same +5 percentage point active position in every case, funded by selling a beta 1.00 holding, and vary only the beta of what is bought: the same +5pp active position active beta active risk from the contributed market factor alone into a beta 0.60 stock -0.0200 0.3600% into a beta 1.00 stock 0.0000 0.0000% into a beta 1.60 stock 0.0300 0.5400% into a beta 2.40 stock 0.0700 1.2600% THE POSITION REPORT IS IDENTICAL IN ALL FOUR ROWS AND THE ACTIVE BETA RUNS FROM MINUS 0.0200 TO PLUS 0.0700, so the sign changes. The market-driven active risk runs from 0.3600 to 1.2600 per cent, a factor of 3.5. SO A +5 POINT POSITION CAN BE RISK-REDUCING OR RISK-ADDING AND THE REPORT LOOKS THE SAME EITHER WAY. A tilt into a beta 1.00 stock contributes exactly zero active beta, which is the only case in which the position report tells the whole story. WHAT FIXES IT IS TO REPORT THE ACTIVE POSITIONS ALONGSIDE THE ACTIVE BETA AND THE TRACKING ERROR RATHER THAN INSTEAD OF THEM, which is why the summary lists tracking error, active money positions and the information ratio together as three benchmark measures rather than offering any one of them alone.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to carry is the scoring, because it inverts the summary's presentation and it is not close. One criterion of seven against seven of seven. The reason the least valid comparator is the most widely used is the reason the summary gives for listing it first, which is that the data is available, and AVAILABILITY IS A PROPERTY OF THE MEASUREMENT PROCESS RATHER THAN OF THE BENCHMARK. Once that distinction is made the whole ordering falls out.

THE ONE PROPERTY THAT SHOULD END THE PEER GROUP ARGUMENT. if every manager in the group has an IR of 0.00, each beats the median 50% of the time if every manager in the group has an IR of 2.00, each beats the median 50% of the time A MEASURE THAT RETURNS THE SAME ANSWER FOR A GROUP OF GENIUSES AND A GROUP OF INCOMPETENTS IS NOT A NOISY MEASURE OF SKILL. it is a measure of something else, namely relative standing, which is a real quantity and is not the one anybody wanted.

The second is that the notional benchmark's perfect score is bought with the 96.68 per cent. It scores seven out of seven by containing the fund's own asset allocation, and containing it is what makes it silent about it. So the honest reporting pack has two numbers in it and not one: the manager against the notional benchmark, and the fund against its liabilities. A TRUSTEE READING ONLY BENCHMARK-RELATIVE REPORTS IS READING ABOUT THE 3.32 PER CENT, and the 96.68 was their own decision, which is precisely why nobody else will report on it for them.

The third is the load ratio result, which is the most directly actionable thing in the lecture. A 25 per cent load ratio on a 60 per cent equity sleeve states a 75 per cent ceiling and delivers 70, because the other sleeves cannot fund the difference. The check takes one line of arithmetic per sleeve and it is not in the summary, in the criteria, or in most mandates. AND THE FAILURE IS ALWAYS IN THE LARGEST SLEEVE, which is where a proportional limit looks most generous and where the fund's risk actually sits.

THE TEST TO RUN ON ANY SET OF PROPORTIONAL LIMITS. for each sleeve: can the OTHER sleeves supply the underweight this one's maximum overweight requires? if not: the stated limit is unreachable and should be restated ONE SLEEVE FAILED HERE OF FOUR, AND IT WAS THE 60 PER CENT ONE. as a rule of thumb the check bites whenever a sleeve's weight exceeds half the portfolio, because then its proportional allowance exceeds the sum of everyone else's.

And the fourth, which generalises past the Dow. The summary's verdict on price weighting is right and its reason is not, and the correct reason is a test you can apply to any index you are offered: DO THE WEIGHTS RESPOND TO ANYTHING THAT IS NOT ECONOMIC SIZE? A four-for-one split moved a weight 33.20 points and left the company tied with one that did nothing. Note that the summary's objection to property indices is a different failure again, appraisal rather than transaction pricing, which breaks measurability rather than unambiguity. Two unsuitable indices, two distinct defects, two different remedies, and calling both of them simply "unsuitable" loses the part that transfers.

And a note on where this leaves the module. Lectures 6 and 7 both take the benchmark as given and work on the difference between the portfolio and it. Everything they compute inherits the choice made here, so an attribution against an inappropriate index decomposes a number that meant nothing to begin with, and a risk-adjusted measure against a peer median adjusts a quantity that is blind to the group's level. THE SEQUENCE MATTERS: the comparator is chosen before the attribution is run, and no amount of decomposition recovers from choosing it badly.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The strategic asset allocation that becomes the notional benchmark is built in strategic asset allocation, and the tracking error and information ratio used as the alternative benchmark measures come from risk budgets, VaR and tracking error. The survivorship arithmetic re-derived here was first measured in relative performance and basis risk. Index construction, including the price-weighted and unweighted forms and why they fail, is treated at length in listed equity, which is where the arithmetic of a badly built index first appears. The beta that active money positions omit is from the capital market line and CAPM. And the conclusion that the right measure is the one whose drivers the subject controls is rates of return, restated.

Where this goes next. Lecture 6 decomposes the benchmark-relative return into sector and stock selection, and it inherits the choice made here completely: an attribution against an inappropriate index decomposes a difference that meant nothing. Lecture 7 adds the risk adjustment, and the peer-group result bears directly on it, since a risk-adjusted measure against a peer median adjusts a quantity that is structurally blind to the group's level. Lecture 8 collects the limitations, and the one-of-seven score plus the unreachable load-ratio ceiling both belong there as cases where the apparatus is precise and its foundation was chosen for convenience. The 96.68 per cent figure is also the reason lecture 8 has to revisit whether manager measurement was ever the important question.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank's version of the benchmark problem is the choice of a funds transfer pricing curve, since that curve plays exactly the notional benchmark's role: it is the internally constructed comparator against which a business unit's margin is measured, and choosing it decides who looks profitable. The parallel with the load-ratio feasibility result is a bank's limit framework, where sub-limits summing to more than the group limit is a known and routinely audited defect, treated in risk appetite. And the peer-group finding has a direct supervisory analogue: a bank compared against a peer group of banks on capital ratios learns its relative standing and nothing about whether the whole group is adequately capitalised, which is why the Basel minima are absolute rather than relative and why the stress tests in stress testing apply a common scenario rather than a peer ranking.

Numbers used here. The three comparators and their claimed advantages, the "appropriate benchmarks" governance requirements, the load ratio and load difference vocabulary, the description of the Dow Jones, and the active money position with its "no beta" warning are all from the ASSA F105 summary in the user's notes. The seven validity criteria are Bailey, Richards and Tierney's standard set and are not in the summary; the scoring of the three comparators against them is mine. Two corrections are recorded in the body rather than left in a note, because a reader would otherwise learn something false: the summary calls the Dow Jones "unweighted arithmetic" when it is price-weighted, and the split argument rather than the absence of weighting is what disqualifies it; and the summary presents the three comparators in ascending order of validity while describing them in descending order of convenience. The variance decomposition uses 200,000 draws and the survivorship figures 4,000 simulations of 101 funds, both with seeded generators. Twenty-nine claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions, all twenty-nine passed, and every emitted value was checked mechanically against the finished page. Two claims failed on the first run and a further five were found to be worthless afterwards. The split claim was a wrong target, since a four-for-one split cuts the weight by 33.20 points to a little over half rather than by more than half. The second was vacuous rather than wrong: I had asserted that the stated total freedom across sleeves exceeds what the sum-to-one constraint permits, and with weights summing to one those quantities are identically equal, so the claim could never have been true. Replacing it with a per-sleeve feasibility test produced the finding that now leads step 4. That prompted a check of every assertion in this lecture for whether it could fail at all, and five could not, including two that were the bare constant True. The worst of them sat under the coin-toss table, whose 50.00 per cent entries had been written by hand rather than computed; that table is now the output of a simulation of a hundred equally skilled managers, which returns exactly 50.00 per cent at every skill level to within a millionth of a per cent. The benchmark-shortfall figure of 53.33 per cent in step 4 exists because replacing another of those empty assertions required constructing the case properly.
