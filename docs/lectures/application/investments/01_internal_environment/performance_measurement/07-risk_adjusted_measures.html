Lectures / Investments / Internal environment / Performance measurement

Four risk-adjusted measures, four different rankings of the same four portfolios

Sharpe, Treynor, Jensen and the summary's pre-specified measure order an identical set of four portfolios four different ways. Along the way: the pre-specified measure turns out to be the Sharpe excess multiplied by the portfolio's own volatility, so it is not an independent measure at all; the tracking-error formula given has no mean subtracted, which understates a good manager's information ratio by 29.55 per cent at a four per cent active return; and two portfolios with identical Sharpe ratios rank oppositely on 95 and 99 per cent value at risk.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Lecture 6 decomposed the excess return without asking what risk was taken to get it. This lecture supplies the adjustment, and the summary's organisation is the most valuable thing in it: measures built on total volatility belong to an entire portfolio and only make sense for efficient ones, while measures built on beta belong to a part of a portfolio and tolerate inefficiency. That distinction is stated and not explained, and explaining it settles which measure to use.

Start with the disagreement. Four portfolios, four measures, and all four rankings differ. Sharpe puts Alpha first and Treynor puts Charlie first. Alpha and Bravo earn the same return with the same beta and volatilities of 18 and 30 per cent: Treynor scores them identically because it cannot see the difference, and Sharpe prefers Alpha by 0.2222. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions.

The reason is the marginal risk contribution. A sleeve inside a fund contributes risk through its covariance with the rest, not through its own volatility. Holding a sleeve's volatility at 30 per cent and varying only its correlation with the rest, its marginal contribution falls from 46.71 per cent of its own volatility at a correlation of 0.95 to minus 27.12 per cent at zero correlation, where a concentrated sleeve actively reduces the fund's volatility. That is why a part of a portfolio is judged on beta.

Two of the summary's measures then turn out not to be what they appear. The pre-specified standard deviation measure rearranges exactly to σ_p × (Sharpe_p − Sharpe_b), verified to machine precision, so it is Sharpe scaled by volatility and favours volatile portfolios with a small edge: a steady portfolio at Sharpe 0.6250 loses to a swingy one at 0.4750. And Jensen's alpha scales with leverage, running from 1.8500 to 11.1000 per cent across the same portfolio geared from a half to three times, while Treynor and the appraisal ratio are exactly invariant.

Then the formula that is worth catching. The tracking error given is √(Σ(R_p − R_b)²/n), with no mean subtracted, so it is a root mean square rather than a standard deviation and exceeds it whenever the manager has added value. At a four per cent mean active return it understates the information ratio by 29.55 per cent, penalising precisely the managers who did best. And the √frequency annualisation assumes independence: at an autocorrelation of 0.60, which a persistent tilt produces by construction, it understates the true annual tracking error by 45.58 per cent.

Feynman test Dividing return by risk sounds like one idea and is at least four, because "risk" can mean the whole wobble, only the part that moves with the market, only the part you chose, or how bad the bad case is. Pick a different one and you get a different winner from the same numbers. Two of the formulas here are not what they look like: one is just another formula in disguise, and another gets bigger if you simply borrow money, so it cannot compare two managers who borrow differently. And the recipe given for measuring how far a manager strays from the benchmark quietly punishes the ones who strayed profitably.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the entire-versus-partial distinction decides which measure is even admissible, and it is the one thing about this topic that is genuinely settled. Using Sharpe to judge a specialist equity manager who runs ten per cent of a fund is a category error, because most of that manager's volatility diversifies away against the other ninety per cent. The arithmetic in step 4 shows a sleeve whose marginal contribution is negative, and no total-volatility measure can represent that.

The second is that a measure the summary presents as independent is a monotone transform of another one, and knowing which is which prevents double counting. Reporting Sharpe and the pre-specified measure side by side looks like two perspectives and is one perspective and a volatility weighting, which systematically favours the riskier portfolio.

The third is that Jensen's alpha is quoted comparatively all the time and cannot bear it. A manager reporting a 7.40 per cent alpha at two times gearing and one reporting 3.70 at one time have identical skill on these numbers. The appraisal ratio, Jensen divided by specific risk, is invariant and is the figure that should be compared.

And the fourth is the tracking-error definition, which matters because the information ratio is the single most-quoted number in active management. Two managers with identical genuine skill will show different information ratios if one is measured on the root mean square and the other on the standard deviation, and the gap grows with the very quantity being measured. That is not a rounding convention, it is a bias against success.

The mental model

SO A JENSEN ALPHA CANNOT BE COMPARED BETWEEN MANAGERS WITHOUT KNOWING THEIR GEARING, and the summary offers it as a comparative measure alongside Treynor. It is invariant to leverage because both numerator and denominator scale linearly, and it has the right interpretation: reward per unit of the risk the manager chose to take, as distinct from the market risk they were given. THE SUMMARY DOES NOT MENTION IT, and it is the measure that does the job the summary wants Jensen to do.

The skewed portfolio's 95 per cent VaR is 16.82 per cent against the symmetric one's 19.66, so it looks SAFER, while its 99 per cent VaR is 60.07 against 31.79. That is the defect that motivates expected shortfall, which averages the losses beyond the threshold instead of reporting the threshold itself, and it is the reason the regulatory measure for trading books moved from value at risk to expected shortfall. SO THE ORDER OF THE SUMMARY'S FOUR FAMILIES IS ALSO A HIERARCHY OF WHAT EACH CAN SEE. Sharpe sees the second moment. The semi-deviation sees which side of the target the dispersion falls. Value at risk sees one point in the tail, and can be gamed by moving losses past it. Stress testing, which the summary lists last, sees the losses no probability statement captured, which is why it exists.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to carry is that the summary's organising distinction is the settled part and everything else is contested. Total volatility is the right denominator when the portfolio IS the investor's exposure, and beta is the right one when it is a sleeve, and the marginal risk contribution says why: at a correlation of 0.25 or below a sleeve with 30 per cent volatility REDUCES the fund's volatility, and the contribution reaches minus 27.12 per cent of its own volatility at zero correlation. A MEASURE THAT DIVIDES BY A SLEEVE'S OWN VOLATILITY IS DIVIDING BY A NUMBER THAT IS NOT THE RISK ANYONE BEARS.

THE TWO QUESTIONS TO ASK BEFORE PICKING A RISK-ADJUSTED MEASURE. is the portfolio being judged the investor's WHOLE exposure? YES -> total volatility is the risk they bear. use Sharpe NO -> only the undiversifiable part matters. use Treynor or the appraisal ratio is the thing being compared a RATE or an AMOUNT? a rate survives a change of scale, so it can rank an amount does not, so it cannot JENSEN AND THE PRE-SPECIFIED MEASURE ARE BOTH AMOUNTS, WHICH IS WHY BOTH FAIL TO RANK. that is the same finding as the IRR in lecture 2 and RAROC in lecture 3, arriving for the third time in this module from a third direction.

The second is that two of the four measures are not what the summary implies. The pre-specified standard deviation measure IS the Sharpe excess times the portfolio's own volatility, an identity verified to machine precision, so reporting both alongside each other is one perspective and a volatility weighting rather than two perspectives. And Jensen's alpha runs 1.8500 to 11.1000 per cent across the same portfolio at different gearing while Treynor holds at 0.0952 exactly. THE APPRAISAL RATIO IS THE MEASURE THE SUMMARY WANTS JENSEN TO BE, and it does not mention it.

The third is the tracking-error definition, and it is worth being precise about the fairness of the criticism. The root mean square is a real convention and the formula is not simply an error. What is not defensible is comparing two managers across the two definitions, because the gap is one-directional and grows with the mean active return: 29.55 per cent understatement at a four per cent mean. AND THE SECOND DEFECT IN THE SAME FORMULA RUNS THE OTHER WAY, since the square-root annualisation understates the true tracking error by 45.58 per cent at an autocorrelation of 0.60. The two errors do not cancel, because they apply to different managers: the first hits whoever added most return, the second whoever held their position longest.

WHY THE TWO TRACKING-ERROR ERRORS DO NOT CANCEL. no mean subtracted inflates the denominator penalises a HIGH MEAN active return sqrt(frequency) rule deflates the denominator flatters a PERSISTENT active position A CONCENTRATED STOCK-PICKER WITH FAST TURNOVER GETS THE FIRST ERROR AND NOT THE SECOND. a patient value manager with a long-held tilt gets the second and not the first. so the two biases sort managers by STYLE rather than washing out, which is worse than a single bias in one direction.

And the fourth, which is the finding I did not plan. Two portfolios with Sharpe ratios agreeing to within 0.50 per cent rank OPPOSITELY on 95 and 99 per cent value at risk: the skewed one shows 16.82 against 19.66 at 95 per cent, looking safer, and 60.07 against 31.79 at 99. SO A 95 PER CENT VaR LIMIT WOULD PREFER THE PORTFOLIO THAT LOSES 60 PER CENT ONE YEAR IN A HUNDRED, because the loss sits beyond the threshold the limit examines. That is precisely the defect that motivates expected shortfall, and it is why the trading-book capital measure moved away from value at risk. The summary lists VaR and stress testing in sequence without saying that the second exists because the first can be gamed by moving losses past the threshold.

And a note on what lecture 8 inherits. This module has now produced, in order: a return definition that depends on who chose the dates, a valuation that depends on an unobservable rate, a company measure whose sign is undetermined at short horizons, a 10.82-year skill inference against a three-year review, a comparator that satisfies one of seven criteria, an attribution split that is a convention, and seven risk-adjusted measures that rank the same portfolios differently. EVERY ONE OF THOSE IS A DISCRETIONARY CHOICE PRESENTED AS A CALCULATION, and lecture 8 exists because the case against performance measurement cannot be made until they have all been counted.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The beta and the security market line come from the capital market line and CAPM, and this lecture inherits the estimation problem measured in company performance, EVA and RAROC, where a one-year confidence interval on beta spanned the break-even point: Treynor and Jensen both divide by or subtract a quantity that imprecise. The marginal risk contribution is the Euler decomposition built in risk budgets, VaR and tracking error, which is also where the tracking error and information ratio are constructed. The efficient-portfolio condition behind "sigma only for efficient portfolios" is from the opportunity set and efficient frontier. The value-at-risk and semi-deviation properties, including the way a 95 per cent threshold can be gamed, were measured in market risk and stress testing and scenario analysis. And the ratio-versus-amount distinction is individual asset performance and lecture 3, restated a third time.

Where this goes next. Lecture 8 closes the module by collecting the limitations, and this lecture supplies it with the largest single count of them: four measures with four rankings, one measure that is another in disguise, one that leverage breaks, two defects in a single tracking-error formula, and a value-at-risk threshold that reverses a verdict. Read alongside lecture 4's 10.82-year inference, lecture 5's one-of-seven comparator score and lecture 6's arbitrary attribution split, the case lecture 8 has to make is not that performance measurement is imprecise but that it is a sequence of discretionary choices presented as a calculation. The GIPS standards that lecture 8 also covers exist to constrain exactly that discretion, which is why they specify definitions rather than methods.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank's version of this lecture is the choice of denominator in a risk-adjusted return, and it made the same journey in the same order. Return on equity divides by accounting capital, which is the total-volatility analogue and wrong for a business unit; risk-adjusted return on capital divides by economic capital, which is the beta analogue and right for a part of the group; and the allocation of diversification benefit left unresolved in lecture 3 is the same problem as the marginal risk contribution here, solved the same way in economic capital. The value-at-risk finding is not an analogy but the same regulatory history: the move from value at risk to expected shortfall for trading-book capital happened because a threshold measure can be gamed by moving losses beyond it, which is documented in market risk.

The two families, the four formulas, the tracking error and information ratio definitions, and the downside family of semi-deviation, value at risk and stress testing are all transcribed from the "risk-return analysis" section of chapter 10 of the ASSA F105 summary in the user's notes. The appraisal ratio is not in the summary and is the standard Treynor-Black measure. Two corrections are recorded in the body rather than in this note, because a reader would otherwise learn something false: the pre-specified standard deviation measure is not an independent measure but exactly the Sharpe excess scaled by the portfolio's volatility, and the tracking error formula as given is a root mean square rather than a standard deviation, which understates the information ratio of any manager with a positive mean active return. Everything numerical is invented for illustration: a 4 per cent risk-free rate, a benchmark at 10 per cent and 16 per cent volatility, four portfolios with returns from 10 to 24 per cent and betas from 0.55 to 2.10, a 10 per cent sleeve against a remainder at 15 per cent volatility, gearing from 0.5 to 3 times, monthly active returns at 4 per cent volatility with means from zero to 4 per cent, autocorrelations from minus 0.20 to 0.60, and a skewed distribution with a 2 per cent chance of a 60 per cent loss whose parameters are solved algebraically so the mean and variance match by construction. The simulations use seeded generators with 240 monthly observations and 400,000 draws respectively. Twenty claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions, all twenty passed, and none can pass without the computation. Three failed on the first run. Two were wrong targets. The third was caught by a monotonicity check rather than by a target: my first version of the marginal-contribution table varied the sleeve's own volatility and its correlation together, and the resulting series was not monotone in either, because the two drivers move the share in opposite directions. Separating them produced both tables now in step 4 and the finding that the contribution turns negative.
