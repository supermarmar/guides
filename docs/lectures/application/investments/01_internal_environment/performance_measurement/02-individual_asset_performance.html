A one per cent fee explains a 12.50 per cent discount to net asset value

A perpetual charge on the assets capitalises, so at an eight per cent discount rate a one per cent annual fee is worth 12.50 per cent of net asset value. That single effect explains a double-digit discount with no appeal to sentiment or irrationality. Along the way: income is 82.50 per cent of a fifty-year total return, the net present value and the internal rate of return swap rankings at 7.4773 per cent, and one asset here has internal rates of return of exactly 20 and 100 per cent.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Lecture 1 fixed the return of a fund. This lecture asks what a single asset contributed, and syllabus objective (l) names three ways of answering: the change in market price, the net present value and the net asset value. Each is exact about something narrower than the question, and the third is not a valuation at all.

Market price change misses the income, and misses more of it the longer the horizon. On an asset growing at 4.50 per cent with a 3.50 per cent yield reinvested, the income is 43.75 per cent of the one-year total return and 82.50 per cent of the fifty-year, with the crossover at 7.59 years. A measure built on price alone therefore reports a minority of the answer for any holding period a long-term investor would use.

Net present value answers what an asset is worth, conditional on a discount rate nobody can observe, and the conditioning is not a technicality. Five par bonds of different maturities all have an internal rate of return of exactly 8.0000 per cent and net present values at a six per cent hurdle from 8.4247 to 31.5237. The internal rate of return cannot see the difference and the net present value can. Worse, the two can rank assets oppositely: searching for the crossover, an early-paying and a late-paying asset have equal net present values at 7.4773 per cent, and below that rate the ranking is the reverse of the internal rate of return's.

And net asset value, which the summary defines as an accounting ratio, equity less intangibles over shares. Scanning for it, the largest single explanation of a discount is arithmetic rather than behavioural: a perpetual management fee capitalises at the discount rate, so a one per cent fee at an eight per cent rate is worth 12.50 per cent of net asset value. Only two of seven adjustments can produce a premium, which is why persistent discounts are the normal state of closed-end vehicles.

Feynman test Three ways to say what one holding did. Watching the price misses the dividends, which are most of the answer over a lifetime. Discounting the cash flows gives a real answer that depends entirely on a rate you have to assume, and the popular alternative avoids assuming a rate by ignoring the question. Then the balance-sheet figure, which is not a valuation at all and reliably overstates what anyone will pay, mostly because the manager's fee is a permanent charge on the assets and permanent charges are worth a lot.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that a price-based performance figure is not an approximation to total return, it is a minority of it. At a horizon beyond 7.59 years the reinvested income exceeds the price growth, and by fifty years it is 82.50 per cent of the total. Any comparison of assets on price change alone will systematically favour low-yielding ones.

The second is that the internal rate of return is not a convenient alternative to the net present value, it is a different question. Five par bonds share an internal rate of return of 8.0000 per cent and differ by a factor of 3.7 in net present value at a six per cent hurdle. The internal rate of return is blind to the one thing that determines which is worth more, which is how long the spread over the hurdle is locked in.

The third is that the internal rate of return can fail to exist uniquely, and the failure is not exotic. An asset with two sign changes here has internal rates of return of exactly 20 and 100 per cent, both correct, and its net present value is negative, then positive, then negative again. The question "what is the internal rate of return" has no answer, while the net present value answers cleanly at every rate.

And a fourth, which matters for anybody valuing a closed-end vehicle. A discount to net asset value is usually discussed as a sentiment phenomenon, and the summary itself lists it under market sentiment. The capitalised management fee alone explains a discount of 12.50 per cent at ordinary parameters, so the behavioural explanation is being asked to account for a residual much smaller than the headline figure.

The simplest measure of an asset's performance is the change in its market price. Total return adds the income, and once the income is reinvested it compounds, so its share of the total grows with the horizon. Net present value, and the rate that decides everything

The net present value discounts an asset's cash flows and subtracts its cost. Its weakness is that the answer is a function of the discount rate, and for a long-dated asset a small change in that rate dominates every other input. THAT IS THE PRACTICAL WARNING FOR ANYBODY READING A DISCOUNT AS AN OPPORTUNITY. The discount is a statement about the equity, the uncertainty is in the assets, and gearing converts the second into the first at a multiple.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to carry is that the internal rate of return is not a shortcut to the net present value, it is a different and weaker question, and the five par bonds settle it. All five have an internal rate of return of exactly 8.0000 per cent, and their net present values at a six per cent hurdle differ by a factor of 3.7. THE MEASURE IS BLIND TO DURATION BECAUSE A RATE HAS NO DURATION ATTACHED, and duration over the hurdle is the entire source of the value difference.

THE THREE FAILURES OF THE INTERNAL RATE OF RETURN, IN ORDER OF HOW OFTEN THEY BITE. it cannot rank five par bonds, one IRR, values from 8.42 to 31.52 it can rank backwards EARLY over LATE at every rate, wrong below 7.4773% it may not exist uniquely -100, +320, -240 has IRRs of 20% and 100% THE FIRST TWO ARE ROUTINE and the third is the one textbooks lead with, which is the wrong emphasis. A conventional asset has one root, so the multiple-root problem almost never arises in practice while the ranking problem arises whenever two assets have different cash flow shapes, which is always.

The second is the crossover rate, which is the practically useful object and is rarely taught as one. The ranking flips at 7.4773 per cent, where both net present values are 15.220117. So the decision does not require knowing the discount rate, it requires knowing which side of 7.4773 per cent it falls on. THAT IS A MUCH WEAKER PIECE OF KNOWLEDGE THAN A POINT ESTIMATE OF THE COST OF CAPITAL, and it is often available when the point estimate is not.

The third is the fee arithmetic, and it changes how a discount to net asset value should be read. A one per cent perpetual fee at an eight per cent discount rate is worth 12.50 per cent of net asset value, and the inverse table gives the fee that fully explains any observed discount. So the sequence is to deduct the capitalised fee first and attribute only the residual to sentiment, illiquidity or valuation disagreement. A TWELVE PER CENT DISCOUNT ON A VEHICLE CHARGING ONE PER CENT IS NOT A PUZZLE, AND A TWO PER CENT DISCOUNT ON THE SAME VEHICLE WOULD BE. The summary lists the discount under market sentiment, which is true and is not the largest term.

WHY ONLY TWO OF THE SEVEN ADJUSTMENTS CAN GIVE A PREMIUM, AND WHY THAT IS THE WHOLE STORY. three push down unconditionally fee, illiquidity, tax on unrealised gains two can push either way intangibles excluded, valuation disagreement one pushes up control or break-up value one amplifies whatever is there gearing A DISTRIBUTION WITH THREE ONE-WAY TERMS AND ONE UPWARD TERM HAS A NEGATIVE MEAN. That is the structural reason persistent discounts are normal for closed-end vehicles and persistent premiums are not, and it is why a premium is informative in a way a discount is not: a premium requires either a control story or a market view that the directors have undervalued the assets.

And the fourth, which is the transferable part. Every one of these four measures is exact about something narrower than the question, and the way to use them is to know which narrowing each performs. Price change narrows away the income, which is 82.50 per cent of a fifty-year total return. The net present value narrows the answer to a stated rate and admits it. The internal rate of return narrows away the rate itself, which is the input that decides, and does so silently. And the net asset value narrows to what the accounts say, which is neither the market's view nor a valuation. THE HABIT WORTH BUILDING IS TO NAME THE NARROWING BEFORE QUOTING THE NUMBER, because in every case the narrowing rather than the arithmetic is where the error enters.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The discounting, the par bond and the relationship between the coupon and the yield are from fixed income pricing, and the reason the fifty-year bond's value moves most is the duration result derived there. The distinction between a total return and a price return continues from rates of return, which fixed the fund-level measure this lecture applies to a single holding. The capitalisation of a perpetual charge is the perpetuity formula from the dividend discount model, applied to a cost rather than to an income. And the closed-end vehicle whose discount is analysed here is the structure introduced in collective schemes and ETFs, where the open-ended alternative has no discount by construction because units are created and cancelled at net asset value.

Where this goes next. Lecture 3 raises the same question at company level, where economic value added and risk-adjusted return on capital are the analogues of the net present value, and where the capital charge plays the role the discount rate plays here. Lecture 4 asks why performance is measured at all and what measuring it does to behaviour. Lectures 5 to 7 build the portfolio machinery, benchmarks and comparators, attribution into sector and stock selection, and the risk-adjusted ratios. Lecture 8 collects the limitations, and the multiple internal rate of return and the crossover rate both reappear there as instances of a measure that appears objective while embedding a choice. The gearing amplification recurs in asset liability mismatching risk, where the levered claim is on a surplus rather than on a net asset value.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank meets all three measures in supervisory rather than performance language. The net present value of a loan at a stated discount rate is the economic value calculation behind the economic value of equity measure in interest rate risk, and the elasticity table computed here is exactly the sensitivity that measure reports. The distinction between an accounting figure and a market value is the fair value hierarchy question in the accounting environment, where a bank's own net asset value is subject to the same gap. And the ranking disagreement between the net present value and the internal rate of return is why loan pricing frameworks charge a hurdle rate on capital rather than ranking facilities by their internal rate of return, which is the mechanism set out in financial management.

Numbers used here. The three measures and the definition of net asset value as equity less intangibles over shares are from objective (l) and chapter 10 of the ASSA F105 summary in the user's notes; the net present value and internal rate of return properties are standard capital budgeting theory. Everything numerical is invented for illustration: price growth of 4.50 per cent against a 3.50 per cent yield, par bonds with an 8.00 coupon at a cost of 100 assessed against six and seven per cent hurdles, the two three-year cash flow patterns, the sign-changing asset at minus 100, plus 320, minus 240, fees from 0.20 to 2.00 per cent, discount rates from five to twelve per cent, and gearing from zero to 75 per cent. Internal rates are found by scanning 200,000 points from minus 95 to plus 500 per cent and bisecting every sign change, rather than solving once, which is what surfaces the two-root case. Nineteen claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions, all nineteen passed, and every table was emitted by the computation and pasted without retyping. Four of the original sixteen were later found to be built from literal arithmetic rather than from the computed tables, so they could not have failed whatever the computation produced; they have been rewritten to test the functions that build the fee and gearing tables, which added three further assertions. One error was caught before the claims ran. The first version of the maturity comparison specified an annuity with no principal repayment, so the "eight per cent asset" paid 40 in total against a cost of 100 and had no internal rate of return at all, while the prose described a bond. Correcting it to a par bond produced the cleaner and stronger result now reported, that all five maturities share an internal rate of return of exactly 8.0000 per cent.
