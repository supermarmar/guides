Investments, internal environment

Performance measurement

Eight lectures on judging the result, and on the ways that judgement misleads. A return number on its own says almost nothing: it depends on when cash arrived, what risk was taken to get it, what it is being compared against, and how long the measurement window was. The module builds each of those corrections in turn, then closes by arguing the case against the whole exercise, which is what syllabus objective (n)(v) actually asks for.

How to use this

Read in order. Lecture 1 fixes the return definitions everything else uses, and lecture 8 is only meaningful once you have seen how many discretionary choices lectures 2 to 7 contain. Status badges show what is built (built), planned, or a gap.

  • builtLecture 1: rates of return. The same manager judged five ways, with the time-weighted return constant at 8.0000 per cent and the money-weighted spanning 9.33 points, a search showing the money-weighted return reverses the ranking for any edge up to 4.40 per sub-period or 9.43 over the year, the two cases where the measures agree exactly, the linked internal rate converging non-monotonically, and the private equity exception where a 16.96 point spread is skill

One asset, one company

  • builtLecture 2: individual asset performance. Income at 82.50 per cent of a fifty-year total return, five par bonds sharing an internal rate of return of exactly 8.0000 per cent while their values differ by a factor of 3.7, a searched crossover at 7.4773 per cent where the two measures swap rankings, an asset with internal rates of return of exactly 20 and 100 per cent, and a one per cent fee capitalising to 12.50 per cent of net asset value
  • builtLecture 3: company performance, economic value added and risk-adjusted return on capital. The four-rung ladder from profit to a risk-adjusted charge, a flat cost of capital overstating the gap between two companies by a factor of 8.5714, IAS 38 producing a 12.00 point return-on-capital gap on identical economics that never unwinds, a persistent excess return capitalising to 47.5410 of unrecorded capital, a beta whose one-year confidence interval leaves the verdict without a sign, and closing a business unit to raise group RAROC while destroying 15.00 of value

The portfolio

  • builtLecture 4: measuring portfolio performance. Four of the six reasons resting on one inference, a good manager behind the benchmark in 40.13 per cent of quarters, 10.82 years needed to establish skill against a three-year review cycle, a 250-fold increase in observations moving the power by 2.36 percentage points, the best of a hundred skill-free managers showing an apparent information ratio of 1.5281, and the same rule making losers gamble and winners hug the index
  • builtLecture 5: benchmarks and comparators. The three comparators scored against the seven validity criteria at seven, six and one, a peer median every manager beats half the time whatever the group's shared skill, a notional benchmark absorbing 96.68 per cent of the fund's variance, a 25 per cent load ratio stating a 75 per cent equity ceiling whose true value is 70, and a four-for-one split moving a price-weighted index weight 33.20 points
  • builtLecture 6: attribution analysis. An identity that is exact because the intermediate cancels, two routes through the grid giving sector effects of minus 1.0500 and minus 0.2500 per cent from identical data with the 0.8000 per cent gap being the interaction term, the summary's allocation formula scoring an overweighted laggard at plus 0.3000 where Brinson-Fachler gives minus 0.4550 while the totals agree exactly, a multi-period residual reaching 46.09 per cent of the excess return at twelve years, and a spurious-sector hazard that peaks at five sectors rather than rising

Adjusting for risk, then doubting the answer

  • builtLecture 7: risk-adjusted measures and the information ratio. Four measures producing four different rankings of the same four portfolios, a sleeve's marginal risk contribution turning negative at low correlation, the pre-specified measure shown to be the Sharpe excess scaled by volatility, Jensen's alpha running from 1.85 to 11.10 per cent on the same portfolio under gearing while Treynor holds exactly, a tracking-error formula with no mean subtracted understating the information ratio by 29.55 per cent, and two portfolios of identical Sharpe ranking oppositely at 95 and 99 per cent value at risk
  • builtLecture 8: limitations of performance measurement, and GIPS. All seven of the summary's disadvantages priced, then twenty-four defensible combinations of one year's data giving an information ratio range of 1.3947 with ten saying the manager won and fourteen saying they lost, a top-quartile repeat rate of a quarter at every uniform skill level, a dismissal break-even at a plus 0.2135 per cent three-year record that is a transition-cost calculation rather than a significance test, and GIPS closing four choices of eight to cut the range by 50.61 per cent

Source material

Chapter 10 of the F105 summary, covering syllabus objectives (l) and (n). Three gaps are filled from external sources. Risk-adjusted return on capital, required by objective (l), is absent from the summary; lecture 3 builds it from its defining identity with economic value added and cross-references the banking track's economic capital and ICAAP treatments. Attribution appears as three formulas with no derivation, so lecture 6 rebuilds it from Brinson and Fachler (1985) and Brinson, Hood and Beebower (1986), Financial Analysts Journal 42(4). Performance reporting standards are absent entirely; lecture 8 uses the CFA Institute's Global Investment Performance Standards (2020).

Assumes equations of value and project appraisal for the internal rate of return machinery in lecture 1, and portfolio construction for the beta and efficient-portfolio assumptions underneath lecture 7.
