Strategic asset allocation: the mean drift never breaches the cap and 56.59 per cent of paths do

Three claims about the long run that do not survive computation. Time does not reduce risk, it reduces risk per year and increases it in total. The optimal equity weight does not depend on the horizon, and Merton's formula contains no T at all. And rebalancing does not add return, at least not by more than one standard error; it cuts volatility by 9.81 per cent and stops a 75 per cent equity cap being breached on more than half of all paths.

Portfolio construction 7 of 10 Reading time, 65 minutes Sources: Samuelson (1969); Merton (1969); Regulation 28 of the Pension Funds Act
Step 1

The 30-second version

Strategic asset allocation is the long-run policy portfolio: the split between asset classes that a fund commits to, expressed with tolerance ranges and a rebalancing rule, and reviewed every few years rather than every quarter. It is where the previous six lectures meet an institution with objectives, a horizon and a regulator.

The horizon is where most of the received wisdom lives, and it does not survive computation. Over T years the standard deviation of log terminal wealth grows as the square root of T, from 0.1692 at one year to 1.1963 at fifty, while the standard deviation of the annualised return shrinks as one over the square root of T, from 0.1692 to 0.0239. Both are correct and they are the same fact stated per year and in total. Which one you quote determines which conclusion you reach.

The shortfall framing fares no better. Measured against cash, the probability of losing falls from 39.36 to 2.75 per cent between one year and fifty, the conditional relative loss rises from 10.87 to 32.37 per cent, and the product of the two rises to a peak of 5.24 per cent at five years and then falls to 0.89. Three defensible measures, two opposite answers, and the choice of measure decides the argument rather than the arithmetic.

Then Samuelson. With independent returns and constant relative risk aversion the optimal allocation is independent of the horizon: Merton's solution is the excess return over risk aversion times variance, and no T appears in it. Simulated optima bracket the closed form at every horizon to within the grid step. So the case for a young investor holding more equity is not a horizon argument, it is a mean-reversion argument in a horizon costume, and at an autoregressive coefficient of minus 0.10 the thirty-year variance is 82.37 per cent of the independent case, which lifts the optimal weight from 0.4501 to 0.5465.

Feynman test Decide the long-run split once, write down how far it may wander before you drag it back, and revisit the whole thing rarely. Two beliefs to abandon on the way. First, that a long horizon makes shares safe: it makes the average year steadier and the final answer wider, and both of those are the same arithmetic. Second, that regularly restoring the split makes money: it does not, it keeps the risk where you set it, which matters because a portfolio left alone drifts towards whatever grows fastest and quietly becomes something the trustees never approved.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that strategic asset allocation is where nearly all of a fund's outcome is decided, and it is decided by a committee that meets rarely, using inputs lecture 6 showed to be barely estimable. Everything in the earlier lectures is a tool for this decision. Nothing in them makes the decision easy.

The second is that the horizon arguments used to justify equity allocations in practice are weaker than they sound, and this lecture separates the parts that follow from the parts that require an extra assumption. Time diversification does not follow. Neither does an age-declining equity glidepath, unless you are prepared to defend mean reversion or to treat future salary as a bond held outside the portfolio. Both defences are respectable and both should be stated out loud, because a glidepath sold on the horizon alone is sold on nothing.

The third is that rebalancing is routinely justified with a return story and the return story does not survive a standard error. Eight thousand thirty-year paths put never-rebalancing ahead of annual rebalancing by 2.8 basis points against a standard error of 2.65, which is 1.07 standard errors and therefore nothing. The volatility reduction is 9.81 per cent and is real. Getting this the right way round matters, because a policy defended on the wrong grounds gets abandoned when the wrong grounds fail.

And a fourth, which is the practical heart of the lecture. An unrebalanced 60/30/10 has a mean equity weight of 67.77 per cent after thirty years, which never breaches a 75 per cent cap, and it breaches that cap at some point on 56.59 per cent of paths. A compliance limit is a statement about paths, not about expectations, and any analysis that reports the expected drift has answered a different question from the one the regulator asked.

Samuelson: the horizon does not enter

With independent returns and constant relative risk aversion, Samuelson (1969) and Merton (1969) showed the optimal allocation is independent of the horizon. In continuous time, w* = (mu - r) / (gamma . sigma^2) and there is no T in it. Checking against a direct search over horizons, maximising the annualised certainty equivalent on 4,000 paths at each point on a 0.05 grid: gamma closed form simulated best w at T = 1, 5, 10, 20, 30 spread 2.0 0.9003 0.950 0.900 0.900 0.950 0.900 0.050 4.0 0.4501 0.450 0.450 0.500 0.450 0.500 0.050 8.0 0.2251 0.250 0.250 0.250 0.200 0.250 0.050 THE SIMULATION BRACKETS THE CLOSED FORM AT EVERY HORIZON AND THE SPREAD IS EXACTLY THE GRID STEP. There is no horizon effect to find. THE INTUITION FOR WHY NOT IS WORTH HAVING. A longer horizon multiplies both the expected excess return and the variance by T, and the optimal weight depends on their ratio, so the T cancels. Adding years adds risk and reward in the same proportion. SO THE STANDARD ADVICE THAT A YOUNG INVESTOR SHOULD HOLD MORE EQUITY DOES NOT FOLLOW FROM HAVING MORE TIME. It requires one of two additional assumptions, and a lifestyle glidepath sold without naming one of them is sold on nothing: (a) RETURNS MEAN-REVERT, so long-horizon variance grows more slowly than T (b) HUMAN CAPITAL is a bond-like asset held outside the portfolio, so a young investor's TOTAL wealth is already bond-heavy and the financial portfolio should offset it Assumption (b) is the stronger of the two and it changes the problem rather than the solution: it says the portfolio was measured against the wrong balance sheet, which is the theme lectures 8 and 9 develop.

Since turnover is a real cost and the volatility reduction is the objective, a band is simply a better instrument, and the reason is that a calendar rule trades when the date says so rather than when the portfolio needs it.

Putting the four parts together

A DEFENSIBLE STRATEGIC ALLOCATION, ASSEMBLED FROM THIS MODULE. (1) OBJECTIVES AND CONSTRAINTS FIRST, not the optimiser. The regulatory caps, the liquidity needs and the liability profile define the feasible set before any return is forecast. (2) CAPITAL MARKET ASSUMPTIONS, shrunk. Lecture 6: the raw sample mean is never the right input, and the optimal shrinkage at twenty years of data is around 0.6. (3) ANCHOR ON THE MARKET, not on a forecast. Lecture 6's reverse optimisation gives a starting portfolio requiring no view at all, and views are documented departures. (4) POLICY WEIGHTS with RANGES, and the ranges set inside any hard limit by enough margin that the band cannot itself permit a breach. Here a 5 per cent band breaches on 0.05 per cent of paths and a 10 per cent band on 5.01, against a cap the mean never reaches. (5) A REBALANCING RULE justified on risk control, not on return, because the return claim is 1.07 standard errors and the risk claim is a 9.81 per cent volatility reduction. (6) A REVIEW CYCLE long enough that the estimation noise of lecture 6 is not being traded on. STEP (6) IS THE ONE MOST OFTEN GOT WRONG. Reviewing a strategic allocation annually against updated capital market assumptions means acting on changes in an input whose standard error exceeds the quantity, which is a machine for generating turnover. THE ARGUMENT FOR A THREE TO FIVE YEAR CYCLE IS NOT ADMINISTRATIVE CONVENIENCE, IT IS THAT THE INPUTS DO NOT CONTAIN ENOUGH NEW INFORMATION IN A YEAR TO JUSTIFY MOVING.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to carry is that three of the four claims tested here have two defensible answers, and in every case the ambiguity comes from a choice of measure rather than from any uncertainty in the arithmetic. Terminal dispersion against annualised dispersion. Probability of shortfall against expected shortfall. Absolute loss against relative loss. THE ARITHMETIC IS NEVER IN DOUBT AND THE CONCLUSION ALWAYS IS, which means the productive question in a trustee meeting is never "what does the data show" but "what are we measuring and why".

THE SAME PATTERN, FOUR TIMES. question measure A says measure B says does time reduce risk? yes, per year no, in total does the chance of loss fall? yes, 39% to 3% no, severity triples is the shortfall growing? yes, in rands no, probability-weighted should the young hold equity? yes, if phi < 0 no, if returns are iid IN NONE OF THE FOUR ROWS IS EITHER SIDE MAKING AN ARITHMETIC ERROR. that is the uncomfortable part, because it means the debate cannot be settled by computing more carefully.

The second is Samuelson's result, which is the single most useful thing in the lecture because it removes an argument people rely on without knowing they are relying on it. The optimal weight is the excess return divided by risk aversion times variance and the horizon cancels, because adding years scales the expected excess return and the variance by the same factor. A glidepath is therefore a bet on mean reversion or a statement about human capital, and a practitioner who cannot say which one is running an unexamined model.

The third is that the rebalancing literature is a good example of a claim surviving on its plausibility. Eight thousand thirty-year paths cannot distinguish the mean returns of rebalanced and unrebalanced portfolios, and 1.07 standard errors is not a finding. The volatility reduction is unambiguous at 9.81 per cent. Both facts have been available to anybody who ran the simulation, and the return story persists because it is more satisfying to say a policy makes money than that it keeps a promise.

WHAT TO SAY ABOUT REBALANCING, IN ORDER OF DEFENSIBILITY. claim evidence here it keeps risk where the board set it 9.81% volatility reduction, unambiguous it prevents a compliance breach 56.59% of paths breach without it a band beats a calendar on cost 94.89% of the benefit at 63.20% of turnover it adds return 1.07 standard errors: NO EVIDENCE THE FIRST THREE ARE ENOUGH TO JUSTIFY THE POLICY. the fourth is unnecessary and it is the one usually offered first.

The fourth is the compliance result, which generalises well beyond Regulation 28. The expected equity weight of an unrebalanced 60/30/10 reaches 67.77 per cent after thirty years and the cap is 75, so an analysis based on expectations concludes there is nothing to manage. More than half of all paths breach. THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN AN EXPECTATION AND A PATH PROBABILITY IS THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN THE QUESTION THAT WAS ANSWERED AND THE QUESTION THAT WAS ASKED, and the same confusion appears wherever a limit is monitored continuously but modelled on averages.

And the fifth. Notice what the constraint cost turned out to be: 5.9 basis points for a fund wanting 92 per cent equity, rising steeply only for one wanting to lever. Regulation 28 is routinely described as a material drag on South African retirement outcomes, and on this universe it is nearly free for any fund with a risk aversion above 1.24. The arithmetic does not settle the policy question, because the universe here is invented and excludes the offshore and unlisted limits that bind for different reasons. IT DOES ESTABLISH THAT THE ARGUMENT CANNOT BE MADE IN THE ABSTRACT, which is a more useful contribution than a number.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The frontier optimum that the Regulation 28 caps are applied to, and the closed-form scalars behind it, are from indifference curves and the optimum portfolio; the result that a constraint costs nothing until it binds is from the opportunity set and the efficient frontier and is confirmed here on a real rule. The instruction to shrink the capital market assumptions before they enter, and to anchor on the market rather than on a forecast, are the two conclusions of limitations of mean-variance and the Black-Litterman response, and the market anchor itself is the capital market line and CAPM. The open question about which risk measure to use, which this lecture finds decides three separate arguments, was left open at the end of lecture 3. Lognormal compounding, autoregressive processes and variance ratios are from statistics, and the empirical return characteristics that make the lognormal assumption itself questionable are in the empirical characteristics of asset prices.

Where this goes next. Lectures 8 and 9 take up the assumption this lecture identified as the stronger justification for a glidepath, that the portfolio was being measured against the wrong balance sheet, and replace the utility function with a liability. That converts the horizon question from a preference into a matching problem, where the answer does depend on the term of the obligations. Lecture 10 formalises the tolerance ranges set out here as a risk budget and measures departures as tracking error. In the portfolio management module, the rebalancing rule becomes a transaction cost and implementation problem, and the drift measured in step 4 is what a transition manager is hired to correct.

Where the bank sits opposite. The path-versus-expectation result is the single most transferable finding here. A bank's risk appetite framework sets limits that are monitored continuously, so the relevant question is the probability that a path touches a limit rather than where the expected exposure sits, and a first-line analysis presenting expected utilisation has answered the wrong question in exactly the way described in step 4. The same distinction separates a point-in-time capital ratio from the buffer a bank must hold to survive a path, which is why supervisors stress the trajectory rather than the endpoint. And the drift result has a direct analogue in a loan book, where an unmanaged portfolio migrates towards whatever originated fastest, so a concentration limit is a rebalancing obligation as much as an origination one.
