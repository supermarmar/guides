The efficient frontier: the one portfolio on it that needs no expected return

The frontier's weights are linear in the target return, which gives two-fund separation exactly: every combination of two frontier portfolios lands back on the frontier, verified here to within nine parts in ten thousand trillion. And the global minimum variance portfolio depends only on the covariance matrix, so its weights are identical whether equity is expected to return 7.25, 11.50 or 15.75 per cent. That is the one point the estimation problem cannot damage.

Step 1

The 30-second version

With two assets the achievable portfolios form a curve, because one weight determines the other. With three they form a region, because two weights are free and the same return can be reached by many mixes with different risks. Three mixes all returning 9.50 per cent here have volatilities of 13.000, 9.500 and 9.596 per cent, so the investor's problem becomes a choice rather than a consequence.

The frontier has a closed form. Minimising variance subject to a target return and a full investment constraint gives a variance that is exactly quadratic in the target return, so the frontier is a parabola in variance-return space and a hyperbola in volatility-return space. On the worked universe the global minimum variance portfolio sits at 8.2947 per cent return and 6.6924 per cent volatility, and everything below it is dominated: returns of 7.295 and 9.295 per cent both carry 8.6495 per cent volatility.

Because the weights are linear in the target return, two-fund separation holds exactly. Combining two frontier portfolios at any mixing weight, including levered ones at 1.5 and minus 0.5, lands precisely on the frontier: the largest discrepancy across seven cases is 1.96 times ten to the minus fifteen. An investor needs two portfolios and a dial rather than a fresh optimisation per target.

And the result worth carrying: perturbing the equity expected return by one standard error, plus or minus 4.25 per cent, moves the equity weight at a 10 per cent target from minus 0.4724 to plus 0.4876. The global minimum variance portfolio does not move at all, because its weights contain no expected return anywhere. That is why minimum-variance portfolios are used far more in practice than their theoretical standing suggests.

Feynman test Line up every mix you could hold and plot each one by how much it earns and how much it wobbles. Most are wasteful: something else earns the same and wobbles less. Throw those away and what is left is a curve, and only its top half is worth considering. The useful surprise is that the whole curve can be built from any two points on it, so a fund manager needs two products rather than one per customer. And the single point at the bottom of the curve is the only one that does not need a forecast, which is why it survives being wrong about the future.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that two-fund separation is why the asset management industry looks the way it does. If every frontier portfolio is a mix of two others, a manager can offer a cautious and an aggressive fund and every client's optimum is a blend. That is not a marketing simplification of the theory; it is exactly what the theory says, and the exactness is verifiable to fifteen decimal places.

The second is that the frontier is an output of three estimated inputs, and one of them cannot be estimated. Moving the equity expected return by a single standard error swings the optimal equity weight from a short position to nearly half the portfolio. A frontier drawn as a line implies a precision that the inputs cannot support, and it should be drawn as a band.

The third is the exception to that, which is the most practically useful thing in the lecture. The global minimum variance portfolio's weights are the inverse covariance matrix applied to a vector of ones, normalised. No expected return appears. So the one point on the frontier that can be computed without forecasting anything is also the one point immune to the worst estimation problem, and that asymmetry explains a great deal of what practitioners actually do.

And a fourth. Constraints are free until they bind. Imposing a no-shorting rule costs exactly nothing at target returns where the unconstrained solution happens to be long-only, and 0.41 per cent of volatility once a weight would have gone negative. The cost of a mandate restriction therefore cannot be assessed in the abstract; it has to be evaluated at the allocation actually wanted.

Solving the frontier in closed form

Minimise w'Vw subject to w'mu = m and w'1 = 1. Two constraints, two Lagrange multipliers, and the solution turns out to be LINEAR in the target return m. Define three scalars: A = 1'V^-1 1 = 223.2747 B = 1'V^-1 mu = 18.5199 C = mu'V^-1 mu = 1.5695 D = AC - B^2 = 7.4361 Then for any target return m: minimum variance = (A.m^2 - 2.B.m + C) / D weights = V^-1 [ (C - B.m).1 + (A.m - B).mu ] / D THE GLOBAL MINIMUM VARIANCE PORTFOLIO is at m = B/A = 8.2947 per cent with variance 1/A, so a volatility of 6.6924 per cent. Its weights are 2.9327 per cent equity, 84.2666 bonds and 12.8007 property, summing to 1.0000000000. Two-fund separation

ANY portfolio on the frontier is a combination of ANY two other frontier portfolios. ADDING AN ASSET CAN NEVER MAKE THE FRONTIER WORSE, because the old frontier remains available by setting the new weight to zero. THE INEQUALITY IS WEAK: the improvement can be exactly zero, and it is zero precisely when the new asset is a linear combination of the existing ones, since then it adds no new direction to the space. THE REDUCTION RUNS FROM 3.37 PER CENT NEAR THE MINIMUM TO 9.79 PER CENT FURTHER UP THE FRONTIER. It is LARGEST where the existing frontier is steepest, because that is where the constraint of only three assets was biting hardest: at high target returns the three-asset solution was forced into concentrated equity, and a fourth growth asset with a 0.35 correlation relieves exactly that. THAT SHAPE IS WORTH KNOWING BEFORE A COMMITTEE DEBATES A NEW ASSET CLASS. The benefit is not uniform; it accrues to the aggressive end of the frontier and barely touches the cautious end. A cautious fund adding an exotic asset class is buying an improvement of about three per cent of its volatility.

The unconstrained frontier continues above that indefinitely by shorting the others, which is arithmetically true and unavailable to most funds.

The frontier is an output, and one point on it needs no forecast

Everything above treats the expected returns, volatilities and correlations as known. Lecture 1 established that the expected return is the worst-known input, with a standard error of 4.25 per cent on equity over twenty years. Here is what that does to the frontier. The instability is lecture 6's subject, and it is the reason a frontier should be drawn as a band rather than a line.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to carry is that the frontier's usefulness and its fragility come from the same place. Its weights are linear in the target return, which is what makes two-fund separation exact and what makes the whole construction tractable. It is also what makes the weights swing violently when the expected returns move, because a linear map with a large coefficient amplifies its input. The elegance and the instability are the same property seen twice.

WHAT DEPENDS ON WHAT, IN THE CLOSED FORM. quantity depends on mu? depends on V? estimable? the GMV weights NO yes YES, reasonably the GMV return yes yes the return, poorly any other frontier portfolio YES yes NO, badly the SHAPE of the frontier yes yes poorly whether an asset improves it yes yes poorly ONE ROW IN FIVE IS ESTIMABLE, AND IT IS THE ONE NOBODY TEACHES AS THE ANSWER. That is the practical asymmetry underneath a great deal of what allocators actually do, and it is visible directly in the algebra rather than requiring an appeal to experience.

The second is two-fund separation, which is worth appreciating as a piece of mathematics before it is appreciated as a business model. Nothing in the setup suggests that the whole frontier should be spanned by any two of its points, and the verification to within two parts in a thousand trillion across seven mixing weights, including levered ones outside the range of the two funds, is the kind of result that would be suspicious if it were not derivable. It is derivable, and it is why an industry that could in principle run a bespoke optimisation per client sells a small number of blended funds instead.

The third is the constraint result, which generalises well beyond short selling. A constraint costs exactly nothing until it binds and then costs something, so its price is a step function of the allocation wanted rather than a property of the constraint. A trustee asking what a restriction costs is asking an unanswerable question until the target is named. The same shape governs concentration limits, liquidity minima, credit rating floors and every other mandate rule, and it is why a mandate should be tested against the intended allocation rather than argued about in principle.

THE COST OF A CONSTRAINT, AS A GENERAL SHAPE. allocation wanted is the constraint binding? its cost well inside the bound NO exactly zero at the bound just zero, but the next step is not free beyond the bound YES positive and rising worked here: 8.50%, 9.50% and 10.50% targets all cost +0.00%, and an 11.30% target costs +0.41% of volatility once the bond weight is driven to 0.002 THE PRACTICAL COROLLARY IS THAT MANDATES ARE OFTEN CHEAPER THAN THEY LOOK AND OCCASIONALLY FAR MORE EXPENSIVE, and which of the two applies is a question about the allocation rather than about the rule.

The fourth is what the fourth asset was worth. Between 3.37 and 9.79 per cent of volatility, concentrated at the aggressive end of the frontier and barely present at the cautious end. That is a real gain and it is smaller than the enthusiasm that usually accompanies a new asset class, and its shape matters: a cautious fund adding an exotic allocation is buying about three per cent of its volatility, which may or may not survive the fees and the governance cost of holding something illiquid and unfamiliar.

And the fifth. The non-monotonicity in the last table deserves more attention than it usually gets. Raising the expected return on equity from 11.50 to 15.75 per cent lowers the optimal equity weight at a fixed 10 per cent target, because less equity is needed to reach the target and the saving is spent on safer assets. So an allocator cannot even sign the effect of a forecast error, let alone size it. ANY INTUITION OF THE FORM "IF I AM TOO OPTIMISTIC ABOUT EQUITIES I WILL HOLD TOO MANY" IS UNRELIABLE, and that is a stronger statement than the usual complaint that optimisers are sensitive to inputs.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The portfolio variance formula, the diversification floor and the estimation asymmetry between means and volatilities are all from portfolio risk and return, whose closing warning about the expected return is quantified here as a swing from a short position to half the portfolio. The covariance matrix that the closed form inverts is the object whose estimation is infeasible at scale, counted in the asset pricing model taxonomy, which is why lecture 5 of this module replaces it with a factor structure. Matrix inversion and quadratic forms are from mathematics, and variance and covariance from descriptive statistics.

Where this goes next. Lecture 3 chooses a point on the frontier using indifference curves, which supplies the investor's preferences that the frontier deliberately excludes. Lecture 4 adds a risk-free asset, at which point two-fund separation becomes far stronger: the frontier straightens into the capital market line and one of the two funds becomes cash, so every investor holds the same risky portfolio. Lecture 5 addresses the covariance estimation problem and lecture 6 the expected-return problem this lecture ends on, including the Black-Litterman response. Later in the track, the frontier is the machinery behind strategic asset allocation in lecture 7 and the risk budget of lecture 10.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank runs the same optimisation with different labels. Its trading book allocation across desks is a frontier problem with a risk budget in place of a target return, and the estimation instability found in step 4 is why limits are set by governance rather than by an optimiser. The observation that a constraint is free until it binds is exactly how a risk appetite framework operates: limits cost nothing while the business is inside them and become the binding determinant of strategy the moment it is not. And the covariance matrix that the closed form requires is the same object the standardised approach to market risk replaces with prescribed correlations, for the estimation reasons counted in the valuation module rather than any regulatory preference for simplicity.
