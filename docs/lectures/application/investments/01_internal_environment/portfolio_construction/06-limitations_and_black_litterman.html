Lectures / Investments / Internal environment / Portfolio construction

The optimiser beats equal weighting 54 per cent of the time and loses to it on average

Simulate twenty years of monthly data from a known truth, optimise on the sample, and score the answer on the truth. The optimiser captures 82.20 per cent of the Sharpe ratio it aims at, wins more often than not, and still loses on the mean, because its losses are larger than its wins. Shrinkage fixes most of it, and Black-Litterman fixes something else entirely: it confines the trade to the assets you actually had a view on.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Lectures 2 to 5 kept running into the same wall: the frontier is exquisitely sensitive to the expected returns and the expected returns cannot be estimated. This lecture measures the damage properly, by simulation, and then covers the two things that actually help.

Take a known truth, draw T months of returns from it, estimate the inputs, optimise, and score the resulting weights under the truth. With twenty years of monthly data the optimiser achieves an out-of-sample Sharpe ratio of 0.4316 against an attainable 0.5250, so it captures 82.20 per cent of what it aims at. Equal weighting requires no estimation at all and scores 0.4576, or 87.15 per cent. The optimiser beats it on 54.07 per cent of samples and loses on the average, because the bad draws are much worse than the good draws are good.

The mechanism is that the weight is linear in the estimated mean, with a slope of 9.9310 per unit here, so one percentage point of estimation error moves the equity weight by 0.0993. The standard error of a twenty-year mean estimate is 4.2485 per cent, giving a one-standard-error weight swing of 0.4219 against a correct weight of 0.2777. The uncertainty in the weight exceeds the weight. And on a 60-asset universe whose true optimum is long-only with gross leverage of exactly 1.0000, the estimated optimum has median gross leverage of 22.31 and shorts 29 of the 60.

Two responses work. Shrinkage pulls the mean estimates towards a common value, and the optimal amount falls as data accumulates, from 0.8 at five years to 0.4 at fifty; at twenty years it lifts the Sharpe ratio from 0.4252 to 0.4736 and takes the optimiser past equal weighting. Black-Litterman does something different and better understood as a discipline than as a Bayesian method: because the posterior deviation is proportional to the view portfolio, a view on one asset moves that asset's weight and nothing else. Overwriting a forecast directly moves all three.

Feynman test Feed the optimiser your best guesses and it treats them as facts. Guess slightly high on one asset and it does not buy slightly more of it, it buys a great deal more and sells whatever that asset resembles. Do the experiment where you know the real answer and the machine gets it substantially wrong, badly enough that splitting your money equally between everything does better. Two fixes: distrust your guesses on purpose by dragging them together, and if you do have a genuine opinion, state it as a comparison against what the market already believes so the trade only touches what your opinion was about.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this is the lecture that decides whether any of the previous four is usable. The closed-form frontier is exact, two-fund separation is exact, the security market line is exact, and none of that helps if the inputs are too noisy to act on. The simulation puts a number on it, and the number is that a naive rule with no inputs at all is competitive with the full apparatus at any realistic sample length.

The second is that the failure is not the optimiser's fault, which changes what to do about it. Given point estimates and instructed to trust them, the optimiser trusts them, and the response is linear and exactly calculable. Anybody who describes an optimiser as unstable has mislocated the problem: it is perfectly stable in its inputs and the inputs are unstable. That is why the fixes all operate on the inputs rather than on the algorithm.

The third is that shrinkage is not a fudge, it is the correct Bayesian answer, and the amount of shrinkage that helps is measurable. Pulling the means to a common value discards information deliberately, and it wins because the information discarded is mostly noise. That the optimal amount declines with sample length, from 0.8 to 0.4 across the range tested, is the shape any statistician would predict and is worth confirming rather than assuming.

And a fourth. Black-Litterman is usually taught as a way of combining views with equilibrium, which is accurate and misses the practical point. Its real property is containment: the trade it recommends lies along the view portfolio, so an analyst who has an opinion about one asset gets a position in one asset. Overwriting a forecast in a plain optimiser produces positions in everything, including assets nobody expressed a view on, and defending those in an investment committee is impossible because there is no argument for them.

The mental model

Measure the damage, locate its cause, then apply the two fixes that address different halves of the problem.

Step 4

The detail

The experiment, and a trap in setting it up

THE ONLY HONEST WAY TO TEST AN OPTIMISER IS TO KNOW THE ANSWER IN ADVANCE. Fix a true mu and V, draw T months of returns from that distribution, estimate mu_hat and V_hat from the draw, optimise on the estimates, then score the resulting weights under the TRUE mu and V. A TRAP FIRST. The obvious portfolio to test is the tangency, w_T = V_hat^-1 (mu_hat - rf) / 1' V_hat^-1 (mu_hat - rf) and it is unusable in simulation. The denominator passes through zero on unlucky samples, so the weights explode and then FLIP SIGN, turning the maximum-Sharpe portfolio into the minimum. A mean certainty equivalent computed this way came out at minus 7,631 per cent, driven entirely by a handful of draws, and told me nothing about the optimiser. THE FIX IS TO SCORE ON THE OUT-OF-SAMPLE SHARPE RATIO USING UNNORMALISED WEIGHTS, w = V_hat^-1 (mu_hat - rf). The Sharpe ratio is invariant to positive rescaling, so the normalisation is irrelevant, and no sign flip occurs. This is also what DeMiguel, Garlappi and Uppal use. benchmarks under the TRUTH the true optimum Sharpe 0.525037 equal weighting, 1/N Sharpe 0.457580 (87.15% of the maximum) minimum variance, needing no means Sharpe 0.492300 (93.76% of the maximum) NOTE THE THIRD ROW BEFORE READING ON. A portfolio that uses no expected returns at all reaches 93.76 per cent of the attainable Sharpe ratio. That sets the bar the mean estimates have to clear.

The limitation neither fix addresses

Shrinkage and Black-Litterman both attack the INPUTS. Neither touches the OBJECTIVE, and the objective assumes variance is the right measure of risk. what the framework ignores why it matters skewness, the third moment two portfolios with equal mean and variance rank equally however differently they are skewed kurtosis, the fourth moment valuation lecture 10 measured a five-sigma day as 1,511 times more likely under a t(6) than a normal correlation changing in stress lecture 1 measured a 0.15 correlation stressed to 0.85 raising portfolio volatility by 111.06% the horizon a single-period model applied to a multi-decade liability, which lectures 8 and 9 address THE THIRD ROW IS THE MOST DANGEROUS BECAUSE IT IS THE MOST HIDDEN. The optimiser treats the covariance matrix as a fixed object, and diversification is bought precisely from the off-diagonal entries that stop holding when it is most needed. SO THE HONEST SUMMARY OF THIS MODULE SO FAR IS THAT MEAN-VARIANCE IS A FRAMEWORK FOR THINKING AND NOT A MACHINE FOR DECIDING. It tells you that covariance matters more than variance, that the frontier exists, that preferences select a point on it and that equilibrium prices follow. It does not produce an allocation anybody should implement without constraining, shrinking and anchoring it first, and lectures 7 to 10 are largely about those three verbs.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to carry is that the optimiser is not unstable and saying so mislocates the problem. Its response to an input is a constant derivative, 9.9310 on the equity weight per unit of equity mean, which is as stable as a function gets. What is unstable is the input: a twenty-year mean estimate carries a 4.25 per cent standard error, and multiplying those together gives a weight uncertainty of 0.4219 against a correct weight of 0.2777. Every fix in this lecture acts on the input, and that is a consequence of getting the diagnosis right rather than a coincidence.

WHERE THE PROBLEM IS AND WHERE IT IS NOT. candidate culprit verdict the optimisation algorithm INNOCENT: linear response, constant derivative the mean-variance objective partly guilty: ignores skewness and stress correlation the expected return estimates GUILTY: standard error larger than the quantity estimated the covariance estimates guilty only when N approaches T THE THIRD ROW IS WHERE ALL THE DAMAGE IS AT THREE ASSETS, and the fourth is where it is at five hundred. THAT DISTINCTION DETERMINES WHICH SHRINKAGE IS WORTH APPLYING, and applying the wrong one is a common and expensive waste of effort.

The second is the mean-median split at twenty years, which is the most professionally useful thing here. The optimiser beats equal weighting on 54.07 per cent of samples and its average Sharpe ratio is below equal weighting's. Both statements are true and they support opposite conclusions. Anybody presenting a backtest can choose which to report, and the choice is usually made unconsciously by whoever built the model. THE ONLY DEFENCE IS TO REQUIRE BOTH, and to be suspicious of any comparison that offers only one.

The third is the corner solution result, which is stronger than the usual complaint that optimisers produce extreme weights. The universe was constructed so the true answer is long-only with gross leverage of exactly 1.0000 at every size, and the estimated answer shorts roughly half the universe at every size. Half, at every size, is what you would see if the sign of each weight were decided by noise. So the no-shorting constraint does not repair the optimisation, it overrides it: the constraint binds on half the positions, which means half the portfolio is chosen by the constraint and not by the optimiser at all.

WHAT A NO-SHORTING CONSTRAINT ACTUALLY DOES HERE. THIS SHOWS IT BINDS ALMOST EVERYWHERE ONCE THE INPUTS ARE ESTIMATED RATHER THAN KNOWN. those two findings are only reconcilable because lecture 2 used the true inputs.

The fourth is that shrinkage changes the answer to the practical question. Raw optimisation at twenty years scores 0.4252 against equal weighting's 0.4576 and loses; shrunk at the optimal amount it scores 0.4736 and wins. So "is the optimiser worth using" has no answer independent of how the inputs are treated, and the DeMiguel result is best read not as a case against optimisation but as a case against optimisation on raw sample moments. The optimal shrinkage falling from 0.8 to 0.4 as the sample lengthens is the reassuring part: the method degrades gracefully towards trusting the data as the data become trustworthy.

And the fifth. Black-Litterman is nearly always presented as Bayesian machinery for blending views with equilibrium, which is what it is and is not why it survives. It survives because the deviation from the market is exactly proportional to the view portfolio, so the covariance matrix cancels and every position traces to a stated opinion. An analyst who thinks equity will lag gets a short in equity and nothing else. The naive alternative gives them a position in bonds they never asked for, and in an investment committee the difference between a portfolio you can explain line by line and one you cannot is the whole of the difference.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The closed-form frontier and the weight instability this lecture quantifies by simulation are from the opportunity set and the efficient frontier, whose no-shorting result is revisited here under estimated rather than known inputs. The utility function and the certainty-equivalent metric are from indifference curves and the optimum portfolio, and the market portfolio that supplies Black-Litterman's prior, along with the reverse-optimisation identity, is the capital market line and CAPM read backwards. The near-singular covariance regime where Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage earns its keep is measured in arbitrage pricing theory and factor construction, and the correlation-in-stress problem is from portfolio risk and return. The non-normality that the objective ignores is the empirical characteristics of asset prices. Bayesian updating, shrinkage estimators and the distinction between mean and median as summary statistics are from statistics.

Where this goes next. Lecture 7 applies all three verbs at once, constraining, shrinking and anchoring, to produce a strategic asset allocation somebody would actually implement, and the implied-return machinery is how a house view is documented in practice. Lectures 8 and 9 replace the utility function with a liability, which changes the objective rather than the inputs and so addresses a limitation this lecture explicitly leaves open. Lecture 10 measures the resulting portfolio against a risk budget, where tracking error against a benchmark plays the role the market prior plays here. In performance measurement, the mean-median asymmetry found in step 4 is the same statistical trap that makes manager selection on short records unreliable.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank meets this lecture as model risk management. The finding that a parameter's standard error can exceed the parameter is the reason an A-IRB model carries a margin of conservatism, which is shrinkage in supervisory language: the estimate is deliberately moved away from the sample towards a safer value by an amount that falls as data accumulate. The corner-solution result is why supervisors constrain model outputs with floors rather than trusting an optimisation, exactly as the no-shorting constraint does here. And Black-Litterman's containment property has a governance analogue in the risk management requirement that every management overlay be attributable to a documented judgement, since an adjustment that moves exposures nobody formed a view about cannot be defended to a validation function.

Black-Litterman is Black and Litterman (1992), "Global Portfolio Optimization", Financial Analysts Journal 48(5), with the reverse-optimisation and containment properties derived here rather than quoted. Everything numerical is invented for illustration: the three-asset truth carried from lecture 2, the 5 per cent risk-free rate, a risk aversion of 4, market weights of 60/30/10, a tau of 0.05, the one-factor universes for the corner-solution test, and the view magnitudes. The simulations use 4,000 trials for the main table and 400 for the corner-solution medians, with seeded generators so every figure is reproducible. Three internal checks: reverse optimisation recovers the market weights to 1.110e-16, a view with uncertainty 1e+12 leaves them unchanged to 1.221e-15, and the containment result was confirmed numerically with non-view deviations of 0.000e+00 and 2.359e-16 before being derived algebraically. The first attempt at the main simulation was discarded because normalising the tangency produced mean certainty equivalents of minus 7,631 per cent driven by sign flips on a handful of draws, and the corner-solution table reports medians for the same reason.
