Portfolio risk and return: an asset with 40 per cent volatility can reduce the risk of a 15 per cent portfolio

Adding an asset reduces portfolio risk if and only if its correlation multiplied by its volatility is below the portfolio's volatility. How volatile it is on its own does not enter the condition. So a 40 per cent volatility asset at a 0.20 correlation helps, and an 18 per cent asset at 0.95 does not, which makes "add safe things" the wrong rule and "add things that move differently" the right one.

Step 1

The 30-second version

This module has no source chapter. Chapter 11 of the F105 summary is a heading with nothing beneath it, while the syllabus requires the opportunity set, the efficient frontier, indifference curves and the optimum portfolio explicitly. Everything here is built from the primary literature, and each lecture names what it drew on.

The whole subject follows from one asymmetry. Expected return is linear in the portfolio weights and variance is not, so a portfolio can have the average return of its parts and less than the average risk. At a correlation of one the volatility is the weighted average, which is the only case where risk behaves like return. Every other case sits below it, and the gap is the diversification benefit.

Diversification has a floor rather than a limit. For N equally weighted assets each with volatility s and each pair correlated at rho, the portfolio volatility tends to s times the square root of rho. At a 0.30 correlation a portfolio of a thousand assets still carries 13.71 per cent volatility against 25 for one, so diversification removes 45.2 per cent and no more, ever. Most of that arrives by about twenty holdings.

And the result that makes the module's central shift concrete: adding an asset reduces risk if and only if its correlation times its volatility is below the portfolio's volatility. A 40 per cent volatility asset at a 0.20 correlation gives a product of 8.00 per cent against 15, so it helps. An 18 per cent asset at 0.95 gives 17.10 and does not. The asset's own risk is not in the condition.

Feynman test If you own two things that go up and down together, owning both is no safer than owning one. If they move independently, the bad days of one land on the good days of the other and the combination is steadier than either. So what matters about a new holding is not how jumpy it is but whether it is jumpy at the same times as everything you already own. A wild asset that zigs when the rest zags is a stabiliser, and a placid one that moves in step is not.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this is where valuation stops being enough. The previous module priced assets one at a time, and an asset's price does not tell you whether to own it, because that depends on what else is held. The shift from an asset's own variance to its covariance with everything else is the entire content of portfolio construction, and it is quantifiable rather than rhetorical: at fifty equally weighted holdings, 93.63 per cent of the portfolio's variance comes from covariance terms.

The second is that the rule for whether to add something is genuinely counterintuitive and genuinely simple. Practitioners reach for low-volatility assets when they want to reduce risk, and the arithmetic says volatility is neither necessary nor sufficient. Getting that one condition right changes which assets are considered diversifiers, and it is the reason hedge funds, commodities and emerging markets appear in institutional portfolios despite each being more volatile than the portfolio they join.

The third is that the benefit is contingent on an input that moves. Every calculation here holds a correlation fixed, and correlations rise in exactly the states where the portfolio's risk matters most. A twenty-asset portfolio built at a 0.15 correlation and stressed at 0.85 sees its volatility rise 111.06 per cent without a single holding changing.

And a fourth. The three inputs are not equally knowable. Over twenty years of monthly data the mean is estimated to plus or minus 5.59 per cent and the volatility to plus or minus 1.141, a ratio of 4.9 times, and the mean's precision depends only on the calendar span rather than the sampling frequency. The input the optimiser is most sensitive to is the one we know least well, which is lecture 6's subject and worth flagging before lectures 2 to 5 assume the inputs are given.

The condition that decides whether an asset helps

A portfolio holds asset 1 alone. WHEN DOES ADDING SOME OF ASSET 2 REDUCE ITS RISK? Differentiate the two-asset variance with respect to the weight in asset 1 and evaluate at w = 1, where the portfolio is entirely asset 1: d Var / dw at w=1 = 2.w.s1^2 - 2.(1-w).s2^2 + 2.rho.s1.s2.(1-2w) evaluated at w=1 = 2.s1^2 - 2.rho.s1.s2 = 2.s1.(s1 - rho.s2) Moving weight AWAY from asset 1, which means adding asset 2, reduces the variance when that derivative is positive. So: ADDING ASSET 2 REDUCES RISK if and only if rho x s2 < s1 NOTE WHAT IS ABSENT FROM THE CONDITION. How volatile asset 2 is on its own does not appear. Where it stops working

Every number above holds a correlation fixed. The correlation is the one input that MOVES when it matters, and it moves in the direction that removes the benefit. A twenty-asset equally weighted portfolio at a 25 per cent single-asset volatility: calm rho 0.20 -> volatility 12.25% stressed rho 0.60 -> 19.69% an increase of 60.73% calm rho 0.30 -> volatility 14.47% stressed rho 0.75 -> 21.83% an increase of 50.87% calm rho 0.15 -> volatility 10.97% stressed rho 0.85 -> 23.15% an increase of 111.06% A PORTFOLIO BUILT AT A 0.15 CORRELATION AND STRESSED AT 0.85 SEES ITS VOLATILITY RISE BY 111.06 PER CENT WITHOUT A SINGLE HOLDING CHANGING. The diversification did not fail to work; it worked exactly as specified, on an input that turned out to be a variable. A LOW MEASURED CORRELATION IS THEREFORE NOT AN UNAMBIGUOUS GOOD. It is a benefit contingent on the measurement continuing to hold, and it stops holding precisely in the states where the portfolio's risk matters most. THAT IS THE SAME STRUCTURE AS THE WRONG-WAY RISK OF THE DERIVATIVES MODULE and the correlation sensitivity of the securitisation tranches in valuation lecture 13. In all three the exposure is to a correlation, in all three the correlation is unobservable, and in all three it moves against the holder.

The three inputs, and how badly each is known

Mean-variance analysis needs expected returns, volatilities and correlations. They are NOT equally estimable, and the ranking is the opposite of their importance. With a 25 per cent volatility asset and monthly data: history mean +/- volatility +/- ratio 1 year 25.00% 5.103% 4.9x 3 years 14.43% 2.946% 4.9x 5 years 11.18% 2.282% 4.9x 10 years 7.91% 1.614% 4.9x 20 years 5.59% 1.141% 4.9x 50 years 3.54% 0.722% 4.9x THE MEAN IS ESTIMATED FROM THE ENDPOINTS AND NOTHING ELSE. Its standard error falls only with the square root of the CALENDAR SPAN, so sampling more frequently does not help it at all: a hundred years of daily data estimates the mean no better than a hundred years of annual data. THE VOLATILITY IMPROVES WITH THE NUMBER OF OBSERVATIONS, so sampling more frequently does help it, and it is consequently about five times better known over any given span. SO THE INPUT THE OPTIMISER IS MOST SENSITIVE TO IS THE ONE WE KNOW LEAST WELL. After twenty years of data, an expected return is known to plus or minus 5.59 per cent, which is wider than the entire equity risk premium being estimated. THAT MISMATCH IS THE SUBJECT OF LECTURE 6, and it is worth flagging here because everything in lectures 2 to 5 assumes the inputs are known exactly.

What diversification does not do

Three things it is routinely credited with and does not deliver: claim what is actually true "it raises expected return" NO. expected return is LINEAR in the weights, so a diversified portfolio earns the weighted average of its parts and never more "it protects against a crash" NO. it protects against IDIOSYNCRATIC events. a crash is the common factor, which is exactly the part that does not diversify "more holdings is always better" ALMOST NEVER MATERIALLY beyond about twenty, where the marginal benefit is small and the marginal cost is not The second is the one that costs money. On a twenty-holding portfolio at a 0.30 correlation with 25 per cent single-asset volatility: total portfolio volatility 14.470% the SYSTEMATIC part 13.693% 89.55% of the variance the DIVERSIFIABLE part 4.677% 10.45% of the variance DIVERSIFICATION HAS ALREADY REMOVED ALMOST ALL OF WHAT IT CAN REMOVE, and what remains is 89.55 per cent of the variance and is the market itself. AN INVESTOR WORRIED ABOUT A CRASH IS WORRIED ABOUT THE PART DIVERSIFICATION CANNOT TOUCH, and needs a hedge, a different asset class, or a smaller position. More names will not help, and the twenty-first holding removes about a tenth of a percentage point of volatility while adding a full set of monitoring, dealing and custody costs.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to carry is the condition, because it replaces an intuition that is wrong with a test that is trivial. An asset helps if its correlation times its volatility is below the portfolio's volatility. Nothing else about it matters for that question: not its Sharpe ratio, not its rating, not whether it is described as defensive. A 40 per cent volatility asset at a 0.20 correlation helps and an 18 per cent asset at 0.95 does not, and anybody can check which case they are in with two numbers and a multiplication.

THE TEST, AND WHAT IT REPLACES. the intuition the test "add safe assets to reduce risk" add assets with rho x s below the portfolio's s "avoid volatile assets" volatility alone is neither necessary nor sufficient "diversify across many names" beyond about twenty, the marginal benefit is a fraction of a percentage point "gold/hedge funds/commodities each is more volatile than the portfolio it joins, are too risky" and each can still reduce its risk THE FOURTH ROW IS WHERE INSTITUTIONAL PORTFOLIOS ACTUALLY DIFFER FROM RETAIL ONES, and the difference is not appetite for risk. It is whether the allocator applies the condition or the intuition.

The second is that diversification has a floor and the floor is where the argument should start rather than end. Beyond twenty holdings there is almost nothing left to remove, and what remains is 89.55 per cent of the variance and is the market itself. An investor worried about a crash is worried about exactly the part that does not diversify, so adding names is not a response to that worry. The response is a hedge, a different asset class, or less of the position, and confusing the two is how portfolios end up with two hundred holdings and the risk of one.

The third is that every number in this lecture is contingent on a correlation, and correlations are not constants. A twenty-asset portfolio built at 0.15 and stressed at 0.85 sees its volatility rise 111.06 per cent with no holding changing. Worse, the effect is largest where the portfolio looked best: a 0.05 calm correlation multiplies by 2.88 under an 0.80 stress against 1.14 for a 0.60 calm correlation. The most diversified-looking portfolio takes the largest proportional shock because it had the most to lose.

THE SAME EXPOSURE UNDER FOUR NAMES, ALL MET IN THIS COURSE. setting the unobservable correlation determines a diversified portfolio how much risk actually diversifies away, and the floor a securitisation tranche which slice bears the loss, though not the total protection from a CDS seller whether the protection pays when it is needed a hedge with a proxy instrument what fraction of the volatility survives the hedge FOUR SETTINGS, ONE PARAMETER, NEVER OBSERVED, AND IN EVERY CASE IT MOVES AGAINST THE HOLDER IN STRESS. That is not four separate lessons about four instruments. It is one lesson about one parameter, and it is the single most transferable thing in the investments track.

The fourth is the estimation asymmetry, which deserves to be flagged before the mathematics gets any more elaborate. Over twenty years the mean is known to plus or minus 5.59 per cent and the volatility to 1.141, and the mean's precision depends only on the calendar span, so no amount of higher-frequency data improves it. The optimiser in lectures 2 to 5 will take expected returns as exact inputs, and the input it is most sensitive to is the one that cannot be estimated. Holding both facts at once is the correct state of mind for the rest of the module.

And the fifth, on what this module is. There is no source chapter: the summary's chapter 11 is a heading over nothing, and the syllabus requires the material regardless. That means everything here is built from Markowitz and the textbook spine rather than checked against a summary, which changes the failure mode. In the valuation module the risk was a source that stated a result without its condition. Here the risk is mine alone, so every lecture in this module states its sources explicitly and every quantitative claim is computed rather than recalled.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. Variance, covariance and correlation are from descriptive statistics. The asset characteristics whose volatilities and correlations are the inputs here are the whole of the asset classes module, and the eight-dimension framework of its first lecture is what a correlation matrix compresses. The counting argument that makes a full covariance matrix unusable, and the factor structure that replaces it, are from the asset pricing model taxonomy. The hedging arithmetic in which variance removed is the correlation squared is the optimal hedge ratio, and this lecture's condition for adding an asset is the same derivative evaluated at a different point. The unobservable correlation that moves against the holder appeared in credit derivatives and again in credit analysis.

Where this goes next. Lecture 2 takes the two-asset curves computed here and traces the whole opportunity set, whose upper boundary is the efficient frontier. Lecture 3 chooses a point on it using indifference curves, and lecture 4 adds a risk-free asset, which turns the frontier into a straight line and produces the capital market line and CAPM. Lecture 5 replaces the covariance matrix with a factor structure for the reason counted in step 4 here, and lecture 6 confronts the estimation problem this lecture flags. Read in order: each is a strict prerequisite for the next.

Where the bank sits opposite. The floor result is the reason a loan book cannot diversify away its exposure to the domestic economy, however many borrowers it has, which is the whole of credit concentration risk: idiosyncratic borrower risk vanishes with the number of loans and the common factor does not. The single asset correlation in the A-IRB capital formula is exactly the rho of step 4, fixed by regulation rather than estimated precisely because it is unobservable and because the capital requirement is so sensitive to it. And the observation that correlations rise in stress is why a bank runs market risk stress scenarios with correlations reset rather than sampled from calm history, and why the diversification benefit recognised across risk types in economic capital is deliberately conservative.
