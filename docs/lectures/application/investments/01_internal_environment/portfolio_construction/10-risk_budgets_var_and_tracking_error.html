Diversifying two bonds raised the measured risk from zero to fifty

Value at risk is not subadditive, and the counterexample takes four lines: two independent bonds each defaulting with four per cent probability have a 95 per cent VaR of zero individually and fifty together. Expected shortfall passes the same test. Along the way, a 99 per cent VaR model cannot be distinguished from one wrong by a factor of two in a year of data, and concentration alone makes a sleeve's risk share exceed its weight share even when every sleeve is statistically identical.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Nine lectures have built a portfolio. This one measures it, allocates its risk deliberately, and finds that the most widely used risk measure in finance fails the one property a risk measure most obviously ought to have.

Start with the identity that makes risk budgeting possible. Adding weighted standalone tracking errors gives 3.4500 per cent against an actual 2.7315, so the sum always overstates. Euler's decomposition sums exactly, to 3.5 parts in a quintillion, and it reveals that a risk budget is not a money budget: alternatives are 10.00 per cent of the money and 13.03 of the risk, credit is 20.00 of the money and 4.49 of the risk.

Then the mechanism, where my first explanation was wrong. I attributed the gap to unequal standalone tracking errors and correlations. Making every sleeve statistically identical leaves a gap of 3.08 percentage points, so that cannot be it. The correct condition is that a sleeve's risk share exceeds its weight share exactly when its weight exceeds the sum of squared weights. Concentration alone creates the gap, because risk is convex in the weight.

And then value at risk. Two independent bonds, each losing everything with four per cent probability, held at fifty notional each. Each alone has a 95 per cent VaR of zero, because 96 per cent of the distribution sits at no loss. Together, the probability of no loss is 92.16 per cent, so the 95th percentile is a fifty loss. VaR of the sum is 50 and the sum of the VaRs is 0: diversification increased the measured risk. Expected shortfall on the identical example gives 51.60 against a sum of 80.00 and passes. The failure is caused by the discrete jump across the quantile, which a continuous test confirms.

Feynman test Decide in advance how much of your total wobble each decision is allowed to use, and be aware that the biggest holding uses more than its share of the money would suggest, purely because risk grows faster than size. Then be careful which number you use for wobble. The popular one asks how bad the twentieth-worst day is and ignores everything past it, which lets two safe-looking things become dangerous when combined. Ask instead for the average of all the bad days, and the paradox disappears. And do not imagine a year of data can tell you whether your number was right, because it cannot even catch an answer that is wrong twofold.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that Euler's decomposition is what makes a risk budget a budget rather than a metaphor. Contributions that sum exactly to the total can be allocated, monitored and attributed; anything else cannot. Adding standalone tracking errors overstates by 0.72 percentage points here, which sounds conservative and is worse than conservative, because it produces a number nobody can reconcile to the portfolio.

The second is that the risk-versus-weight gap is usually explained wrongly, including by me on the first attempt. It is not primarily about a sleeve being volatile or correlated: it survives making every sleeve statistically identical. It is about concentration, and the test is whether a weight exceeds the sum of squared weights. That reframing matters because it means a risk budget constrains position sizing even in a universe of identical assets, which the volatility explanation would not predict.

The third is that value at risk fails subadditivity, and the failure is not exotic. The counterexample uses two ordinary bonds and arithmetic a trustee can follow, and it delivers the conclusion that combining two positions raised the measured risk from zero to fifty. Any risk system built on VaR will occasionally reward concentration and penalise diversification, and the Basel market risk framework moved to expected shortfall for exactly this reason.

And a fourth, which should temper confidence in any tail number. A 99 per cent VaR model is accepted at 95 per cent confidence on anything from zero to six exceptions in 250 days. A model whose true exception rate is two per cent, twice what it claims, is accepted 76.37 per cent of the time. Backtesting cannot validate a tail measure on a year of data, and a governance process that treats a clean backtest as evidence of a correct model has misunderstood what the test can deliver.

A mechanism claim that failed its own test

MY FIRST EXPLANATION OF THAT GAP WAS THAT IT COMES FROM UNEQUAL STANDALONE TRACKING ERRORS AND UNEQUAL CORRELATIONS. If that were the whole story, making every sleeve statistically identical would make the gap vanish. It does not: identical tracking errors, identical correlations, weights 0.40 / 0.30 / 0.20 / 0.10 risk shares 0.4308 / 0.3000 / 0.1846 / 0.0846 largest gap 0.0308 THE GAP SURVIVES, so the explanation was wrong. Working out why gives a better answer. Under equicorrelation at rho, with weights summing to one, share_i = w_i [ (1 - rho) w_i + rho ] / [ (1 - rho) sum(w^2) + rho ] which equals w_i if and only if w_i equals sum(w^2). On these weights sum(w^2) = 0.3000, so: sleeve weight against sum(w^2) risk share UK equity 0.4000 ABOVE above its weight overseas equity 0.3000 EQUAL exactly its weight credit 0.2000 below below its weight alternatives 0.1000 below below its weight CONCENTRATION ALONE CREATES THE GAP, because tracking error is convex in the weight, so a larger holding contributes disproportionately more risk than money. The gap vanishes only when the WEIGHTS are equal, which the equal-weight case confirms exactly, to zero. THAT MATTERS BEYOND THE ARITHMETIC. Under the volatility explanation, a risk budget would have nothing to say about a universe of identical assets. Under the correct one it constrains position sizing in every universe, and the largest position is always over-contributing.

The fundamental law of active management

Grinold (1989): the information ratio achievable by a manager is approximately IR = IC x sqrt(breadth) where the INFORMATION COEFFICIENT is the correlation between the forecast and the outcome, and BREADTH is the number of independent bets taken per period. WHAT IT SAYS IS UNCOMFORTABLE. An information coefficient of 0.05 is a five per cent correlation between forecast and outcome, which is very nearly no skill: IC = 0.02 needs 625 independent bets for an information ratio of 0.50 IC = 0.05 needs 100 IC = 0.10 needs 25 SO A MANAGER WITH ALMOST NO FORECASTING ABILITY CAN DELIVER A RESPECTABLE RECORD BY TAKING ENOUGH INDEPENDENT BETS, and a manager with real skill on a handful of positions cannot. That is the quantitative case for systematic breadth over concentrated conviction, and it is a stronger argument than it is usually given credit for.

Where the fundamental law is overstated

THE WORD "INDEPENDENT" IS DOING ALL THE WORK, and the standard warning is that correlated bets "collapse breadth to one". Test it by imposing a common factor on the bets: correlation between bets information ratio as % of independent EFFECTIVE breadth 0.00 1.0212 100.0% 104.3 0.25 0.9597 94.0% 92.1 0.50 0.8352 81.8% 69.8 0.90 0.6367 62.4% 40.5 0.99 0.5978 58.5% 35.7 Effective breadth is recovered by inverting the formula, as the squared ratio of the information ratio to the information coefficient, against a nominal breadth of 100. THE DEGRADATION IS REAL AND MONOTONE AND IT IS NOT A COLLAPSE. At a correlation of 0.99 between every pair of bets, one hundred nominal positions behave like 35.7 independent ones and retain 58.5 per cent of the information ratio. A genuine collapse to a single bet would leave one tenth of it. WHY NOT? Because even at 0.99 each bet retains one per cent of independent variation, and the manager is forecasting that component too. THE CLAIM THAT CORRELATION DESTROYS BREADTH IS DIRECTIONALLY RIGHT AND QUANTITATIVELY OVERSTATED, and the useful version is the effective breadth column, which can be computed rather than asserted.

Value at risk, and expected shortfall

VALUE AT RISK at confidence a is the loss exceeded with probability 1 - a. EXPECTED SHORTFALL is the average loss given that the VaR is exceeded. Under normality both have closed forms, with the mean set to zero so the comparison is about the tail alone: confidence VaR ES ES / VaR 90.0% 23.0679% 31.5897% 1.3694 95.0% 29.6074% 37.1288% 1.2540 99.0% 41.8743% 47.9739% 1.1457 99.5% 46.3649% 52.0551% 1.1227 99.9% 55.6242% 60.6076% 1.0896 THE RATIO IS MODEST AND FALLS AS THE CONFIDENCE RISES, which is a property of the normal tail and not of the measures. Reporting VaR alone conceals the disagreement.

The counterexample

A risk measure ought to be SUBADDITIVE: the risk of a combined position should not exceed the sum of the parts, because diversification cannot make things worse. Artzner, Delbaen, Eber and Heath (1999) call a measure COHERENT if it is subadditive, monotonic, positively homogeneous and translation invariant. VALUE AT RISK IS NOT COHERENT, and the counterexample is short. Two INDEPENDENT bonds, each losing its entire value with probability 4 per cent, held at 50 notional each. ONE BOND ALONE P(loss = 0) = 0.9600 P(loss = 50) = 0.0400 P(loss <= 0) = 0.9600, which EXCEEDS 0.95, so the 95th percentile of loss is 0 VaR_95(A) = VaR_95(B) = 0 BOTH TOGETHER P(0 defaults) = 0.921600 loss 0 P(1 default) = 0.076800 loss 50 P(2 defaults) = 0.001600 loss 100 P(loss <= 0) = 0.921600, which is BELOW 0.95 P(loss <= 50) = 0.998400, which is above 0.95 VaR_95(A + B) = 50 VaR(A) + VaR(B) = 0 VaR(A + B) = 50 DIVERSIFYING TWO INDEPENDENT POSITIONS RAISED THE MEASURED RISK FROM ZERO TO FIFTY. Nothing about the positions changed; only the measure's view of them did. THE SAME EXAMPLE UNDER EXPECTED SHORTFALL AT 95 PER CENT, averaging over the worst five per cent of outcomes: ES(A) = ES(B) = [ 0.0400 x 50 + 0.0100 x 0 ] / 0.05 = 40.0000 ES(A + B) = [ 0.001600 x 100 + 0.048400 x 50 ] / 0.05 = 51.6000 ES(A) + ES(B) = 80.0000 which is at least ES(A + B) = 51.6000 EXPECTED SHORTFALL PASSES. The reason is that it looks at the whole tail rather than one point of it, so it cannot ignore a loss that sits just beyond the quantile. WHAT CAUSES THE FAILURE? The claim is the discrete jump in the loss distribution across the quantile. Test it on continuous losses, where there is no jump: two independent standard normal losses VaR(A) = 1.6381 VaR(B) = 1.6375 VaR of the average = 1.1587 the average VaR = 1.6378 SUBADDITIVE, COMFORTABLY. THE DISCRETENESS IS THE CAUSE, which is why the failure shows up in credit and insurance portfolios, whose losses are lumpy, and rarely in equity portfolios, whose losses are not. THAT IS ALSO WHY THE BASEL MARKET RISK FRAMEWORK MOVED FROM VALUE AT RISK TO EXPECTED SHORTFALL.

One four times wrong is still accepted one time in eight. THAT IS THE PRACTICAL PROBLEM WITH VALIDATING ANY TAIL MEASURE. At a 99 per cent confidence level the events being tested are rare by construction, so a year of data contains almost no information about them. A CLEAN BACKTEST IS NOT EVIDENCE OF A CORRECT MODEL, and a governance process that treats it as such has mistaken the absence of evidence for evidence of absence. The response in practice is to test at lower confidence levels, where exceptions are frequent enough to count, and to supplement the count test with tests of whether the exceptions cluster, since a correct model should produce independent breaches.

A fund that tells three managers they each have a third is telling them something it has not calculated, and the manager who takes the most risk per pound is being given the largest budget by accident.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to carry is the subadditivity counterexample, because it is short enough to reproduce from memory and it settles an argument that is otherwise conducted on authority. Two bonds, four per cent default probability each, fifty notional each. Individually the 95 per cent VaR is zero because 96 per cent of the distribution sits at no loss. Together the no-loss probability is 92.16 per cent, so the quantile moves into the one-default state and VaR becomes fifty. DIVERSIFICATION RAISED THE MEASURED RISK FROM ZERO TO FIFTY, and anybody defending VaR has to account for that.

WHAT THE COUNTEREXAMPLE DOES AND DOES NOT SHOW. claim status VaR can penalise diversification PROVEN, by construction ES avoids it on this example PROVEN: 80.00 against 51.60 the cause is discreteness in the loss distribution TESTED: continuous losses are subadditive VaR always fails FALSE, and it usually does not THE FOURTH ROW MATTERS AS MUCH AS THE FIRST. VaR is subadditive on continuous losses, which is why it survived for so long in equity risk systems and failed in credit and insurance ones, where losses are lumpy by nature.

The second is the mechanism I got wrong and had to correct in public, because the correction is the more useful result. I attributed the risk-versus-weight gap to unequal tracking errors and correlations. Making every sleeve statistically identical left a gap of 3.08 percentage points, so that was not it. The condition is that a sleeve's risk share exceeds its weight share exactly when its weight exceeds the sum of squared weights, so concentration alone produces the gap. THAT MEANS A RISK BUDGET CONSTRAINS POSITION SIZING EVEN AMONG IDENTICAL ASSETS, which the volatility explanation would not have predicted.

The third is that the fundamental law is both stronger and weaker than it is usually presented. Stronger, because it holds to within 9.5 per cent across a twenty-fold range of breadth in simulation, and because an information coefficient of 0.05, which is almost no skill, delivers an information ratio of 0.50 on a hundred independent bets. Weaker, because "correlation collapses breadth to one" is quantitatively wrong: at a pairwise correlation of 0.99, a hundred bets behave like 35.7 and retain 58.5 per cent of the information ratio. The effective breadth can be computed by inverting the formula, and computing it is better than asserting a collapse.

EFFECTIVE BREADTH, COMPUTED RATHER THAN ASSERTED. correlation between bets effective breadth out of 100 0.00 104.3 0.50 69.8 0.90 40.5 0.99 35.7 A COLLAPSE TO ONE BET WOULD LEAVE AN EFFECTIVE BREADTH OF 1 AND A TENTH OF THE INFORMATION RATIO. neither happens, because each bet retains some independent variation and the manager is forecasting that too.

The fourth is the backtesting result, which should change how a risk report is read. A 99 per cent VaR model is accepted at 95 per cent confidence on anything from zero to six exceptions in 250 days, and a model whose true exception rate is double what it claims passes 76.37 per cent of the time. A CLEAN BACKTEST IS NOT EVIDENCE OF A CORRECT MODEL. It is the absence of evidence that the model is badly wrong, which is a much weaker statement, and the gap between those two readings is where a great deal of false comfort lives.

And the fifth, closing the module. Ten lectures have produced a framework that is exact wherever it can be checked and unreliable wherever it has to be estimated. The closed-form frontier, two-fund separation, the security market line, Euler's decomposition and Redington's conditions are all identities and all verified here to machine precision. Every input to them is noisy enough that a naive equal-weight rule is competitive. THE FRAMEWORK IS A WAY OF THINKING ABOUT A PORTFOLIO AND NOT A MACHINE FOR PRODUCING ONE, and the professional skill is knowing which of its outputs to trust and which to constrain, shrink, anchor or ignore.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The covariance algebra behind Euler's decomposition, and the observation that risk is convex in the weight, are from portfolio risk and return. The benchmark against which tracking error is measured is either the market portfolio of the capital market line and CAPM or the dynamic liability benchmark of liability hedging and dynamic liability benchmarks, and the choice between them is the reporting question asset-liability modelling raised. The four risk measures listed at the end of indifference curves and the optimum portfolio are finally compared here, and the fat tails that make VaR and expected shortfall diverge were measured in the empirical characteristics of asset prices. The independence assumption that breadth requires is the same one arbitrage pricing theory needs for its residuals, quantified in arbitrage pricing theory and factor construction. Quantiles, the binomial distribution and statistical power are from statistics.

Where this goes next. This completes the portfolio construction module. The risk measurement module takes the six risk types a mandate must monitor and treats each in turn, using the measures compared here; the performance measurement module attributes realised returns to the decisions this module budgeted for, and inherits both the benchmark problem of lecture 4 and the mean-versus-median trap of lecture 6. The portfolio management module implements the resulting mandate, where the transaction costs and liquidity that this module has treated as free become the binding constraints. The external environment module supplies the regulatory limits that lecture 7 applied.

Where the bank sits opposite. This is the lecture with the closest regulatory parallel of the ten. The move from value at risk to expected shortfall in the market risk framework was made for precisely the subadditivity reason demonstrated in step 4, and the discreteness diagnosis explains why the problem is most acute in credit risk, whose loss distribution is lumpy and heavily skewed. Euler's decomposition is how a bank allocates economic capital to business units, and the result that concentration alone makes a unit's risk share exceed its size share is the analytical basis for a concentration risk add-on. The backtesting arithmetic is the traffic-light test applied to internal models, and the finding that a year of data cannot detect a model wrong by a factor of two is why supervisory approval rests on process and documentation rather than on the exception count alone.
