The optimum portfolio: getting risk aversion wrong by a factor of two costs 42 basis points

The optimum sits where an indifference curve touches the frontier, at a target return of the minimum-variance return plus D over lambda times A. Because the utility is quadratic and flat at its peak, halving the risk aversion from 4 to 2 costs 0.4163 per cent of certainty equivalent and doubling it to 8 costs 0.1041. Set that against lecture 2, where one standard error on the equity expected return swung the optimal equity weight across nearly a whole portfolio.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The frontier says which portfolios are worth considering. It does not say which one to hold, because that depends on the investor. Preferences enter through mean-variance utility, expected return less half the risk aversion times the variance, and the set of equally-preferred combinations is an upward-opening parabola in volatility-return space.

Maximising that utility along the frontier gives a closed form: the optimal target return is the global minimum variance return plus D divided by lambda times A. Two consequences follow immediately. As risk aversion rises the optimum tends to the minimum-variance portfolio at 8.2947 per cent, and as it falls to zero the optimum runs off to infinity, which is why a risk-neutral investor has no interior solution. The tangency condition verifies numerically: the frontier's slope in variance space equals half the risk aversion to 5.5 parts in a trillion.

The result worth carrying concerns how much precision the preference deserves. An investor whose true risk aversion is 4 but who holds the portfolio built for 2 loses 0.4163 per cent of certainty equivalent; holding the one built for 8 loses 0.1041. The utility function is flat at its peak because its first derivative there is zero, so a factor-of-two error in the most subjective input in the exercise costs a few tens of basis points.

And the framework's own foundations are shakier than its precision suggests. Mean-variance utility is exact only if returns are normal, which lecture 10 of valuation refuted, or if utility is quadratic, which implies a bliss point: at a risk aversion of 4 the investor strictly prefers a certain 25 per cent return to a certain 30.

Feynman test Draw curves through all the combinations of risk and reward you would be equally happy with. They bend upward, because each extra unit of wobble needs more reward than the last. Slide the best curve down until it just kisses the menu of available portfolios, and that touching point is what to hold. The useful thing is that the curve is nearly flat where it touches, so being somewhat wrong about how much you dislike risk barely matters. Being wrong about what the investments will return matters enormously.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this lecture settles a priority question the previous two raised. Lecture 2 showed the frontier moves enormously with the expected return input. This one shows the choice of point along the frontier is worth tens of basis points. So the effort in a real allocation exercise should go into the inputs and not into eliciting the client's risk aversion to two decimal places, which is where most of the questionnaire time actually goes.

The second is that risk aversion has no natural units and therefore has to be elicited indirectly. Two routes work: the extra return demanded per unit of volatility, which at a risk aversion of 4 and 15 per cent volatility is 60 basis points, and the fraction of wealth someone would pay to avoid a fair gamble, which at the same risk aversion and a fifty-fifty bet of plus or minus 20 per cent is 8.000 per cent. The second is a question a trustee can actually answer.

The third is that the utility function's defects are not abstract. Quadratic utility satiates, so beyond a return of one over the risk aversion the investor prefers less to more, and it has increasing absolute risk aversion, so a richer investor holds fewer rands of risky assets. Neither is defensible, and the honest position is that mean-variance survives because it is tractable rather than because either of its justifications holds.

And a fourth. The framework is silent on whether variance is the right measure of risk at all. It penalises upside and downside identically, so two portfolios with the same mean and variance rank equally however differently they are skewed. That silence is why lecture 10 returns to semi-variance, value at risk and expected shortfall once the machinery is in place.

What the utility function quietly assumes

Mean-variance utility is EXACT in only two cases and neither is comfortable: (a) RETURNS ARE NORMALLY DISTRIBUTED, so the mean and variance are the whole distribution. Valuation lecture 10 showed they are not: a five sigma day is 1,511 times more likely under a t with six degrees of freedom than under a normal. (b) UTILITY IS QUADRATIC IN WEALTH, whatever the distribution. Case (b) has a defect that is easy to state and impossible to defend. Writing the utility over the return itself, U(m) = m - (lambda/2) m^2, which is maximised at m = 1/lambda: lambda the BLISS POINT 1.0 100.00% 2.0 50.00% 4.0 25.00% 8.0 12.50% AN INVESTOR WITH A RISK AVERSION OF 4 IS SATIATED AT A 25 PER CENT RETURN AND STRICTLY PREFERS IT TO ANYTHING HIGHER: a certain return of 20% gives utility 12.0000% a certain return of 25% gives utility 12.5000% <- the peak a certain return of 30% gives utility 12.0000% a certain return of 40% gives utility 8.0000% a certain return of 50% gives utility 0.0000% THAT IS NOT A SUBTLETY, IT IS AN ABSURDITY, and it is a property of the functional form rather than of the parameter. Quadratic utility also has INCREASING absolute risk aversion, meaning a richer investor holds FEWER rands of risky assets, which is the opposite of what is observed. SO THE HONEST POSITION IS THAT MEAN-VARIANCE IS USED BECAUSE IT IS TRACTABLE and because the errors are small in the region that matters, NOT because either of its two justifications holds. Both defects live far outside the range of returns any real portfolio contemplates, which is a defence of the practice and not of the theory. AND THE OMISSION THAT DOES BITE IN RANGE IS SKEWNESS. Mean-variance ignores the third moment entirely, so two portfolios with the same mean and variance rank equally however differently they are skewed. A portfolio that loses small amounts often and gains large amounts rarely ranks identically to its mirror image, and no investor is indifferent between those.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to carry is the flatness result, because it settles a question of professional priority rather than of theory. The utility is quadratic and its first derivative vanishes at the optimum, so the loss from a mis-specified risk aversion is second order in the error. Halving lambda from 4 to 2 costs 0.4163 per cent of certainty equivalent. Set that against lecture 2, where one standard error on the equity expected return moved the optimal equity weight from minus 0.4724 to plus 0.4876, and the ordering is unambiguous.

WHERE THE EFFORT SHOULD GO, AND WHERE IT USUALLY GOES. input error a practitioner might make what it costs the risk aversion a factor of two about 0.42% of CE the expected returns one standard error on one asset nearly the whole portfolio the volatilities a few points small, and estimable anyway the correlations the hardest to estimate material at the tails THE RISK QUESTIONNAIRE GETS AN HOUR OF THE CLIENT'S TIME AND THE CAPITAL MARKET ASSUMPTIONS GET A PARAGRAPH IN AN APPENDIX. The arithmetic says that is the wrong way round.

The second is the asymmetry within that flatness, which is easy to miss. Being too aggressive costs four times as much as being equally too cautious, 0.4163 against 0.1041, because the frontier steepens as the target return rises. An error towards more risk buys volatility faster than an equal error towards less risk gives it up. So where the parameter is genuinely uncertain, and it always is, the cheaper side to err on is the cautious one, and that is a result rather than a platitude.

The third is that the utility function's defects are worse than they are usually admitted to be, and the defence of the practice is not a defence of the theory. Quadratic utility satiates at one over the risk aversion, so at lambda equal to 4 the investor strictly prefers a certain 25 per cent to a certain 30, and it has increasing absolute risk aversion, so wealth reduces the rand amount held in risky assets. Both are indefensible as descriptions of anybody. The reason mean-variance survives is that both defects live far outside the range any real portfolio contemplates, and that is a statement about the range rather than about the function.

THE TWO JUSTIFICATIONS FOR MEAN-VARIANCE UTILITY, AND WHAT IS WRONG WITH EACH. justification status returns are normal REFUTED, valuation lecture 10: a five sigma day is 1,511 times more likely under a t(6) than under a normal utility is quadratic REFUTED by its own implications: a bliss point, and increasing absolute risk aversion NEITHER HOLDS, AND THE FRAMEWORK IS USED ANYWAY. The honest defence is tractability plus a small error in the relevant region, which is a good enough reason and should be stated as the reason rather than dressed as a derivation.

The fourth is the equity premium connection, which is the most uncomfortable thing in the lecture. The risk aversion that reconciles the consumption model with observed equity returns, twenty or above, implies an optimal equity weight of 7.41 per cent on this universe. Almost nobody holds that. So the pricing puzzle and the allocation puzzle are one puzzle viewed from two sides: no plausible risk aversion explains the returns equities delivered, and the risk aversion that would explain them implies an allocation nobody chooses. A resolution has to give on one side or the other, and most of the literature quietly gives on the utility function.

And the fifth. The framework decides which portfolios are worth considering and, given a preference, which one to hold. It says nothing about whether variance is the right measure of risk, and it cannot, because the question sits outside it. Mean-variance is the only common measure that is symmetric in upside and downside, and it is the only one with a closed-form optimum. THAT TRADE IS WHY IT SURVIVES: every asymmetric measure describes risk better and none can be optimised in a line of algebra, so each of them costs the two-fund separation result that makes the whole apparatus usable.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The closed-form frontier, the A, B, C and D scalars, the two-fund separation result and the expected-return sensitivity that this lecture weighs its own findings against are all from the opportunity set and the efficient frontier; the portfolio variance formula and the estimation asymmetry between means and volatilities are from portfolio risk and return. The refutation of normality that undermines the first justification for mean-variance utility is the empirical characteristics of asset prices, and the consumption model whose relative risk aversion this lecture equates with lambda is in asset pricing models and factors. Expected utility and the concavity of the utility function are from statistics.

Where this goes next. Lecture 4 adds a risk-free asset, at which point the tangency solved here changes character entirely: the frontier becomes a straight line, the indifference curve touches it at a point on the capital market line, and the risk aversion determines only how much cash to hold rather than which risky assets to own. Lectures 5 and 6 attack the input problem this lecture identifies as the binding one, first through factor structure and then through Black-Litterman, which is best understood as a way of not having to state expected returns at all. Lecture 7 applies the machinery to strategic asset allocation, lectures 8 and 9 replace the utility function with a liability, and lecture 10 returns to the risk measures named in the closing table.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank does not maximise a utility function, it optimises against a constraint set, which is the same problem with the preference replaced by a regulator. Where this lecture's investor picks a point on the frontier according to lambda, a bank picks one according to its capital ratio and its risk appetite, and the flatness result has a direct analogue: the choice of point within the appetite matters far less than whether the risk estimates feeding it are right. The four risk measures in the closing table map onto banking practice precisely, with expected shortfall having displaced value at risk in the market risk framework for exactly the tail reason given here. And the utility function's indifference to skewness is why a bank's credit portfolio, whose loss distribution is the most skewed object on its balance sheet, is never managed on a variance basis.
