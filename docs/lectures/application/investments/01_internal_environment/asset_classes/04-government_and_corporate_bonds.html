Government and corporate bonds: every feature is an option, and somebody paid for it

A bond looks like the simplest thing in the module and it carries the most machinery. Four different numbers all legitimately called "the yield". A capital structure in which one default event produces three unrelated losses. Index linkage that does not quite reach the maturity date. And a zoo of features (calls, puts, conversion, sinking funds) that reduces to a single rule: work out who holds the option, and the price follows.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A bond is a dated promise to pay coupons and repay principal, and the reason it fills a lecture is that almost every element of that sentence can be varied. The coupon can be fixed, floating or indexed to prices. The principal can be repaid at one date, over several, or at the issuer's choosing. The promise can rank ahead of or behind other promises. Each variation is an option or a swap held by one of the two parties, and each has a price.

Start with the yield, because the word is used for four different quantities. From a single three-point market, the three-year spot rate is 9.6972 per cent, the three-year par yield is 9.6039 per cent, and the gross redemption yield of the particular bond observed is 9.6007 per cent. Those differ by under ten basis points on a gently sloping curve and by 36 on a steeper one. The one-year forward rate applying to year three alone is 11.0123 per cent, which is 132 basis points above the spot rate and is not a competing measure of the same thing at all.

Move to credit and the arithmetic gets sharper. A company with R300m senior secured, R250m senior unsecured and R150m subordinated debt, defaulting at an enterprise value of R450m, delivers recoveries of 100, 60 and nil per cent. At a 3 per cent default probability the fair spreads are nil, 120 and 300 basis points. One issuer, one default, three different credits, and the subordinated spread is 2.5 times the senior unsecured one.

Then the features. An investor who accepts a 4 per cent coupon on a convertible instead of the 9 per cent the issuer's straight debt pays has spent R194.48 of present value on an equity option; at a 30 per cent volatility that option is worth R200.75, and the terms are fair at a volatility of 28.87 per cent. A bond callable at par in three years and trading at 108 has a yield to maturity of 6.6770 per cent and a yield to call of 5.0591 per cent, and quoting the first overstates the return by 162 basis points.

Feynman test Lending to a government for ten years has one number attached to it and four ways of writing that number down. Lending to a company adds the question of what happens if it fails, which depends entirely on who else lent it money and on what terms. Everything else in this lecture is somebody selling somebody an option and disguising the premium as a coupon.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this is the largest asset class most institutions hold and the one whose cash flows are most nearly contractual, which makes it the natural matching asset for a liability. A life fund with guaranteed annuity payments, a pension fund with pensions in payment, a bank with a liquidity buffer to hold: all three reach for bonds first, and all three need the yield conventions right because their liability discount rate is derived from them.

The second reason is that the four yields are not a pedantic distinction. A liability is a set of dated payments, so it must be valued off spot rates. A market quote for a bond is a gross redemption yield. Using the second where the first belongs introduces an error that is small on a flat curve and large on a steep one, and the direction is systematic rather than random. The steeper curve in step 4 widens the gap from 9.6 to 35.7 basis points on a three-year instrument, and the error grows with maturity.

The third reason is that seniority is where the framework's security dimension acquires teeth. Lecture 1 treated security as the identity of the issuer modified by the instrument, and the modification turns out to dominate: a subordinated claim on a strong company can be a worse credit than a senior claim on a weak one, and the waterfall arithmetic is how you find out. This is also where the investments and banking tracks meet, because loss given default by seniority is exactly what an LGD model estimates.

And a fourth reason that is about the source rather than the subject. The F105 summary's asset classes chapters cover money market instruments, private debt, private equity, asset-backed securities, structured products, infrastructure, commodities and funds. They do not have a section on conventional government and corporate bonds. The material is scattered through the indices chapter and the valuation chapter instead, which means an examination answer built only from the summary would be missing the most important asset class in the module. This lecture fills that in.

Step 4

The detail

The four yields, and which one your question wants

The source's valuation chapter lists them: spot rate or zero-coupon yield, gross redemption yield, coupon rate or par yield, and forward rate. They are all rates and they answer different questions. The spot rate for term n is the rate applying to a single payment at time n, which makes it the discount rate for a cash flow and therefore for a liability. The par yield is the coupon a new bond of that maturity would have to carry to be issued at 100. The gross redemption yield is one particular bond's internal rate of return, which mixes the whole curve in proportions set by that bond's coupon. The forward rate is the rate the curve implies for a future period, and it is not an alternative measure of today's n-year rate.

Three market observations, and everything else derived. 1-year zero-coupon bond yields 8.00% 2-year 9% annual coupon bond priced at 100.00 3-year 10% annual coupon bond priced at 101.00 BOOTSTRAPPING, shortest bond first, imposing no arbitrage: z1 = 8.0000% df1 = 0.925926 100 = 9 df1 + 109 df2 -> df2 = 0.840979 -> z2 = 9.0454% 101 = 10 df1 + 10 df2 + 110 df3 -> df3 = 0.757554 -> z3 = 9.6972% FORWARD RATES, implied by those spots and not forecast by anybody: f(0,1) = 8.0000% f(1,2) = (1+z2)^2/(1+z1) - 1 = 10.1010% f(2,3) = (1+z3)^3/(1+z2)^2 - 1 = 11.0123% PAR YIELD at three years, the coupon that prices the bond at exactly 100: sum of discount factors = 2.524459, 100 x df3 = 75.7554 c = (100 - 75.7554) / 2.524459 = 9.6039% GROSS REDEMPTION YIELD of the 3-year 10% bond at 101, solving for the single rate that discounts its own cash flows to its own price: 101 = 10/(1+y) + 10/(1+y)^2 + 110/(1+y)^3 -> y = 9.6007% FOUR NUMBERS, ONE MATURITY, ONE MARKET: 3-year spot 9.6972% discount a single 3-year cash flow at this 3-year par yield 9.6039% issue a new 3-year bond at this coupon this bond's GRY 9.6007% this bond's own IRR, coupon-dependent 1-yr forward at year 2 11.0123% a DIFFERENT object, 132 bp above the spot The first three span 9.6 basis points. On an upward-sloping curve the spot rate is the highest of the three same-maturity measures, always, because a coupon bond discounts its early payments at lower short rates. And the GRY sits below the par yield when the bond trades above par, and above it when the bond trades below par, which is why the second curve reverses their order.

Index-linked bonds, and the gap the lag leaves

An index-linked bond indexes the principal, and therefore the coupon paid on that principal, to a published price index. It converts a nominal promise into a real one, which is exactly what a fund with inflation-linked liabilities needs. The conversion is not quite complete, for a mechanical reason: a price index is published in arrears, so the indexation has to reference an index value some months old, and the last few months of inflation before redemption are therefore not hedged.

What an indexation lag leaves unhedged, as a one-off shortfall in real redemption value. That is a LEVEL SHIFT in the real redemption value, incurred once, and not a drag that compounds over the bond's life, which is a distinction candidates routinely get wrong. It becomes material only when inflation itself is high: at 25% a four-month lag costs 7.7%. The lag length is set in the bond's conditions of issue and differs by market, so read the instrument rather than assuming a convention. AND THE BREAKEVEN IS NOT AN INFLATION FORECAST. From the identity in lecture 1: conventional 10.50%, index-linked real 4.50% -> breakeven 5.7416% breakeven = expected inflation + inflation risk premium - ILB liquidity discount inflation risk premium ILB illiquidity implied expected inflation 0 bp 0 bp 5.7416% 40 bp 0 bp 5.3416% 40 bp 30 bp 5.6416% 60 bp 10 bp 5.2416% The two adjustments push in OPPOSITE directions. The risk premium is what a nominal investor charges for bearing inflation uncertainty, so it lifts breakeven above expected inflation. The illiquidity of the index-linked market depresses the real yield that market clears at, which lifts measured breakeven too, and neither is observable. So breakeven is a good market indicator and a poor forecast, and an investor buying index-linked bonds because breakeven looks high is implicitly asserting that both unobservable adjustments are small.

Seniority: one default, several unrelated losses

On default the enterprise is worth what it is worth, and the claims are paid in order until the money runs out. That single sentence is the entire content of seniority, and its consequence is that the phrase "the credit risk of the issuer" does not name a quantity. What exists is a default probability, which belongs to the issuer, and a loss given default, which belongs to the instrument.

R700m of debt: R300m senior secured, R250m senior unsecured, R150m subordinated. Recovery at four different enterprise values on default. EV R700m secured 100% unsecured 100% subordinated 100% EV R450m secured 100% unsecured 60% subordinated 0% EV R300m secured 100% unsecured 0% subordinated 0% EV R200m secured 67% unsecured 0% subordinated 0% Note the shape. The subordinated tranche goes from whole to nothing between R700m and R450m, a 36% fall in enterprise value, while the secured tranche is untouched until value falls below R300m, a 57% fall. Subordinated debt is not slightly worse than senior debt; its loss profile is a different function. FAIR SPREADS AT THE R450m OUTCOME, taking a 3.00% annual default probability and approximating the spread as PD x LGD (ignoring risk premia and the timing of default): senior secured LGD 0% spread 0 bp senior unsecured LGD 40% spread 120 bp subordinated LGD 100% spread 300 bp = 2.5x the unsecured THIS IS THE JOIN WITH THE BANKING TRACK. That approximation is the A-IRB expected loss identity, EL = PD x LGD x EAD, with EAD taken as par. The investor calls the output a credit spread and the bank calls it expected loss, and they are computing the same quantity from the same two inputs. What the bank adds on top is unexpected loss, which is what regulatory capital covers and what the bond investor bears without compensation beyond the risk premium the market happens to be paying.

Covenants: a promise with a computable trigger point

Covenants appear in the source's private debt section, and they belong to the public bond discussion too. A covenant converts a lender's hope into a measurable threshold, and its value is that it acts before default rather than distributing losses after it. The right way to read one is as headroom.

EBITDA R300m, interest R80m, so interest cover is 3.75x against a 3.00x covenant. breach occurs when EBITDA falls to 3.00 x R80m = R240m headroom: EBITDA can fall 20.0% before the covenant bites NOW ADD DEBT AT 10%, WHICH IS WHAT THE COVENANT EXISTS TO CONTROL: new debt interest cover EBITDA headroom breaches at EBITDA R 0m R 80.0m 3.75x 20.0% R240.0m R 100m R 90.0m 3.33x 10.0% R270.0m R 200m R100.0m 3.00x 0.0% R300.0m R 300m R110.0m 2.73x already breached R 400m R120.0m 2.50x already breached The covenant caps new borrowing at R200m with no negotiation required, because beyond that the ratio breaches on UNCHANGED earnings. SO AN INTEREST COVER COVENANT IS A LEVERAGE LIMIT WRITTEN IN THE LANGUAGE OF EARNINGS, and the two properties that follow are the reason lenders like it and borrowers resent it. It tightens automatically when earnings fall, which is precisely when the borrower most wants to borrow. And it is denominated in EBITDA, an accounting measure the borrower has some discretion over, which is why definitions of EBITDA in loan documentation run to pages and why "covenant EBITDA" and "reported EBITDA" are different numbers.

Convertibles: the coupon give-up is an option premium

A convertible bond is a bond the holder may exchange for a fixed number of shares. The source lists it among bond-like instruments without decomposing it, and the decomposition is the whole of it: a convertible is a straight bond plus a long call on the issuer's equity, and the investor pays for the call by accepting a coupon below what the issuer's straight debt would carry.

R1,000 face, 4.0% annual coupon, 5 years, conversion ratio 40 shares. The issuer's straight 5-year debt yields 9.0%. Share price R20.00. conversion price = face / ratio = R25.00 conversion value = ratio x S = R800.00 investment value = PV of the bond alone at 9.0% = 40 x 3.889651 + 1000 x 0.649931 = R805.52 So the bond floor (R805.52) exceeds the conversion value (R800.00), which is the normal case for a convertible issued out of the money. The convertible must trade above both. WHAT THE INVESTOR PAID, if it is issued at par: accepts 4.0% instead of 9.0%, giving up R50 a year for 5 years PV of the give-up = R50 x 3.889651 = R194.48 per share on conversion = R194.48 / 40 = R4.8621 WHAT THE OPTION IS WORTH, Black-Scholes, strike R25.00, 5 years, r 9%, vol 30%: ignoring dividends R7.0063 per share -> R280.25 for 40 shares with a 3% dividend yield R5.0188 per share -> R200.75 for 40 shares THE DIVIDEND CORRECTION REMOVES 28.4% OF THE OPTION'S VALUE, and it is not optional: a convertible holder receives coupons, not dividends, so five years of forgone dividends is a real cost of holding the option unexercised. Ignoring it overstates the embedded option by more than a quarter and would make almost any convertible look cheap. give-up paid R194.48 option received R200.75 difference R 6.27, marginally in the investor's favour BREAK-EVEN VOLATILITY: the give-up is exactly fair at sigma = 28.87%. volatility option value, 40 shares verdict against the R194.48 give-up 15.0% R116.07 issuer wins comfortably 20.0% R144.49 issuer wins 25.0% R172.79 issuer wins 28.87% R194.48 fair 30.0% R200.75 investor wins 40.0% R255.21 investor wins WHICH MEANS A CONVERTIBLE IS A VOLATILITY POSITION. The buyer is long volatility and the issuer is short it, whether or not the word appeared in the negotiation, and the break-even volatility is the single number that says who got the better of it. An issuer with a stable share price is selling something cheap; one whose shares are about to become volatile is not.

Embedded calls, and why a quoted yield needs a date attached

An 8% annual coupon bond, 8 years to maturity, callable at par in 3 years, trading at 108.00 because rates have fallen since issue. yield to maturity 6.6770% yield to call 5.0591% YIELD TO WORST 5.0591% the only figure an investor may safely assume difference 162 basis points ACROSS PRICES, showing where the call starts to bind: price 100.00 YTM 8.0000% YTC 8.0000% worst 8.0000% (they coincide) price 104.00 YTM 7.3218% YTC 6.4900% worst 6.4900% (call binds) price 108.00 YTM 6.6770% YTC 5.0591% worst 5.0591% (call binds) price 112.00 YTM 6.0628% YTC 3.7004% worst 3.7004% (call binds) At par the two yields coincide. Above par the call always binds, because the issuer can retire at 100 debt the market values above 100. Below par the call is worthless to the issuer and yield to maturity governs. The source notes that the FTSE gilt indices handle optional redemption stocks by choosing the date that gives the lower curve, which is the same yield-to-worst discipline applied at index level. It is worth seeing that an index construction rule and a dealing-desk convention are answering the same question.

The South African index families, and two construction details that cost money

The source sets out the local bond index structure. The ALBI holds the twenty largest fixed-interest bonds by liquidity and market capitalisation, with sub-indices at one to three, three to seven, seven to twelve and over twelve years, weights revised monthly and constituents quarterly, and it excludes bonds with under a year to run. Its sub-families are GOVI for the ten largest government bonds and OTHI for the rest. The CILI does the same job for inflation-linked bonds, split into IGOV for government, ISOE for state-owned enterprises and ICORP for corporates. The credit indices cover listed corporate bonds, reviewed quarterly, with a composite that is a weighted average of a fixed and a floating sub-index.

Two details in that description have consequences. The ALBI is a clean price index, where the FTSE gilt indices the source also describes use dirty prices with accrued interest included. A clean-price index shows a sawtooth as accrual builds and drops away at each coupon date, so it is not a total return series and cannot be used as one without adding income separately. And the exclusion of bonds with under a year to run means an index tracker is a forced seller of every holding twelve months before it matures, on a schedule every other tracker shares. That is a predictable flow, and predictable flows are expensive for the people who have to execute them.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

One rule collapses the whole zoo of bond types

Bond markets generate names faster than concepts: callable, puttable, convertible, exchangeable, sinking fund, extendible, step-up, index-linked, floating rate, perpetual with a reset. A candidate who learns the names learns a list. The rule underneath is that each feature is either an option or a swap, and if it is an option then exactly one side holds it, and that side pays.

feature who holds it what it is how it is paid for call at a set price ISSUER short call for investor receives a wider the investor spread sinking fund ISSUER short call on investor receives a wider chosen bonds spread, and bears selection coupon deferral ISSUER short option investor receives a wider on hybrid capital spread put at a set price INVESTOR long put investor accepts a lower coupon conversion into INVESTOR long call on investor accepts a lower equity equity coupon (R194.48 of PV here) covenant trigger LENDER a control right priced into the whole rather than an package; standalone value is option on price hard to separate floating coupon NEITHER a swap of fixed no premium either way; it for floating changes WHICH risk you hold index linkage NEITHER a swap of nominal no premium either way, but for real the LAG is a real shortfall THE TEST THAT FOLLOWS. When you meet an unfamiliar bond, ask three questions in order. Who may change the cash flows? When will they want to? And is the compensation in the coupon or in the price? Three answers and the instrument is priced in principle, whether or not you can value the option precisely. AND THE ASYMMETRY WORTH REMEMBERING. An option held by the issuer is exercised when it suits the issuer, which is systematically when it hurts the investor: calls are exercised after rates fall, deferrals are used when the issuer is weak. So issuer-held options are worth more than a naive valuation suggests, because the exercise is correlated with the investor's other losses rather than independent of them.

What the source's omission costs, and what a good answer supplies instead

The summary's asset classes chapters go straight from money market instruments to private debt, so conventional bonds appear only obliquely: as index constituents in chapter 5, as pricing formulae in chapter 9, and as "alternative bond-like instruments" in a portfolio management list that names corporate bonds, agency bonds, convertibles, distressed private debt, insurance-linked securities, catastrophe bonds, interest rate and inflation swaps, credit default swaps, asset-backed securities and green bonds in a single breath. That list is a good prompt and a poor treatment, because it puts a government agency bond and a catastrophe bond in the same sentence when they share nothing but a coupon.

An answer built only on the summary would therefore be able to name the South African index families and price a bond given a curve, and would have nothing to say about seniority, covenants, callability or the conversion decision. Those are the parts of the asset class an investor actually has to form a view on, because the curve is given to everybody and the structure is not.

The summary is also silent on the one thing that most changes a bond's risk in practice, which is that the same issuer's paper can rank in three places at once. The framework's security dimension asks who owes you the money, and for a corporate bond the honest answer is that the issuer owes it and the queue decides whether that matters.

That is exactly what makes bonds the matching asset for a dated liability, and it is why the whole apparatus of duration and cash flow matching lives in this asset class and nowhere else in the module. The row that most often gets a thin answer is SPREAD. For a government bond the income is contractually fixed, so all of the variability is capital and all of the capital variability is the yield curve. For a corporate bond there is a second source, the credit spread itself, and for sub-investment-grade paper spread volatility can dominate rate volatility entirely. Saying "bonds are less volatile than equities" without separating those two components is the kind of answer that is right for the wrong reason.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The eight dimensions and the argument about which of them describe a pairing come from the asset class framework, and the breakeven inflation identity and the tax figures in step 5 are taken straight from it. The quotation conversions and the discount-against-yield distinction are from money market instruments, which is the same arithmetic under a year. Bond pricing mechanics, duration, convexity and the accrued interest conventions are in financial instruments; the Black-Scholes valuation of the embedded conversion option is from derivatives.

Where this goes next. Lecture 5 takes the same credit questions to instruments with no market price, where the covenant package does the work a rating and a bid-offer spread do here. Lecture 10 returns to seniority in its engineered form, where tranching manufactures the waterfall rather than inheriting it from a company's history. The curve construction in step 4 is developed properly in valuation, and the index families in indices.

Where the same arithmetic appears with a regulator attached. The PD times LGD identity in step 4 is the expected loss calculation at the centre of credit risk, and the LGD by seniority worked example is what an LGD model is built to estimate from recovery data. A bank holding government bonds in its buffer meets them again under liquidity risk, where the haircut applied depends on the same index-eligibility and issue-size questions raised here. Interest rate risk on a held-to-maturity bond book is interest rate risk in the banking book, which is the same duration arithmetic pointed at a balance sheet rather than a portfolio.

A note on the source, because it matters here more than elsewhere. The F105 summary's asset classes chapters (6 and 7) have no section on conventional government or corporate bonds. Its treatment of the class is confined to the index families in chapter 5, the pricing formulae and yield taxonomy in chapter 9, and a single list of bond-like instruments in the portfolio management chapter. This lecture takes the taxonomy, the index construction details and the four-yield vocabulary from those places and supplies seniority, covenants, callability and the convertible decomposition from standard external material, since the summary does not cover them.

Numbers used here, and where they came from. The four-yield taxonomy, the ALBI, GOVI, OTHI, CILI, IGOV, ISOE and ICORP structures with their term buckets and review frequencies, the clean-price convention, the one-year exclusion rule and the FTSE gilt treatment of optional redemption stocks are all from the F105 summary chapters 5 and 9; index methodologies change, so treat them as the source's statement rather than as current JSE or FTSE methodology.
