Listed equity: a residual claim, measured four incompatible ways

Equity is the last claim in the queue, which makes its risk a leveraged version of the business's own. Two things follow that a description of the asset class usually skips. The measured return depends entirely on how the index was built, and four defensible schemes give four answers spanning 45 percentage points on the same two shares. And the sensitivity of earnings to sales is a product of two leverage ratios, one of which the source defines in a way that is not an elasticity at all.

Step 1

The 30-second version

An ordinary share is a residual claim: the holder receives what is left after every other claimant has been paid, in perpetuity, with no promise attached. That single structural fact generates the whole of the asset class. There is no contractual cash flow, so valuation is a forecast rather than a discounting exercise. There is no maturity, so the framework's term dimension has no answer. And the residual sits behind fixed costs and interest, so the equity holder's earnings move by a multiple of the business's sales.

Take the leverage first, because it is the part that can be computed exactly. With sales of 1,000, variable costs of 600, fixed costs of 200 and interest of 80, operating leverage is 2.0000 and the degree of financial leverage is 1.6667, so total leverage is 3.3333. A 10 per cent fall in sales cuts profit before tax by 33.3 per cent, and a 30 per cent fall eliminates it entirely. No measure of share price volatility shows that directly.

Then the measurement. Two shares, one doubling and one halving, produce an index return of plus 25 per cent unweighted, zero geometric, minus 20 per cent price-weighted and plus 10 per cent market capitalisation weighted. Only the last corresponds to a portfolio anybody could have held. The source calls the first two unsuitable and the arithmetic shows why: a geometric index has a structural downward bias that grows with dispersion, and a price-weighted index gives influence according to how often a company has split its stock.

And the valuation. Rearranging the Gordon growth model gives an implied return of dividend yield plus growth, which is a fact plus a forecast. At a 3 per cent yield the implied return runs from 6 to 12 per cent as the growth assumption moves from 3 to 9 per cent, so an implied equity risk premium is mostly an assumption about g wearing a market observation's clothes.

Feynman test Buying a share means agreeing to be paid last, forever, out of whatever is left. Everything difficult about the asset class comes from those three words. There is nothing to discount that anyone promised you, nothing to redeem, and a claim that is geared to the business twice over: once by its fixed costs and again by its debt.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that equity is where the framework's spread dimension does most of its work and its term dimension collapses entirely. A bond tells you when you get your money back; a share never does, so the holding period is chosen by the investor and the asset has no opinion. That makes equity a poor matching asset for a dated liability and an excellent one for an open-ended real liability, which is exactly the split between an annuity book and a with-profits fund.

The second reason is index construction, which sounds like a technicality until you notice that every performance measurement in the module is relative to one. If the benchmark is built badly, tracking it faithfully produces a portfolio nobody wanted. The source is right that unweighted and geometric indices are unsuitable, and it does not say why; the arithmetic in step 4 says why in a way that transfers to any index you are asked to assess.

The third reason is that the leverage cascade is the mechanism connecting a company's operations to its share price, and it is computable from published accounts. A candidate asked why one share is riskier than another can say "higher beta", which is a restatement, or can decompose it into operating leverage from the cost structure and financial leverage from the balance sheet, which is an explanation. The second answer also tells you what would change it.

And a fourth reason, which is about reading a source critically. The summary's list of index families contains an error of direction in its description of value indices, and two constituent counts that are out of date. None of them is important on its own, and finding them is a demonstration of the habit that matters most in this module: every ratio in the list has price in it, so the direction of each is determined by arithmetic rather than by memory.

Step 4

The detail

What an ordinary share is, and the two hybrids beside it

An ordinary share confers a residual claim on profits and on assets in a winding up, plus a vote. It has no maturity and no promise. Everything that distinguishes equity from debt in the framework follows from those two absences: the term dimension has no contractual answer, and the security dimension cannot be answered by reference to a queue position because the share is the bottom of the queue.

A preference share sits between. It carries a stated dividend, usually cumulative, ranking ahead of the ordinary dividend and behind all debt, generally without a vote. In legal form it is equity, so its dividend is not tax deductible for the company and is paid out of after-tax profits. In economic substance it is closer to perpetual subordinated debt. That mismatch between form and substance is the whole reason the instrument exists: it lets a company raise something debt-like without the ratios that debt would move, and it lets a corporate investor receive a dividend rather than interest, which lecture 1's tax arithmetic shows is worth 2.16 percentage points to a South African company on an 8 per cent return.

A convertible was treated in lecture 4 from the bond side. Seen from here it is the reverse trade: an equity investor who wants a floor buys the bond and the option rather than the share.

The leverage cascade, and a definition worth correcting

The source's accounting ratio list gives operating leverage as contribution over EBIT and financial leverage as interest over EBIT, the reciprocal of interest cover. The first is exactly the elasticity of EBIT to sales. The second is a level measure of indebtedness and is not the elasticity of anything, and using it as one gives an answer wrong by a factor of four on the worked accounts.

Index construction: four schemes, four answers

The source sets out four families and dismisses two of them. Unweighted arithmetic averages the price relatives; geometric takes their geometric mean; price weighting sums the prices, which is what the Dow Jones and Nikkei do; market capitalisation weighting is what almost everything else does. The dismissal is right and the reasons are worth having in a form you can reproduce.

Chain-linking: keeping the index continuous through a corporate action

The source states the rule precisely: the index should change only because of price, rights issues, buybacks, new issues, mergers and changes of constituent. Read carefully that is a list of the events which must not move the index, handled by adjusting a divisor.

Three constituents, market-cap weighted, index standing at 1,000.0. share price shares (m) market cap (Rm) A R50.00 100.0 5,000.0 B R80.00 50.0 4,000.0 C R20.00 250.0 5,000.0 total 14,000.0 divisor = 14,000.0 / 1,000.0 = 14.0000 SHARE A NOW MAKES A 1-FOR-4 RIGHTS ISSUE AT R30, against a market price of R50. theoretical ex-rights price = (100 x 50.00 + 25 x 30.00) / 125 = R46.0000 shares in issue 100m -> 125m A's market cap R5,000m -> R5,750m, up by the R750m of new cash subscribed total market cap R14,000m -> R14,750m WITHOUT ADJUSTMENT the index reads 14,750.0 / 14.0000 = 1,053.57, a +5.36% JUMP caused by shareholders paying in cash, which is not a market movement. THE DIVISOR IS THEREFORE RESET: 14,750.0 / 1,000.0 = 14.7500 and the index stays at 1,000.0. The divisor moves +5.36%; the index moves 0.00%. The same reset happens on a new issue, a buyback, a merger and a constituent change. The test is the one the source states: the index must move only because prices moved. NOTE WHAT THIS MEANS FOR A TRACKER. Every divisor change is an event at which the tracking portfolio's weights no longer match the index, so the tracker must trade. The index is costless to rebalance and the portfolio is not, and the gap between them is where tracking error comes from even in a perfectly run fund.

Valuation: the implied return is a fact plus a forecast

The source's fundamental analysis method is a two-step: project earnings from global, local, industry and firm factors, then compare with the market by an intrinsic value calculation using a dividend discount model, or by a relative value comparison of accounting ratios against competitors. The second step is where the arithmetic bites.

Note also that this interacts with lecture 1's tax row: a buyback delivers the same cash as a capital gain rather than as income, which for a taxable individual is worth 2.16 percentage points on an 8 per cent return and for an exempt fund is worth nothing.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The benchmark is a portfolio decision disguised as a measurement decision

An index looks like an instrument for observing the market. It is not: it is a rule for holding one, and every rule embeds a view. Market capitalisation weighting buys more of whatever has risen, which is a momentum tilt nobody chose. Equal weighting rebalances into whatever has fallen, which is a value and small-cap tilt nobody chose either. Price weighting is not a view at all, merely an accident of share splits, which is why the source is right to reject it.

scheme the implicit portfolio rule the tilt it embeds market cap hold every share in proportion to momentum and size: the its size; never trade unless the weight rises with the price, index changes constituents so winners are bought equal weight hold the same rand amount of each value and small cap, and it share; rebalance periodically requires constant trading price weighted hold ONE SHARE of each company no economic content at all; a split changes the weight fundamental weight on sales, book, dividends an explicit value tilt, which (the source's or earnings rather than price at least is chosen openly growth and value index families) THE ONLY SCHEME THAT NEEDS NO TRADING is market cap weighting, because the weights move with the prices automatically. Every other rule has to be rebalanced, which costs money that the index does not pay and the tracking portfolio does. THAT IS THE HIDDEN ARGUMENT FOR CAP WEIGHTING, and it is a better one than any claim about efficiency. A cap-weighted index is the unique weighting that a buy-and-hold investor can replicate at zero turnover, so it is the only one whose published return is achievable in principle by everybody at once.

The consequence for the growth and value indices in the source's list is that they are not measurements of a market segment but rules for holding a tilted portfolio, and the tilt has to be rebalanced as prices move. Their published returns are therefore gross of a turnover cost the cap-weighted index does not incur, and comparing the two on published returns alone flatters the tilted one.

What the source is missing, and what an answer should supply

As with bonds, the summary's asset classes chapters have no section on listed equity. What exists is an index list in chapter 5 and a fundamental analysis method in chapter 9, and those are both about how to measure or value equity rather than what it is. Three things a good answer has to supply from elsewhere.

The first is the residual claim itself, which is where every property of the asset class comes from and which the source never states. The second is the leverage cascade, which the source gives the ingredients for and does not assemble, and which is the bridge between a company's operations and its share's risk. The third is the distinction between an index as a measurement and an index as a portfolio rule, which matters because the entire performance measurement module compares portfolios to benchmarks and never asks whether the benchmark was holdable.

One further omission worth naming, because it recurs. The source's market behaviour section covers sentiment measures, discounts to net asset value, share volumes, initial public offering activity and cross-sectional return patterns, all of which describe how equity prices move away from fundamentals. Nothing in the asset class treatment connects that to the valuation method in chapter 9, which assumes prices return to intrinsic value. Both can be true, and an answer that holds them together (fundamental value as an anchor, sentiment as the deviation, and no statement about how quickly the deviation closes) is more honest than one that picks a side.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The eight dimensions, the tax arithmetic for dividends against interest against capital gains, and the currency hedging threshold are all from the asset class framework. The convertible seen from the bond side, and the waterfall that puts equity at the bottom of it, are in government and corporate bonds. The accounting ratios and the cost of equity that the implied return is estimating are developed in financial analysis and cost of capital, and the capital structure decision behind the financial leverage figure is in capital structure.

Where this goes next. Lecture 7 removes the listing and asks what changes: the residual claim is identical and the price, the exit and the governance are not. Lectures 12 and 13 cover the vehicles through which most investors actually hold equity. The index construction arithmetic here is the foundation for indices, and the argument that a benchmark is a portfolio rule rather than a measurement is what performance measurement has to confront before any attribution can mean anything.

Where the same asset appears with different questions attached. Equity valuation as a discipline, including the dividend discount model developed properly and the relative valuation the source describes, is valuation. How much of it to hold, and the correlation questions the framework cannot express, are portfolio construction. For a bank, its own equity is the loss-absorbing layer rather than an investment, which is capital management, and the leverage cascade in step 4 is a simplified version of what a stress test does to a borrower's income statement in credit risk.
