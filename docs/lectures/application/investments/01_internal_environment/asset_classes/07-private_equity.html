Private equity: the same residual claim, wrapped in a structure that decides who gets paid

Delisting a share changes nothing about the claim and everything about the arrangement around it. Capital is committed rather than invested, so the investor owes money it has not paid. Returns are reported before they are realised, so the first years show a loss the fund has not made. And the manager's share is not 20 per cent of profits until a catch-up band in which its marginal share is 100 per cent. Every one of those is arithmetic, and none of it is in the source.

Asset classes 7 of 14 Reading time, 50 minutes Sources: F105 summary chapters 6 and 7; Income Tax Act s12J and its 2021 sunset
Step 1

The 30-second version

The source gives four kinds of private equity. Venture capital funds start-ups with no profits and close investor involvement. Development capital funds expansion at mature firms. Restructuring capital recapitalises distressed private companies, where a listed company would simply have a rights issue. Leveraged buyouts acquire companies mostly with debt, split into management buy-outs where the existing team buys the owners out and buy-ins where an external team does. The underlying claim in all four is the ordinary share of lecture 6. What differs is everything around it.

The structure begins with the seven-stage fund cycle the source sets out, running eight to twelve years from initial fund raising through two closings, an investment period, realisations, the handling of undrawn commitments, and finally the manager's remuneration. Three consequences follow that the source states in words and never quantifies.

The first is the J-curve. On the fund modelled in step 4, the reported internal rate of return is minus 9.091 per cent after one year and minus 5.736 per cent after two, turning positive only in year three and settling at 13.675 per cent. Nothing went wrong. Fees are drawn from the first day and value is realised from the fifth, so an investor judging a fund on its year-two number is measuring the fee schedule.

The second is the waterfall. On a fund contributing R100 at outset and distributing once at year ten, the manager receives nothing below a 2.1589 times multiple, then receives every rand of the next R28.97 through the catch-up, and only afterwards settles at 20 per cent of profits. Its marginal share in that band is 100 per cent, which makes exit timing near the hurdle the sharpest incentive in the structure.

The third is the undrawn commitment. To hold R100m of private equity an investor must commit about R16.10m a year and carry roughly R47.5m of undrawn obligations, 0.48 times the value held. That is a binding call on cash with little notice, in an asset class where the calls accelerate exactly when everything else has fallen.

Feynman test You promise a manager money it has not asked for yet, it spends years buying companies you cannot see priced, it charges you for the privilege from day one, and the number it reports in the meantime is negative for reasons that have nothing to do with how the companies are doing. Then at the end there is a formula that decides how the winnings are split, and in one narrow band of that formula the manager takes all of them.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that a commitment is not an investment, and the framework has no dimension for the difference. An investor who has committed R100m and drawn R40m holds a R40m asset and a R60m obligation, and the obligation has no market, no price and a call notice measured in days. Every other asset in this module is something you own; this is partly something you owe.

The second reason is that private equity is measured with statistics that the manager largely controls. The valuation of unrealised holdings is an estimate the manager makes. The timing of capital calls is the manager's choice and moves the reported internal rate of return without changing the money returned. Which funds appear in a performance database depends on whether they are still reporting. Step 5 lists them together, and the list is longer than most investors realise.

The third reason is that the fee structure is genuinely complicated rather than merely expensive, and understanding the catch-up is what separates an answer that says "two and twenty" from one that can compute the manager's share at a given outcome. The source describes the hurdle in a garbled phrase, which is worth correcting rather than reproducing.

And a fourth reason, specific to a South African candidate. The source's note that private equity funds carry no tax under "SJ12" refers to the section 12J venture capital company regime, which allowed investors a deduction for subscriptions to approved venture capital companies. That regime sunset on 30 June 2021 and was not extended. An answer relying on it is describing a rule that no longer admits new investment.

Step 3

The mental model

Seven stages, one J-shaped return series, and a waterfall with a band nobody expects.

Step 4

The detail

The four types, and what actually differs between them

Venture capital backs start-ups with no profits, and the source's note that investors are closely involved is the defining feature rather than a detail: with no earnings to analyse, the investor's contribution is governance and contacts. Development capital funds expansion at an established firm, so there is a trading history and the underwriting resembles ordinary equity analysis. Restructuring capital recapitalises a distressed private company, and the source makes the right comparison: a listed company in the same position would do a rights issue, so private restructuring capital exists because the public route is closed.

Leveraged buyouts acquire a company using mostly debt, in a management buy-out where the existing team buys out the owners or a management buy-in where an external team does. The source's structural note is the important one: the acquisition debt is non-recourse to the fund, sitting at the portfolio company. So a failed deal loses the equity cheque and nothing more, while a successful one delivers a levered return. The fund's outcome distribution is therefore right-skewed even though every individual deal is geared, which is why a portfolio of ten buyouts can lose money on three and still return a multiple.

TO HOLD R100m OF PRIVATE EQUITY NAV an investor must commit R16.10m A YEAR and will carry about R47.5m of UNDRAWN COMMITMENTS at any moment that is 0.48 times the NAV held The undrawn commitment is a legally binding obligation to produce cash on short notice, with no market, no price and, as the source itself says at stage three, "little notice to hand over cash". The framework has no dimension for it, because every other asset in this module is something you own and this is partly something you owe. AND THE TRAP IS PROCYCLICAL. Managers call capital faster when they see opportunities, which is after markets have fallen. Distributions dry up at the same moment, because exits close when buyers disappear. So the undrawn commitment converts into a cash demand exactly when the investor's liquid assets are worth least and its private equity holding is the largest share of a shrunken portfolio it has ever been. That combination is the denominator problem, and it has ended real programmes rather than merely embarrassed them.

A listed company that fails stays in the index until it is delisted, and the index records the loss. A private fund that fails stops reporting to the database, and the database records nothing. Reporting is voluntary in the first place, so the survivorship filter operates twice: once on which funds ever reported, and again on which continue to. The same argument applies to the "low correlation" claim on the same line. A correlation computed on smoothed, self-reported, survivor-only valuations is not a measurement of anything, and lecture 5's smoothing arithmetic shows the volatility understatement is about a factor of two before the survivorship filter is even considered.

Which is why the multiple is the more honest of the two statistics for a fund with a credit facility, and why the two together are the minimum an investor should accept.

What the source gets right, and the two things it gets wrong

The seven-stage cycle is a genuinely good piece of structure and it is not something a candidate would derive unaided. Its observation that the acquisition debt is non-recourse to the fund is the key to why buyout returns are right-skewed at the fund level. Its four security features (high failure rates, close investor relations, less regulation, management incentives) are the right four, and the pairing of "high expected returns" with "survivorship bias" on the same line shows the author knew the second undermined the first even if the arithmetic was not done.

Two things need correcting. The hurdle is not "fund return plus 8 per cent"; it is an 8 per cent preferred return on contributed capital, paid to the limited partners before the manager takes anything, and the catch-up that follows is the structure's most consequential feature. And the section 12J venture capital company deduction sunset on 30 June 2021, so a South African answer that relies on it is describing a closed regime. Investors who subscribed before the sunset and hold for the required period retain their deduction; nobody new can obtain one.

One omission is worth naming because it is the whole of the investor's experience. The source describes the fund's lifecycle and never describes the J-curve, so a candidate reading only the summary would know that fees start at the first closing and that proceeds come later, without ever connecting those two facts into the shape of the reported return. That connection is the single most useful thing to take from this lecture.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The residual claim, the leverage cascade and the argument that a share's risk is the business's risk geared twice are from listed equity, and the claim itself is identical here. The appraisal smoothing arithmetic that halves reported volatility, and the argument that private markets sell illiquidity rather than tolerate it, are from private debt, which reaches the same conclusion about reported statistics by a different route. The eight dimensions and the tax treatment of gains against income are from the asset class framework. Internal rates of return, money multiples and the reinvestment assumption are developed in capital budgeting methods.

Where this goes next. Lecture 8 meets infrastructure from the equity side, where the fund structure here is combined with the long-lived asset of lecture 5. Lectures 12 and 13 cover the other pooled vehicles, and lecture 13's hedge fund fee structures are a variation on the waterfall computed here. The commitment pacing arithmetic in step 4 is a liquidity management problem that belongs to portfolio management, and the valuation of unrealised holdings to valuation. The IRR-against-multiple argument is the money-weighted against time-weighted return distinction that performance measurement has to settle before any private markets attribution can mean anything.

Where the same structures appear elsewhere. A bank lending into a buyout is writing the non-recourse acquisition debt described here, and prices it through the seniority and covenant machinery of credit risk. The undrawn commitment is the mirror image of the undrawn facility in money market instruments: there the investor was writing the option, here the investor has bought an obligation. A pension fund's holding of private equity is governed in South Africa by Regulation 28 limits, which sit alongside the prudential material in South African regulation.

Numbers used here, and corrections made. The four private equity types, their features, the seven-stage cycle, the eight-to-twelve year life, the two-to-three and three-to-five year investment and holding periods, the non-recourse nature of acquisition debt, and the pairing of high expected returns with survivorship bias are all from the F105 summary chapters 6 and 7. Two items are corrected: the hurdle is an 8 per cent preferred return rather than "fund return plus 8 per cent", and the section 12J venture capital company deduction sunset on 30 June 2021 and is closed to new investment, which should be checked against SARS's current position before use. Everything else is invented for illustration: the fund's call, investment, valuation and realisation schedule, the 20 per cent portfolio growth rate, the 2 per cent fee and 20 per cent carry, the fund-of-funds terms, the failure rates and survivor returns, and the four funds compared on IRR against multiple. The arithmetic on all of them is exact and none is a market observation.
