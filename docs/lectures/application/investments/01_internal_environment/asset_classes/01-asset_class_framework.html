The asset class framework: eight questions, and three of them are not about the asset

Security, yield, spread, term, expenses, marketability, tax and currency. Applied mechanically the framework is a checklist, and a checklist is worth a few marks. Applied properly it does something harder: it shows that tax, marketability and currency describe a pairing of asset and investor rather than the asset itself, so the same holding can be the best and the worst choice in the same market depending on who owns it and for how long.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Any investment can be described along eight dimensions, and the discipline of always using the same eight is what stops an answer about infrastructure being a different kind of answer from one about commercial paper. The dimensions are security, meaning who owes you the money and how likely they are to pay; yield, meaning the size and form of the return; spread, meaning the variability of that return; term; expenses; marketability; tax; and currency.

Five of the eight are properties of the asset. A treasury bill has the sovereign as issuer whoever holds it. Three of the eight are not. Tax depends on the holder: an 8 per cent return paid as interest is worth 4.40 per cent to a South African individual at the top marginal rate, 5.84 per cent to a company and the full 8.00 per cent to a retirement fund. Marketability depends on the holding period: a 10 per cent round trip on direct property costs 0.42 per cent a year if held for twenty-five years and 10.00 per cent if held for one. Currency depends on where the liabilities are, and whether hedging reduces risk turns on a correlation with an exact threshold rather than on a preference.

The consequence is that the ordering of assets is not stable across investors. Of interest, dividends and capital gain, the individual prefers capital gain, the company prefers the dividend, and the exempt fund is indifferent. Nothing about the assets changed. The ranking reversed because the taxpayer did.

So the framework is not a scoring system that produces a winner. It is a way of making the comparison complete, which then hands the actual decision to the liability being backed. Portfolio construction, four modules later, is where the eight answers become a holding; this lecture is where they become answerable at all.

Feynman test Ask the same eight questions of a treasury bill and a shopping centre and you get sixteen answers you can lay side by side. Five of those questions are about the thing you are buying. Three are about you. That is why two sensible investors, looking at the same asset on the same day at the same price, can be correct in disagreeing about it.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is examination mechanics, and it is the least interesting but the most immediately useful. A question asking you to discuss the suitability of an asset class carries marks distributed across the dimensions, and candidates lose them by writing three paragraphs about security and yield and forgetting that tax and expenses are on the mark scheme too. The framework is a memory device with eight slots. Fill all eight, briefly, before enriching any of them.

The second reason is that the framework is what makes the remaining thirteen lectures in this module comparable rather than merely sequential. Every asset class from here on is presented in the same eight-part order, which means the interesting content is the differences between the answers rather than the answers themselves. Private debt and private equity share five of eight answers almost exactly; the two where they diverge are the whole distinction. You cannot see that without a common structure.

The third reason is the one worth actually learning. Three of the dimensions are relational, and treating them as properties of the asset produces advice that is wrong rather than merely incomplete. Recommending a listed equity portfolio to a taxable individual on the grounds that it is more marketable than property, when the individual has a thirty-year horizon and no intention of trading, prices a benefit the client will never consume. The marketability premium is real and it is paid for; whether it is worth paying for is a question about the client.

And a fourth reason, which is about honesty in the modelling that follows. The framework has no dimension for the liability. It also has no dimension for the correlation of an asset with everything else already held, which is the single quantity modern portfolio theory says matters most. Both omissions are deliberate, because the framework is a description device rather than an optimiser, but a candidate who does not know they are omissions will use the framework for a job it cannot do.

Step 4

The detail

Security: who owes you the money, and what stands behind the promise

Security is the likelihood of receiving the promised payments, and it decomposes into the identity of the issuer, the risk attaching to the instrument, and the regulation surrounding the issue. Those three are distinct and a good answer keeps them distinct. The issuer sets the ceiling: a sovereign borrowing in its own currency and a start-up borrowing from a venture lender are not the same credit whatever the documentation says. The instrument then modifies the issuer's credit, upwards through seniority, collateral and covenants, or downwards through subordination. Regulation is the third layer, and it works in both directions: a regulated collective scheme carries protections a private placement does not, while a bank deposit sits inside a resolution regime that can convert it.

Covenants are worth naming individually because they are the mechanism by which a lender to a private borrower recovers some of the protection that a rating and a public market would otherwise provide. The source lists interest and capital cover limits, liquidity ratio limits, restrictions on dividends, limits on issuing equal or higher-ranking debt, minimum securitised asset values, and constraints on varying the collateral. Each is a trigger that gives the lender a seat at the table before default rather than after it, which is the entire point.

Yield: how much, and in what form, and against what

Yield asks the size of the return, and it splits twice. The first split is real against nominal. The relationship is multiplicative rather than additive, and the additive shortcut always overstates the real return by an amount equal to the inflation rate times the real return.

The second split is income against capital gain, and it is the one that connects yield to tax. A holding that delivers its return as coupons or rent produces cash the investor can spend and the fiscus can tax now. A holding that delivers the same return as capital appreciation produces neither until it is sold. For a tax-exempt fund with benefits to pay, the first is more useful. For a taxable individual with no immediate need for cash, the second is worth measurably more, and step 4's tax subsection puts a figure on it.

Spread: the variability of the return, and a word that means three things

Spread in this framework means the dispersion of the return, and it decomposes into capital volatility and income volatility. The decomposition earns its place because the two matter to different investors. An investor consuming the income cares about how reliable the income is; an investor measured on total return can nearly ignore income variability, because it contributes very little of the total variance in most asset classes.

Total return = income yield + capital return, so with correlation p between them sd_total^2 = sd_income^2 + sd_capital^2 + 2 p sd_income sd_capital and the share of total variance attributable to income variability is (sd_income^2 + p sd_income sd_capital) / sd_total^2. Income variability is 1.2% of the gilt's total variance and 22.3% of the property's, because a void is both an income event and a large one. So a fund paying pensions out of rent is exposed to a dimension a total-return investor can treat as noise, and the SAME ASSET is riskier for one than the other without anything about the asset changing. Note also that private equity's income share (13.7%) is LOWER than property's (22.3%) despite having half again the income volatility. Capital volatility dominates the denominator, so a high income volatility in absolute terms can still be second order.
The word "spread" is doing three jobs Within this syllabus, spread means the dispersion of returns when it appears in this framework, the credit spread over the risk-free curve when it appears in a bond or CDS discussion, and the bid-offer spread when it appears under marketability. The source summary uses all three within a few pages. In an examination answer, use the word once and say which meaning you intend, or avoid it and write "variability of return", which cannot be misread.

Term: stated by the asset, judged by the liability

Term is the period over which the asset delivers, and it comes in three shapes. A conventional bond has a fixed term. Equity and direct property are undated, with a term set by the investor's decision to sell rather than by the contract. A private equity fund has an uncertain term: the source describes a cycle of eight to twelve years, with two to three years of investing, three to five years of holding, and an extension possible if realisations are slow. That third category is the awkward one, because the investor commits capital without knowing when it comes back, and an undrawn commitment is a liquidity obligation rather than an asset.

Term sits at the boundary of the framework's two halves. The asset states its term, which makes it look like a property of the asset. What the term is worth is entirely a question about the liability it is matched against, which makes it relational. That is why the figure marks it in amber rather than blue or purple.

Expenses: the drag, and what a fee has to buy

Expenses are the annual cost of holding the asset, and they are the dimension candidates most reliably underweight, because a fee quoted as a percentage of assets sounds small next to a return quoted as a percentage of assets. Compounded over an investment horizon it is not small, and the right way to see that is as a share of the profit rather than a share of the fund.

The source makes a related point in the other direction, and it is a fair one: private markets can carry lower expenses on some lines. Private debt saves administration relative to a public issue, and private equity avoids the audit, listing and reporting costs of a public company while giving the owner more direct control. Fund-of-funds structures then hand back more than they save through double fees. So the dimension is genuinely two-sided and an answer that assumes private is always dearer is being lazy.

Marketability: a cost you never incur is not a cost

Marketability is the ability to sell the holding when you want to, at a price close to its value, in the quantity you hold. The source decomposes it into issue size, ease of valuation, and divisibility, and all three deserve their place. Issue size drives the depth of the market. Ease of valuation drives how far a buyer's price can drift from a modelled value without anyone being able to say it has: a listed share has a price, a shopping centre has a valuer's opinion. Divisibility decides whether you can sell part of the holding at all, which is why a R400m building is less marketable than R400m of a bond even when both would find a buyer.

The cost of imperfect marketability shows up as a round-trip transaction cost, and that cost is only paid when the asset is traded. So it must be annualised over the holding period before it can be compared with anything else in the framework.

Which of the two you are is a fact about the liability side of the balance sheet.

Tax: the dimension that is not about the asset at all

Tax asks what the fiscus takes and when. Three things are taxed differently: interest, which is income; dividends, which attract a withholding tax; and capital gains, which are taxed on realisation at an inclusion rate rather than in full. South African rates for the 2024/25 year of assessment give a top individual marginal rate of 45 per cent, a capital gains inclusion rate of 40 per cent for individuals producing a maximum effective rate of 18 per cent, a corporate rate of 27 per cent with an 80 per cent inclusion rate producing 21.6 per cent, and dividends tax of 20 per cent from which South African resident companies are exempt on dividends received. Retirement funds pay none of the three.

The same 8.00% gross return, delivered three ways, to three holders. holder as interest as dividend as capital gain prefers individual, 45% marginal 4.40% 6.40% 6.56% capital gain company, 27% 5.84% 8.00% 6.27% the dividend retirement fund, exempt 8.00% 8.00% 8.00% indifferent THE RANKING REVERSES. The individual prefers the gain to the dividend; the company prefers the dividend to the gain, because inter-company dividends are exempt while its capital gains inclusion rate is twice the individual's. Both taxpayers face 2.16 pp between their best and worst form; the fund faces none. An adviser who describes an asset's tax treatment without naming the holder has not answered the question. There is no such thing as the tax position of a bond.

There is a second effect, and it is the one candidates most often miss. Capital gains tax is charged on realisation, so an unrealised gain is an interest-free loan from the fiscus that compounds alongside the investment. The value of that deferral grows with the holding period and can be computed exactly.

That is comparable to the entire expense advantage of a tracker over an active fund, earned by doing nothing. An asset that pays its return as income cannot defer anything. So the yield dimension and the tax dimension are not separable: the FORM of the return changes what the tax RATE costs, and the interaction is worth more than either dimension looks worth alone. Two caveats an examiner would want. First, the exempt fund gains nothing from deferral, so this is another reversal rather than a general truth. Second, deferral is not free of risk: it concentrates the tax liability at a single future date under whatever rate then applies, and inclusion rates have been raised twice since 2001.

Currency: a threshold, not a preference

Currency asks what money the asset pays in, and it matters because a return earned in dollars and spent in rands is two bets rather than one. For a rand investor holding a foreign asset, the rand return is approximately the local return plus the rate of rand depreciation, so the variance of the rand return is the sum of the two variances plus twice the covariance. Whether hedging reduces risk therefore depends on the correlation, and there is an exact threshold rather than a matter of taste.

When the currency is twice as volatile as the asset, no correlation short of perfect negative dependence makes the unhedged holding safer, so for a volatile-currency investor the hedging question answers itself. And the cost side is settled by COVERED INTEREST PARITY: the forward rate embeds the interest differential, so hedging locks in the domestic-minus-foreign rate gap and gives up the expected depreciation. If uncovered parity roughly holds, those offset, and the hedge decision is about VARIANCE rather than about expected return.

Which is the answer to the question the module's ninth lecture raises, of whether currency is a return source or a residual. It is neither by default. It is a position, entered deliberately or by omission, whose risk contribution has a sign that depends on a correlation the investor has to estimate, and whose expected return contribution is close to zero if the parity conditions approximately hold. A fund that has not hedged has taken the position; it just has not written it down.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The eight dimensions are not independent, and the interactions carry more value than the dimensions

Presented as a checklist, the framework invites eight separate paragraphs. Almost everything interesting in this lecture came from a pair rather than a single dimension, and there are enough pairs to make the point structural rather than anecdotal: eight dimensions give twenty-eight pairs, of which at least five bind hard.

The examination consequence is concrete. When a question asks you to compare two asset classes for a given investor, the marks in the upper bands are almost never in the eight one-line answers. They are in noticing that this investor is tax exempt, so the yield-form argument collapses; or that this fund faces an outflow next year, so the marketability argument that looked second order becomes the binding one. The framework gets you to the shortlist. The interactions decide it.

What the framework leaves out, and why knowing that is part of knowing the framework

Three omissions matter. The first is the liability. Nothing in the eight dimensions asks what the money is for, and yet the previous section showed the liability deciding the marketability answer, the term answer and the currency answer. The framework describes assets in isolation and then relies on the reader to supply the other half of the comparison, which is fine as long as the reader knows they are supposed to.

The second is correlation with the rest of the portfolio. The framework treats each asset one at a time, so an asset's contribution to portfolio risk, which depends on its covariance with what is already held rather than on its own volatility, has no slot. This is not a small gap. An asset with high standalone spread and low correlation can reduce total portfolio risk, and the framework as stated will mark it down on the spread dimension and never see the offsetting benefit. The portfolio construction module is where that is repaired.

The third is price. The framework can establish that an asset is suitable and say nothing about whether it is worth its current price. Suitability and value are separate judgements, and the valuation module handles the second. The source summary treats several dimensions as if they were verdicts, describing private equity as high yielding and infrastructure as high yielding, when what both descriptions really mean is that these assets have historically been priced to offer more, in compensation for illiquidity and for risks the framework catalogues elsewhere. Compensation for a risk is not a free lunch, and an answer that lists high expected return as an advantage of an asset class without naming what is being borne for it has double counted.

One place where the source's framing needs correcting

The summary lists security sub-items for private debt that include "no credit rating" and "covenants" side by side, as though both were features of the asset. They are not the same kind of thing. The absence of a rating is an information problem sitting with the investor, who must now do the credit analysis a rating agency would otherwise have done and carries the cost of doing it badly. Covenants are a contractual remedy that partially substitutes for that information. Reading them as two bullets on a list loses the relationship between them, which is that private debt investors accept an information disadvantage and are compensated partly in yield and partly in control rights. Say that in one sentence and the two bullets become an argument.

Step 7

Connections

Where this goes next. Every remaining lecture in this module applies these eight dimensions to one asset class, in the same order, so the framework is the reading key for the whole of asset classes. Lectures 3 to 5 work through lending, short and long. Lectures 6 and 7 cover ownership. Lectures 8 and 9 cover real assets, with lecture 9 returning to the currency dimension as a source of return in its own right. Lectures 10 and 11 cover repackaged claims, where the security dimension is engineered by tranching rather than inherited from an issuer.

What it assumes. The mechanics of the instruments are taken from financial instruments, which covers bond structure, the yield curve, duration and convexity, and derivatives for the forward contract underlying the hedging arithmetic. The compounding used throughout the expense and tax blocks is from time value of money. The variance decomposition in the spread section is the two-variable case of the result in probability.

What repairs its omissions. Correlation with the rest of the portfolio is handled in portfolio construction, and price against value in valuation. The liability side, which the framework leaves to the reader, is the subject of portfolio management. The measurement of what the eight dimensions produced after the fact belongs to performance measurement.

Where the same framework appears elsewhere. A bank's treasury asks these eight questions of its liquid asset buffer and gets answers constrained by regulation rather than by preference, which is why liquidity risk in the banking track reads like a version of this lecture with the marketability dimension made compulsory. The security dimension, quantified, is the whole of credit risk.

Numbers used here, and where they came from. The eight dimensions and their sub-items are from the F105 summary, chapters 6 and 7. South African tax rates are SARS published rates for the 2024/25 year of assessment: 45 per cent top marginal, 40 per cent and 80 per cent capital gains inclusion rates for individuals and companies, 27 per cent corporate, 20 per cent dividends tax. Covered interest parity is standard. Everything else is invented for illustration: the 8 per cent gross return, the volatilities in the spread table, the round-trip costs, the fee levels, and the correlation values. They were chosen to span the plausible range and the arithmetic on them is exact, but no figure here is a market observation.
