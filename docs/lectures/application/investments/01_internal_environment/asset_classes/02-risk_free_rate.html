The risk-free rate: three conditions, no instrument that meets all of them

The syllabus asks what a risk-free return means and which assets "may be assumed to be risk-free in practical work". Those five words are the answer. A return is risk-free only if it carries no default, no reinvestment and no inflation risk, and nothing meets all three, so the risk-free asset is defined by the objective rather than by the instrument. Funding an eighteen-year liability with treasury bills exposes you to a 34 per cent shortfall or a 50 per cent surplus depending on a rate path nobody knows.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A return is risk-free when both the amount and the timing are certain, and that requires three separate things: no default risk, so the issuer pays; no reinvestment risk, so nothing has to be rolled at an unknown future rate; and, if the objective is expressed in real terms, no inflation risk. No single instrument delivers all three, which is why the syllabus asks what may be assumed risk-free in practical work rather than what is risk-free.

The consequence is that the risk-free asset is defined by the liability, not by the market. For an eighteen-year nominal liability it is an eighteen-year nominal government zero-coupon bond. For a real liability it is an index-linked zero. For a collateralised derivative it is the rate the collateral earns, which is the overnight rate. A treasury bill is the safest instrument in the market and the wrong answer to three of those four questions.

How wrong is measurable. Funding an eighteen-year obligation by rolling 91-day bills, starting at a 9 per cent rate, produces a 34.26 per cent shortfall if short rates fall to 4 per cent and a 50.15 per cent surplus if they rise to 14. The eighteen-year zero produces exactly the required amount with certainty. Both instruments are default-free throughout; only one is risk-free for the purpose.

Then there is the question of whether a government bond is default-free at all. A sovereign borrowing in its own currency can always create the currency, so nominal default is a choice rather than an inability, and that is why a rand government bond is the local risk-free asset for a rand liability. What it is not is free of inflation risk, which is precisely the risk that creating currency generates.

Feynman test "Risk-free" does not mean safe. It means you know exactly how much you will have and when. A treasury bill is about as safe as anything in the world and tells you nothing about what you will have in eighteen years, because you have to keep buying new ones at prices you cannot see yet. The risk-free asset is whichever one exactly matches what you owe.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this is the most consequential single number in actuarial work and the one most often chosen by convention. Discounting an eighteen-year liability, a 100 basis point difference in the rate changes the value by about 18 per cent. On a R10bn book that is R1.8bn, decided by a choice that frequently receives less scrutiny than a mortality assumption worth a fraction of it.

The second reason is that the same 100 basis point error matters wildly differently in different applications, and knowing the ranking is how to allocate effort. It moves a liability value by 18 per cent, an implied equity risk premium by a full percentage point in the opposite direction, a Sharpe ratio by a third of its value, a CAPM cost of equity by either 100 basis points or nothing at all depending on whether you were consistent, and a five-year option value by 8 per cent. Step 4 sets those out together.

The third reason is that the practical answer changed after 2008 and the change is not always reflected in older material. Interbank offered rates were treated as near risk-free and used to discount derivatives; the crisis established that they were not, and the market moved to discounting collateralised trades at the overnight rate. The reference rate reform that follows from that (ZARONIA in South Africa) is the transition lecture 3 flagged.

And a fourth reason specific to an actuary working in a currency with a sub-investment-grade sovereign rating. The published methods for constructing a local risk-free rate involve stripping a default spread out of a government bond yield, and the input is a judgement. Whether to do that at all, or to put the sovereign risk in the assets rather than the discount rate, is a real methodological choice with a large numerical consequence, and it must not be made twice.

Every path above is default-free from beginning to end. SO A TREASURY BILL IS DEFAULT-FREE AND IS NOT RISK-FREE FOR THIS PURPOSE. It is the safest instrument in the market and the wrong one, and that distinction is exactly what the syllabus objective is pointing at. THE MIRROR-IMAGE ERROR IS EQUALLY COMMON. Using an eighteen-year rate to discount a one-year obligation exposes the fund to price risk it need not take: the eighteen-year bond's value moves with the curve while the obligation does not. Neither error is about credit. Both are about matching the term of the asset to the term of the promise, which is the term dimension from lecture 1 doing the work.

Is a government bond default-free?

A sovereign borrowing in its OWN currency can always create the currency, so nominal default is a policy choice rather than an inability. One borrowing in a FOREIGN currency can be genuinely unable to pay, and many have been. The market prices the difference, and a hard-currency spread can be read as an upper bound on default risk. At a 60 per cent loss given default, roughly the historical average sovereign restructuring haircut: hard-currency spread implied annual PD 10-year cumulative 25 bp 0.417% 4.09% 100 bp 1.667% 15.47% 250 bp 4.167% 34.66% 500 bp 8.333% 58.11% 1000 bp 16.667% 83.85% READ THAT AS AN UPPER BOUND AND NOT AN ESTIMATE. The spread is expected loss PLUS a risk premium PLUS a liquidity premium, so dividing the whole spread by the loss given default overstates the default probability. This is the identical caution lecture 4 applied to breakeven inflation: the direction of the bias is known and its size is not. THE PRACTICAL CONSEQUENCE FOR A SOUTH AFRICAN ACTUARY is clean. The rand-denominated government bond is the local risk-free asset for a rand liability, because the liability is in rands and the issuer can always produce rands. What it is not is free of INFLATION risk, and the mechanism by which a government avoids nominal default (creating currency) is precisely the mechanism that generates inflation. The two risks are not independent; they are substitutes, and a nominal government bond has swapped one for the other. WHICH IS WHY AN INFLATION-LINKED GOVERNMENT BOND IS A DIFFERENT CREDIT IN SUBSTANCE. It removes the government's escape route, so a sovereign under fiscal pressure has a stronger incentive to default on real debt than on nominal debt. That is a small effect in most countries and it is not zero, and it is the reason index-linked issuance is limited even where demand for it is high.

A 100 basis point difference of view about the default spread moves the risk-free rate by 100 basis points, and step 4's final subsection shows what that does downstream. THE HONEST ALTERNATIVE, and it is often the better one for actuarial work: do not strip anything out. Use the local nominal government yield to discount local nominal liabilities and put the sovereign risk where it belongs, in the assets being valued rather than in the discount rate. THE TWO APPROACHES ANSWER DIFFERENT QUESTIONS: stripping the spread out asks "what would a genuinely risk-free rand rate be?" which is the right question for a valuation of a risky asset, where the risk is priced separately leaving it in asks "what does it cost to fund a rand obligation using the safest available rand instrument?" which is the right question for a liability valuation AND MIXING THEM DOUBLE COUNTS. Discounting a liability at a spread-stripped rate, and then holding capital against the sovereign risk of the bonds backing it, charges for the same risk twice. That is a real and common error and it always inflates the reported deficit.

The real risk-free rate, which is the hardest of them

The closest observable real risk-free rate is the yield on an index-linked government bond, and lecture 4 established three separate contaminations. the observed ILB real yield 4.50% less the ILLIQUIDITY premium embedded in it, illustratively -0.30% = a liquid-equivalent real risk-free rate 4.20% THE SIGN MATTERS AND IS EASY TO GET BACKWARDS. An index-linked market is smaller and less liquid than the nominal market, so an index-linked bond must offer a HIGHER yield to clear. The observed yield therefore sits ABOVE the rate a perfectly liquid real bond would offer, and the adjustment is a subtraction. The offsetting adjustment does not belong here at all: the INFLATION RISK PREMIUM is something the NOMINAL bond pays and the index-linked bond does not. The two adjustments move measured breakeven inflation in opposite directions, which is lecture 4's conclusion restated, and neither is observable. AND THE INDEXATION LAG MEANS EVEN THIS IS NOT FULLY REAL: a 3-month lag at 6% inflation leaves 1.467% of purchasing power unhedged a 3-month lag at 12% inflation leaves 2.874% a 4-month lag at 6% inflation leaves 1.961% a 4-month lag at 12% inflation leaves 3.850% SO THERE IS NO OBSERVABLE REAL RISK-FREE RATE. There is an index-linked yield with three adjustments of unknown size, two of them pointing down and one up. For an actuary discounting real liabilities that is not an academic problem: it is the discount rate, and the honest presentation is a range with the adjustments stated rather than a point estimate with them buried.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The answer changed in 2008, and the change was about collateral

Before the financial crisis, interbank offered rates were treated as approximately risk-free and used to discount derivatives, on the reasoning that large banks did not fail. The crisis established that they could, and the spread between an interbank offered rate and an overnight indexed swap became a traded measure of bank credit and funding stress.

the spread between a term interbank rate and an overnight indexed swap 5 bp normal conditions: the two are effectively the same rate 50 bp stress: banks are charging each other for term unsecured lending 200 bp crisis: the term interbank market has effectively closed WHAT FOLLOWED IS NOW STANDARD PRACTICE. A fully collateralised derivative is discounted at the rate the COLLATERAL earns, because that is the actual funding cost of the position. For cash collateral posted overnight, that rate is the overnight rate. So the practical risk-free curve is built from overnight index swaps rather than from government bonds or from interbank rates. THE LOGIC IS WORTH SEEING BECAUSE IT IS NOT AN ANALOGY. If a trade is collateralised daily in cash, and the cash earns the overnight rate, then the position can be funded at the overnight rate with certainty. That is a genuinely risk-free funding rate for that position, established by contract rather than by assumption about an issuer. It is the closest thing to a risk-free rate that exists, and it exists only for collateralised positions. WHICH IS WHY LECTURE 3'S BENCHMARK TRANSITION SITS UNDERNEATH THIS WHOLE LECTURE. An overnight rate compiled from actual transactions (ZARONIA in South Africa, and its equivalents elsewhere) is the reference the modern framework rests on. And a term rate compounded from an overnight series contains no term premium at all, which is exactly the property a discounting curve wants and a lending benchmark does not, which is why the transition has been harder for loan markets than for derivative markets.

Four practical answers, and how to choose between them

the purpose the rate to use valuing a collateralised derivative the overnight index curve. the collateral rate IS the funding rate, by contract. discounting a NOMINAL liability the government nominal zero curve, read at the liability's own term. not a single rate, a curve. discounting a REAL liability the index-linked curve, adjusted for the indexation lag and for the illiquidity premium, with the adjustments stated a CAPM, Sharpe or equity risk premium a government bond whose term matches the horizon calculation of the analysis, AND the same rate used to derive whatever risk premium is applied to it THE FOURTH ROW IS THE ONE THAT SAVES THE MOST TROUBLE. The consistency requirement is free and it removes the largest common error: mixing a risk-free rate from one source with a risk premium estimated against a different one. Step 4 showed that a 100 basis point error in the rate is worth 100 basis points of cost of equity if the premium is held fixed and nothing at all if the premium is implied from the same rate. AND A FIFTH ANSWER, WHICH IS SOMETIMES CORRECT AND ALWAYS UNPOPULAR: there may be no suitable rate, in which case the honest output is a range. A thirty-year real liability in a market whose index-linked curve extends to fifteen years has no observable discount rate at the term required. Extrapolating it is a modelling choice with a number attached, and presenting the number without the choice is where most disputes about deficits begin.

Where this connects back to the framework

Lecture 1 argued that three of the eight dimensions describe a pairing of asset and investor rather than the asset itself. The risk-free rate is the cleanest possible demonstration of that claim, and it is worth closing on it. The same instrument is risk-free for one investor and not for another, and no property of the instrument changed.

the same 91-day treasury bill, held by: a money market fund with daily RISK-FREE. the term matches, the credit is redemptions sovereign, and inflation over 91 days is small relative to the return. a pension fund with 18-year real NOT RISK-FREE AT ALL. it carries the full liabilities reinvestment risk of step 4, worth a 34 to 52 per cent shortfall, plus eighteen years of unhedged inflation. a bank managing its liquidity buffer RISK-FREE for the purpose, and the regulator agrees: it is the highest-quality liquid asset and attracts no haircut. a foreign investor whose liabilities NOT RISK-FREE. the currency dimension from are in another currency lecture 1 dominates, and at a 15 per cent exchange rate volatility the currency risk is an order of magnitude larger than anything the bill itself contributes. THE INSTRUMENT IS IDENTICAL IN ALL FOUR CASES. What differs is the liability, and the liability is what the framework's term and currency dimensions were pointing at all along. So the syllabus objective's phrase "in practical work" is not a softening. It is the technical content: risk-free is a relation between an asset and an obligation, and asking for the risk-free rate without naming the obligation is asking an incomplete question.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The eight dimensions, and specifically the argument that term and currency describe a pairing rather than the asset, are from the asset class framework, and step 5 closes by demonstrating that claim on a single treasury bill held four different ways. The quotation conventions, the repo haircut and the reference rate transition are from money market instruments. The spot curve, the distinction between a spot rate and a redemption yield, the indexation lag and the breakeven decomposition are all from government and corporate bonds, which is the lecture this one leans on most. The implied equity risk premium is from listed equity and the option sensitivity from structured products.

Where this is used. Every discounted quantity in the module rests on this choice, so the lecture is a prerequisite rather than a topic. Valuation uses it as the discount rate and as the input to every relative valuation. Portfolio construction uses it as the intercept of the efficient frontier and in every Sharpe ratio comparison, including the break-even correlation test from lecture 9. Performance measurement uses it to separate a manager's return from the return available for nothing, and derivatives uses it in the forward and in discounting, where step 5's collateral argument determines which curve.

Where the same question has a regulator's answer. A bank's liquidity buffer is defined by regulation in terms of high-quality liquid assets, which is a supervisor specifying what may be assumed risk-free for one specific purpose, in liquidity risk. The zero risk weight applied to domestic sovereign exposures under the standardised approach is the same assumption made for capital purposes, in credit risk, and it is one of the more contested features of the framework precisely because of the arithmetic in step 4. A bank's own discount curve for interest rate risk in the banking book is the subject of interest rate risk, and the overnight benchmark underneath all of it is administered by the South African Reserve Bank.
