Hedge funds: not an asset class, a fee structure with permissions attached

A hedge fund holds the same securities as everybody else. What distinguishes it is what it is permitted to do with them (short, lever, concentrate, hold illiquid positions) and how the manager is paid. Both are computable. The performance fee is a call option worth 51 per cent more at a 30 per cent volatility than at 15, and on plausible figures the fee captures 86 per cent of the alpha it is charged on.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The F105 summary says hedge funds aim for "high or absolute returns" across "many assets", lists five types (global macro, market-neutral, event-driven, multi-strategy and contrarian, the last glossed as taking the opposite view to the market) and leaves "Features" as an empty heading. So this lecture is built from outside the source, and the first thing to establish is that a hedge fund is not an asset class at all: it is a legal wrapper with unusual permissions and an unusual fee.

Take market-neutral, the type whose name promises the most. A long book of R100 and a short book of R100 has zero net exposure and three return drivers: the spread between the two books, the interest earned on the short sale proceeds, and the frictions of borrowing stock and financing the longs. On the worked figures the strategy returns 10.90 per cent, of which 8.00 points are the cash rate. In a zero-rate world the same stock selection delivers 2.90 per cent.

Zero net exposure does not mean zero risk. Two books each running at 20 per cent volatility with a 0.90 correlation give a spread volatility of 8.94 per cent, which is genuinely low, and at three times leverage it becomes 26.83 per cent, which is more volatile than the equity market. If the true correlation is 0.70 rather than 0.90, the levered volatility is 46.48 per cent instead: a factor of 1.73 from one parameter estimated in calm conditions.

Then the fee. A performance fee above a high-water mark is a call option on the fund's own net asset value, so its value rises with volatility: 2.078 per cent of assets at a 15 per cent volatility and 3.142 per cent at 30, a 51 per cent increase with no improvement in skill whatever. And because it crystallises annually and is never repaid, two investors in the same fund with the same gross return can pay 5.60 or 12.45 per cent of carry depending only on the order in which the returns arrived.

Feynman test A hedge fund manager buys and sells the same shares and bonds as a unit trust manager. The differences are that he may borrow to buy more, sell things he does not own, put a quarter of the fund in one idea, and lock your money up for a year, and that he keeps a fifth of the gains and none of the losses. Everything interesting follows from that last clause.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the question "should we allocate to hedge funds" is malformed, and recognising that is most of the answer. There is no hedge fund asset class to allocate to. There are strategies, each with its own exposures, and the useful question is what each one is exposed to and whether that exposure is available more cheaply somewhere else. Step 5 puts a number on the second half.

The second reason is that this is where the fee arithmetic from lecture 7 acquires its sharpest form. Private equity's carry is charged once, over the fund's life, above a preferred return. A hedge fund's is charged annually, usually with no hurdle, and never repaid, which makes it a series of one-year options rather than a single long-dated one. That difference is worth several percentage points and it is entirely a matter of contract design.

The third reason is that the published performance data for this class is the least usable in the module. Reporting is voluntary, failed funds stop reporting, joining funds may backfill their history, and illiquid positions are marked by the manager. Every one of those filters biases the same way, and lecture 5's smoothing arithmetic applies on top: a fund reporting a 0.35 monthly autocorrelation has a true volatility 1.44 times its reported one.

And a fourth reason that matters for an actuary advising a fund. "Absolute return" is a description of an objective rather than a property of an outcome. A fund targeting cash plus 5 per cent with a 10 per cent volatility loses money in 9.68 per cent of years and has a 39.89 per cent chance of at least one down year in five. There is no index to blame when it does, which is the real consequence of an absolute return mandate.

The high-water mark is path dependent, and the path is not the manager's skill

A 5.4 percentage point difference produced entirely by the ORDER in which the returns arrived. The mechanism is simple once seen. Two good years in succession set a new high-water mark twice, so carry is paid twice. The losses that follow reduce the net asset value and are never repaid, and the recovery in year five is measured from a mark set at the peak. The manager was paid for gains that did not survive. WHICH HAS A DIRECT CONSEQUENCE FOR HOW A FUND MUST BE ACCOUNTED FOR. Two investors in the same fund, arriving at different times, face different high-water marks. An investor buying after a drawdown inherits a mark above the current value and receives a free option; one buying at a peak receives none. A single fund-level mark therefore cross-subsidises between investors, which is why serious funds run EQUALISATION accounting with series or equalisation credits, and why a fund that does not is quietly transferring value between its own holders.

"Absolute return" examined

Take the source's phrase literally. A fund targeting cash plus 5 per cent, so an expected 13 per cent at an 8 per cent cash rate, and ask how often it loses money. fund volatility P(a negative year) P(at least one negative year in five) 4.0% 0.06% 0.29% 6.0% 1.51% 7.34% 10.0% 9.68% 39.89% 15.0% 19.31% 65.79% 20.0% 25.78% 77.49% AT A 10 PER CENT VOLATILITY AN ABSOLUTE RETURN FUND LOSES MONEY IN 9.68 PER CENT OF YEARS and has a 39.89 per cent chance of at least one down year in five. So "absolute return" describes an objective and not an outcome, and a fund that has never had a down year is either running very little risk or not marking honestly. The second possibility is detectable with the autocorrelation test above. THE USEFUL RESTATEMENT is that an absolute return mandate changes the BENCHMARK from an index to cash. That changes the measurement and not the risk, and it has one consequence worth stating plainly: there is no index to blame. A long-only manager down 20 per cent in a market down 25 has outperformed and will say so. An absolute return manager down 20 per cent has lost 20 per cent. For a trustee that cuts both ways. The absence of an index to hide behind is the honest feature of the mandate, and it also removes the only defence a manager has in a bad year, which is one reason absolute return funds are harder to hold through a drawdown than an equity fund with the same loss.

So an investor accepting a two-year lock-up in order to access illiquid assets, and paying 2 and 20 for it, has surrendered liquidity and received nothing for it unless the manager generates genuine alpha on top. WHICH IS THE HONEST FRAME FOR THE WHOLE ASSET CLASS: the lock-up is not the problem, and the fee charged on top of the lock-up is. A one-year lock-up that bought a 90 basis point premium at a 30 basis point cost would be an excellent trade for a fund with no short-term liquidity needs. At 2 and 20 it is not one, and the difference is the fee rather than the structure.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

How much of the alpha reaches the investor? The calculation that decides the allocation

A hedge fund's return divides into three parts. Some is ordinary market exposure, which anybody can buy for a few basis points. Some is exposure to well-documented risk premia (value, momentum, carry, illiquidity, selling volatility) which are available in cheaper wrappers. And some is genuine alpha, meaning return the investor could not have obtained any other way. Only the third part justifies the fee, and the fee is charged on all three.

What a hedge fund actually is, stated once

NOT a distinct asset class. Hedge funds hold the same instruments as everybody else: the shares of lecture 6, the bonds of lecture 4, the derivatives of lecture 11, the distressed debt of lecture 5. IT IS a combination of four permissions and one fee: permission what it enables what it exposes the fund to SHORT SELLING profit from falling prices, borrow cost, recall risk, and and hedge the market out unlimited loss on the short leg LEVERAGE make a small spread margin calls at the worst moment, material which is the mechanism by which relative-value funds fail CONCENTRATION express conviction idiosyncratic risk that the granularity assumption of every model in this module assumes away ILLIQUIDITY capture the premium from a valuation that is the manager's lecture 5 own estimate, and a lock-up plus THE FEE: 2% of assets and 20% of gains above a high-water mark, crystallised annually, never repaid, and usually without a hurdle. EVERY RISK IN THE ASSET CLASS TRACES TO ONE OF THOSE FIVE. That is a more useful organising claim than any list of strategy names, because it survives the invention of new strategies: a new fund doing something the five types do not describe will still be using some combination of the same four permissions and charging the same fee.

The eight dimensions, and the two rows that cannot be answered

SECURITY depends on what the fund holds, which the investor may not be told. and there are TWO additional layers the framework does not anticipate: the prime broker, which holds the assets and finances the longs, and the fund's own solvency, since a levered fund can fail while its assets are merely impaired. YIELD usually none. gains are accumulated and realised on redemption, which for a taxable investor is the good treatment from lecture 1 and for an exempt fund is neutral. SPREAD REPORTED at a fraction of the truth wherever illiquid positions are (variability) held, by a factor of 1.11 to 1.73 on the autocorrelation table. and the distribution is usually negatively skewed, because several of the strategies are short volatility in substance. TERM set by the lock-up and notice period, not by the assets. this is the one dimension the wrapper genuinely controls, and step 4 priced it. EXPENSES the highest in the module: 2% plus 20%, path dependent, and capturing 86% of the alpha on the central case. MARKETABILITY poor by contract rather than by nature. a fund holding liquid equities with a one-year lock-up has manufactured illiquidity, and should be paid for it in the other direction. TAX depends on the vehicle and the investor's jurisdiction, and this is a genuine reason offshore structures exist rather than a sinister one. CURRENCY whatever the fund reports in, but a global macro fund's currency exposure is its STRATEGY rather than an incidental feature, so the dimension cannot be separated from the mandate. TWO ROWS CANNOT HONESTLY BE ANSWERED FROM OUTSIDE THE FUND. Security, because the holdings may not be disclosed, and spread, because the reported figure is produced by the manager. That is the deepest problem with the asset class and it is structural rather than behavioural: the two dimensions an investor most needs are the two the wrapper is least obliged to reveal. WHICH IS WHY THE DUE DILIGENCE THAT MATTERS IS OPERATIONAL rather than analytical: who values the portfolio, who holds the assets, who audits, what the prime broker's rehypo- thecation rights are, and whether the administrator is independent. Every large hedge fund failure has turned on one of those five questions rather than on a return forecast.

What the source gives, and what an answer must supply

The summary supplies the five type names and the phrase "high or absolute returns" across "many assets", and leaves the features heading empty. The type names are worth keeping because they are the industry's own vocabulary and an examiner will use them. Everything else in this lecture is external, and a candidate should be able to supply: what each type is exposed to, the fee structure with its high-water mark and the absence of a hurdle, why zero net exposure is not zero risk, the three-part return decomposition, the data biases, and the operational due diligence questions.

One remark in the source's favour. Its gloss on the contrarian type, "opposite view to market", is the only feature-level content it offers about any of the five, and it happens to name the strategy whose risk is hardest to state: being right eventually and wrong for long enough is indistinguishable from being wrong, once the position is levered. That is the mechanism behind the best-known relative-value failures, and the source's four words point at it without developing it.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The fee waterfall, the carry-as-option logic and the survivorship arithmetic come from private equity, and the differences (annual crystallisation, no hurdle, no clawback) are what make the hedge fund version more expensive. The appraisal smoothing model and the illiquidity premium that a lock-up exists to capture are from private debt. The Sharpe ratio test for whether an exposure earns its place is from commodities and currency, and the securities lending revenue that funds the short book is from collective schemes and ETFs, which is the other side of the same trade. The written-option payoff shape recurs from structured products.

Where this goes next. Lecture 14 closes the module with the newer routes into old asset classes, most of which are new wrappers rather than new assets, so the arithmetic in this lecture and lecture 12 is the tool for assessing them. The factor regressions in step 5 belong to performance measurement, which is where alpha is separated from beta properly, and the decision about how much of a portfolio can bear a lock-up is portfolio construction. The short selling, leverage and derivative positions the wrapper permits are the subject of derivatives.

Where the bank is the counterparty. A hedge fund's prime broker finances its longs, lends it stock to short, and holds its assets, which makes the fund a counterparty exposure measured in counterparty credit risk: a levered fund's default is precisely the wrong-way exposure that framework exists to capture. The margin calls that end relative-value strategies are a liquidity event for both parties, in liquidity risk. The bank's own trading desks run several of these strategies directly, capitalised under market risk, where the short-volatility payoff shape is what the tail measures are designed to catch. South African hedge funds have been regulated as collective investment schemes since 2015, under the framework administered by the FSCA.
