Securitisation: redistributing a fixed expected loss, and the parameter that decides who bears it

Tranching creates nothing. On a pool with a 0.80 per cent expected loss the three tranches' losses sum to exactly 0.80 per cent, and all the structure does is put 95.70 per cent of it onto 3 per cent of the capital. What makes securitisation dangerous rather than merely clever is that the split depends entirely on an unobservable correlation: raise it from 10 to 60 per cent and the senior tranche's expected loss multiplies by 1,850 while the pool's does not move at all.

Asset classes 10 of 14 Reading time, 50 minutes Sources: F105 summary chapter 6; Vasicek single-factor model
Step 1

The 30-second version

An asset-backed security is a claim on a pool of assets held in a separate company, sliced into tranches paid in a fixed order. The source's types are the standard ones: mortgage-backed securities, credit card receivables, collateralised loan, bond and debt obligations, and, marked as its own case, insurance-linked securities including catastrophe bonds. The mechanism is identical in all of them and the underlying risk is not.

The arithmetic first, because it settles what securitisation can and cannot do. Take a granular pool with a 2 per cent default probability, a 40 per cent loss given default and a 15 per cent asset correlation, so an expected pool loss of 0.80 per cent. Split it at 3 and 7 per cent. The equity tranche's expected loss is 25.52 per cent, the mezzanine's 0.83 per cent and the senior's 0.0015 per cent. Weighted by size those sum to exactly 0.80 per cent. Nothing was created. The structure moved 95.70 per cent of the pool's expected loss onto 3 per cent of its capital, which is what makes the other 97 per cent look safe.

Then the parameter that decides everything. The pool's expected loss does not depend on correlation at all. The tranches' do, enormously and in opposite directions. Raising correlation from 2 to 60 per cent cuts the equity tranche's expected loss from 26.67 to 14.50 per cent and lifts the senior tranche's from effectively nil to 0.1970 per cent. A senior tranche is short correlation and an equity tranche is long it, so two investors in the same deal need opposite views on one number that neither can observe.

Insurance-linked securities use the same machinery for a risk that is genuinely independent of financial markets. A catastrophe bond with a 1.10 per cent expected loss paying a 5.50 per cent spread is priced at a multiple of 5.00 times expected loss, and its low correlation with equities rests on physics rather than on a statistical regularity. That makes it the only diversifier in this module whose case does not depend on the correlation holding up.

Feynman test Put a thousand loans in a box, then sell three claims on the box: one that takes the first losses, one that takes the next few, and one that takes only what is left after a disaster. The total loss in the box has not changed by a cent. All you have done is decide who cries first. How much the last claim should cost depends entirely on whether the loans go bad together, and nobody knows that number.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this is the one asset class in the module whose risk can be computed rather than estimated, given the pool parameters, and the computation is short. That makes it the best available test of whether you understand what a credit portfolio's loss distribution looks like, which is the same understanding the banking track's capital models are built on.

The second reason is that the correlation sensitivity is the mechanism of the 2007 to 2009 crisis stated precisely, without narrative. Senior tranches of structured credit were rated on correlation assumptions estimated from periods containing no housing downturn. Understating the correlation by a factor of three understates the senior expected loss by a factor of 66 on the worked figures, which is exactly the distance between AAA and unsellable.

The third reason is that the source's own description of the structure is unusually precise and repays close reading. It names the two-way absence of recourse, the bankruptcy-remote special purpose vehicle, the multi-tranche format, over-collateralisation, and the full cash flow sequence in both directions. Almost all of that is right. What it omits is excess spread, which is usually the larger form of credit enhancement, and the retention requirement that post-crisis rules impose to stop the originator selling everything it underwrites.

And a fourth reason, for an actuary specifically. Insurance-linked securities are the point at which the investments syllabus and the general insurance syllabus meet. The pricing is actuarial (attachment probability, expected loss, conditional severity, a load), the structure is a securitisation, and the buyer is an investment fund. Knowing all three vocabularies for the same instrument is the whole skill.

Step 3

The mental model

One pool, one loss distribution, three claims on it, and the parameter that reallocates them.

Step 4

The detail

The tranche arithmetic, and what it proves securitisation cannot do

Model the pool with the single-factor framework: each borrower defaults if a latent variable falls below a threshold, the variable has a common component with weight given by the asset correlation, and the pool is granular enough that idiosyncratic risk washes out. The pool's loss rate is then a function of one systematic factor, and a tranche's expected loss is the expectation of its share of that loss.

A granular pool: PD 2.00%, LGD 40%, asset correlation 15%. Expected pool loss = PD x LGD = 0.8000%, and note that this does NOT depend on correlation. Securitisation creates no value of its own. What it does is put 95.70% of a fixed expected loss onto 3% of the capital structure, which is what allows the remaining 97% to be sold as low risk. MAPPING EXPECTED LOSS TO AN INDICATIVE RATING (illustrative thresholds, not an agency scale): equity 25.5205% -> B or below mezzanine 0.8252% -> BBB senior 0.0015% -> AAA which reproduces the source's own statement that senior is AAA, mezzanine is around BB and the equity claim is paid last. The source gives the ordering; the model gives the reason, and the reason is that a 93-point-thick tranche sitting above a 7% attachment point is only reached by an event with a probability of about one in a thousand. WHAT THE SOURCE MEANS BY "APPEAL TO MORE INVESTORS AND REDUCE THE COST OF BORROWING" IS NOW MEASURABLE. A single unsliced note on this pool would have to carry the pool's own 0.80% expected loss, so it would price like a BB credit. Slicing it lets 93% of the notional price like AAA and confines the BB pricing to a small piece. If the market's required spread is a convex function of expected loss, the weighted average cost of funding falls even though the total expected loss has not moved. That is the entire economic case for tranching, and it depends on the pricing convexity rather than on any risk having been removed.

Two investors in the same deal require opposite views on the same parameter, and only one of them can be right. THE MECHANISM IS NOT MYSTERIOUS. High correlation means losses arrive together: either almost nothing defaults, or a great deal does. That makes the small-loss outcomes less likely, which helps the equity tranche because it loses in every ordinary year, and it makes the large-loss outcomes more likely, which is the only thing that can ever reach the senior tranche. AND THE PARAMETER IS NOT OBSERVABLE. It is estimated, usually from a period containing no crisis, and the estimate is a single number standing in for a dependence structure. Understate it from 45% to 15% and the senior expected loss is understated by a factor of 66: correlation 45% senior expected loss 0.0971% correlation 15% senior expected loss 0.0015% which is the distance between a tranche a pension fund can hold and one it cannot. No fraud is required for that to happen; a reasonable-looking estimate from a benign sample is sufficient.

That combination, a tiny absolute number that is highly elastic, is exactly what makes a AAA structured tranche a different object from a AAA sovereign bond.

IT HAS A CATCH THAT IS ENTIRELY DOCUMENTARY. Excess spread only protects if it is TRAPPED rather than released to the equity holder, and what traps it is a performance trigger written into the deal. A structure that releases excess spread while performance is good has given away its own protection at exactly the moment when retaining it was cheapest, which is why the trigger definitions are where an investor should actually read a prospectus. Note also how these interact with the tranching. The 7% attachment point of the senior tranche and the 4.76% over-collateralisation are not additive in a simple way: the over-collateralisation IS part of what creates the subordination. An answer that adds every form of enhancement together has usually double counted.

Insurance-linked securities: the same structure, actuarially priced

The source marks insurance-linked securities as its own case within the asset-backed family, and describes them as interest payments secured by insurance risk premiums, with a payment to the insurer if an insurance event occurs, "like a CDS", as an alternative to reinsurance. That is a good description. Its cash flow note is more precise still: the insurer does not sell assets to the vehicle, the vehicle invests in money market assets, and if the insurance event occurs those assets meet the insurer's losses, while if it does not the principal returns to investors.

A catastrophe bond is priced off three numbers a catastrophe model supplies: attachment probability 1.50% the chance the layer is touched at all exhaustion probability 0.60% the chance the layer is wiped out entirely expected loss 1.10% the mean loss to the note conditional expected loss given attachment = 1.10 / 1.50 = 73.33% of principal That last figure is characteristic and worth pausing on: if the layer is touched at all it is usually mostly gone, because catastrophe severity distributions have long tails and a narrow layer is either missed or overwhelmed. spread over collateral multiple of EL risk load 1.50% 1.36x 0.40% 2.50% 2.27x 1.40% 4.00% 3.64x 2.90% 5.50% 5.00x 4.40% 8.00% 7.27x 6.90% THE MULTIPLE IS THE MARKET'S PRICE OF RISK and it is the number to argue about, exactly as a reinsurance layer's rate on line is. Why the ILS diversification argument is structural rather than empirical

That is a strong claim and it is only as good as the 5.5% spread and the 1.10% expected loss. THE STRUCTURAL POINT IS DIFFERENT AND MORE DURABLE. Commodities' low correlation with equities is a statistical regularity that can break, and step 4 of lecture 9 showed why it is state-dependent. A cyclone's independence of the JSE is a fact about physics. That is the only case in this module where the diversification argument does not rest on an estimate that might not hold. THREE HONEST QUALIFICATIONS, and they matter: 1. THE DEEP TAIL IS NOT INDEPENDENT. A large enough catastrophe damages the insurance sector, the reinsurance sector and the wider economy at once, so the independence fails precisely in the states where diversification was supposed to help. 2. THE COLLATERAL IS INVESTED. In 2008 some catastrophe bond collateral sat in vehicles that themselves lost value, introducing a financial-market correlation that the structure was designed to exclude and nobody had priced. Post-crisis deals restrict collateral to government money market funds for exactly this reason. 3. THE EXPECTED LOSS IS A MODEL OUTPUT, produced by a small number of vendor catastrophe models. Every investor in the market shares that model risk, and no amount of diversification across bonds addresses a common error in the model that priced all of them.

The special purpose vehicle, and what "no recourse" is worth in both directions

The source states the separation precisely, and it runs both ways: note holders have NO RECOURSE to the originator's other assets the originator's creditors have NO RECOURSE to the securitised pool Both require the vehicle to be BANKRUPTCY REMOTE: a separate legal entity whose assets cannot be drawn into the originator's insolvency. scenario an ABS holder an unsecured creditor of recovers the originator recovers pool performs, originator fails 100.0% 30.0% pool fails, originator solvent 60.0% 100.0% both fail 60.0% 30.0% ROW ONE IS THE WHOLE POINT. The note holder is indifferent to the originator's solvency, which is what allows a weak originator to issue highly rated paper: the rating attaches to the pool and the structure rather than to the name on the door. That is genuinely useful, and it is how a bank with a mediocre rating funds prime mortgages at close to sovereign spreads. AND IT IS ALSO THE STRUCTURE'S CHARACTERISTIC FAILURE MODE. An originator that keeps none of the risk has a weakened incentive to underwrite carefully, because the consequences of bad underwriting land on somebody else. Post-crisis rules therefore require the originator to RETAIN a slice, conventionally 5% of the exposure, so that it holds some of what it sells. Read the source's cash flow description again with that in mind. "Excess collateral returned" to the issuer is the retained economic interest, and it is doing far more work than the phrase suggests: it is simultaneously the originator's profit, the deal's first-loss protection, and the alignment mechanism that makes the whole structure trustworthy. Those three roles can conflict, and the trigger that decides whether the excess is released or trapped is where the conflict is resolved.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

A structured AAA and a sovereign AAA are different objects wearing the same label

A rating is a statement about expected loss, or about default probability, depending on the agency's scale. Two instruments with the same expected loss can therefore share a rating and behave completely differently, and structured credit is where that gap is widest. The worked figures let the difference be stated precisely rather than asserted.

And MARKETABILITY is where the asset class differs most from its own prospectus, because a market that requires every buyer to model a pool cannot function when everybody stops trusting models at once.

What the source gets right, and the two omissions worth filling

Its structural description is unusually good. The two-way absence of recourse, the bankruptcy-remote vehicle, the tranche ordering with indicative ratings, the observation that multi-tranching appeals to more investors and reduces the cost of borrowing, the amortising-or-not distinction, and the full cash flow sequence in both the ordinary and the insurance-linked case: all of that is correct and most of it a candidate would not derive unaided. Its treatment of insurance-linked securities as a variant of the same machinery, with the money market collateral and the reinsurance agreement in place of an asset sale, is precise.

Two omissions matter. Excess spread is absent, and it is usually the larger form of credit enhancement: 8.36 points against 4.76 after three years on the worked figures. Retention is absent, and it is the regulatory answer to the incentive problem the structure creates. The source's phrase "excess collateral returned" gestures at the retained interest without naming what it is for.

And one thing to be careful with rather than to correct. The source says insurance-linked securities are "like a CDS". That is a good analogy for the payoff, since both pay on a defined event, and a poor one for the risk, because a credit default swap references a corporate credit that is correlated with everything else in a portfolio while a catastrophe bond references a physical event that is not. The similarity is in the contract shape and the difference is in the entire reason for holding it, so the analogy helps a candidate remember the mechanics and misleads them about the purpose.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The seniority waterfall and the identity that a spread compensates for PD times LGD are from government and corporate bonds, where one issuer's default produced three different losses; here the waterfall is manufactured rather than inherited. The private loans that fill most pools are from private debt, and securitisation is what converts an illiquid whole-loan position into rated paper, reintroducing every problem that lecture removed. The Sharpe ratio test applied to catastrophe bonds is from commodities and currency.

Where this goes next. Lecture 11 covers structured products, which use the same special purpose vehicle and the same tranching logic to package a derivative payoff rather than a loan pool, and the source's credit-linked note description is the bridge between the two. The valuation of a tranche, as opposed to the estimation of its expected loss, belongs to valuation, and the credit derivatives that make synthetic tranching possible are in derivatives.

Where this is the same calculation with a supervisor watching. The single-factor model used in step 4 is the Vasicek framework behind the internal ratings-based capital formula, set out in A-IRB capital, where the same correlation appears as a regulatory constant rather than an estimate. The granularity assumption that lets the pool loss be a function of one factor is exactly what credit concentration risk examines when it fails. A bank that securitises must decide whether the assets leave its balance sheet, which is a derecognition question in IFRS 9 impairments and a capital question in capital management. The insurance-linked securities in step 4 sit on the other side of a reinsurance decision, and the counterparty credit exposure the collateralisation removes is measured in counterparty credit risk.

Numbers used here, and where they came from. The instrument types, the tranche ordering with senior AAA, mezzanine around BB and an equity claim paid last, the bankruptcy-remote vehicle, the two-way absence of recourse, over-collateralisation, the amortising distinction and the full cash flow sequence in both the ordinary and insurance-linked cases are all from the F105 summary chapter 6. The single-factor loss distribution is the Vasicek model, standard. Everything else is invented for illustration: the PD, LGD and correlation, the 3 and 7 per cent attachment points, the R105 pool against R100 of notes with its yield, coupon and fee assumptions, the catastrophe bond's three probabilities and its spread grid, the reinsurer default probabilities, and the three recovery scenarios. The rating thresholds used to map expected loss to a letter are illustrative and are not any agency's published scale. The arithmetic on all of them is exact and none is a market observation.
