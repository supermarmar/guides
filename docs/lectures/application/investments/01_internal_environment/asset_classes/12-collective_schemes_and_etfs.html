Collective schemes, index funds and ETFs: the wrapper changes nothing about the assets and everything about the risks

A fund is not an asset class. It is a container, and the useful questions are what the container does that holding the assets directly would not. Three answers: it divides the indivisible, solving the R1bn problem from lecture 8; it manufactures daily liquidity from assets that do not have it; and it wraps the whole thing in a fee stack where the cheap component is usually not the expensive one. The second of those is the one that fails.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The F105 summary lists "Index funds → Features" and "ETFs → Features" as headings with nothing beneath them, so this lecture is built from outside it. What the source does supply, in its portfolio management chapter rather than its asset classes chapter, is the three ways to track an index: full replication holding the index weights, partial replication holding a representative selection, and synthetic replication using cash and derivatives. Those three, plus its list of arguments for and against tracking, are the spine of what follows.

Start with what a fund is for. Lecture 8 established that reaching a 4 per cent income volatility in direct property needs about R1bn, because buildings are indivisible. A collective scheme solves that by dividing the pool, and it is the only solution: no amount of analysis makes a shopping centre divisible. That is the wrapper's first and least controversial job.

Its second job is where the risk lives. An open-ended scheme offers dealing at net asset value, daily, on a portfolio that may not be daily liquid. That is liquidity transformation, and it is the same trade a bank makes between demand deposits and term loans. The remedies are from the same family too: swing pricing, gates, notice periods and side pockets are a fund's version of a liquidity buffer.

Then the arithmetic. Partial replication with 30 of 100 stocks carries a 3.82 per cent tracking error against essentially zero for full replication, which saves perhaps 3 basis points of dealing: a trade whose answer depends entirely on whether the fund is measured on tracking error or on net return. An ETF's price stays near its net asset value inside a band set by the cost of arbitraging it, which is 19 basis points on large-cap equity and 535 in stressed high yield. And a 15 basis point index fund inside a 35 basis point platform with 75 basis points of advice costs 1.30 per cent a year, of which the fund is 12 per cent.

Feynman test A fund is a box you put assets in so that lots of people can own a share of them. The assets do not know they are in a box. What changes is that you can now buy a hundredth of a building, that you have been promised your money back on Tuesday whether or not anyone wants to buy a building on Tuesday, and that four separate people now take a fee for the arrangement.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this is how almost every investor actually holds almost every asset class in this module. The eleven lectures before this one described what to buy; this one describes the thing you are really buying, which is a unit in a scheme with its own terms, its own liquidity promise and its own costs. Getting the asset right and the wrapper wrong is a common and expensive mistake.

The second reason is that the liquidity promise is the fund industry's central risk and it is invisible in normal conditions. An open-ended property fund offering daily dealing has promised something the underlying cannot deliver, and the promise holds until enough people test it. Every suspension of a daily-dealt illiquid fund is the same event, and lecture 3's distinction between credit risk and rollover risk is exactly the right lens.

The third reason is that the source's own objections to index tracking are sharper than most textbook treatments and one of them is specifically actuarial. "Actuarial risk high" means that a fund tracking an index perfectly has zero tracking error and an entirely unmanaged mismatch against its liabilities. On the worked figures a perfect tracker loses 11 per cent of surplus on a 100 basis point fall in rates while its tracking error stays at zero. Those are two different objectives and they can be opposed.

And a fourth reason about honesty in fee comparison. Index funds are cheap and the wrapper around them frequently is not. Arguing about a 15 basis point expense ratio inside a 130 basis point total cost is arguing about 12 per cent of the problem, and lecture 7's fund-of-funds arithmetic is the institutional version of the same error.

Step 4

The detail

The three legal forms, and the one difference that generates everything else

An open-ended collective scheme issues and cancels units on demand at net asset value, so the number of units varies with demand and the investor always transacts at the fund's own valuation. A closed-ended fund has a fixed number of shares which trade between investors, so the fund's capital is stable and the share price is whatever the market pays. An exchange-traded fund is a hybrid: it trades on exchange like a closed-end fund, and it has an open-ended creation mechanism operated by authorised participants who exchange baskets of the underlying securities for blocks of units and back again.

In South Africa the open-ended form is governed by the Collective Investment Schemes Control Act of 2002, administered by the Financial Sector Conduct Authority, which sets the valuation, dealing and disclosure requirements. Listed vehicles, including exchange-traded funds, additionally meet the exchange's listing requirements. That regulatory framing is the source's "less regulation" observation about private equity read in reverse: a collective scheme is one of the most heavily regulated wrappers available, and the protections it carries are a genuine part of what the fee buys.

The consequential difference is not the listing. It is that an open-ended fund must produce cash when investors leave, and a closed-ended fund and an in-kind ETF need not. Everything else in this lecture follows from that sentence.

Partial replication, and how much tracking error it actually costs

Going from 5 to 30 stocks cuts the tracking error from 10.90% to 3.82%. Going from 50 to 95 cuts it only from 2.50% to 0.57%. So most of the benefit of holding more names arrives early, and the last names are bought almost entirely to remove a tracking error that is already small. AND THOSE LAST NAMES ARE THE EXPENSIVE ONES: the smallest constituents, the least liquid, the widest spreads. That is the entire case for partial replication, and the source's description of it, "hold a partial selection representing the index characteristics, multi-factor model, lower costs than full replication, higher tracking error, higher research costs", is exactly right including the research cost, which is the price of choosing which names to drop. SYNTHETIC REPLICATION, the source's third method, achieves a near-zero tracking error with cash and a total return swap, and it substitutes a counterparty exposure for a sampling error. The source's remark that it is "same as partial replication" is true of the cost saving and understates the difference in the risk taken: partial replication's error is diversifiable and symmetric, and a swap counterparty's default is neither.

The ETF arbitrage band: why the price tracks NAV, and when it stops

Authorised participants keep an ETF's price near its net asset value by creating units when the ETF is dear and redeeming when it is cheap. The arbitrage only pays once the mispricing exceeds the friction, so the price trades inside a band whose width is the cost of doing the trade IN THE UNDERLYING. underlying create fee underlying dealing ETF spread band large-cap domestic equity 5.0 bp 10.0 bp 8.0 bp +/- 19 bp developed market equity 5.0 bp 20.0 bp 12.0 bp +/- 31 bp domestic government bonds 5.0 bp 25.0 bp 15.0 bp +/- 38 bp investment grade credit 8.0 bp 60.0 bp 35.0 bp +/- 86 bp emerging market local bonds 10.0 bp 150.0 bp 80.0 bp +/-200 bp high yield credit, stressed 10.0 bp 400.0 bp 250.0 bp +/-535 bp THE BAND IS A PROPERTY OF THE UNDERLYING'S LIQUIDITY, NOT OF THE ETF. An ETF on large-cap equity holds within 19 basis points of net asset value because arbitraging it is nearly free. An ETF on stressed high yield can sit 535 basis points away without any arbitrage becoming profitable, and nothing has broken when it does. WHICH REFRAMES THE FAMOUS STRESSED-MARKET ETF DISCOUNTS. They are the band widening because the underlying stopped trading, and there is a reasonable argument that the ETF price is the BETTER of the two numbers: it is where somebody will actually deal, whereas the net asset value is struck off quotes that may be stale, indicative, or a dealer's best guess. AND THE HONEST WARNING FALLS OUT OF THE SAME ARITHMETIC. An ETF provides intraday liquidity IN THE WRAPPER. It cannot manufacture liquidity in the underlying, and an investor who believes otherwise has misread which of the two they own. THE IN-KIND MECHANISM IS THE REAL INNOVATION, and it is usually described as a tax or cost efficiency when its more important effect is on fairness between holders. When an open-ended fund meets redemptions it sells assets and the dealing costs fall on the remaining holders. When an ETF redeems in kind, the departing authorised participant takes securities rather than cash, so the remaining holders are not diluted by the departure. That is a genuine structural improvement and it has nothing to do with the exchange listing.

AND LECTURE 8'S GEARING IDENTITY APPLIES AGAIN: at 0% gearing a 20% NAV discount implies a 20.0% discount to the assets at 30% gearing 14.0% at 50% gearing 10.0% THE STRUCTURAL TRADE IS CLEAN ONCE STATED. A closed-end fund never faces redemptions, so it can hold genuinely illiquid assets without a liquidity mismatch, which is why investment trusts and listed private equity vehicles take that form. The cost is that the investor's exit is at a market price rather than at NAV. Those are the same fact: the illiquidity has been moved from the fund's balance sheet to the shareholder's exit, and somebody has to hold it. An open-ended fund makes the opposite choice, and the mismatch it creates is the subject of step 5.

The source's three objections to index tracking, developed

The source's list of tracking arguments is unusually pointed. Three items are objections worth expanding, and one is specifically actuarial. 1. "DIFFICULT TO FIND INDEX" No published index matches a fund's liability profile. An index is defined by a market and a liability is defined by a promise, and the two coincide by accident if at all. A fund with inflation-linked liabilities of eighteen years' duration has no index to track, so tracking one means tracking somebody else's problem well. 2. "ACTUARIAL RISK HIGH", which is the sharpest of the three Tracking an index minimises tracking error against the INDEX and says nothing about the mismatch against the LIABILITIES. A perfectly run tracker therefore has a tracking error of zero and an entirely unmanaged actuarial mismatch: liability duration 18 years, tracked bond index duration 7 years rates +100 bp: liabilities -18.0% assets - 7.0% SURPLUS +11.0% rates +200 bp: liabilities -36.0% assets -14.0% SURPLUS +22.0% rates -100 bp: liabilities +18.0% assets + 7.0% SURPLUS -11.0% A PERFECT TRACKER LOSES 11% OF SURPLUS ON A 100 BASIS POINT FALL IN RATES WHILE ITS TRACKING ERROR STAYS AT ZERO. Two objectives, opposed, and only one of them is being measured. 3. "BUYING HIGH" A capitalisation-weighted index holds more of whatever has risen, so a tracker mechanically buys after strength and sells after weakness. Lecture 6 showed this is the momentum tilt nobody chose, and also why cap weighting is the only scheme requiring no rebalancing. Both are true and they are the same property. THE HONEST BALANCE, using the source's own positives, which are real: lower costs, lower volatility, lower downside, diversification achieved, and the efficient markets hypothesis doing the philosophical work. The three objections do not overturn them. What they establish is narrower and more useful: "track an index" is an ASSET-ONLY answer to a liability-driven question, and the reconciliation has to happen somewhere, which is what the portfolio construction module is for.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

An open-ended fund is a bank, and the resemblance is exact

A bank takes deposits repayable on demand and makes loans repayable over years, and manages the gap with a liquidity buffer, a central bank facility and, in extremis, a suspension. An open-ended fund sells units redeemable daily and holds assets that may take weeks to sell, and manages the gap with cash, borrowing lines and, in extremis, a suspension. These are not analogous problems. They are the same problem, and the vocabulary is different only because the regulators are.

In both cases the incentive to leave early is created by the pricing, not by the assets. A depositor runs because par is par. A fund investor redeems because today's NAV is struck before the fund has sold anything, so the cost of raising their cash falls on whoever stays. WHICH IS WHY SWING PRICING IS THE INTELLECTUALLY CORRECT REMEDY and gates are the crude one: swing pricing removes the incentive by charging the leaver, whereas a gate merely delays them and can intensify the rush to be inside the gate.

The ETF's in-kind redemption is a third answer and a better one, because it removes the transfer entirely: the departing holder takes securities and sells them at their own cost. That, rather than the exchange listing or the intraday price, is the structural argument for the ETF form, and it holds for any underlying liquid enough to be delivered in a basket.

What is missing from the source, and what an examination answer needs

The heading is empty, so everything here is external, and it is worth being explicit about what a candidate should be able to supply unaided. The legal forms and the difference between them. The three replication methods, which the source does give elsewhere, with the tracking error arithmetic. The creation and redemption mechanism and why it keeps an ETF near net asset value. The closed-end discount as part of the return. The full cost cascade rather than the expense ratio. And the liquidity transformation problem with at least two named remedies.

One thing the source's own list does supply that most treatments omit, and it deserves the last word. Among its arguments for index tracking it includes "diversification achieved" and among its objections "difficult to find index" and "actuarial risk high". Held together those three make the sophisticated point: a tracker delivers diversification cheaply and reliably, and diversification is not the same thing as suitability. A well-diversified portfolio that does not match the liabilities is a well-solved version of the wrong problem, and no amount of tracking precision repairs that.
