Private debt: the same credit question without a price, a rating or an exit

Lending directly rather than buying a bond removes three things at once: the market price, the rating agency's shared credit work, and the ability to sell. Each removal has a cost, and the yield premium is what pays for them. Work the arithmetic and less than half the headline spread turns out to be premium the investor keeps. The rest is the bill for doing yourself what a public market was doing for free.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Private debt is lending by non-bank lenders directly to borrowers, and the source names five kinds: direct lending, ordinary senior loans from a fund rather than a bank; mezzanine debt, which mixes debt and equity features; venture debt to start-ups; infrastructure debt; and distressed debt, bought at a significant discount from borrowers already in difficulty. They share a structure and differ so much in return distribution that treating them as one asset class is the main error to avoid.

Start with the yield, because that is what draws the money in. A direct lending deal at 11 per cent against a comparable public bond at 9 per cent looks like 200 basis points of free lunch. Decompose it and the picture changes: extra expected credit loss, the origination and diligence work a rating agency would otherwise have done once for everybody, and the monitoring cost over the life of the loan. On the illustrative split in step 4 the investor keeps 90 of the 200 basis points, or 45 per cent of the headline.

Then the source's one-word note on yield, "early payments", which is the most consequential thing in its private debt section. A borrower who prepays after two years raises the lender's internal rate of return from 11.5486 to 12.1863 per cent, which reads as good news and is not. Measured over the five-year horizon the lender actually committed to, with reinvestment at 8 per cent, prepayment at year two returns 9.5734 per cent a year against 10.9188 per cent for holding to maturity: 135 basis points worse. A 2 per cent prepayment penalty recovers only 35 of those, about a quarter.

And the two types at the ends of the range are not really credit at all. Mezzanine with a 5 per cent equity warrant returns 15.000 per cent if the equity is worthless and 21.245 per cent if it triples, so it is priced off a distribution rather than a spread. Distressed claims bought at 40 cents return between minus 100 per cent and plus 84 per cent a year depending on a recovery forecast. Both sit under a fixed income heading and neither behaves like one.

Feynman test A bond is a loan somebody else has already priced, rated and made tradable. Private debt is the same loan with all three of those removed, and the extra yield is the invoice for doing them yourself. Whether the trade is good depends on whether you can do them cheaper than the market did, and on a small deal you cannot.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that private debt is where the framework's marketability dimension is deliberately sold rather than merely accepted. An investor buying a direct loan is not tolerating illiquidity as a side effect; illiquidity is the product, and the premium is the price. That makes it the clean test case for lecture 1's argument that marketability is a property of the pairing: a fund with twenty-five year liabilities and no redemption risk should be a natural buyer, and a fund facing monthly redemptions should not be in the asset class at all, at any spread.

The second reason is that the absence of a rating is a cost with a computable size, and the computation produces a conclusion the source does not draw. Credit analysis is a fixed cost per deal. Spread over a R10m loan it is 100 basis points a year; over a R200m loan it is five. So private debt has a minimum efficient deal size, which sits well above the small end of the market, which contradicts the source's own claim that the asset class serves small and medium enterprises. Step 5 works that through.

The third reason is that the covenant package is the whole of the lender's protection between origination and default, and the source's list mixes two kinds of covenant that behave completely differently. Maintenance covenants fail on deterioration and are an early warning system. Incurrence covenants fail only on an action, so a borrower sliding quietly downhill breaches nothing. Reading a term sheet without separating them is reading it without understanding what was bought.

And a fourth reason, which is about the numbers in every private debt marketing document you will ever see. With no market price, valuations come from a model or an appraisal, and appraisals are smoothed. Under the standard smoothing model with a plausible parameter, a reported volatility of 6 per cent corresponds to a true volatility of 12 per cent, and any Sharpe ratio computed on the reported series is overstated by a factor of two. The correction is arithmetic and the effect is enormous.

Step 4

The detail

The five types, and why only two of them are credit

Direct lending is a loan from a non-bank lender on terms a bank would recognise: senior, secured, covenanted, floating or fixed, with a stated term. Infrastructure debt is the same structure against a specific long-lived asset, and the source's separate heading reflects the collateral rather than any difference in mechanics. Those two are credit instruments and the framework applies to them straightforwardly.

Venture debt is a loan to a company with no profits, which means repayment depends on the next equity round rather than on operating cash flow. The lender is therefore underwriting the equity market's appetite, not the borrower's earnings, and normally takes warrants to be compensated for it. Mezzanine debt the source describes as "debt and equity finance", with conversion rights and an equity option on default, and its return distribution is genuinely hybrid. Distressed debt is a claim on a borrower already failing, bought at what the source calls a significant discount, where the entire return is a recovery forecast. Those last three have return distributions with a long right tail and a real probability of total loss, which is not what fixed income means.

Security: what the absence of a rating actually costs

The source's security note for private debt reads: SMEs' first issue, an alternative to a bank loan, no credit rating, covenants, and freeing up credit lines. The last point is from the borrower's side and is worth keeping in view, because it explains supply: a company that borrows from a fund preserves its bank facilities for working capital, which has option value it can measure. The lender's side of the same trade is the "no credit rating" item, and that is a cost with a size.

A rating is a FIXED COST SHARED by every holder of the paper. Remove it and every lender repeats the credit work privately. Covenants: the source's list, split into the two kinds that behave differently

The source gives seven: interest and capital cover limits, liquidity ratio limits, dividend limits, a limit on issuing equal or higher-ranking debt, minimum securitised asset value, restrictions on varying the collateral, and further IFRS 9 provisions. That is a good list and it conceals a distinction that decides what the package is worth.

It fails on deterioration, whether or not the borrower does anything, which gives the lender a seat at the table while there is still something to negotiate over. An INCURRENCE covenant fails only on an action. A borrower sliding quietly downhill and proposing nothing breaches none of them, so the lender learns about the problem when the interest payment is missed. THE ODD ONE OUT IS WORTH NOTICING. A covenant referencing "further IFRS 9 provisions" tests the BORROWER'S OWN impairment charge, which only makes sense where the borrower is itself a lender: a non-bank finance company, a leasing business, a microlender. It is a maintenance covenant on a number the borrower's own management estimates, which makes it powerful and contestable in equal measure. AND THE MARKET DEVELOPMENT THE SOURCE DOES NOT MENTION. Institutional private credit has moved substantially towards COVENANT-LITE terms, which means keeping the incurrence column and dropping the maintenance one. That removes the early warning while leaving the subordination unchanged, so it is a real reduction in the lender's position and should be paid for in spread. Whether it is depends on how much money is chasing deals.

Yield: the source says "early payments", and it is the most important two words in the section

Private loans prepay. The borrower repays early when it can refinance more cheaply, which is when rates have fallen or its own credit has improved, so the lender's best loans go away and the worst ones stay. That is adverse selection through the exit door, and it is the callable bond of lecture 4 in a private wrapper.

THE SPLIT IS ILLUSTRATIVE AND THE STRUCTURE IS NOT. Three of the four components are costs the investor bears and only one is a premium it earns, so comparing gross yields between private and public debt overstates the advantage by construction. An answer that quotes the 200 basis points and stops has not answered the question. AND THE THREE COSTS SCALE DIFFERENTLY. Expected credit loss scales with the amount lent. Diligence is fixed per deal, so it falls with deal size. Monitoring is roughly fixed per deal per year, so it falls with size and rises with term. That is why the economics of private debt are the economics of DEAL SIZE, which is a completely different sentence from the economics of credit.

The source is right to put it in its private debt list, and an investor who allocates to it out of a bond budget has mismeasured its risk by a wide margin.

Marketability and valuation, which are the same problem twice

The source's marketability note is blunt and correct: not widely held, not actively traded. Its expenses note is also correct and cuts the other way: administration costs are lower than for a public issue, because there is no listing, no prospectus and no continuing disclosure obligation. Both are consequences of the same fact, which is that there is no market. The consequence the source does not draw is what happens to the reported numbers when there is no price.

With no market price, a valuation is a model or an appraisal, and an appraiser anchors on the previous valuation. The standard model of that behaviour, from Geltner's work on property returns, is a partial adjustment: r*(t) = a r(t) + (1 - a) r*(t-1) where r* is the REPORTED return, r the true return, and a the fraction of new information reflected each period. The first-order autocorrelation of the reported series equals 1 - a: reported autocorrelation 0.80 -> a = 0.20 -> true sd is 3.00x reported reported autocorrelation 0.60 -> a = 0.40 -> true sd is 2.00x reported reported autocorrelation 0.30 -> a = 0.70 -> true sd is 1.36x reported reported autocorrelation 0.00 -> a = 1.00 -> no smoothing A private debt or property return series with strongly positive autocorrelation is telling you its own smoothing parameter. The correct response is to unsmooth before using it in any risk calculation, not to admire the low volatility.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The source says private debt serves SMEs, and its own economics say it cannot

The security note opens with "SMEs first issue", which is the standard account of why the asset class exists: banks retrenched after the financial crisis, mid-sized companies could not reach the public bond market, and funds stepped into the gap. The account is true about the origin and it does not survive the arithmetic about the destination.

Diligence is a fixed cost per deal. At R500,000 it is 100 basis points a year on a five-year R10m loan and five basis points on a R200m one. Since the whole strategy exists to capture an illiquidity premium of perhaps 90 basis points, a R10m deal costs more to underwrite than it can possibly earn. A fund can only reach the small end by spending less on diligence, which means underwriting worse, which raises the expected credit loss component and eats the premium from the other direction.

So direct lending funds cluster in the mid-market, and the genuinely small borrower is served by a bank using a scorecard. That is not a lesser technology; it is a rating by another name, with the fixed cost of building the model shared across tens of thousands of accounts instead of one. The economics that push private debt away from small borrowers are the same economics that make a scorecard the right answer for them, which is a point the investments and banking tracks make from opposite directions and rarely at the same time.

The honest version of the source's claim is therefore narrower and more useful: private debt serves borrowers too large for a scorecard and too small or too complex for a public issue, which is a real gap and a much smaller one than "SMEs".

What the investor is actually buying is information and control, not yield

Set the yield to one side and ask what a private lender has that a bondholder does not. It has the borrower's management on the telephone. It has a covenant package tested every quarter. It has a say in whether the borrower may pay a dividend, borrow more, or swap the collateral. And it has a position small enough in number of holders that a restructuring can be negotiated rather than litigated.

The private lender swaps LIQUIDITY AND PRICE TRANSPARENCY for INFORMATION AND CONTROL, and the yield premium is the balancing item rather than the substance of the deal. Which explains a behaviour that otherwise looks irrational: experienced private credit investors will decline a deal at a wide spread and accept one at a narrow spread with a better covenant package. They are not being paid in spread. They are being paid in the ability to act before the loss happens, and lecture 4's arithmetic shows why that is worth more than a coupon: once the borrower defaults, the recovery is set by the waterfall and nothing the lender does afterwards changes it much.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The eight dimensions and the argument that marketability is a property of the pairing rather than of the asset are from the asset class framework, and this lecture is the cleanest illustration of that claim in the module. Seniority, the waterfall arithmetic and the treatment of prepayment as an issuer-held option come from government and corporate bonds. The credit facilities and factoring arrangements in money market instruments are private debt at the short end, priced the same way.

Where this goes next. Lecture 7 takes the private markets machinery, the fund structure, the drawdown cycle and the carried interest, and applies it to equity rather than debt, where the same smoothing and valuation problems appear in a more acute form. Lecture 8 meets infrastructure again from the equity side. Lecture 10 shows what happens when private loans are pooled and tranched instead of held, which converts an illiquid whole-loan position into rated paper and reintroduces every problem this lecture removed.

Where the same problems appear with a supervisor watching. The diligence-versus-scorecard argument in step 5 is the origination question in application scoring, where the fixed cost of a model is shared across a portfolio rather than a deal. The expected credit loss component of the spread is estimated formally in credit risk, and the covenant-triggered forbearance that follows a maintenance breach is a significant increase in credit risk under IFRS 9 impairments, which is also what the source's unusual "further IFRS 9 provisions" covenant is pointing at. Concentration by construction is the subject of credit concentration risk, and a portfolio of twenty private loans is exactly the case where the granularity assumption fails hardest.

Numbers used here, and where they came from. The five instrument types, the seven covenants, the security and marketability notes and the observation that administration costs are lower are all from the F105 summary chapter 6. The partial adjustment model of appraisal smoothing and the variance ratio a/(2 − a) are from Geltner's work on unsmoothing property returns (1991 and 1993), and the identification of the smoothing parameter from the reported series' first-order autocorrelation is standard. Everything else is invented for illustration: the 11 and 9 per cent yields and the four-way split of the 200 basis point spread, the R500,000 diligence cost, the R100 loan and its 8 per cent reinvestment assumption, every parameter of the mezzanine deal, the 40 cent purchase price and the three distressed scenarios with their probabilities. The arithmetic on all of them is exact and none of them is a market observation.
