New ways of investing in old asset classes: the framework as a test for innovation

The syllabus closes objective (i) with this phrase and the summary has nothing under it, which is fitting: the topic is not a list to memorise but a method. Every innovation here changes exactly one or two of lecture 1's eight dimensions, degrades a third, and leaves the underlying cash flows untouched. Naming which dimension moved is the whole of the assessment, and it works on innovations that have not been invented yet.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The F105 syllabus lists "new ways of investing in old asset classes" as the last bullet of the objective on specialist financial instruments, and the summary supplies nothing for it. That absence is an opportunity rather than a problem, because the useful answer is not a catalogue of products, which would date within a year, but a test that survives new products: every innovation in this space changes one or two of the eight dimensions from lecture 1, and the classification is the assessment.

Four examples make the method concrete. A secondary purchase of a private equity fund interest at lecture 7's year-four net asset value earns 17.6849 per cent against the primary investor's 13.675 per cent, and 24.0738 per cent at a 15 per cent discount. The gain is the entire J-curve. Dimension changed: marketability, on a structure lecture 7 described as unbreakable.

A co-investment alongside a fund, typically fee-free, delivers the gross return instead of the net. A 70/30 split between fund and co-investment lifts the net return from 10.8019 to 12.0613 per cent, an improvement of 1.26 percentage points a year achieved by writing a cheque rather than by finding a better manager. Dimension changed: expenses, and nothing else.

Synthetic exposure through a total return swap costs a financing spread every year, where a physical holding costs a round trip once. At a 40 basis point spread against a 30 basis point round trip the break-even holding period is 0.81 years, which is why swaps dominate short-horizon exposure to illiquid markets and barely exist in large-cap equity. And fractionalisation takes lecture 8's divisibility problem, which needed R1bn to solve, and solves it with R250,000. Both of those improve one dimension and degrade security, by putting a counterparty between the investor and the asset.

Feynman test Someone offers you a new way to own something old. Ask which of the eight questions from the first lecture now has a different answer. If none of them does, you have been sold a repackaging. If one of them does, ask whether it was the question that was actually bothering you, and then ask what got worse, because something always does.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this is the module's closing lecture and the framework has earned the right to be used as a tool rather than described. Thirteen lectures applied eight dimensions to thirteen asset classes. This one turns the framework round and applies it to the wrappers, which is where all the genuine change of the last three decades has happened. The assets are old; the ways of holding them are not.

The second reason is examination-shaped. A question on this bullet cannot be answered by recall, because the examiner can name a product that postdates every textbook. It can be answered by method: name the dimension, say whether it was binding for the investor in the question, and say what the innovation cost elsewhere. That answer works on anything.

The third reason is that the innovations with the largest genuine effect are the least glamorous. Fractionalisation is transformative for an investor with R200,000 and irrelevant to one with R1bn. Co-investment adds 1.26 percentage points for anybody with the staff to underwrite a deal in days. Neither involves a new asset, a new market or a new theory, and both beat most of what is marketed as innovation.

And a fourth reason, which is the module's own conclusion. Not one innovation in this lecture changes the underlying cash flows. The rent is the rent, the coupon is the coupon, the company earns what it earns. Every device here rearranges who bears what and who is paid for it, which is exactly what lecture 10 established about tranching: the pool's expected loss does not move.

Secondaries: buying a fund interest after the J-curve

SO A SECONDARY MARKET DOES TWO DISTINCT THINGS. For the buyer it removes the J-curve and may add a discount. For the seller it creates an exit from a structure lecture 7 described as effectively unbreakable, which is a change in the term dimension for one party and the marketability dimension for the other. MARKETABILITY. The assets, the fees and the fund's term are all unaltered. That is the cleanest single-dimension innovation in this lecture.

Co-investment: removing a fee layer, measured

Lecture 7 showed a 2 per cent management fee and 20 per cent carry costing the investor 4.20 percentage points at a 15 per cent gross return on invested capital, so a net 10.8019 per cent. A co-investment alongside the fund, conventionally offered without fees to significant limited partners, delivers the gross return. fund / co-invest split blended net return improvement 100% / 0% 10.8019% - 80% / 20% 11.6415% +0.8396 pp 70% / 30% 12.0613% +1.2594 pp 60% / 40% 12.4811% +1.6792 pp 50% / 50% 12.9009% +2.0990 pp 30% / 70% 13.7406% +2.9387 pp A 70/30 SPLIT LIFTS THE NET RETURN BY 1.26 PERCENTAGE POINTS A YEAR, achieved by writing a cheque rather than by finding a better manager. Over a ten-year programme that compounds into a materially different outcome from an identical portfolio of assets selected by an identical manager. WHICH DIMENSION CHANGED? EXPENSES, and nothing else at all. The assets are the same assets, the selection is the same selection, and the exposure is identical. THE COSTS THAT DO NOT APPEAR IN THE ARITHMETIC, and they are why this is not universal: STAFFING. The investor must be able to underwrite a single deal on the manager's timetable, which is days rather than months. That is a permanent internal cost set against an annual saving. ADVERSE SELECTION. The deals offered as co-investments are the ones the manager cannot or will not fund alone. Sometimes that is because the cheque is large and the fund is nearly committed, which is benign; sometimes it is because the manager's own conviction is thin, which is not. The investor cannot always tell which. CONCENTRATION. A co-investment is one asset, so lecture 10's granularity assumption fails entirely and the position's idiosyncratic risk is undiversified. VINTAGE. The co-investment closes when the deal does, so it carries no diversification across time, which is one of the few free things a fund programme provides.

Lecture 11's argument about a capital guarantee being an unsecured claim applies without modification: at a 1 per cent annual counterparty default probability and a 40 per cent loss given default, the expected credit cost is 40 basis points a year, which on these figures is the entire financing spread again.

Fractionalisation: lecture 8's divisibility problem, solved cheaply

Lecture 8 established that income volatility falls as 0.2035 divided by the square root of the number of buildings, so reaching 4.07 per cent needs twenty-five buildings and roughly R1bn at R40m each. route unit size capital for 25 buildings income volatility direct ownership R40,000,000 R1,000,000,000 4.07% a fractional platform R 10,000 R 250,000 4.07% a listed property fund R 100 R 2,500 4.07% what each wealth level can reach: R 200,000 direct: cannot buy one building at all fractional: 4.55% R 1,000,000 direct: cannot buy one building at all fractional: 4.07% R 10,000,000 direct: cannot buy one building at all fractional: 4.07% R 200,000,000 direct: 5 buildings, volatility 9.10% fractional: 4.07% R1,000,000,000 direct: 25 buildings, volatility 4.07% fractional: 4.07% THE CHANGE IS REAL AND IT IS LARGE. A factor of four thousand in the capital needed to reach the same diversification. And it is the one dimension where a wrapper does something no amount of analysis can substitute for: a shopping centre is indivisible, and only a divided claim on it makes a small holding possible. Note that the collective scheme of lecture 12 already did this, and did it better on the capital requirement. What fractional platforms add is the ability to choose the individual asset rather than taking the fund's portfolio, which trades diversification for control. An investor buying fractions of three specific buildings has divisibility without diversification, which is the worst of both and is easy to do accidentally. AND THE COST IS ALWAYS THE SAME COST: the investor holds a claim on a PLATFORM rather than on a building. Four questions settle whether that is acceptable: is the asset-holding structure BANKRUPTCY REMOTE from the platform, as in lecture 10? who values the assets and how often, given lecture 8's circularity problem? what is the exit? does the platform make a market, or does it hope one exists? what happens to the units if the platform itself fails? The first and last are the ones that have actually gone wrong in practice, and neither is a question about the buildings.

Listed private assets, and securitising new collateral

Two further routes complete the set, and both have already been computed elsewhere in this module. Listed private assets, meaning listed private equity vehicles, listed infrastructure funds and real estate investment trusts, take an illiquid pool and give it a daily price. The dimension changed is marketability; the dimension degraded is spread, because the listed price carries equity sentiment in the short run and, as lecture 8's identity showed, a discount to net asset value understates the market's view of the underlying by a factor of one minus gearing. Lecture 12's closed-end discount decomposition applies without modification.

Securitising new collateral extends lecture 10's machinery to cash flows that have never been packaged before: royalty streams, litigation finance receivables, solar installation payments, trade receivables from new sectors. The dimensions changed are marketability, since an unrated cash flow becomes rated paper, and security, since tranching manufactures a senior claim. What is degraded is the reliability of the correlation estimate, and this is the one case where the degradation is worse than in the established markets: lecture 10 showed the senior tranche's expected loss rising by a factor of 1,850 between a 10 and a 60 per cent correlation, and a pool with no loss history has no basis for choosing between them.

The recurring pattern in new collateral Every new securitisable asset class arrives with a short, benign loss history, because it has only existed during a favourable period. The correlation estimate is therefore drawn from a sample containing no downturn, which is exactly the condition lecture 10 identified as producing a systematically understated senior expected loss. This is not a prediction about any particular market. It is an observation that the structural feature which caused the problem in residential mortgages is present by construction in every genuinely new pool, and that the first cycle is the test.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The three questions, which work on innovations that do not exist yet

The value of a framework is that it outlasts its examples. Everything named in this lecture will be superseded, and the method will not, because it rests on the observation that an investment can only be changed in eight ways and a wrapper can only reach some of them.

1. WHICH DIMENSION DOES IT CHANGE? If the honest answer is none, the innovation is a repackaging and the fee is the only real change. That is the commonest case and it is worth saying without embarrassment. If the answer is one or two, name them and proceed. 2. WAS THAT DIMENSION BINDING FOR THIS INVESTOR? This is where lecture 1's central argument returns. Three dimensions (tax, marketability, currency) describe a PAIRING of asset and investor, so an innovation touching one of them helps some investors and not others: fractionalisation transformative at R200,000, irrelevant at R1bn secondaries valuable to a seller who needs an exit, and to a buyer with capital and no legacy programme. worthless to a patient investor already invested at the right vintage co-investment valuable only to an investor with underwriting staff synthetic exposure valuable only for short horizons or illiquid underlyings factor indices valuable only to an investor currently paying alpha fees for beta exposure, which is the point of measuring first listed private assets valuable to an investor who needs daily pricing, and a cost to one who does not, because the sentiment comes free SO THE SAME INNOVATION IS PROGRESS AND WASTE DEPENDING ON THE HOLDER, and a recommendation that does not name the investor has not been made. 3. WHAT DID IT DEGRADE? Six of the seven innovations here improve marketability or expenses. Four of them degrade SECURITY by inserting a counterparty between the investor and the asset: a swap dealer, a platform, an issuer, a special purpose vehicle. That is not an argument against any of them. It is a reason to price it, and lecture 11 gave the method: an issuer at a 1% annual default probability and a 40% loss given default costs 1.96% of principal over five years, which frequently exceeds the saving the innovation was bought for.

What none of them changes, which is the module's conclusion

Not one innovation in this lecture alters the underlying cash flows. The rent a shopping centre collects does not depend on whether it is owned directly, through a fund, through a listed trust or in ten-thousand-rand fractions. A company's earnings do not depend on whether its shares are held physically or through a swap. A loan pool's expected loss, as lecture 10 proved arithmetically, does not depend on how it is tranched.

WHAT THE MODULE HAS ESTABLISHED, ACROSS FOURTEEN LECTURES the eight dimensions describe every holding, and three of them describe a PAIRING rather than the asset, which is why the same asset ranks differently for different investors (lecture 1) the quoted number is rarely the return: a discount rate is not a yield, a headline spread is mostly cost, an IRR is not a multiple, a participation rate is an output, and a reported volatility may be half the truth (lectures 3, 5, 7, 11, 8) in every structure somebody has sold something: dividends, volatility, credit protection, a prepayment option, or the upside above a cap (lectures 4, 9, 11) redistributing risk does not reduce it, and the parameter deciding the redistribution is usually the one nobody can observe (lectures 10, 8, 5) the wrapper's honest contributions are divisibility, tax treatment and governance, and its usual cost is a counterparty (lectures 12, 13, 14) AND THE ONE SENTENCE THE WHOLE MODULE SUPPORTS: an asset's cash flows come from the economic activity underneath it, and everything else in finance is a decision about who bears which part of them and who is paid for bearing it. That is not a cynical claim. It is why the framework works: the eight dimensions are the complete list of ways that decision can be varied.

Where the syllabus goes from here

This closes objective (i), the specialist financial instruments. Objective (j) is derivative contracts, their trading, payoffs and use by an investment manager, which is the derivatives module and which several lectures here have leaned on without developing. Objective (k) is where actuarial techniques build an investment strategy, which is the portfolio construction and management modules, and where the reconciliation this module kept deferring finally has to happen: an asset-only description of thirteen asset classes has to be turned into a portfolio for a specific liability.

Three threads were left deliberately open and are picked up there. The correlation between asset classes, which the eight-dimension framework has no slot for and which portfolio theory says dominates. The liability, which decided the marketability, term and currency answers throughout and was never itself described. And the smoothing correction, which four separate lectures established and which has to be applied before any of these classes can be compared in an optimiser.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes, which is the whole module. The eight dimensions and the pairing argument are from the asset class framework, and this lecture is that framework used as a tool rather than described. The J-curve and the fee wedge the secondaries and co-investment calculations extend are from private equity. The divisibility arithmetic is from property and infrastructure, the tranching correlation problem from securitisation and insurance-linked securities, the issuer credit pricing from structured products, the closed-end discount from collective schemes and ETFs, and the beta-against-alpha decomposition from hedge funds. Every calculation in this lecture extends one from earlier in the module rather than introducing a new method, which is deliberate.

Where the module goes next. This closes syllabus objective (i). Objective (j), derivative contracts and their use by an investment manager, is derivatives, which develops the swaps, futures and options that several lectures here used without deriving. Objective (k), using actuarial techniques to build an investment strategy, is portfolio construction and portfolio management, where the three threads left open (correlation between classes, the liability itself, and the smoothing correction) have to be resolved. Valuation supplies the prices this module took as given, and performance measurement judges the result.

Where the same innovations appear from the other side. A bank is the counterparty in most of them: the swap dealer in synthetic exposure, the platform's banker, the arranger of a new securitisation. Its exposure to the investor is in counterparty credit risk, and its own decision to securitise new collateral is a capital and derecognition question in capital management. The correlation estimate that decides a new pool's senior tranche is the same parameter, and the same problem, as in A-IRB capital, where a regulator fixes it precisely because it cannot be reliably estimated. Fractional and platform-based investment offerings are a conduct and licensing matter for the FSCA.
