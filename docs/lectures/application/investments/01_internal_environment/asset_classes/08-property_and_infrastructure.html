Property and infrastructure: two asset classes whose risk is a gearing multiple of something else

A building is a perpetuity, so its value moves on a capitalisation rate nobody observed, with an effective duration of twenty-two years at a four and a half per cent yield. A regulated utility earns whatever a regulator allows, geared, so a fifty basis point determination moves the equity return by a hundred and twenty-five. In both cases the headline risk (tenants, traffic) is the smaller one, and the real exposure is to a number decided elsewhere.

Step 1

The 30-second version

These two sit together because they share a shape: a long-lived physical asset producing a contracted income stream, valued by capitalising that income, with no market price to check the answer against. They differ in who decides the income. For property it is a tenant and a lease. For infrastructure it is usually a regulator or a government contract, and occasionally the travelling public.

Property first. A building is a perpetuity, so its value is the net rent divided by a yield, and its effective duration is one over that yield: 14.29 years at 7 per cent and 22.22 years at 4.5 per cent. A 25 basis point move in the capitalisation rate is worth 3.45 per cent of capital at the first and 5.26 per cent at the second. That yield is inferred from comparable transactions rather than observed, so the input that dominates the valuation is the one with the least evidence behind it.

Buildings are also lumpy, which turns the framework's divisibility sub-item into arithmetic. On five-year leases with a 40 per cent chance of a nine-month void at each expiry, expected income is 94.0 per cent of full rent whatever the portfolio size, but income volatility is 20.35 per cent with one building, 6.43 per cent with ten and 4.07 per cent with twenty-five. At R40m a building, getting income volatility down to 4 per cent needs roughly R1bn of property. The same asset class is a different risk at a different scale.

Infrastructure next. A regulated utility at 60 per cent gearing earning an allowed 7 per cent on its asset base delivers 11.50 per cent to equity. Cut the allowed return by 50 basis points and equity gets 10.25 per cent: a 125 basis point fall, amplified by exactly one over one minus gearing. The regulator's five-yearly determination is a bigger risk than demand or operations, and the source's own security list puts regulation second.

Feynman test You buy a building for twenty times its rent. The rent is written down in a lease and you know it. The twenty is a number a valuer inferred from what somebody else paid for a different building last year, and almost all of your gain or loss comes from that number changing. Infrastructure is the same trick with a regulator holding the multiplier instead of a valuer.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that both classes are chosen for liability matching and both are worse at it than they look. Property is described as a real asset because rents review upward, and infrastructure often has explicit inflation linkage in its regulatory formula, so both attract funds with inflation-linked liabilities. The matching is genuine and it is partial: rent reviews are periodic rather than continuous, void periods break the income entirely, and a regulator can reset the allowed return at the next determination.

The second reason is that the valuation of both is a model, which means everything lecture 5 established about appraisal smoothing applies here with a larger parameter. The source itself names the mechanism in one word, circularity: a valuer values by reference to comparables, and where transactions are scarce the comparables are other valuations. A property index with a reported 6 per cent volatility and an 0.80 autocorrelation implies a true volatility of 18 per cent.

The third reason is that indirect property, the listed vehicle, is not the same investment as direct property even when it holds the same buildings, and the difference is gearing. A 20 per cent discount to net asset value at 40 per cent gearing implies the market is marking the buildings only 12 per cent below the valuer, and at 70 per cent gearing only 6. Reading a sector NAV discount as a statement about property values, without dividing by one minus gearing, gets the answer wrong by a factor of two or three.

And a fourth reason, which is about the source's own words. It lists infrastructure under spread as both a "defensive asset" and carrying "demand risk", which reads as a contradiction and is not. It is two different assets: an availability-based contract has zero demand elasticity, and a demand-based concession has an operating leverage of 2.5 on the worked figures. Quoting one expected return for the pair is the error the pairing invites.

The mental model

Two long-lived assets, two multipliers set by somebody else, and the gearing that amplifies both.

Term and reversion: an under-rented building is two cash flows, not one

A lease fixes the rent until the next review, at which point it moves to the market rate. If the passing rent is below the estimated rental value the property is under-rented, and the two income streams have different risk: the passing rent is contractual and short, the reversion to market rent is uncertain and long. They therefore take different yields, which is the whole content of the term and reversion method.

A shop let at R500,000 passing, three years to the next review, ERV R700,000. Term yield 6.0% (the passing rent is contractual and secure). Reversion yield 6.5% (the market rent in three years is not). THE OTHER DIFFERENCE BETWEEN DIRECT AND INDIRECT is what you are exposed to in the short run. A listed property share trades every day with the equity market and reprices with equity sentiment; the buildings underneath do not. Over long horizons the two converge because the same rents are being capitalised. So indirect property is property in the long run and equity in the short run, and which of those matters depends on the holding period, which is lecture 1's argument again.

Infrastructure: the types, and where the risk actually sits

The source splits infrastructure into economic assets (highways, sewerage, energy distribution, telecommunications) and social assets (schools, universities, prisons, hospitals, public housing). Its security list is natural monopolies, regulation, the government's role, privatisation, political risk, operational risk and interest rate risk, which is the right list and is not in order of importance. Regulation is the dominant one, and the reason is gearing.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Both are sold as real assets, and the linkage is weaker than the label

Property and infrastructure attract funds with inflation-linked liabilities because rents review upward and regulatory formulae usually contain an inflation term. The claim is directionally right and the matching is partial in three specific ways, each of which can be stated precisely rather than gestured at.

The same scepticism deserves to be applied to the property and infrastructure claim, and the source does not apply it.

What both asset classes really sell is a claim on somebody else's decision

Strip away the physical asset and each is a stream of payments whose size is set by a party the investor does not control and cannot easily predict. For property that party is the valuer, in the short run, and the tenant market in the long run. For regulated infrastructure it is the regulator. For an availability-based contract it is a government counterparty. Only for a demand-based concession is the investor exposed to something as impersonal as a market.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The eight dimensions, and the divisibility sub-item that the void arithmetic prices, are from the asset class framework, whose spread decomposition also gave property the highest income share of variance in the module at 22.3 per cent. The appraisal smoothing model, the variance ratio and the identification of the smoothing parameter from autocorrelation are from private debt, and infrastructure debt appears there as the lending side of the same assets. The operating leverage arithmetic applied to the toll road is from listed equity, and the fund structure through which most infrastructure equity is held is in private equity. Perpetuity valuation and the duration of a level perpetuity are in time value of money.

Where this goes next. Lecture 9 covers commodities, the other real asset, and returns to currency as a source of return. Lecture 12's collective schemes are the vehicle through which the divisibility problem computed here is usually solved. Property valuation as a discipline, including the yield derivation this lecture takes as given, is in valuation; the index construction problems belong to indices; and the question of how much illiquid property a fund can hold given its liabilities is portfolio construction.

Where the same assets appear with a different question attached. A bank lending against commercial property meets the same valuation uncertainty as a loan-to-value covenant problem, in credit risk, where the collateral value that determines loss given default is the same appraiser's opinion computed here. Property exposure concentrated by sector is the worked example in credit concentration risk. The long duration established in step 4 makes property a structural interest rate position, which is the subject of interest rate risk, and the physical exposure of long-lived infrastructure to a changing climate is climate risk.
