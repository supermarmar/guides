Commodities and currency: two positions that produce no income and are held anyway

Every other asset in this module is a claim on somebody. A commodity is a thing, and a currency is a unit of account, and neither owes you anything. That changes what a return can even mean: for a commodity it is entirely price less carry, and a one per cent monthly contango costs 11.36 per cent a year with the spot price unchanged. For a currency the expected return is zero under parity, so the whole question is what the position does to the variance.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The source gives commodities five physical categories, energy, precious metals, base metals, agriculture and meat and livestock, an index built from twenty-four liquid futures contracts, and derivatives that exist to reduce farmers' risks by fixing type, quality, delivery date, quantity, delivery site and package size. Its features are brief: real returns driven by real economics, low correlation, short term. Every one of those is true and each hides an arithmetic point.

Start with the one that dominates everything. Almost nobody holds physical commodities, so a commodity investment is a rolling futures position, and its return has three parts: the collateral return, the spot return and the roll return. At a one per cent monthly contango the roll return is minus 11.36 per cent a year and minus 45.28 per cent over five, with the spot price unchanged throughout. Collateral at seven per cent still leaves the investor 23.26 per cent down over five years on a commodity that did not move.

Second, a commodity's yield is negative. Storage, insurance and spoilage are a cost, offset only by a convenience yield that exists when somebody needs the physical thing today. The cost of carry relation makes that measurable: with a spot of 100, a one-year future at 95, a risk-free rate of 8 per cent and 2 per cent storage, the implied convenience yield is 15.13 per cent. It is the only asset in this module whose yield row answers "you pay".

Third, currency. Covered interest parity fixes the forward rate, so hedging a dollar asset back into rands at an 8 against 4 per cent differential adds 3.85 per cent a year rather than costing anything. That is not a free lunch: uncovered parity says the rand is expected to depreciate by the same amount, so the hedge locks in what the unhedged position expects and changes the variance rather than the mean. The carry trade is the bet that it does not, and at a 15 per cent currency volatility it loses money 39.88 per cent of the time.

Feynman test A share pays dividends, a bond pays coupons, a building pays rent. A barrel of oil sends you a storage bill, and you cannot even hold it, so you hold a promise to buy oil next month which you replace every month at whatever the next month costs. Do that in a market where next month is dearer than this month and you lose money steadily while the oil price does nothing at all.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the gap between a commodity price and a commodity investment is the largest gap between a headline and a return anywhere in this module. An investor who reads that oil rose 40 per cent over five years and finds their commodity fund flat has not been defrauded; they have paid the roll. Understanding that before allocating is the difference between a considered position and a disappointment.

The second reason is that the framework's yield dimension finally meets an asset that answers negatively, which forces the question the other lectures let you avoid. Every rand of commodity return must come from the price. So the case for holding one is a forecast about prices, not a claim on an issuer, and it has to be argued as a forecast.

The third reason is that currency is the one exposure an investor acquires by doing nothing. A fund that has not hedged has a position; it simply has not written it down. Lecture 1 gave the hedging threshold as a variance condition; this lecture supplies what the hedge costs, which is the missing half of the decision and which is often negative for a high-rate domestic investor.

And a fourth reason, about the source's most-quoted phrase. "Low correlation" is the standard argument for commodities and it is incomplete on its own. Adding an asset improves a portfolio if and only if its Sharpe ratio exceeds the correlation times the existing portfolio's Sharpe ratio, which turns "low correlation" into a break-even number: 0.3750 on the figures in step 4. If a commodity carries no risk premium at all, the break-even correlation is zero and it must be a hedge rather than a diversifier.

The mental model

Two income-free positions, two decompositions, and the two tests that decide whether either belongs in a portfolio.

Step 4

The detail

The three returns, and why the roll dominates

The source describes the physical categories and the index and then stops, which leaves the most important mechanism unstated. A commodity investor almost never takes delivery. What they hold is a futures contract, fully collateralised, which expires and must be replaced with the next one. If the next contract costs more than the expiring one, the replacement is a loss, and it happens every month for as long as the position is held.

That single arithmetic fact accounts for most of the gap between commodity index returns and the prices quoted in the financial press, and the source's description of the index as a "weighted average of prices" conceals it entirely, because an index of futures is not an index of prices. THE PRACTICAL TEST BEFORE ALLOCATING: look at the shape of the curve, not the level of the price. A view that oil will rise 20% over three years is not enough. At a 1% monthly contango the curve will take 30.36% off you over the same period, so the view has to be that oil rises more than 30% before the position breaks even.

What "weighted average" hides: production weighting

The source calls the GSCI a "weighted average of prices of 24 liquid futures contracts". The weights are the point, and they are WORLD PRODUCTION QUANTITIES. Not equal weights, and not market capitalisation, which does not exist for a commodity because nobody issues one. sector production-weighted equal sector weight energy 58.0% 20.0% industrial metals 12.0% 20.0% precious metals 5.0% 20.0% agriculture 19.0% 20.0% livestock 6.0% 20.0% AN OIL SHOCK, AND WHAT EACH SCHEME REPORTS: energy +50%, everything else flat -> production-weighted +29.00%, equal +10.00% energy +25% -> +14.50%, +5.00% energy -25% -> -14.50%, -5.00% energy -50% -> -29.00%, -10.00% A PRODUCTION-WEIGHTED COMMODITY INDEX IS CLOSE TO AN OIL INDEX WITH A GARNISH. So an investor allocating to "commodities" for diversification, and buying the production-weighted version, has bought a single-factor exposure with a small hedge attached. That matters for the source's low-correlation claim, because it prompts the right question: low correlation with WHAT? Oil is an input cost to most of the equity market, so an oil-dominated index has a genuine economic reason to move against equities in a supply shock and with them in a demand shock. The correlation is therefore state-dependent rather than stable, which is the least convenient property a diversifier can have. The weights are illustrative here; index methodologies are published and change, so check the current sector split rather than assuming this one.

That surprises people who assume a hedge must cost something, and it is not a free lunch: under uncovered interest parity the rand is EXPECTED to depreciate by the same 3.8462%, so the hedge locks in what the unhedged position expects on average. THE HEDGE CHANGES THE VARIANCE AND NOT THE MEAN, which is exactly what lecture 1 concluded from the variance side. Put the two together and the currency decision is fully specified: hedge unless the correlation is below minus one half of the volatility ratio, and know that the hedge itself is close to mean-neutral either way. AND IT REVERSES FOR THE OTHER PARTY. A dollar investor hedging rand assets back into dollars sells rands forward at a discount and GIVES UP 3.8462% a year. Same trade, opposite sign, and neither party is being clever. Anyone claiming that hedging is "expensive" or "free" has failed to say which side of the differential they are on.

It therefore looks excellent on a Sharpe ratio and behaves terribly in the tail, and a Sharpe ratio is precisely the wrong statistic for it. Note the direction of the tail. The losing side is rand WEAKNESS, and emerging market currencies weaken in global risk-off episodes, which is when the investor's equities are also falling. So the carry trade's losses are correlated with everything else going wrong, which is the opposite of a diversifier. UNCOVERED INTEREST PARITY SAYS THE EXPECTED RETURN IS ZERO. Its historical failure is the forward premium puzzle, a contested empirical regularity rather than a law. Treat any strategy resting on it as taking a position, not as harvesting a premium.

That is a demanding test, it is testable, and it is what the source's "low correlation" claim has to clear. AND THE ROW THAT MATTERS MOST IS THE SECOND. If commodities carry no risk premium at all, so that their expected return equals the risk-free rate, their Sharpe ratio is zero and the break-even correlation is ZERO. They then help only if the correlation is NEGATIVE, which is to say they must work as a HEDGE and not as a diversifier. Whether commodities carry a risk premium is genuinely contested. The classical argument is the source's own: futures markets exist to let producers lay off risk, so the speculator on the other side should be paid for taking it, and that payment shows up as backwardation. If that were still reliably true the roll return would be positive on average, and the table in the first worked block shows what a persistently positive roll would be worth. It is not reliably true, which is the same observation as the contango asymmetry above.

The eight dimensions, for an asset with no cash flow at all

SECURITY there is no issuer and no promise. a barrel of oil cannot default and cannot pay you. counterparty risk lives in the FUTURES CONTRACT and the clearing house behind it, not in the commodity. YIELD NEGATIVE: storage, insurance and spoilage, less any convenience yield. the only asset in this module whose yield row answers "you pay". SPREAD very high, and genuinely measured, because futures are priced daily. after four lectures of smoothed valuations this is a relief: the volatility here is real and there is no unsmoothing to do. TERM the source says SHORT TERM and is right for a reason it does not give: the CONTRACT expires, so any long-term holding is a rolling programme and the roll is where the money goes. EXPENSES storage and insurance for the physical; roll cost and margin for futures. an 11.36% annual roll cost appears in no fee table anywhere. MARKETABILITY excellent for the liquid futures and poor for the physical. the index's twenty-four contracts are chosen FOR liquidity, which is a selection the index makes on the investor's behalf. TAX usually a capital gain, since there is no income to tax, which for a taxable individual is the best treatment in the framework: 6.56% net on 8.00% gross from lecture 1, against 4.40% if it were interest. CURRENCY commodities are priced in dollars almost universally, so a rand investor holds a dollar position whether or not the commodity is mined in Limpopo. that is the clearest case in the module of the currency dimension being ECONOMIC rather than contractual. THE ROW THAT BREAKS THE PATTERN IS YIELD. Every other asset in this module produces something: interest, a dividend, rent, a regulated return, a share of profits. A commodity produces a bill. So the entire case for holding one rests on the price rising faster than the carry costs, which is a forecast about the world rather than a claim on anybody in it, and it should be argued as a forecast.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Currency is a return source only if you can say whose expectation you are betting against

The module's own framing for this lecture is currency "as a return source rather than a residual", and it deserves a careful answer rather than an enthusiastic one. Covered interest parity is an arbitrage relation and holds. Uncovered interest parity is a prediction and does not, reliably. Everything about currency as a return source lives in that gap.

The honest framing is not "return source or residual" but "chosen or inherited", and an unhedged holding is a carry position inherited by default whose direction depends on which side of the differential you sit. AND THE ASYMMETRY THAT MATTERS FOR A RAND INVESTOR: the domestic rate is the high one, so an unhedged foreign holding is a SHORT carry position. It expects to lose the differential and gains when the rand strengthens. A dollar investor holding rand assets unhedged has the opposite position and the opposite tail. Identical assets, opposite exposures, and the framework's currency row cannot express that without naming the investor's home currency, which is exactly lecture 1's point about pairing.

Commodities are the module's cleanest test of whether a premium exists at all

Every other asset class in this module has an argument for a positive expected return that does not depend on price forecasting. A bond pays a coupon for bearing credit and duration risk. A share is a residual claim on profits that exist. Property collects rent. Infrastructure collects a regulated return. Commodities have none of that, so the case must be that commodity prices rise faster than the carry, or that producers pay speculators to take risk off them, or that the diversification is worth having on its own.

THE THREE ARGUMENTS FOR A COMMODITY ALLOCATION, AND WHAT EACH REQUIRES 1. PRICES RISE IN REAL TERMS over the long run, so holding them earns something. requires: a view that real commodity prices trend upward. the long historical record is ambiguous at best, because technology has repeatedly cut the real cost of extracting and growing things. the source's "real returns, driven by real economics" is this argument, stated without the evidence. 2. THE INSURANCE ARGUMENT: producers want to lay off price risk, so speculators taking the other side get paid, and the payment appears as backwardation and therefore as a positive roll return. requires: that backwardation is the normal state. the contango asymmetry in step 4 says it is not, because the carry ceiling binds and the backwardation floor does not. and the source itself names the mechanism ("derivatives reduce farmers' risks"), which identifies who the natural seller is, without noticing that financialisation has since supplied a great many willing buyers. 3. THE DIVERSIFICATION ARGUMENT, which needs no premium at all. requires: correlation below 0.3750 if there IS an 8% expected return, and below ZERO if there is none. and it requires the correlation to hold in the states that matter, which the production-weighting analysis says it may not, because an oil-heavy index moves WITH equities in a demand shock and AGAINST them in a supply shock. THE HONEST POSITION: argument 3 is the strongest and it is conditional, argument 2 is testable and the test has been failing, and argument 1 is a forecast. An answer that asserts all three at once has asserted more than the evidence supports.

What the source gets right, and the one thing worth restating

Its five physical categories are the standard split and worth memorising. Its list of what a commodity derivative fixes (type, quality, delivery date, quantity, physical delivery site, package size) is unusually precise and explains why standardisation is hard for commodities in a way it is not for financial futures: a barrel of Brent and a barrel of Bonny Light are not the same thing, and a contract has to say which. Its observation that derivatives exist to reduce farmers' risks identifies the natural seller correctly.

Its two security items, a supply squeeze from China and uncertainty in the oil sector, are examples rather than a framework, and they date the note. The durable statement of the same point is that a commodity's price is set by physical supply and demand with inelastic short-run supply, so shocks are absorbed in price rather than in quantity, which is why commodity volatility is high and genuinely so.

The one thing worth restating is the index description. Calling the GSCI a weighted average of prices leaves out both the production weighting and the fact that it is built on futures rather than prices, and the second omission is the more serious because it conceals the roll return. A candidate who can say "an index of futures is not an index of prices, and the difference is the roll" has the single most useful sentence about this asset class.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The currency hedging threshold, the eight dimensions and the tax arithmetic that makes a capital gain the best treatment for a taxable individual are from the asset class framework; this lecture supplies the cost side of the hedging decision that lecture 1 left open. The index weighting arguments extend listed equity, where the same question of what a weighting scheme implicitly buys was settled for shares. The contrast with smoothed valuations, and the observation that commodity volatility needs no unsmoothing, is against private debt and property and infrastructure.

What it needs from elsewhere. Futures pricing, margining, the clearing house and the mechanics of rolling a position belong to the derivatives module, which covers the source's chapter 8 properly; this lecture uses only the cost of carry relation and takes the rest as given. Forward contracts and the arbitrage behind covered interest parity are in derivatives, and the Sharpe ratio condition in step 4 is derived in portfolio construction, which is where the correlation the whole diversification argument turns on is actually estimated.

Where this goes next. Lecture 10 covers securitisation, where the cash flows are engineered rather than physical. Lecture 11's structured products use exactly the derivative building blocks this lecture leans on. The index construction questions raised here are settled properly in indices, and the macroeconomic drivers of commodity prices and exchange rates, which this lecture takes as exogenous, are the subject of the external environment.

Where the same exposures appear with a regulator attached. A bank's commodity and foreign exchange positions sit in its trading book and attract capital under the market risk framework in market risk, where the same cost-of-carry and parity relations are used to mark positions and the same tail behaviour drives the capital charge. A bank's own currency mismatch between assets and liabilities is a structural position rather than a trading one, handled in liquidity management. The commodity producer on the other side of the farmer's hedge is a corporate borrower whose creditworthiness depends on the price, which is a wrong-way exposure in counterparty credit risk.

Numbers used here, and where they came from. The five physical categories, the twenty-four-contract index, the six specifications a commodity derivative fixes, the observation that derivatives exist to reduce farmers' risks, and the yield, spread and term notes are all from the F105 summary chapter 7. The cost of carry relation and both interest parity conditions are standard. The Sharpe ratio condition for adding an asset is standard portfolio theory. Everything else is invented for illustration: the contango and backwardation levels, the spot and forward prices, the storage cost, the sector weights (which are illustrative and change; check the published methodology), the 8 and 4 per cent interest rates and R18.00 spot, the 15 per cent currency volatility, and every return and volatility in the Sharpe ratio table. The arithmetic on all of them is exact and none is a market observation.
