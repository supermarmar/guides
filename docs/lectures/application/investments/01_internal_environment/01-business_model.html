Lectures / Investments / Internal environment

A chain of six charges quoted as under two per cent a year takes 52.08 per cent of a forty-year outcome

Platform, fund-of-funds, manager, custodian, dealing and administration come to 1.9500 per cent a year, and none of the six is obviously unreasonable on its own. Compounded over forty years at a 7 per cent gross return, they take more than half the gross value, because the household pays a number it was quoted annually and forfeits a fraction it was never shown. Along the way: the custodian's withholding tax reclaim is worth 150.00 per cent of the asset manager's whole fee, and the summary's four advantages and four disadvantages of intermediation are not commensurable, since the advantages are structural and the principal disadvantage compounds.

Step 1

The 30-second version

This is the first of six overview lectures, and it answers the question every later module presupposes: who are the participants and how do they get paid? The F105 summary names them all in chapter 4 and prices none of them. Putting numbers on the chain changes the emphasis substantially.

Six links sit between a household's money and the underlying asset: a platform at 0.2500 per cent, a fund-of-funds layer at 0.7500, the asset manager at 0.6000, a custodian at 0.0500, dealing costs inside the fund at 0.2000, and administration at 0.1000. That totals 1.9500 per cent a year, and none of the six is indefensible on its own. Compounded over forty years against a 7 per cent gross return it takes 52.08 per cent of the gross outcome: 779.9121 out of 1497.4458 on a starting 100. The household was quoted an annual percentage and forfeits a lifetime fraction it was never shown.

Two properties of that arithmetic ran against my expectations. The loss is concave in the fee, not convex: halving the charge cuts the forty-year loss to 58.87 per cent of itself rather than to half, and doubling it raises the loss to only 1.49 times, because the loss cannot exceed the gross value. And the fee's share of the outcome falls as the gross return rises, from 53.45 per cent at a 3 per cent return to 51.10 at 10, because a fixed percentage is a larger fraction of a smaller return. So the cost problem is worst in low-return assets and low-return decades, which is why the argument about fees got louder as yields fell.

The summary says households need intermediaries because diversification is key. That is right for an incomplete reason. A pot of 1,000 can hold one share before dealing charges pass one per cent, leaving 30.0000 per cent of specific risk; a pot of a million holds 200 and leaves 2.1213. What does the work is the minimum dealing charge, a fixed cost, and removing it lets a 10,000 pot hold 500 names rather than 9, cutting specific risk from 10.0000 to 1.3416 per cent. That is not hypothetical: it is what zero-commission broking and fractional shares actually did.

The summary's best line is its definition of an intermediary: they sell liabilities to buy other liabilities. Unpacking it shows why a unit trust needs no capital and a bank needs 10.50 per cent, since only the unit trust defines its liability as its assets. The pension fund is the interesting case, carrying a real mismatch with no regulatory capital, which means the capital is the sponsoring employer's covenant and sits off the fund's own balance sheet.

Two further findings. The investment bank's three stated objectives conflict internally: a 15 per cent discount to fair value produces a 17.65 per cent first-day pop and transfers 15 of every 100 from issuer to buyers, five times a 3 per cent underwriting fee, and that same pop is the sentiment proxy the market behaviour lecture found missing from the summary's list. And of eight custodian services listed as administration, reclaiming withholding tax is worth 0.9000 per cent a year against a 0.6000 per cent manager fee, which is 150.00 per cent of it, achieved by filing forms rather than forecasting.

Step 2

Why this matters

The rest of the investments track is about how to invest well. This lecture is about who captures the result, and the arithmetic in section 4.1 is large enough to swamp most of what follows. An active manager who genuinely adds 0.5 per cent a year gross is a rare and valuable thing; a chain that takes 1.95 per cent a year is a commonplace one. Any assessment of a manager, a strategy or an asset class that ignores the delivery chain is measuring the wrong quantity, and the compounding means the error grows with the horizon rather than staying constant.

It matters for the structure of the industry as well. Section 4.2 shows the value of intermediation falling as the pot grows, which is why the largest asset owners bring management in-house and smaller ones do not, and why the pension consolidation agenda in most jurisdictions is fundamentally a cost argument. A scheme with a billion under management can negotiate fees, appoint its own custodian, reclaim its own withholding tax and run its own dealing desk. A scheme with ten million cannot do any of those things, and pays a multiple of the larger scheme's total cost for a worse service.

There is a professional point too. The value chain is where conflicts of interest live, and the summary's own list of investment bank objectives contains one, in that the same institution advises on the issue price and underwrites the unsold portion. Section 4.4 quantifies why that matters: the underpricing is five times the fee, so a bank optimising its own risk rather than the issuer's proceeds has far more room to do damage through the price than through the charge. An adviser who examines the fee schedule and not the pricing has audited the smaller number.

Finally, the mismatch framing in section 4.3 is the bridge between this track and the banking and life insurance tracks. All three describe institutions that sell one kind of liability to buy another, and the differences between them reduce to which mismatch each accepts and who provides the capital against it. Understanding the investing institution as a balance sheet rather than as a portfolio is the shift that makes the actuarial treatment of it possible.

Step 3

The mental model

Money flows one way along the chain and charges are deducted at every link. Everything else in this lecture follows from noticing that the charges are annual and the money is compounding.

A household has savings and wants them to grow. A company has projects and wants money. Between those two facts sits an industry, and every part of it is paid.

Now the thing that makes the arithmetic counterintuitive. If somebody takes 2 per cent of your money every year for forty years, how much have they taken? The instinctive answer is 80 per cent, which is obviously too much, so the instinct corrects itself to "well, less than that, maybe 40". Both are wrong for the same reason: they are thinking about the money taken and not about the money that would have grown.

Here is the right way to see it. Without charges, your 100 becomes 1,497 over forty years at 7 per cent. With 1.95 per cent taken annually, you compound at 5.05 per cent instead, and 100 becomes 718. The difference is 780, which is more than half of what you would have had. You did not pay 780. You paid a series of small annual amounts, and the growth on those amounts, and the growth on that growth.

And the second insight, which is the one that reorganises how to think about the chain. Two per cent of a 7 per cent return is a bit more than a quarter of it. Two per cent of a 3 per cent return is two thirds of it. The fee is the same number and it means something completely different depending on what the assets return, which is why the whole industry's cost structure came under attack in a decade of low yields and not before.

The last piece is what the intermediary is. It sells you something, a unit or a policy or a pension promise, and buys something else with the proceeds. If the thing it sells you is defined as "whatever the things it bought are worth", it can never be short, so it needs no capital. If the thing it sells you is a promise with a number on it, then the two sides can diverge, and somebody has to stand behind the gap. That somebody is the whole subject of solvency regulation.

Formally, if the gross return is g and total annual charges are f, then after n years the household holds (1 + g - f)^n against a gross (1 + g)^n, and the charge's share of the gross outcome is 1 - ((1 + g - f) / (1 + g))^n. That expression rises with n towards one and falls with g, which is the pair of results in section 4.1.

The annual drag costs 1.82 per cent of the outcome over one year and 52.08 per cent over forty. The household paid a figure it was quoted as under two per cent and forfeited more than half its terminal wealth. That is not a criticism of any individual link: it is the arithmetic of compounding applied to a series of small deductions, and it is the single most important number in the business model.

Two properties of the relationship surprised me and both are worth knowing. First, the loss is concave in the fee rather than convex. Halving the charge to 0.9750 per cent cuts the forty-year loss from 779.9121 to 459.1257, which is 58.87 per cent of it rather than 50, and doubling the charge raises the loss to 1158.3311, only 1.49 times rather than twice. The reason is a ceiling: the loss cannot exceed the whole gross value, so the function must flatten. The first basis points of fee cost more than the last ones.

That comparison is the actionable form of the whole section. Negotiating ten basis points off the manager's fee cuts the forty-year share by 1.86 points; removing the fund-of-funds layer cuts it by 15.77 points, 8.5 times as much. The number of layers matters more than the size of any one of them, and the layer most easily removed is rarely the one under negotiation.

4.2 Whether a household is better off going direct

The summary states that diversification is key for households and that they therefore need intermediaries. Test the claim, because a household can buy shares directly and the question is at what size that stops being unwise. Charge the greater of a fixed minimum per trade or a percentage, and hold as many names as keeps the total assembly cost under one per cent of the pot.

A pot of 1,000 holds one share and carries almost twice the market's risk. A pot of a million carries essentially the market's risk.

A pot of 1,000 can hold a single share, leaving 30.0000 per cent of specific risk on top of the market's 18.00, for a total of 34.9857 per cent. A pot of a million holds 200 names and carries 18.1246 per cent, which is the market's risk plus almost nothing. So the summary's conclusion is correct.

Its reason is incomplete, and the incompleteness matters because it dates the claim. What produces the result is the minimum dealing charge, which is a fixed cost: it falls as a percentage only when the pot is large. Diversification is not intrinsically unavailable to small investors; it is priced out of reach by a fee structure.

Removing the minimum charge lets the same pot hold 500 names rather than 9, cutting specific risk from 10.0000 to 1.3416 per cent.

At a zero minimum charge a 10,000 pot holds 500 names and specific risk falls to 1.3416 per cent, which is better diversified than most active funds. That is not a thought experiment: zero-commission broking and fractional shares did exactly this, and the consequence is that the summary's "need for intermediaries" describes a cost structure that has changed rather than a permanent feature of markets. The remaining reasons to use a fund are the other three advantages, not diversification, and section 4.6 weighs them.

The capital is the sponsoring employer's covenant, which does not appear on the fund's own balance sheet. That is why covenant strength is an investment question rather than merely a credit one: a scheme with a weak sponsor is running an unsupported mismatch, and the correct response is a different asset allocation rather than a different discount rate.

4.4 The investment bank's three objectives cannot all be met

The summary lists three objectives for an investment bank bringing an issue: the best possible price, the lowest possible issue cost, and meeting the funding requirement. The first two belong to the issuer and the bank is paid by the issuer, so the tension is not between bank and client but inside the objectives themselves.

Offer price against outcome, on an issue with a fair value of 100

   the offer price   first-day   money raised   value transferred   did the issue
   as a % of fair    pop         by the issuer  to the buyers       clear?
                100%       0.00%       100.0000              0.0000   no, likely undersubscribed
                 95%       5.26%        95.0000              5.0000   yes
                 85%      17.65%        85.0000             15.0000   yes
                 70%      42.86%        70.0000             30.0000   yes

A 15 per cent discount produces a 17.65 per cent pop, because the pop is measured against the lower offer price.

A 15 per cent discount to fair value produces a 17.65 per cent first-day pop and transfers 15 of every 100 of value from the issuer to the buyers. Against a 3 per cent underwriting fee, the underpricing is five times as large. So "the lowest possible issue cost" measured as a fee is measuring the smaller number, and "the best possible price" and "meeting the funding requirement" pull directly against each other: a price high enough to be best is a price that may not clear.

And that same first-day pop is the sentiment proxy the market behaviour lecture found missing from the summary's own list of five. So the number is simultaneously a cost to the issuer and a measure of market mood, which means when to issue and at what price are one decision and not two. An issuer bringing a deal in a high-sentiment window accepts a larger pop and raises more money anyway, because the fair value it is discounting from is higher. That is why issuance is procyclical, and it is the mechanism behind two of Baker and Wurgler's proxies at once.

4.5 The custodian's tax reclaim is worth more than the manager's fee

The summary lists eight custodian services and treats them as administration: custody of documents, exercising voting rights, income collection, reclaiming withholding tax, cash management, securities settlement, foreign exchange and stock lending. One of those has a return consequence larger than most active management.

Getting from the headline rate to the treaty rate saves 0.4500 per cent, nine times the custodian's own 0.0500 per cent fee. 4.6 The summary's four advantages against its four disadvantages

The summary gives four advantages of intermediation and four disadvantages, presented as a balanced list. Section 4.1 to 4.5 have now priced most of them, so the balance can be assessed.

   the summary's advantage         what it is worth here              the matching
                                                                      disadvantage
   diversification achieved        specific risk 30.0000% to 2.1213%  additional costs: 1.9500% a year
   pooling resources               the fixed dealing charge shared    inflexible products

Four against four implies a balance. The advantages are mostly structural and one-off; the principal disadvantage is annual and compounds.

The two sides are not commensurable, and presenting them as a symmetric list of four implies a balance the arithmetic does not support. The advantages are structural: diversification is achieved once and then held, pooling is a property of the vehicle, and the tax reclaim is a capability. The principal disadvantage is an annual percentage that compounds, and section 4.1 showed it reaching 52.08 per cent of terminal wealth over forty years.

The honest summary is conditional rather than balanced. Intermediation is worth paying for when the pot is small, when the assets are hard to reach directly, and when a capability like tax reclaim is bundled in. It is worth progressively less as the pot grows, which is precisely why large funds insource and small ones do not, and why the number of layers matters more than the size of any one of them. Removing the fund-of-funds layer alone takes the annual charge from 1.9500 to 1.2000 per cent and the forty-year share from 52.08 to 36.31.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Three things a practitioner watches in the value chain, and the first is not the size of any fee.

Count the layers before negotiating any of them. Section 4.1's chain has six links and the one that receives all the attention is the third. A fund that negotiates its manager's fee from 0.60 to 0.50 per cent has saved 0.10; a fund that removes a fund-of-funds layer has saved 0.75. The practitioner's first question about a delivery structure is therefore how many entities are being paid a percentage of assets, and the second is what each one does that could not be done by one of the others. Very often the custodian could hold the assets the platform is holding, or the manager could be appointed directly rather than through a wrapper, and the saving from removing a layer is an order of magnitude larger than the saving from squeezing one.

The charges that compound matter categorically more than the charges that do not, and the distinction is not the one people use. A transaction cost of 1 per cent paid once is a smaller lifetime cost than an annual charge of 0.10 per cent held for forty years, because the second is taken repeatedly from a growing base. Section 4.1's arithmetic makes that comparison computable, and it inverts the ordinary intuition that a large visible cost is worse than a small invisible one. This is also why the transition management lecture's conclusion and this one's are consistent rather than contradictory: a one-off 5 per cent transition cost is genuinely large, and it is still smaller than an extra 0.20 per cent a year for thirty years.

Look for the charges that are not expressed as percentages at all, because those are the ones nobody negotiates. Section 4.4's underpricing is five times the underwriting fee and appears in no fee schedule. Section 4.5's unreclaimed withholding tax is 150 per cent of the manager's fee and appears as a lower dividend rather than as a charge. The bid-offer spread inside a fund, the cash drag on uninvested balances, the securities lending revenue retained by the agent rather than passed to the fund: none of these is quoted, all of them are real, and together they frequently exceed the quoted costs. A cost review that reads the fee schedule has read the part that was designed to be read.

On the summary itself, the correction I would record is one of framing rather than fact. Its four-against-four list of intermediation advantages and disadvantages is a genuinely useful enumeration and it implies a symmetry that does not exist, because one side is structural and the other compounds. And its explanation of why households need intermediaries names diversification where the operative constraint is the fixed component of dealing charges, which is why the claim has weakened materially in the last decade while the summary's wording has not.

Connections

What this assumes. Nothing in the investments track precedes this lecture, which is why it is first, though it uses compounding as set out in financial management. The specific risk that diversification removes is quantified in portfolio risk and return, and the tracking consequence of holding too few names is in equity index tracking, which independently found withholding tax to be 71.22 per cent of a tracker's shortfall. The dealing costs inside the chain are priced in transition management and overlays, and the sentiment reading of the first-day IPO pop is in behavioural finance, market behaviour.

Where the bank sits opposite. The bank appears twice in this chain and is paid differently each time. As an investment bank it earns the fee and controls the price in section 4.4, which is an advisory and underwriting business whose risk is the unsold portion, and the capital held against that risk is treated in market risk. As a custodian it earns the smallest fee in the chain while delivering the largest saving, which is a scale and operational business whose risk is settlement failure and process error rather than market movement, covered in operational risk. And section 4.3's mismatch table is the banking track's opening subject seen from outside: the bank is the intermediary whose two sides differ most, which is why it carries the largest capital requirement of the four, set out in the banking business model.

Numbers used here. The four common stakeholders, the two other stakeholders, the four advantages and four disadvantages of intermediation, the investment bank's three objectives and eight roles, and the eight custodian services are all transcribed from chapter 4 of the ASSA F105 summary in the user's notes. Two observations are recorded against it, with the examinable position stated alongside each. First, its four-against-four list of intermediation advantages and disadvantages implies a symmetry that does not exist, because the advantages are structural and one-off while the principal disadvantage is annual and compounds to 52.08 per cent of a forty-year outcome. Second, its explanation that households need intermediaries for diversification names the wrong operative constraint: what produces the result is the fixed component of dealing charges, and removing that minimum lets a 10,000 pot hold 500 names rather than 9, which is what zero-commission broking and fractional shares did. Everything numerical is invented for illustration: a chain of six charges from 0.0500 to 0.7500 per cent totalling 1.9500, gross returns from 3 to 10 per cent, horizons from one to forty years, a minimum dealing charge from zero to 10 currency units against a 0.50 per cent percentage charge, pots from 1,000 to a million, specific volatility of 30 per cent against a market's 18, capital requirements of 8.00 and 10.50 per cent, issue discounts from zero to 30 per cent against a 3 per cent underwriting fee, a 3.00 per cent dividend yield with withholding rates of 30 and 15 per cent, and dividend yields from 0.40 to 5.00 per cent. Eighteen claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions, all eighteen passed, and none can pass without the computation. Three failed on the first run. One was a genuine reversal: I asserted that the lifetime loss is convex in the fee so that halving the charge would save more than half the loss, and it is concave, saving only 41.13 per cent, because the loss is bounded above by the gross value. The other two were my thresholds, since the underpricing is exactly five times the fee rather than more than five, and a 0.50 per cent yield makes the tax reclaim exactly a quarter of the fee, a tie removed by changing the yield to 0.40 per cent. Three further claims were flagged as unfalsifiable by a vacuity check and rewritten. Two figures I had computed by hand for the prose were also wrong and were corrected against the script: removing the fund-of-funds layer gives a 36.31 per cent forty-year share rather than 36.86, and doubling the charge raises the loss 1.49 times rather than 1.53. Every headline finding states the region in which it holds: the compounding result strengthens with the horizon and weakens with the gross return, the direct-holding conclusion depends entirely on the fixed component of dealing charges, the capital requirement follows from whether the liability is defined as the assets, and the custodian's tax capability scales with the portfolio's dividend yield.
