Appointing sixteen managers makes the fund significant and every single manager unjudgeable

Sixteen managers each at an information ratio of 0.50 give the fund an aggregate 2.0000 and a three-year t-statistic of 3.4641, comfortably significant. Every one of those managers still sits at 0.8660 individually, and the trustees must nonetheless decide about each. Sixteen decisions at a five per cent error rate make it 55.99 per cent likely that at least one skilled manager is wrongly dismissed. That is the arithmetic of "delegate tasks but not responsibility": the responsibility scales with the appointments and the evidence per appointment does not.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Lecture 2 set out what the fund is for. This lecture covers who decides that and who answers for it, which the F105 summary treats in unusual detail: a trust deed of twelve items, six trustee roles, five trustee concerns, eleven institutional investment practices, two board categories with five roles each, and three ESG factor groups. Two of its statements have consequences it does not draw.

The first is its central principle, that a trustee can delegate tasks but not responsibility. Appointing more managers improves the fund: sixteen independent managers each at an information ratio of 0.50 give an aggregate of 2.0000, a factor of four, with a three-year t-statistic of 3.4641. But each manager's own t-statistic stays at 0.8660 regardless of how many colleagues they have, so the trustees can see the fund doing well and cannot see which manager is doing it.

That becomes a governance problem rather than a measurement one because the trustees must still decide about each manager separately. Sixteen decisions at a five per cent error rate make it 55.99 per cent likely that at least one skilled manager is wrongly dismissed, against 5.00 per cent with a single manager. The error rate per decision never changed; the number of decisions did. That is the multiple testing arithmetic the market behaviour lecture found behind the mood anomalies, arriving here as a constraint on how wide a delegation a trustee body can actually supervise.

And there is no way round it except one. A manager at an information ratio of 0.50 needs 16.00 years to reach a t-statistic of two, and trustees review every three. So the evidence is never available on the timetable the decision is taken on, and the honest response is to appoint fewer managers and keep them longer.

The second finding prices the summary's phrase "tackling conflicts of interest", which it lists without an example. The largest conflict in asset management is arithmetical: an ad valorem fee means ten per cent asset growth is worth exactly ten times as much to the manager as one per cent of outperformance. The fee formula treats a pound gathered and a pound earned as the same pound and the client values only the second. A performance fee does not fix this, it converts it: the fee is 40.0000 on a 20 per cent gain and zero on a 20 per cent loss, so it is an option written by the client, and it replaces the incentive to gather assets with an incentive to raise volatility.

Then the ESG contradiction. The summary says the primary focus is financial and ESG must not compromise it, and separately says ESG factors increase returns. If the second is true the first is unnecessary. Computing an exclusion settles it: a 20 per cent screen creates 10.0000 per cent tracking error, and the expected return cost is exactly zero unless the excluded names were expected to outperform. So one word is doing two jobs, and all six of the summary's own examples of ESG raising returns turn out to be ordinary financial analysis of unpriced liabilities rather than ethics.

Step 2

Why this matters

The governance structure determines which of the techniques in this track ever get used. A trustee body meeting quarterly, advised by consultants, appointing managers on three-year cycles and judged against a peer group cannot implement a fifty-year liability hedge even if the arithmetic says it should, because the decision-making apparatus does not operate on that timescale. Section 4.1 makes that concrete: the evidence a trustee needs to discharge the responsibility they cannot delegate takes 16 years to accumulate and the review cycle is 3, so the structure guarantees decisions taken on insufficient evidence.

The delegation result also explains something otherwise puzzling about industry structure. Multi-manager and fund-of-funds arrangements are widely criticised for their fees, and lecture 1 showed a fund-of-funds layer at 0.7500 per cent being the largest single link in the chain. Section 4.1 gives the defence: with sixteen managers the aggregate information ratio is four times a single manager's. It also gives the rebuttal, which is that nobody can tell which of the sixteen deserves reappointment. The structure buys diversification of manager risk and pays for it in governance quality, and both halves of that trade are computable.

The conflict of interest arithmetic in section 4.2 matters because it is not a matter of anybody's integrity. A manager who wants to serve clients well and a manager who wants to maximise revenue face the same fee formula, and the formula rewards asset growth ten times more heavily than outperformance at plausible magnitudes. That is why fee structure is a governance question rather than a procurement one, and why the summary is right to list conflicts of interest under good governance rather than under ethics.

The ESG material matters for a specific professional reason. An actuary advising trustees on ESG is being asked to distinguish two things that use one vocabulary: factors that are financially material and currently unpriced, which are ordinary analysis and arguably compulsory, and preferences that trade return for values, which are the beneficiaries' to hold and not the trustees'. Section 4.3 separates them arithmetically, and the separation is what makes fiduciary advice possible rather than merely fashionable.

Step 3

The mental model

A trust separates who owns the assets from who benefits from them, and everything in governance follows from managing that separation. Delegation extends the chain and multiplies the places where it can go wrong.

A trust is an arrangement where one person holds property for the benefit of another. The trustee is the legal owner: their name is on the assets, they sign the contracts, they can be sued. The beneficiary is the one the assets are for.

That split is the whole point and the whole problem. It is the point because it lets someone who cannot manage money have money managed for them. It is the problem because the person with the power is not the person with the interest.

Trust law's answer is a duty rather than a rule: act in the beneficiary's interest, prudently, fairly between them, and do not profit yourself. And then one line that carries more weight than the rest: you may hand the work to an expert, and you may not hand over the answering for it.

Now think about what that means when you hire sixteen fund managers. Each of them is better at investing than you are, which is why you hired them, and collectively they make the fund distinctly better. But at the end of three years you must decide whether each one has earned their keep, and you cannot tell. Not because you are inattentive: because three years of one manager's results simply do not contain enough information to distinguish a good manager from a lucky one. You have sixteen coin flips to call and no way to call them.

So you will get some wrong. And here is the arithmetic that should be uncomfortable: the more managers you hire, the better the fund and the more certain it becomes that you have sacked somebody who was good. Sixteen decisions, each with a one-in-twenty chance of error, is better than even odds of at least one mistake. You improved the portfolio and degraded the stewardship, using the same action.

The way out is not better analysis. It is fewer appointments held for longer, which is exactly what nobody's incentives point towards, because a trustee who changes nothing looks idle and a trustee who changes managers looks diligent.

Formally, with n managers whose active returns are independent and each at information ratio IR, the fund's information ratio is IR x sqrt(n), and its t-statistic after T years is that times sqrt(T). Each manager's own t-statistic remains IR x sqrt(T), independent of n. If each reappointment decision carries an error probability p, the chance of at least one error across n decisions is 1 - (1-p)^n.

4.2 "Tackling conflict of interest", priced

The summary lists tackling conflicts of interest as a feature of good governance and gives no example. The largest one in asset management is structural rather than moral. A manager paid a percentage of assets earns from the size of the pot, and the size can be grown two ways.

Ten per cent asset growth is worth exactly ten times as much to the manager as one per cent of outperformance. The ratio is precisely the ratio of the two percentages, because the fee formula multiplies assets by a rate and does not care how the assets got there. The formula treats a pound gathered and a pound earned as the same pound, and the client values only the second. So the manager's allocation of effort between investing and selling is not the client's preferred one, and no amount of good intention changes the arithmetic. The using options lecture priced that asymmetry precisely: the writer's exposure is one-sided and the holder's cost is the premium. Here the client pays the premium in a form that does not appear on any invoice, which is an incentive to raise volatility, since an option's value rises with volatility and the client's position does not. A performance fee therefore converts the conflict rather than removing it, replacing the incentive to gather assets with the incentive to take risk.

4.3 The ESG objective and the ESG performance clause contradict each other

The summary states that the primary focus is financial and ESG must not compromise it, then states that ESG factors increase returns and that trustees have a fiduciary duty to consider them. If the second is true the first is unnecessary; if the first is needed the second is false. Resolve it by computing what an exclusion actually costs, starting with the dispersion it creates.

So both of the summary's statements are true in different regions and it does not say which region it is describing.

The resolution is that one word is doing two jobs. An exclusion that removes an expected loser is a financial decision needing no ESG justification at all, and an exclusion that removes an expected winner is an ethical decision, which the "must not compromise" clause forbids trustees from making on the beneficiaries' behalf. The examinable position is both clauses as given; the complete answer separates the two cases and notes that only the second requires a fiduciary argument, and that the second is arguably not the trustees' decision to take at all.

The clause earns its place only for exclusions taken on values grounds, which is a much narrower category than the ESG label suggests.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Three things a practitioner watches in a governance structure, and the first is a count rather than a judgement.

Count the decisions the trustees must take, not the assets they oversee. Section 4.1's result depends on the number of appointments and not on their size, so a fund with sixteen managers and a fund with one manager face the same evidence per decision and sixteen times the decision count. The diagnostic question in any governance review is therefore how many discretionary judgements this body is required to make per cycle, and whether the evidence supporting each one exists. A trustee body making four decisions well is governing; the same body making forty is processing. And the remedy is structural rather than exhortatory: consolidate mandates, lengthen appointment terms, and move the monitoring of individual managers to a delegated committee whose sole job it is.

Read the fee schedule as a description of what the manager is being asked to do. Section 4.2's arithmetic means an ad valorem fee is a contract to gather assets with investment performance as a secondary consideration, and a performance fee is a contract to take risk. Neither is dishonest and neither is what the client wants. The practitioner's response is to notice what the structure rewards and then to supply the missing constraint elsewhere: a risk limit alongside a performance fee, a capacity limit alongside an ad valorem one. The fee cannot be fixed and the incentive it creates can be bounded.

On ESG, insist on the distinction section 4.3 draws before discussing anything else. Almost every disagreement about ESG in a trustee meeting is two arguments wearing one name: whether a factor is financially material and unpriced, which is an evidential question, and whether the fund should forgo return for values, which is a mandate question about whose preferences count. Separating them usually dissolves the disagreement, because both sides agree that unpriced carbon liabilities should be analysed and both sides agree that the trustees cannot spend the members' money on the trustees' ethics. What remains is a much narrower and more tractable conversation about which category a particular exclusion falls into.

On the summary, two items are recorded. Its ESG objective and performance clauses point in opposite directions, and the resolution is that the word "ESG" covers a financial category for which the caveat is unnecessary and an ethical category for which the caveat is prohibitive. And its central delegation principle, which is correct and well stated, has an arithmetic consequence it does not draw: the responsibility that cannot be delegated becomes harder to discharge in exact proportion to how widely the tasks are delegated, so the principle contains a limit on delegation that the summary presents as unlimited.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The objectives this governance structure exists to set are in objectives, liabilities and constraints, whose peer-group conflict is a governance question this lecture answers, and the fee chain the conflict of interest operates on is in the business model. The t-statistic arithmetic in section 4.1 is derived in measuring portfolio performance, the multiple testing result it reuses is in behavioural finance, market behaviour, and the fundamental law that makes the aggregate information ratio rise with the square root of the manager count is in active versus passive. The option asymmetry a performance fee reproduces is priced in using options, and the tracking error an exclusion creates uses the same model as equity index tracking.

Where this goes next. Lecture 4 turns this governance into a document, covering the mandate and the statement of investment principles, where the trust deed's restrictions on holdings become explicit instructions and the localisation and prudential limits are written down. Section 4.2's conclusion feeds directly into it, since the fee structure and the risk limits are mandate terms and the argument here is that they must be set together. Lecture 5 on vehicles and wrappers determines which governance regime applies at all, since a unit trust, a life policy and a pension fund are supervised differently. Lecture 6 covers the board's own decisions on the issuer side, where the executive and non-executive roles set out here are exercised on capital budgeting rather than on investment.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank's board faces the same executive and non-executive split with an additional layer the summary does not cover, since banking supervision imposes specific board-level responsibilities for risk appetite and internal control that the trust framework leaves to general fiduciary duty. That apparatus is set out in risk management, and its data and reporting requirements are the subject of BCBS 239. Section 4.2's conflict of interest is also a banking conduct matter rather than merely a commercial one: remuneration structures that reward volume over quality are precisely what post-crisis conduct rules police, and the operational and conduct exposure they create is in operational risk. And the ESG material connects to the climate risk work banks now undertake under supervisory instruction, in climate risk, where the unpriced-liability argument of section 4.3 appears as a scenario analysis requirement.
