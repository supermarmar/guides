The break-even reinvestment rate is the implied forward rate, exactly, so the summary's two methods are one

The summary lists the reinvestment-rate comparison and the forward-rate derivation as separate techniques for choosing between a short and a long bond. They agree to 0.00e+00 in every case tested, because they are the same equation solved for the same unknown, and knowing that converts the rule "a high rate might be unlikely" into something precise: preferring the long bond is exactly a bet that future short rates will fall below the market's own forward. Along the way: a two-year into thirty-year policy switch is 17.6 times the size of lecture 5's anomaly and perfectly symmetric, and only 57.50 per cent of a 200 basis point spread is a genuine pick-up.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Lecture 5 covered anomaly switching, which needs many small chances and does not have them. This lecture takes the summary's other two active bond techniques. Policy switching takes the duration exposure that ruins an anomaly switch and takes it deliberately. Yield pick-up needs neither skill nor timing, and is the one of the three a long-horizon institution can actually run.

The central result is an identity the summary conceals by listing two methods separately. The break-even reinvestment rate, the rate a short bond's proceeds must earn after it matures to match a long bond, equals the implied forward rate exactly, to 0.00e+00 in every case, because both solve (1+sA)m(1+r)n−m = (1+sB)n for r. So the summary's rule "a high rate might be unlikely, so bond B is better" becomes precise: preferring the long bond is a bet that future short rates will be below the market's own forward.

Policy switching is then the same exposure lecture 5 had to avoid. A matched anomaly switch has exactly zero duration profit and loss; a two-year into thirty-year switch changes duration by 26.5403 and makes or loses 13.2701 per cent on a 50 basis point move, which is 17.6 times lecture 5's anomaly and perfectly symmetric. That symmetry is what "high risk" means. And the breadth consequence is severe: a curve view is one bet, so four calls a year need an information coefficient of 0.25, five times what anyone demonstrates.

The summary's insistence that the curve "does not move uniformly" then earns its place. A parallel fall favours the thirty-year by 11.8483 per cent, a flattening favours it by 16.5877, and a steepening reverses the verdict entirely, costing 16.5877 relative to the short bond. So a manager right about the level and wrong about the shape can still lose, which is why the two must be decided separately.

On yield pick-up, the summary's own list of what a spread contains is the whole answer, because only part of it is a pick-up. Of 200 basis points, the expected loss of 60 is compensation for money you will actually lose, so the genuine premia are 115 basis points, 57.50 per cent of the spread. And the liquidity component is not a return but a bet that you will never be a forced seller: a one in twenty chance of a forced sale at an eight per cent discount leaves five basis points of the forty-five, and one in ten wipes it out entirely.

Feynman test Two ways to choose between a five-year and a ten-year bond turn out to be the same sum: work out what rate you would need on the money when the short bond matures, and that number is already quoted in the market as the forward rate. So buying the long bond is not a clever calculation, it is a wager that rates will be lower than the market already expects. Betting on rates outright is enormous compared with hunting small pricing gaps, and it loses exactly as much as it wins. And being paid extra to hold risky or illiquid bonds is only extra if you never have to sell in a hurry and the market has not correctly guessed how many will default.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the identity turns a vague rule into a testable proposition. "A high reinvestment rate is unlikely" invites a judgement about plausibility; "the required rate is the forward rate, so this is a bet against the market's expectation" invites a specific view about where short rates will be, which can be stated, defended and monitored. Same arithmetic, entirely different quality of conversation.

The second is the scale contrast. Lecture 5's anomaly was worth 0.7538 per cent and this lecture's policy switch 13.2701, so a manager doing both has a portfolio whose result is decided almost entirely by the curve view. Anyone reporting an anomaly-switching programme alongside an unhedged duration position is reporting the wrong number.

The third is that the level and shape views are separable and are usually conflated. The same 50 basis point expectation produces opposite verdicts depending on where in the curve it lands, and the summary's separation of the two is the reason. A manager who says "rates will fall" has said half of what a trade requires.

And the fourth is that most of what is quoted as a spread pick-up is not one. Overstating a 115 basis point premium as 200 is an exaggeration of 73.91 per cent, and the liquidity portion is contingent on something the manager may not control. That matters for how a mandate should describe a credit allocation: as compensation for a balance sheet property rather than as a return.

Step 3

The mental model

One identity, one enormous symmetric bet, two separable curve views, and a spread that is mostly not a pick-up.

The break-even reinvestment rate is the implied forward rate

The summary gives two separate methods for comparing a short bond with a long one. The reinvestment-rate method asks what rate bond A's proceeds must earn after it matures to match bond B at bond B's maturity. The forward-rate method derives the forward rate implied by the two spot rates. THEY ARE THE SAME NUMBER. Holding A to year m and reinvesting to year n must equal holding B to n: (1+sA)^m x (1+r)^(n-m) = (1+sB)^n so r = [ (1+sB)^n / (1+sA)^m ]^(1/(n-m)) - 1 and the implied forward rate between m and n is defined by exactly that equation. Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to carry is the identity, because it changes what the decision is. The break-even reinvestment rate and the implied forward rate agree to 0.00e+00 in every case, so THE REQUIRED RATE IS ALREADY QUOTED IN THE MARKET. That turns "a high reinvestment rate is unlikely" from a judgement about plausibility into a specific claim: preferring the long bond is a bet that future short rates will be below the forward. Same arithmetic, and a completely different quality of argument, because the second version can be stated, defended and monitored.

WHAT THE IDENTITY LETS YOU SAY INSTEAD. BEFORE "bond A would need to reinvest at 6.00%, which seems high, so buy bond B" AFTER "the market's 5y5y forward is 6.00%. buying bond B is a bet that it lands below." Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. Spot and forward rates and the bootstrapping that produces them are from yield curves and bootstrapping, and the duration arithmetic is from fixed income pricing. The duration mismatch that policy switching takes deliberately was measured as a hazard in bond anomaly switching, and the level-against-shape decomposition and the finding that the level factor carries most of the variance are from liability hedging and dynamic benchmarks. The breadth test is from active versus passive. The credit spread decomposition draws on credit analysis, and the forced-sale cost is the execution arithmetic from liquidity risk. Several of the eleven instruments are built in securitisation and insurance-linked securities and private debt.

Where this goes next. Lectures 7 to 11 are the implementation module, and two threads from here run straight into it. The symmetry of a policy switch can only be broken with options, which is lecture 8, and that is the single most useful thing derivatives do for a duration view. And the interest rate and inflation swaps that appear in the summary's yield pick-up list belong in lecture 9, where a swap expresses a duration or inflation view without the bond-selection problem lectures 4 to 6 have been about. Lecture 11 on transition management is where the cost of acting on a curve view is priced properly, since a large duration change is a large trade.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank makes the policy-switching decision continuously as its banking book duration choice, and the identity in this lecture is the arithmetic behind its transfer pricing curve: the forward rate is what the treasury charges a business unit for term funding, so a unit that prefers long funding is betting against the bank's own forward, which is set out in pricing. The spread decomposition is the bank's version too, and it is regulated rather than optional: the expected-loss component must be provisioned under IFRS 9 and only the residual is income, which is the mechanism in credit risk. And the liquidity premium's dependence on never being a forced seller is exactly what the liquidity coverage ratio exists to test, treated in liquidity risk.
