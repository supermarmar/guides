"Low risk" and "limited chances" are the same fact, and it is the reason anomaly switching cannot produce a respectable information ratio

The summary presents anomaly switching as low risk with limited chances, as though that were an attractive combination. At two anomalies a year the information coefficient needed for an information ratio of 0.50 is 0.3536, seven times what anyone demonstrates, and it only clears the bar at a hundred a year, which contradicts "limited chances". Along the way: the switch is paid for twice so a ten-year trade needs a 6.63 basis point anomaly at a 25 basis point round trip, a 1.5075-year duration mismatch destroys the whole prize, and the coupon effect the summary describes is a tax result rather than an anomaly at all.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Lecture 4 covered the passive bond techniques. This lecture takes the first of the summary's three active ones, anomaly switching, which moves between similar bonds when a spread is temporarily wide and switches back to crystallise. The summary is unusually self-critical about it, naming a flaw in each of its three detection methods, and the arithmetic shows those three flaws to be one flaw in three forms.

Start with the hurdle. Because the position is reversed, the transaction cost is paid twice, so the break-even anomaly is two round trips divided by the modified duration. On a ten-year bond at a 25 basis point round trip that is 6.63 basis points, and on a two-year bond 27.08. So anomaly switching is a long-bond activity: a ten basis point anomaly is worth 1.3414 per cent on a 25-year bond and 0.1846 on a two-year one.

Then the risk the summary calls "unstable and sensitive" without explaining. Two bonds are never identical, so a switch carries a residual duration mismatch, and a curve move multiplies it. A half-year mismatch on a 50 basis point move produces 0.2500 per cent against a 0.7538 per cent anomaly profit, 33.17 per cent of the prize, and the curve move wins outright above a 1.5075-year mismatch. The signal is measured in basis points and the noise in tens of them.

The coupon effect is the most misleading item, because it looks like an anomaly and is not. A zero-coupon bond delivers 100 per cent of its return as capital gain and a 12 per cent coupon bond delivers minus 69.00 per cent, so with capital gains tax below income tax the tax-equivalent gross yield falls from 10.6222 to 3.1215 per cent as the coupon rises. A high-coupon bond yielding more is therefore correctly priced for a taxed investor, and switching into it captures nothing unless your own tax position differs from the marginal investor's.

And the breadth test settles the strategy. At two anomalies a year the required information coefficient is 0.3536, seven times what anyone demonstrates; it clears 0.05 only at a hundred a year, which contradicts "limited chances". The tension resolves only if the anomalies are large rather than numerous: two 200 basis point anomalies a year net 29.1505 per cent after four round trips, and a 200 basis point gap between similar bonds is not an anomaly, it is a credit event.

Feynman test Two nearly identical bonds occasionally drift apart in price. Buy the cheap one, sell the dear one, wait for them to converge, then reverse. The catch is you pay dealing costs twice, so the gap has to be wide enough to cover both, and because the bonds are not quite identical you are also making an accidental bet on interest rates that can be three times bigger than the gap you were chasing. Worse, one of the standard signals, that bonds with big coupons yield more, is not a mispricing at all but a tax effect, so acting on it earns nothing. And the strategy is safe precisely because opportunities are rare, which is also why it cannot make much money.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the double cost changes which trades are worth doing, and it is the one piece of arithmetic the summary gestures at without performing. A twenty-seven basis point hurdle on a two-year switch rules out almost every observable anomaly at that maturity, and a 3.73 basis point hurdle on a 25-year bond admits many. The maturity, not the anomaly, decides whether the trade exists.

The second is that the duration mismatch is the dominant risk and is invisible in the signal. A manager selecting bond pairs for similarity of issuer, sector and maturity may still leave a year of duration mismatch, and a year of mismatch on a 50 basis point move is two thirds of a ten basis point anomaly's entire profit. The trade must be duration-neutral to a fraction of a year or it is a rate bet in disguise.

The third is that acting on the coupon effect earns nothing for the marginal investor and is a real opportunity for anyone whose tax position differs. That makes it not an anomaly but a tax arbitrage, and the distinction determines who can trade it: a pension fund and a taxed individual should hold different coupons of the same issuer, and neither is exploiting a mispricing.

And the fourth is that the summary's two-word summary of the strategy contains its own refutation. "Low risk, limited chances" is presented as a favourable pair and the fundamental law says the second half destroys the first half's value. A strategy that is safe because opportunities are rare cannot generate a respectable information ratio, and that is arithmetic rather than opinion.

Step 3

The mental model

A double hurdle, a mismatch that swamps the prize, a tax effect masquerading as an anomaly, and a breadth test the strategy fails on its own description.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The duration arithmetic that converts a yield anomaly into a price gain is from fixed income pricing, and the curve shapes a mismatch is exposed to are from yield curves and bootstrapping. The breadth test and the 0.05 information coefficient benchmark are from active versus passive, and the finding that five bonds span a thirty-line index, which is why a bond manager cannot get breadth, is from bond passive management and liability-driven investment. The contrast with equity styles, where breadth was the route to a respectable information ratio, is active equity styles. The transaction costs are the execution arithmetic from liquidity risk, and the credit assessment that a 200 basis point gap actually requires is in credit analysis.

Where this goes next. Lecture 6 takes the summary's other two active bond techniques, policy switching and yield pick-up, which are the opposite kind of trade: policy switching takes deliberate views on the curve's level and shape, so the duration mismatch that ruins an anomaly switch is the entire position, and the summary calls it high risk accordingly. Yield pick-up moves into credit and illiquidity for a spread, which is where the 200 basis point gaps that fall outside this lecture's coherent band actually live. Lectures 7 to 11 then supply the derivative implementations, and lecture 9 on swaps matters here because a swap can express a duration view without the bond-selection problem this lecture has been about.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank's version of anomaly switching is basis trading in the treasury book, and the doubled-cost result is the same: a trade intended to be unwound pays the bid-offer twice, which is why basis positions are sized against a holding period rather than against the apparent mispricing, as set out in market risk. The duration mismatch finding is the bank's residual gap risk after a hedge, treated in interest rate risk, where a hedge matched on notional rather than on sensitivity leaves exactly this exposure. And the coupon and tax result is the mechanism behind coupon-stripping and dividend-washing structures, which is why tax authorities treat them as avoidance: the gain is a transfer between investors of different tax status rather than a return on capital.
