Step 1

The 30-second version

Transition management appears four times in the F105 summary, once under each instrument, and never as a topic in its own right. Assembling those four passages gives a complete framework: a trigger, five costs, four implementation routes, and three uses. This lecture prices all of it, and the summary is wrong twice in ways worth knowing.

The five costs are broker commission, bid-offer spreads, tax liabilities, admin costs and pricing impacts, listed flat as though comparable. On a one-day trade tax is the largest at 3.6000 per cent, which reproduces lecture 7's finding that capital gains tax was 1.8000 of a 2.2400 per cent saving. But four of the five are linear in trade size and market impact is not: it grows as the square root of the amount traded, so its share climbs from 7.33 per cent of the total on a tenth of a day's volume to 71.43 per cent on a hundred days' worth, overtaking the other four combined at 16.0000 times average daily volume. On any transition large enough to need managing, the cost the summary names last dominates, and it is the only one not quoted in advance.

"Better to transact entire asset classes" follows from that convexity and is conditional in a way the summary does not say. Shorting one index future costs 0.1414 per cent in impact against 0.8944 for selling two hundred constituents, a ratio of 6.32, because the same value is a smaller fraction of a deeper market. Reverse the liquidity and the advice reverses: against a thin future, the physical route is cheaper for any transition below 0.60 of each name's daily volume. It is a statement about relative liquidity, not about size, and it fails exactly where funds most want to use it.

On "no costs incurred", the summary is plainly wrong and its conclusion survives. The itemised bill for a supposedly costless six-month futures position is 0.4814 per cent in spreads, rolls, margin drag and impact, and the basis risk at one standard deviation adds 0.8000 more, which exceeds the whole itemised bill. Set that against the physical route's 5.0000 per cent and futures still win by 3.7186. The conclusion is right and the reason is wrong.

Comparing all four routes over horizons from a month to thirty years gave a result I did not expect: the swap is cheapest at every horizon up to about twenty years and the futures route is never cheapest at all, because rolling quarterly costs 0.3200 per cent a year in spreads against the swap's 0.2500 fee. That is lecture 9's finding again. The physical route wins only beyond twenty years, so the summary's strategic-against-tactical split is real and the dividing line falls between the derivative routes and the physical one.

Then the summary's most interesting and least explained use, consolidating a stock selection profit by shorting index futures. Shorting the index cuts the expected return from 8.5000 to 2.5000 per cent and cuts the return per unit of total risk from 0.4480 to 0.4167, which is the opposite of what the trade is assumed to do, because market risk is rewarded and uncorrelated with the stock-specific bet so removing it destroys diversification. The real case is tax: shorting locks in the gain without realising it, deferring 3.6000 per cent of capital gains tax, which is 7.48 times the futures route's own cost. In a tax-exempt fund the trade has no case at all.

Step 2

Why this matters

Everything in lectures 1 to 10 assumes a portfolio can be changed into another portfolio. This lecture is about what that assumption costs, and the answer is large enough to invalidate decisions made without it. A strategic allocation change worth 50 basis points a year in expected return is a bad decision if implementing it costs 5 per cent, and the arithmetic in section 4.1 shows that a full physical transition of a taxable portfolio can easily reach that figure. The transition cost is therefore not an operational detail: it is a term in the investment decision, and it belongs in the analysis before the decision rather than in the invoice afterwards.

The topic also matters because transition costs are the most systematically underestimated numbers in institutional investment. Commission and spreads are quoted, invoiced and visible. Market impact is none of those things: it appears as a worse average execution price than the price at the moment the decision was taken, which nobody records unless somebody chooses to. A fund that measures its transition costs by adding up its brokers' invoices will conclude they were 0.20 per cent when they were 5.00, and will keep making decisions on that basis.

There is a governance dimension too. A transition sits between two managers, and neither owns it. The outgoing manager's performance record ends at the decision date and the incoming manager's begins when the assets arrive, so the cost falls into a gap that no performance measurement captures. That is why transition management became a separate industry with separate mandates, and it is why the implementation shortfall framework of section 4.6 exists: to attribute a cost that the ordinary measurement machinery loses.

For an actuary the relevance is that transition costs are asymmetric in a way that affects advice. Recommending a change requires the fund to pay the cost with certainty in exchange for a benefit that is uncertain. That asymmetry biases the correct answer towards fewer, larger changes rather than continuous optimisation, and it is the honest quantitative argument for the strategic allocation being reviewed every three years rather than every quarter, which lecture 4 approached from the entirely different direction of statistical significance.

Step 3

The mental model

Trading costs divide into what you are charged and what you cause. The first is quoted, linear and small. The second is unquoted, convex and usually larger.

Imagine you want to sell a house on a street of identical houses. The estate agent's fee is 2 per cent, quoted in advance, and it does not change whether you sell one house or ten.

Now suppose you own all ten. Sell the first at the going rate. The second is harder, because the only buyer who wanted a house on that street has one. By the fifth, you are cutting the price to find buyers who had not been looking. By the tenth, the street has a reputation.

The agent's fee is still 2 per cent on each. But the price you actually got fell as you went, and nobody sent you an invoice for the difference. That difference is market impact, and notice its shape: the loss per house grew as you sold more, so the total grew faster than the number of houses. Doubling the number of houses more than doubles the cost.

That is why the fifth cost on the summary's list behaves nothing like the first four, and why every technique in this lecture is really the same technique: find a deeper market. An index future is the same exposure traded where the buyers are. One future on a liquid contract is one house on a street with a thousand buyers.

And there is a second cost that is even harder to see. Suppose you sell slowly, one house a month, to avoid spoiling the street. If prices rise during that year, the houses you have not yet sold went up and you did not benefit. If they fall, the houses you did not sell fell too. Trading slowly reduces the impact and increases the chance that the market moves before you finish. You cannot minimise both, and the whole job of a transition manager is finding where the two curves cross.

Formally, market impact follows an approximately square-root law: the price concession is proportional to sqrt(Q / ADV), where Q is the quantity traded and ADV the average daily volume. That functional form matters more than its coefficient. Because the exponent is below one, the cost per unit traded falls with size, which is why splitting a trade across days helps. Because the total is concave in the daily rate but the number of days raises exposure to price drift, the optimum is interior. Both facts are computed in sections 4.1 and 4.6.

The summary's four transition routes, one per instrument

   route        what the summary says about it
   physical     buy or sell the underlying over a LONG period, at favourable
                prices, with careful analysis. this is the SAA route
   futures      long or short over a SHORT period, no capital gains tax,
                useful where the assets are illiquid. this is the TAA route
   options      unlike futures, the downside risk is reduced and less upside
                profit is available, because a premium is paid
   equity swap  more flexible than futures, a small bank fee, larger contracts
                can be arranged over a longer period; credit risk increases

                gives immediate rebalancing on a TAA change

Four routes and three uses: a strategic change, a tactical change, and a policy switch. Section 4.4 prices all four against each other.

Step 4

The detail

4.1 The five costs, and why one of them is not like the others

Take the summary's five components and put a number on each for a trade equal to one day's average volume, on a position carrying a 20 per cent unrealised gain taxed at 18 per cent.

The summary's five transition costs, on a one-day trade

   the summary's cost   what it is                            on a 1x ADV   is it linear
                                                              trade         in size?
   broker commission    a quoted rate on the value traded         0.1500%   yes
   bid-offer spread     half the quoted spread, each side         0.2000%   yes
   tax liabilities      CGT at 18% on a 20% unrealised gain       3.6000%   yes
   admin costs          custody, settlement and reporting         0.0500%   yes
   pricing impacts      the price moved by your own order         1.0000%   NO

   total on a 1x ADV trade                                       5.0000%

Tax is the largest single component at this size, which reproduces lecture 7's finding. Only the last one is convex.

Tax dominates at this trade size, at 3.6000 per cent of 5.0000, and that agrees with lecture 7, where a futures transition saved 2.2400 per cent of which capital gains tax was 1.8000. That is worth holding on to, because it means the single most effective transition technique for a taxable investor is not a better execution algorithm: it is any structure that changes the exposure without realising the gain, which is what sections 4.4 and 4.5 are about.

But the ordering is not fixed, because one component scales differently from the rest. Commission, spread, tax and admin are all proportional to the value traded, so their percentage cost is constant. Market impact follows the square-root law, so it rises without limit as the trade grows relative to the market's capacity to absorb it.

   trade size as a   linear costs     market impact   total     impact's share

                 1.0        4.0000%          1.0000%   5.0000%           20.00%

               100.0        4.0000%         10.0000%   14.0000%           71.43%

The four linear costs never move. Impact's share climbs from 7.33 to 71.43 per cent and crosses the other four combined at 16.0000 times daily volume.

Impact overtakes all four other costs combined at exactly 16.0000 times average daily volume, and it is the majority of the total from 20 times upwards. So the summary's flat list is misleading in precisely the case that matters: on a transition large enough to require managing, the cost it names last is the one that dominates, and it is the only one not quoted in advance. The four a fund can get a price for are the four that matter least. That is the first correction, and it is a correction of emphasis rather than of fact: the list is complete and its ordering implies a comparability that does not hold.

4.2 "Better to transact entire asset classes" is about liquidity, not size

The summary advises transacting entire asset classes and gives no reason. The reason is section 4.1's convexity, and making it explicit changes when the advice applies. Selling two hundred constituents means each name absorbs a trade sized against its own daily volume. Shorting one index future means the whole value is measured against the future's volume, which is far larger.

The same transition, two routes

   route                          what is traded          impact per   total
                                                          leg          impact
   sell 200 constituents          0.80x each name's ADV       0.8944%     0.8944%
   short one index future         0.02x the future's ADV      0.1414%     0.1414%

A saving of 0.7530 per cent from the same value being a smaller fraction of a deeper market.

The future's impact is 0.1414 per cent against the physical route's 0.8944, a ratio of 6.32, and the entire saving comes from the same value being a smaller fraction of a deeper market. Nothing about the exposure changed. That is the whole mechanism, and it explains why the advice is conditional.

When the advice reverses: a thin future against a small transition

   the transition's size    impact trading   impact via a   which route
   against each name's ADV   physically       thin future    is cheaper
                      0.01           0.1000%         0.7746%   physical
                      0.05           0.2236%         0.7746%   physical
                      0.20           0.4472%         0.7746%   physical
                      0.80           0.8944%         0.7746%   the future

Against a future absorbing 0.60 of its own daily volume, the physical route wins for any transition below 0.60 of each name's volume.

"Better to transact entire asset classes" is therefore a statement about relative liquidity and not about size. It holds whenever the derivative market is deeper relative to the trade than the underlying market is, which for large-capitalisation equity indices is nearly always. It fails for asset classes with no liquid futures market, which is exactly where funds most want to use it: property, private equity, infrastructure, emerging market small capitalisation. The summary's note that futures make illiquid assets "useful" for a tactical change is the same observation from the other side, and it only works when a liquid proxy exists.

4.3 "No costs incurred" is false, and the conclusion survives anyway

The summary states that a tactical change executed with futures incurs no costs. It is right that there is no capital gains tax and no custody transfer, which between them are the bulk of the physical route's bill. It is wrong that there are no costs, and the bill is itemisable for a six-month position rolled quarterly.

The bill for a "costless" six-month futures position

   the cost the summary says is not incurred            amount
   the future's bid-offer spread, in and out             0.0800%
   rolling the future 2 times                            0.1600%
   cash drag on 10% margin for 0.5 years                 0.1000%
   market impact on the future                           0.1414%

   total, before basis risk                              0.4814%
   basis risk at one standard deviation                  0.8000%

The largest cost of the costless route is the basis risk, which exceeds the whole itemised bill and appears on no invoice.

The itemised bill is 0.4814 per cent and the basis risk at one standard deviation is 0.8000 per cent, which exceeds it. That pattern recurs throughout this lecture: the largest cost is consistently the one that does not appear on any invoice. Lecture 7 established that basis risk is irreducible when the hedge instrument is not the exact exposure, and a transition is precisely that situation, since the future tracks the index and the portfolio does not.

Now the comparison the summary is actually making, which is fair. Against the physical route's 5.0000 per cent, the futures route costs 0.4814 plus 0.8000 of basis risk, a total of 1.2814, saving 3.7186 per cent even counting basis risk in full. So the summary's conclusion is right and its reason is wrong. The examinable position is that a tactical change through futures avoids capital gains tax and custody transfer, and the complete answer adds the spread, the roll, the margin drag and the basis. Saying "no costs" in an exam invites the follow-up question and does not survive it.

4.4 The four routes over a horizon

The summary describes four routes without comparing them. Price all four on the same transition at horizons from a month to thirty years, charging the physical route once and letting the derivative routes accumulate their rolls, fees and premiums.

     0.25y    5.0000%   0.3514%   3.5000%   0.2625%   swap

The swap is cheapest until about twenty years. The futures route is never cheapest at any horizon tested, which I did not expect.

The futures route is never cheapest, and the reason is lecture 9's: rolling quarterly costs 0.3200 per cent a year in spreads against the swap's 0.2500 per cent fee, so the swap dominates the future on cost at every horizon rather than only at long ones. The physical route wins beyond about twenty years, once the accumulated swap fee exceeds the one-off tax and impact charge. So the summary's strategic-against-tactical split is real and its dividing line falls between the derivative routes and the physical one, not between futures and everything else. A fund holding a synthetic exposure for two decades should own the assets.

The options route is never cheapest at any horizon and that does not settle anything, because a cost table cannot rank it against the others. The summary's own note is that it reduces downside risk and forfeits upside through the premium, which is what the premium buys. Lecture 8 priced that floor exactly: it is the strike, so a protective structure has a known worst case and the other three routes do not. Never cheapest is not never right, and the options route is the only one whose total cost is known at inception.

4.5 Consolidating a stock selection profit

The summary's last transition use is its most interesting and least explained: "stock selection profit consolidated, remove the profit and loss from the sector, short index futures". A manager has picked stocks well within a sector and wants to keep the gain without keeping the sector exposure. Shorting the index removes the market component and leaves the stock-specific one.

Holding the stocks against holding them and shorting the index

   position                       expected   volatility   information   what the
                                  return                  ratio         return is
                                                                        exposed to
   hold the stocks outright         8.5000%     18.9737%        0.4480   market and skill
   hold them and short the index    2.5000%      6.0000%        0.4167   skill only

The hedge lowers the return from 8.5000 to 2.5000 per cent and lowers the return per unit of total risk from 0.4480 to 0.4167.

The result is the opposite of what the trade is usually assumed to do. Shorting the index lowers the return per unit of total risk, from 0.4480 to 0.4167, and the reason is diversification. The market return and the stock-specific return are uncorrelated, so holding both gives a combined volatility of 18.9737 per cent against 18.00 and 6.00 for the parts, while the returns add in full. Removing a rewarded and uncorrelated risk destroys that diversification, and the ratio falls even though the volatility falls a great deal more than the return does.

Selling triggers 3.6000 per cent in capital gains tax and shorting the index triggers none. The summary's phrase "profit consolidated" is exactly right and more precise than it looks: the profit is secured without being realised, in both the market sense and the tax sense.

The consequence the summary does not draw is that this trade belongs to a taxed investor. Most pension funds are tax-exempt, so for the largest category of institutional investor the tax case vanishes and section 4.5's risk arithmetic says the trade is mildly harmful. A tax-exempt fund that wants the stock-specific bet without the market should not hold the stocks and short the index; it should simply hold less of the market elsewhere, which costs nothing.

4.6 Implementation shortfall, and the two costs the summary omits

The summary's five costs are all costs of trading. Perold pointed out in 1988 that the largest cost of a transition is often not trading at all: it is the price moving between the decision and the execution, and the portion of the trade never completed. Decompose the gap between the paper portfolio and the real one.

Implementation shortfall on a transition, 15 per cent of which never executed

   the component                                        amount   share of the
                                                                 shortfall
   delay cost: the price moved before trading began     0.6800%         29.31%
   market impact and spread on what was traded          1.0200%         43.97%
   commission and admin on what was traded              0.1700%          7.33%
   opportunity cost on the 15% never executed           0.4500%         19.40%

   total implementation shortfall                       2.3200%        100.00%

The two components the summary does not name come to 1.1300 per cent, 48.71 per cent of the total, just under half.

Delay and opportunity cost together are 1.1300 per cent of a 2.3200 shortfall, 48.71 per cent, just under half. The largest single component is one the summary does name, market impact at 1.0200 per cent, which agrees with section 4.1. So the summary's list is incomplete rather than wrong: it captures the largest component and misses about half the total.

The two directions cannot both be minimised, and that tension is the whole reason transition management is a job. Trading slowly reduces impact from 1.0000 per cent at one day to 0.2236 over twenty days, and raises the chance of not finishing, so impact and opportunity cost move in opposite directions as execution speed changes.

The speed trade-off, and where it bottoms out

   days spread over   impact (falls with   chance of not   opportunity   total
                      slower trading)      finishing       cost
                    1                1.0000%             2.00%        0.0600%   1.0600%
                    2                0.7071%             4.00%        0.1200%   0.8271%
                    5                0.4472%            10.00%        0.3000%   0.7472%
                   10                0.3162%            20.00%        0.6000%   0.9162%
                   20                0.2236%            40.00%        1.2000%   1.4236%

Not monotone. The total falls then rises, with a minimum at five days at 0.7472 per cent against 1.0600 at one day and 1.4236 at twenty.

The total is not monotone in trading speed. It falls and then rises, bottoming at five days at 0.7472 per cent against 1.0600 at one day and 1.4236 at twenty. The optimum is interior, which is the entire quantitative justification for employing a transition manager: there is a right answer, it is not "as fast as possible" or "as slowly as possible", and finding it requires knowing both curves. A fund that trades as slowly as it can to be careful ends up paying 1.4236 rather than 0.7472, which is worse than trading everything in a single day.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Three things a practitioner knows about transitions that no cost table conveys, and the first is about who is measured.

A transition is unowned, and unowned costs are unmeasured. The outgoing manager's record stops at the termination date, the incoming manager's starts when the assets arrive, and the trustees see two performance reports neither of which contains the transition. That is why the cost is systematically underestimated: not because it is hard to compute, but because no one is accountable for the number. The correct governance response is to measure the transition against the paper portfolio explicitly, which is what implementation shortfall was invented for, and to appoint someone whose mandate is that number alone. A fund that does not do this is not paying less; it is not looking.

Market impact is information leakage, and information leaks through people. The square-root law in section 4.1 is a description of what happens, not an explanation. What actually happens is that the market learns a large seller exists and prices accordingly, and it learns through the ordinary mechanics of asking for a price. Every broker approached for a quote knows something valuable, and the cost of a transition is partly a function of how many people were told. That is why transition managers use crossing networks and portfolio trades rather than working orders in the open market, and why the number of counterparties invited to bid is itself a cost decision. None of this appears in any formula.

The tax result in section 4.1 dominates everything else for a taxed investor, and it changes what a transition is for. If capital gains tax is 3.6000 per cent of the position and every other cost combined is 1.4000, then the correct objective is not "change the portfolio cheaply". It is "change the exposure without changing the ownership", and every derivative route in this lecture is a way of doing that. A practitioner facing a large unrealised gain does not run an execution algorithm; they run an overlay, hold the assets indefinitely, and let the tax charge die with the investor or the fund. That is a legitimate and enormous consideration that the summary touches in one clause, "no capital gains tax", and does not develop.

On the summary itself the correction I would emphasise is section 4.3's. Writing "no costs incurred" in an examination answer is a trap, because the follow-up question is obvious and the itemised bill is not small: 0.4814 per cent plus 0.8000 of basis risk, against a genuine 5.0000 per cent saving. The conclusion is right by a wide margin. Stating it as "no costs" converts a strong argument into one that collapses under a single question, and an examiner who knows the material will ask that question.

What this assumes. The four instruments priced here are built in forwards and futures, options and swaps, and each route's costs come from the lecture that priced it: the basis risk and roll cost from hedging with forwards and futures, the premium and protective floor from using options, and the fee and counterparty exposure from using swaps. The strategic and tactical allocation decisions that trigger a transition are in active versus passive, and the review-frequency argument that section 2 connects to is in measuring portfolio performance. The tracking error a partial physical transition leaves behind is quantified in equity index tracking.

Where this goes next. Lectures 12 and 13 on behavioural finance close this module and bear on section 4.6 directly: the delay cost is a behavioural quantity, since it measures how long a decision sat unexecuted, and the reluctance to complete a trade at a worse price than the decision price is loss aversion operating on a reference point. The unfilled portion of a transition is often a choice rather than a constraint, and lecture 12's framing material explains why that choice gets made. The overview lectures on mandates and governance also pick up section 5's accountability gap, since the remedy is a mandate that makes the transition somebody's number.

Where the bank sits opposite. The bank is on the other side of every route here, and its economics are the mirror image: what the fund experiences as market impact the bank experiences as the risk of warehousing a position it must unwind, which is priced through the market risk framework in market risk. A portfolio trade in which a bank guarantees a price for an entire transition is the bank selling certainty and taking the impact risk itself, and the spread it charges is the price of that transfer. The margin and collateral flows a synthetic transition generates are the same funding mechanics as in currency hedging, and their systemic form is in liquidity risk. The tax deferral of section 4.5 is also the bank's product: an equity swap that replicates a holding without transferring it exists partly because the tax treatment differs, which is a structuring business rather than a trading one.
