The case for indexing does not need the efficient markets hypothesis, and the summary says it does

The value-weighted active return of every investor together is exactly zero before costs, verified to machine precision. That is an accounting identity about who holds what, so the average actively managed pound must underperform by exactly the fee difference whether markets are efficient or not. The summary lists "EMH assumption" as a drawback of index tracking; it is not required. Along the way: an information coefficient of 0.05 needs 100 independent bets a year to reach an information ratio of 0.50, core-satellite leaves the information ratio unchanged at 0.6000 whatever the satellite weight, and "buying high" turns out to be the same objection as "EMH assumption" stated twice.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The performance measurement module asked how to judge a manager. This module asks what a manager should do, and it starts with the only question that has a settled answer: whether to try at all. The summary gives passive and active management as two lists of techniques with advantages and drawbacks. One item on its list of drawbacks is wrong, one is an advantage, and two are the same point twice, and establishing that takes an identity rather than an argument.

Every share is held by somebody. So if passive investors hold the index by construction, active investors between them must also hold the index, and their value-weighted average active return is exactly zero before costs. Constructing a market of twelve holdings split between five arbitrary active managers and a passive holder, the aggregate active return is zero to machine precision whatever anyone's skill. The average actively managed pound therefore underperforms the average passive pound by exactly the fee difference, 0.90 per cent at a one per cent fee against ten basis points.

That does not depend on the efficient markets hypothesis at all, which is what makes the summary's inclusion of "EMH assumption" among the drawbacks of index tracking a genuine error. What the hypothesis bears on is the opposite claim, that skill exists and can be found, and there the constraint is arithmetic too. By the fundamental law, an information coefficient of 0.05, which is about as good as anyone demonstrates, needs 100 independent bets a year to reach an information ratio of 0.50. A concentrated manager with ten genuine insights needs a forecast correlation of 0.1581 to get there.

On the replication methods, the summary's qualitative trade-off has a shape worth knowing: tracking error from omitting names falls from 5.4582 per cent at 25 holdings to 2.5044 at 100 and to exactly zero at all 500, so the marginal value of extra names collapses. And core-satellite turns out not to do what it appears to: the fund's information ratio is 0.6000 at every satellite weight, identical to machine precision, because alpha and tracking error both scale linearly. It is a risk-budgeting and fee device, not a return-enhancing one.

Then "buying high", the summary's least explained phrase. A capitalisation-weighted index weights by price, so with mean-reverting pricing noise it holds more of whatever is overvalued: at a 30 per cent noise the drag against equal weighting is 9.3737 per cent. But at zero noise the drag is exactly zero, so the objection is the claim that markets are inefficient, restated, and cannot be listed alongside "EMH assumption" as a separate item.

Feynman test Somebody owns every share. So if the index-trackers own their slice of everything, everyone else must own their slice of everything too, which means the trying-hard investors collectively get exactly the market return before fees and less after. No view about whether markets are clever is needed for that; it is just counting. Trying hard can still work for you personally, but only if you have a small edge applied a great many times, and a hundred separate bets a year is more than most managers make. And two of the standard complaints about index funds turn out to be the same complaint, which is that markets get prices wrong, which is the thing being argued about rather than a separate reason.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the identity settles the aggregate question completely, and almost every argument about active management is conducted as though it were an empirical dispute. It is not. The average active pound underperforms by the fee difference as a matter of arithmetic, so the only live question is whether a particular investor can select the managers who beat the average, which is the question performance measurement lecture 4 showed takes 10.82 years to answer.

The second is that the breadth requirement is the constraint nobody quotes when discussing active styles. The summary lists five styles and no style overcomes the need for 100 independent bets at a plausible skill level. A high-conviction concentrated manager is not a different route to the same information ratio; it is a route that requires implausible skill.

The third is that core-satellite is widely sold as a way to get active returns cheaply, and the arithmetic says it changes the amount of active risk and nothing about its quality. That matters for how trustees should think about it: as a decision about the risk budget and the fee bill, not as a way to improve the manager.

And the fourth is the drafting point about the summary's own list. Nine items, of which two duplicate others, one is an advantage listed as a drawback and one is not required at all. For the exam the list as given is what is marked, so it must be learned as it stands, and knowing which items are real is what allows a candidate to write about them rather than recite them.

The detail

The arithmetic of active management, which is an identity

Every share in the market is held by somebody. If the passive investors hold the index by construction, the active investors between them must also hold the index, so their value-weighted average active return is exactly zero before costs. THAT IS AN ACCOUNTING IDENTITY, NOT A THEORY ABOUT EFFICIENCY. Demonstrate it. Manager 3 beat the index by 4.07 per cent and manager 5 lost 1.60, and the sizes weight them so that the total is exactly zero. MECHANISM TEST, AND IT MATTERS. The passive holder's own active return is +0.5523 per cent, which is NOT zero. "Passive" means holding the INDEX, and the residual left over after the active managers have chosen their positions is not the index unless the active managers happen to hold the index between them. SO A PASSIVE INVESTOR IS ONLY TRULY PASSIVE IF THEY TRACK THE INDEX DELIBERATELY, and in aggregate the non-active residual is a different thing from an index fund.
NOW THE COST CONSEQUENCE, which is the whole of Sharpe's argument: annual fee active manager passive fund the gap the active manager must earn BEFORE costs merely to match 0.75% 0.10% 0.65% 1.00% 0.10% 0.90% 1.50% 0.15% 1.35% 2.00% 0.20% 1.80% THE REQUIRED GROSS OUTPERFORMANCE EQUALS THE FEE DIFFERENCE EXACTLY, which follows immediately from the identity: if the average active gross return equals the index, the average active net return is the index less the fee gap. SO THE AVERAGE ACTIVELY MANAGED POUND MUST UNDERPERFORM THE AVERAGE PASSIVE POUND BY EXACTLY THE FEE DIFFERENCE. And nothing in that argument mentions market efficiency. IT HOLDS IN A WILDLY INEFFICIENT MARKET AND IN A PERFECTLY EFFICIENT ONE, which is why "EMH assumption" does not belong on a list of the drawbacks of index tracking. Indexing needs the arithmetic, and the arithmetic is unconditional. WHAT THE ARGUMENT DOES NOT SHOW is that any particular investor should index. The average is not the distribution: if half the active pounds beat the index by two per cent gross and half lose two per cent, the average is still zero, and an investor who can identify the first half should be active. THAT SELECTION PROBLEM IS THE SUBJECT OF PERFORMANCE MEASUREMENT LECTURE 4, and its answer was 10.82 years.

What the efficient markets hypothesis actually bears on

If markets are inefficient then skill exists and the investor's problem is FINDING it. That is a different question from whether the average active pound underperforms, and it has its own arithmetic constraint. The fundamental law of active management relates the information ratio to the quality and the quantity of a manager's decisions: IR = IC x sqrt(breadth) where the information coefficient IC is the correlation between the manager's forecasts and outcomes, and breadth is the number of INDEPENDENT bets per year. Inverting it: information independent bets per year needed for an information ratio of coefficient 0.25 0.50 0.75 1.00 0.02 156 625 1406 2500 0.05 25 100 225 400 0.10 6 25 56 100 0.20 2 6 14 25 AT AN INFORMATION COEFFICIENT OF 0.05, WHICH IS A FORECAST CORRELATION OF FIVE PER CENT AND ABOUT AS GOOD AS ANYBODY DEMONSTRATES, AN INFORMATION RATIO OF 0.50 NEEDS 100 INDEPENDENT BETS A YEAR. And the requirement is QUADRATIC in the skill: halving the information coefficient quadruples the breadth needed, verified at every level in the table. TURN IT ROUND FOR A CONCENTRATED MANAGER. Ten independent bets a year require an information coefficient of 0.1581 to reach an information ratio of 0.50, which is a forecast correlation of 15.81 per cent. That is three times the level anybody demonstrates. SO THE PRACTICAL CONSTRAINT ON ACTIVE MANAGEMENT IS BREADTH, NOT INSIGHT, and the summary's list of five styles is silent on it. A high-conviction manager holding twenty names is not taking a different route to the same information ratio; they are taking a route that requires implausible forecasting accuracy. THE WORD "INDEPENDENT" IS ALSO DOING WORK: a manager holding a hundred names that are all expressions of one view about interest rates has a breadth of one, not a hundred, which is the same correlated-bets result measured in portfolio construction lecture 10.

Full, partial and synthetic replication

The summary gives three replication methods with qualitative trade-offs: full replication has zero tracking error and is expensive, partial replication is cheaper with higher tracking error and higher research cost, and synthetic replication behaves like partial. Put numbers on it, for a 500-name index whose holdings carry 28 per cent specific volatility: names held share of the index tracking error cost of trading them all by count from omission relative to full replication 25 5.00% 5.4582% 5.00% 50 10.00% 3.7566% 10.00% 100 20.00% 2.5044% 20.00% 200 40.00% 1.5336% 40.00% 350 70.00% 0.8198% 70.00% 500 100.00% 0.0000% 100.00% FULL REPLICATION GIVES EXACTLY ZERO TRACKING ERROR, as the summary says, and that is what makes it the benchmark case rather than a good idea. Tracking error falls monotonically with the number of names. THE SHAPE IS THE USEFUL PART. Going from 25 names to 100 cuts the tracking error from 5.4582 to 2.5044 per cent, and searching for the point at which it halves from its 25-name level gives 87 names. So roughly a fifth of the index gets you more than half the benefit, and the last 300 names buy 0.8198 per cent of tracking error between them. THAT IS THE SAME COLLAPSING-MARGINAL-VALUE SHAPE AS THE KEY-RATE INSTRUMENT SCHEDULE IN PORTFOLIO CONSTRUCTION LECTURE 9, and it has the same implication for a mandate: SPECIFY A TRACKING ERROR AND LET THE MANAGER CHOOSE THE NUMBER OF NAMES. A mandate that specifies the number of holdings is constraining the wrong variable. AND SYNTHETIC REPLICATION IS NOT SIMPLY "LIKE PARTIAL", which is how the summary has it. Holding cash and an index future reproduces the index return with essentially no name-selection tracking error at all, so its tracking error comes from a different source: basis risk between the future and the index, plus the roll. That is a substitution of one risk for another rather than a point on the same trade-off curve, and it is the subject of lecture 7.

Core-satellite does not change the information ratio

The summary offers core-satellite as a method: track the core passively and manage satellites actively. Work out what it does. Take a satellite manager with an information ratio of 0.60 and an 8.00 per cent tracking error against their own benchmark: satellite weight fund alpha fund tracking error fund information ratio 5.00% 0.2400% 0.4000% 0.6000 10.00% 0.4800% 0.8000% 0.6000 20.00% 0.9600% 1.6000% 0.6000 40.00% 1.9200% 3.2000% 0.6000 100.00% 4.8000% 8.0000% 0.6000 THE FUND'S INFORMATION RATIO IS 0.6000 AT EVERY SATELLITE WEIGHT, IDENTICAL TO MACHINE PRECISION, because the alpha and the tracking error both scale linearly with the weight and the ratio cancels. SO CORE-SATELLITE IS A RISK-BUDGETING DEVICE AND NOT A RETURN-ENHANCING ONE. It sets HOW MUCH active risk the fund runs and cannot improve the QUALITY of it. A trustee choosing a ten per cent satellite rather than a forty per cent one is choosing to run a quarter of the active risk and to receive a quarter of the expected alpha, at the same efficiency. WHAT IT DOES CHANGE IS THE FEE BILL, so check whether that makes it free money: satellite weight blended fee fund alpha alpha net of the fee difference 5.00% 0.1400% 0.2400% 0.2000% 10.00% 0.1800% 0.4800% 0.4000% 20.00% 0.2600% 0.9600% 0.8000% 40.00% 0.4200% 1.9200% 1.6000% 100.00% 0.9000% 4.8000% 4.0000% IT DOES NOT. The net alpha still rises monotonically with the satellite weight, so shrinking the active portion does not create value; it reduces the active fee AND the active return in the same proportion. THE HONEST DESCRIPTION IS THEREFORE A GOVERNANCE AND COST DECISION RATHER THAN AN INVESTMENT ONE. Core-satellite lets the trustees set an explicit active risk budget and pay active fees only on the portion where active risk is being taken, which is worth having for exactly those two reasons and for neither of the reasons it is usually sold on.

"Buying high", and why it is not a separate objection

The summary lists "buying high" among the disadvantages of index tracking without explaining it. The argument is that a capitalisation-weighted index weights by PRICE, so if prices contain any noise the index systematically holds more of whatever is currently overvalued and less of whatever is undervalued. Quantify the drag against equal weighting, assuming the noise fully reverts: pricing noise, one cap-weighted minus equal-weighted return standard deviation when the noise fully reverts 0.00% 0.0000% 10.00% -0.9997% 20.00% -4.0560% 30.00% -9.3737% 40.00% -17.1862% THE DRAG GROWS WITH THE NOISE AND IS SUBSTANTIAL AT PLAUSIBLE LEVELS: 9.3737 per cent at a 30 per cent pricing noise. AT ZERO NOISE THE DRAG IS EXACTLY ZERO, which confirms the mechanism and also settles the status of the objection. THE CALCULATION ASSUMES THE NOISE REVERTS, WHICH IS THE ASSUMPTION IN QUESTION. If prices are efficient there is no noise to revert, the first row applies, and cap-weighting costs nothing. SO "BUYING HIGH" IS NOT AN INDEPENDENT OBJECTION TO INDEXING. IT IS THE CLAIM THAT MARKETS ARE INEFFICIENT, RESTATED. Two of the summary's nine items are therefore the same objection stated twice, and it is an objection to the efficient markets hypothesis rather than to index tracking. THAT DOES NOT MAKE IT WORTHLESS. It identifies the specific mechanism by which inefficiency would hurt a cap-weighted indexer, which is more useful than the bare assertion, and it is the argument behind fundamental indexation and equal-weighted alternatives. THE POINT IS THAT IT CANNOT BE COUNTED TWICE.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to carry is the difference between an identity and an argument, because it decides which debates are worth having. The aggregate active return is zero before costs by counting, so the average active pound underperforms by the fee gap unconditionally. NO EVIDENCE ABOUT MARKET EFFICIENCY CAN OVERTURN THAT, and no amount of manager skill can either, because skill redistributes the zero rather than raising it. The only question the identity leaves open is selection, and performance measurement lecture 4 priced that at 10.82 years.

WHAT THE IDENTITY DOES AND DOES NOT SETTLE. SETTLES the average active pound underperforms by the fee gap SETTLES that this is true in efficient and inefficient markets alike LEAVES OPEN whether YOU can identify the above-average managers LEAVES OPEN whether the fee gap is worth paying for a manager you can identify MOST ARGUMENTS ABOUT ACTIVE MANAGEMENT CONFUSE THE FIRST TWO WITH THE LAST TWO. the first two are arithmetic and the last two are about your own selection ability, which is a claim about you rather than about markets.

The second is the breadth requirement, which is the most useful single number for assessing an active proposition. An information coefficient of 0.05 needs 100 independent bets a year, and the relationship is quadratic, so a manager half as good needs four times the breadth. A CONCENTRATED HIGH-CONVICTION MANDATE IS NOT A DIFFERENT ROUTE TO THE SAME INFORMATION RATIO, it is a route requiring a forecast correlation of 0.1581 on ten bets, three times what anybody demonstrates. And the word "independent" carries most of the weight: a hundred positions all expressing one macro view have a breadth of one, which is the correlated-bets result from portfolio construction lecture 10 in a different setting.

The third is core-satellite, and the finding is worth stating plainly because the structure is usually sold on the wrong grounds. The fund's information ratio is 0.6000 at every satellite weight, exactly, because alpha and tracking error scale together. SO IT SETS HOW MUCH ACTIVE RISK THE FUND RUNS AND CANNOT IMPROVE THE QUALITY OF IT, and the net alpha still rises with the satellite weight, so shrinking the active portion is not free money either. What it genuinely buys is an explicit active risk budget and active fees paid only where active risk is taken, which are governance and cost benefits and are enough on their own.

THE THREE QUESTIONS TO PUT TO ANY ACTIVE PROPOSAL. what is the fee gap against the passive alternative? section A how many INDEPENDENT bets a year does the process make? section B what information coefficient does that breadth imply for section B the information ratio being promised? THE THIRD IS THE ONE THAT IS NEVER ASKED and it is arithmetic rather than judgement: an information ratio and a breadth together imply a forecasting accuracy, and the implied figure is often outside anything ever measured.

And the fourth, which is about reading a source rather than about investing. Nine items on the summary's list, of which two duplicate others, one is an advantage recorded as a drawback, and one is not required at all. The pattern is that a list assembled for recall accumulates items that sound like distinct reasons, and the way to test one is to ask what would have to be true for the item to bite. FOR "EMH ASSUMPTION" THE ANSWER IS NOTHING, BECAUSE THE ARITHMETIC IS UNCONDITIONAL; FOR "BUYING HIGH" THE ANSWER IS THAT MARKETS ARE INEFFICIENT, which is the same condition as the item two rows above. For the exam, reproduce the list. For practice, it reduces to three points, and the least appreciated of them is that finding an apt index is the hardest part of the whole exercise.

What this assumes. Where the bank sits opposite. A bank meets the arithmetic of active management as the zero-sum property of trading revenue, and it draws the same conclusion in a different vocabulary: client-driven flow business is preferred to proprietary position-taking precisely because the first earns a spread and the second is a bet against the market's aggregate, which is documented in market risk. The breadth argument also has a direct banking form: a credit portfolio's diversification depends on the number of INDEPENDENT exposures rather than the number of names, which is the single-name and sector concentration problem in credit risk, where correlated obligors in one sector are one bet however many facilities are written.
