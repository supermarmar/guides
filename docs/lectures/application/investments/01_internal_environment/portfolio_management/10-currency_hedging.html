A Turkish bond yielding 25 per cent and a Swiss one yielding minus 0.5 both return exactly 9 per cent once hedged

Covered interest parity means a fully hedged foreign bond earns the domestic rate and nothing else, identically, whatever the foreign yield. So the hedging decision is never about capturing a yield: it is a currency view, and the break-even is exactly the forward premium. Along the way: the optimal currency hedge ratio is 100 per cent only when the asset and the currency are uncorrelated, which is a coincidence rather than a rule, and a fund at South Africa's 45 per cent offshore limit needs 13.50 per cent of its total value in cash after a 30 per cent rand move, on a position that has not lost a cent.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The F105 summary has no standalone currency section. Its currency material sits in three places: a forward hedging example with five disadvantages, a one-line note that forex forwards are priced by covered interest parity, and the currency-matching case for swaps. That third item is the whole lecture, because the consequence of covered interest parity is the single most important fact about currency hedging and the summary states the premise without drawing it.

Invest a rand offshore, earn the foreign yield, and convert back at the forward rate. The forward rate is set so that the round trip earns the domestic rate exactly. On a 9 per cent domestic yield, a United States bond at 4.00 per cent, a Japanese one at 0.50, a Turkish one at 25.00 and a Swiss one at minus 0.50 all return 9.0000 per cent hedged, identical to machine precision. The forward premium absorbs the entire differential by construction, so a foreign yield advantage is not an opportunity once it is hedged. This is lecture 6's yield pick-up result in a new setting: the apparent free lunch is a quoting convention.

What follows is the useful reframing. The hedged return is the domestic rate; the unhedged return is the foreign yield plus whatever the spot rate does. Those cross at a currency move of 4.8077 per cent, which is exactly the forward premium. So the question is never "do I want the foreign yield", it is "do I expect the rand to weaken by more than the forward premium", and that is a currency view with nothing else attached.

On how much to hedge, the summary's ratio formula gives an answer that surprises people. Minimising the variance of the domestic-currency return gives h* = 1 + rho x sigma_asset / sigma_fx, which is one only when the asset and the currency are uncorrelated. Emerging equity at a 0.35 correlation wants 1.6067, more than fully hedged, and developed equity at minus 0.20 wants 0.7600. A mandate forbidding short currency positions therefore rules out the variance-minimising answer more often than not, though the cap is cheap: it costs emerging equity 1.6445 percentage points of volatility against the 8.2637 that hedging is worth in the first place.

Currency's share of risk also splits the decision in two. It is 86.21 per cent of a foreign government bond's variance and 38.78 per cent of foreign developed equity's, so those should not share a policy. I expected the correlation to reorder the four assets and it does not: emerging equity's positive correlation lifts its currency share from 24.97 to 30.79 per cent and still leaves it below developed equity's. The volatility ratio decides the ordering and the correlation only adjusts it.

Then the operational finding, which is where currency overlays actually fail. A forward settles in cash and the hedged asset does not. A fund at the 45 per cent Regulation 28 limit facing a 30 per cent rand move must find 13.50 per cent of its total value in cash, while the gain sits in offshore assets it cannot realise in time. The fund has not lost a cent and still has to settle.

Step 2

Why this matters

For a South African fund this is not a marginal topic. Regulation 28 of the Pension Funds Act permits 45 per cent of a retirement fund's assets offshore, raised from 30 per cent in February 2022, so nearly half a typical fund's assets carry currency exposure. At a 15 per cent rand-dollar volatility, that allocation alone contributes more risk than most funds' entire domestic equity selection decision. Whether to hedge it is therefore a bigger lever than almost anything in lectures 1 to 6, and it is usually decided by a single line in an investment policy statement.

The topic also matters because the reasoning most often given for going offshore is the wrong one. "Foreign bonds yield more" is a statement about unhedged returns dressed up as a statement about opportunity, and section 4.1 shows it evaporating the moment the currency is hedged. The genuine reasons to hold foreign assets are diversification, access to industries the domestic market lacks, and reducing the concentration of a fund's assets in the same economy that pays its members' wages. None of those is a yield argument, and a candidate who reaches for the yield argument has misunderstood the parity relation.

The third reason is that the currency decision is where investment and operations collide hardest. Every other decision in this module can be implemented by instructing a manager. A currency overlay is a continuing programme requiring margin, settlement, collateral, a counterparty relationship, and a cash buffer sized for a move nobody expects. The 2022 gilt crisis in the United Kingdom was a liability hedge working exactly as designed and producing a cash call larger than the liquid buffer available to meet it, and the mechanism generalises directly to a currency overlay.

For an actuary there is a fourth angle. A fund's liabilities are almost always denominated in the domestic currency and linked to domestic inflation, while its assets increasingly are not. Currency exposure is therefore a mismatch in the technical sense: it is a difference between the asset and liability cash flow bases, exactly like the interest-rate and inflation mismatches lecture 9 addressed with swaps. Treating it as an asset-allocation question rather than a matching question is the common error.

Step 3

The mental model

A currency hedge does not remove a risk you chose to take. It removes a risk that arrived attached to something else, and it does so by taking a second position that behaves nothing like the first.

Suppose someone offers you a Turkish government bond paying 25 per cent when your own market pays 9. It looks like an obvious trade until you ask who else can see it. Everybody can, and the people who trade currencies for a living have already worked out what a Turkish lira must be worth in a year's time for the two deals to be worth the same.

That is all a forward rate is. It is not a forecast. It is the number that makes the round trip pointless. If it were any other number, you could borrow at 9, invest at 25, sell the lira forward, and collect the difference with no risk at all, and the first person to notice would do it in enough size to move the forward rate to where it belongs.

So when you hedge the Turkish bond, you get 9 per cent. Not roughly 9. Exactly 9, to as many decimal places as you care to compute. The forward is priced to make it so.

Which means the 25 per cent was never available as a yield. It was available as a bet: the lira might not fall as far as the forward says it will. That is a perfectly respectable bet and it is a currency bet, and the moment you describe it that way, you can ask whether you have any reason to think you will win it. Most funds do not.

Now the second half, which is where currency differs from everything else in this module. When you hedge an equity you own, you must decide how much to hedge, and the obvious answer of "all of it" is wrong unless the equity and the currency move independently. If the shares tend to rise when the currency rises, the two exposures reinforce each other, and cancelling the currency exposure completely leaves you under-cancelled. You need to sell more currency forward than you own. That is a short position, and most mandates say no.

Formally, covered interest parity sets the forward rate F from the spot rate S and the two interest rates as F = S x (1 + r_domestic) / (1 + r_foreign). A fully hedged foreign investment converts at S, compounds at r_foreign, and converts back at F, so the return is (1 + r_foreign) x F / S - 1, which substitutes to r_domestic exactly. The relation is enforced by arbitrage rather than by economics, which is why it holds to machine precision and why the covered version differs from uncovered interest parity, which is an economic hypothesis and fails empirically.

The detail

4.1 The hedged foreign return is the domestic rate, exactly

Take a 9 per cent domestic yield and four foreign markets spanning the plausible range, including one yielding far more and one yielding less than nothing. Price the one-year forward by covered interest parity in each case and compute the round trip.

Four foreign bonds, hedged

   the two markets                          foreign   forward     hedged     the
                                            yield     premium     return     domestic
                                                      per year               yield
   South Africa 9.00%, United States 4.00%     4.00%     4.8077%    9.0000%      9.00%
   South Africa 9.00%, Japan 0.50%             0.50%     8.4577%    9.0000%      9.00%
   South Africa 9.00%, Turkey 25.00%          25.00%   -12.8000%    9.0000%      9.00%
   South Africa 9.00%, Switzerland -0.50%     -0.50%     9.5477%    9.0000%      9.00%

Four foreign yields spanning 25.5 percentage points. One hedged return, identical to machine precision.

The Turkish case is the instructive one. Its yield exceeds the domestic yield, so the forward premium turns negative at minus 12.8000 per cent: the lira is quoted forward at a discount large enough to consume the entire 16 point yield advantage. Hedging the Turkish bond and hedging the Swiss bond produce literally the same number, despite a 25.5 point gap in their yields. The yield differential is not an opportunity once it is hedged, and any presentation of offshore bonds that leads with their yields is describing an unhedged position without saying so.

Now the region in which the finding holds, which is where the actual decision lives. Parity constrains the forward and therefore the hedged position. It says nothing about the unhedged return, which is the foreign yield compounded by whatever the spot rate does.

Hedged against unhedged, on the United States case

   what the rand does   unhedged return   hedged return   which won
                -20.00%         -16.8000%          9.0000%   hedged
                -10.00%          -6.4000%          9.0000%   hedged
                  0.00%           4.0000%          9.0000%   hedged
                  4.81%           9.0024%          9.0000%   unhedged
                 10.00%          14.4000%          9.0000%   unhedged
                 20.00%          24.8000%          9.0000%   unhedged

A positive move is a weakening rand, which helps a foreign holding. The crossing is at 4.8077 per cent.

The break-even currency move is 4.8077 per cent, which is the forward premium, verified as an identity rather than a coincidence. That reframes the entire decision. The hedging question is not about yields, diversification, or foreign growth: it is a bet on whether the rand weakens by more than the forward premium. Every other consideration belongs to the decision to hold the foreign asset, which is separate and comes first.

The relation that fails, and it matters, is the uncovered version. Uncovered interest parity holds that the expected future spot rate also equals the interest-rate ratio, so high-yielding currencies are expected to depreciate by exactly their yield advantage. Empirically they do not, and the gap is persistent enough to have its own name, the forward premium puzzle, and its own trade, the carry trade. That failure is the reason a currency view can be worth holding. It is not a reason to assume one is right.

4.2 Currency's share of the risk depends on what it is attached to

The hedging decision is usually written as one policy for the whole offshore allocation. It should be at least two, because the currency's contribution to total variance depends on the local asset's volatility and on its correlation with the currency. Take a 15 per cent rand-dollar volatility against four foreign asset types.

The currency's share of an unhedged foreign holding's variance

   the foreign asset            local   currency   correlation   unhedged   currency's
                                vol     vol        with the FX   total vol  share of
                                                                            variance
   a foreign government bond     6.00%     15.00%          0.00      16.16%      86.21%
   a foreign corporate bond      9.00%     15.00%          0.10      18.25%      71.62%
   foreign developed equity     18.00%     15.00%         -0.20      21.00%      38.78%
   foreign emerging equity      26.00%     15.00%          0.35      34.26%      30.79%

An unhedged foreign government bond is 86 per cent a currency position and 14 per cent a bond. That is not a bond holding.

An unhedged foreign government bond is overwhelmingly a currency position wearing a bond's name: 86.21 per cent of its variance is the currency. Foreign developed equity is 38.78 per cent, less than half. A single hedging policy applied to both is therefore making a large decision about the bond and a modest one about the equity, and the usual practice of hedging foreign bonds fully and foreign equity partially or not at all follows directly from this table rather than from convention.

I expected the correlation to reorder the four and it does not. Emerging equity's positive 0.35 correlation lifts its currency share from 24.97 per cent, which is where it would sit at zero correlation, to 30.79. That is a material adjustment of nearly six points, and it still leaves emerging equity below developed equity's 38.78 despite the correlation running the other way. The ratio of the two volatilities decides the ordering and the correlation only adjusts it.

The share as a function of local volatility alone, at zero correlation

   local volatility   currency volatility   currency's share of variance at rho = 0
               3.00%                 15.00%                                    96.15%
               6.00%                 15.00%                                    86.21%
              12.00%                 15.00%                                    60.98%
              15.00%                 15.00%                                    50.00%
              30.00%                 15.00%                                    20.00%

The share falls monotonically and passes through exactly 50 per cent where the two volatilities are equal.

The dividing line is where the local volatility equals the currency volatility, at 15 per cent each, and the share passes through exactly one half there. That gives a usable rule of thumb: anything less volatile than the currency is mostly a currency position. Cash, short bonds and government bonds are all on that side of the line, which is why unhedged offshore cash is such a strange holding: it is a pure currency bet with a small interest coupon attached.

4.3 The optimal hedge ratio is one only when the correlation is zero

The summary gives the optimal hedge ratio as the correlation times the ratio of the standard deviations, which lecture 7 applied to a cross-hedge where the hedging instrument proxies the asset. Currency is structurally different, because the exposure is embedded in the asset rather than proxied by it. Writing the domestic-currency return as the local return plus the currency return, hedging a fraction h of the currency, and minimising the variance over h gives h* = 1 + rho x sigma_asset / sigma_fx.

The variance-minimising currency hedge ratio

   the foreign asset            correlation   optimal hedge   what that means
                                with the FX   ratio h*
   a foreign government bond           0.00          1.0000   hedge in full
   a foreign corporate bond            0.10          1.0600   OVER-hedge
   foreign developed equity           -0.20          0.7600   UNDER-hedge
   foreign emerging equity             0.35          1.6067   OVER-hedge

Full hedging is optimal in exactly one row, and only because that row's correlation was set to zero.

The result worth carrying is that a 100 per cent hedge is optimal only by coincidence. Where the asset and the currency move together, cancelling the currency exposure exactly leaves the combined position under-cancelled, and the variance-minimising answer is to sell more currency forward than the portfolio holds. Emerging equity here wants 1.6067, which is a short currency position of 60.67 per cent of the holding's value on top of the hedge. Most mandates forbid that outright, so the optimum sits outside the permitted set for two of the four asset types.

Price what the constraint costs before concluding it is a problem.

Volatility at the optimum, at full hedging, and unhedged

   the foreign asset            vol at h*   vol at h=1   vol at h=0   cost of being
                                                                      capped at h=1
   a foreign government bond      6.0000%      6.0000%     16.1555%        0.0000 pp
   a foreign corporate bond       8.9549%      9.0000%     18.2483%        0.0451 pp
   foreign developed equity      17.6363%     18.0000%     21.0000%        0.3637 pp
   foreign emerging equity      24.3555%      26.0000%     34.2637%        1.6445 pp

The cap costs emerging equity 1.6445 points. Hedging at all is worth 8.2637 to the same portfolio.

The cap costs the uncorrelated bond nothing, because its optimum is already one, and costs emerging equity 1.6445 percentage points of volatility, the largest of the four. Set that against what hedging is worth in the first place: full hedging takes emerging equity from 34.2637 to 26.0000 per cent, a saving of 8.2637 points. The constraint forfeits a fifth of what the hedge is worth, a ratio of 5.02. That is the honest defence of the common policy: full hedging is never optimal and is nearly always close enough, and the alternative requires a mandate change to permit short currency positions that most trustees would not grant and should probably not grant.

4.4 "Not useful for unknown future income", quantified

The last of the summary's five disadvantages is that a forward is not useful for unknown future income. That applies to every equity portfolio, because you cannot know a year ahead what the holding will be worth, and the forward must be struck today for a fixed amount. Hedge the starting value of 100, the only figure available at inception, and read the residual.

What is left when the asset does not end where it started

   the foreign asset   the hedge covers   what is left   the residual exposure
   ends the year at    (sold forward)     unhedged       as a % of the position
                 60.00             100.00         -40.00                   66.67%
                 80.00             100.00         -20.00                   25.00%
                100.00             100.00           0.00                    0.00%
                120.00             100.00          20.00                   16.67%
                150.00             100.00          50.00                   33.33%

The hedge is exact at exactly one outcome. A 40 per cent fall leaves a short currency position of 66.67 per cent of what remains.

The hedge is exact at one outcome out of infinitely many, and the two directions of error are not equivalent. An under-hedge, when the asset rises, leaves currency exposure the fund had already accepted by holding the asset. An over-hedge, when the asset falls, creates a short position in a currency the fund does not own, which nobody decided to take and which most mandates prohibit. A falling market is therefore the dangerous direction for a currency overlay, and it is the direction in which everything else is also going wrong.

Rebalancing the hedge shrinks the residual, and the rate at which it does so is worth comparing with lecture 8.

   rebalances a year   the asset's move between rebalances   as a % of the
                       at one standard deviation             annual residual
                     1                              18.0000%          100.00%
                     2                              12.7279%           70.71%

Quarterly rebalancing halves the residual. Daily rebalancing cuts it to 6.32 per cent of the annual figure.

The residual falls as the square root of the frequency, so rebalancing genuinely helps. That is the opposite of lecture 8's delta-hedging result, where the total error was identical at one rebalance a year and at 250. The difference is structural rather than a matter of parameters: a currency hedge's error is linear in the asset's move, so halving the move halves the error, while a delta hedge's error is quadratic, so quartering the error per rebalance and quadrupling the count leaves the total unchanged. Two hedging problems that look alike behave in opposite ways, and the exponent is why.

4.5 The hedge settles in cash and the asset does not

The failure mode of currency overlays in practice is not the arithmetic above. A forward settles in cash on a date, and the hedged asset settles when you sell it, which may be never. So a currency move that leaves the fund's total value untouched still generates a demand for cash. Regulation 28 permits 45 per cent offshore, so size the demand at that limit and below it.

Cash needed to settle a currency hedge after an adverse move

               15%              10%                            1.50%
               15%              20%                            3.00%
               15%              30%                            4.50%
               30%              10%                            3.00%
               30%              20%                            6.00%
               30%              30%                            9.00%
               45%              10%                            4.50%
               45%              20%                            9.00%
               45%              30%                           13.50%

At the Regulation 28 limit and a 30 per cent move, 13.50 per cent of the fund is due in cash against a position that has not lost anything.

A fund at the 45 per cent limit facing a 30 per cent rand move owes 13.50 per cent of its total value. The offshore assets have gained the same amount in rand terms, so the fund has not lost a cent, and it still has to settle. Worse, the assets that gained are the offshore ones, which cannot be sold, repatriated and settled inside a margin cycle, while the obligation is due in domestic cash. A fully hedged fund is less liquid than an unhedged one, and the gap widens exactly when the currency moves most.

That is the 2022 gilt episode in miniature: a hedge performing as designed, producing a cash call larger than the liquid buffer, against a gain sitting in assets that could not be realised in time. The requirement here is linear in both the offshore weight and the size of the move, so there is no threshold at which the problem announces itself. It scales smoothly, which is precisely why it is easy to under-provision for.

Currency hedging has almost no disadvantages of its own. It inherits them from whichever instrument implements it, which means the choice between a forward, a swap and an option for a currency hedge is decided by exactly the arguments in lectures 7 to 9 with no currency-specific adjustment. The two genuinely currency-specific facts are covered interest parity, which fixes the hedged return, and the exposure being embedded in an asset of uncertain value rather than held separately, which is what makes the hedge amount unknowable in advance. Everything else is borrowed.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Three things distinguish a practitioner's handling of currency from a textbook one, and the first is a question of sequence rather than technique.

The decision to hold the asset and the decision to hold the currency are separate, and confusing them is the most common error in the field. A fund buys foreign equity for diversification and access to industries its own market lacks. Those reasons say nothing whatever about whether it wants dollar exposure, which arrived attached. Ask any trustee why the fund is 40 per cent offshore and you will get a good answer; ask why it is 40 per cent long dollars and you will usually get the same answer repeated, which is not an answer. The two decisions have different rationales, different time horizons and different people who should be making them. Section 4.1 exists to make the separation impossible to avoid: once you know the hedged return is the domestic rate exactly, the currency position is visibly a distinct bet.

Hedging is not a risk-free act, and the risk it introduces is a different kind from the one it removes. It converts a market exposure, which fluctuates and requires nothing, into an operational obligation, which requires cash on dates you do not choose. A fund that is unhedged loses money when the rand strengthens and does nothing about it. A fund that is hedged loses nothing and must wire 13.50 per cent of its value. Those are not the same risk measured differently: one can be absorbed by doing nothing and the other cannot. That asymmetry does not appear in any volatility calculation, including every one in this lecture, which is why the volatility comparison in section 4.3 is a necessary and badly incomplete way to make the decision.

The variance-minimising hedge ratio answers a question few funds are actually asking. Section 4.3 minimises the volatility of the asset's domestic-currency return, which is the right objective only if the fund's obligations are fixed in domestic currency and its horizon is one period. A fund with inflation-linked liabilities in an economy whose currency depreciates when domestic inflation rises may find that foreign currency exposure hedges the liability rather than adding risk to the asset. The correct objective is the volatility of the surplus, not of the assets, and against that objective the optimal currency hedge is frequently much lower than section 4.3 suggests. That is the same lesson as lecture 4's liability-driven investment applied to a different exposure, and it is the reason the "hedge everything" default is not obviously right even where it is permitted.

On the summary itself, the correction I would make is structural rather than factual. It states that forex forwards are priced by covered interest parity and separately lists the disadvantages of currency hedging, without connecting them. The connection is the entire subject: because parity holds, the hedged return is fixed at the domestic rate, so every disadvantage in that list is a cost incurred to obtain a known outcome rather than a cost of pursuing a return. Presenting the two facts in adjacent sections without joining them lets a candidate learn both and understand neither.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The forward contract and its pricing are built in forwards and futures, and the swap route to the same exposure is in using swaps, whose currency-matching case is the summary's other treatment of this material. The hedge ratio arithmetic generalises the cross-hedge in hedging with forwards and futures, and the contrast in section 4.4 is with the delta-hedging result in using options. The yield illusion the parity relation dissolves is the same one priced in bond policy switching and yield pick-up, where the break-even reinvestment rate turned out to be the implied forward rate. Foreign assets as a diversifier rest on the correlation structure in portfolio risk and return.

Where this goes next. Lecture 11 covers transition management, where a currency overlay is one of the transitions and the cash-settlement problem of section 4.5 is the constraint that decides how a transition is sequenced. Lectures 12 and 13 on behavioural finance bear on section 4.1 directly: the failure of uncovered interest parity is an empirical regularity that persists despite being widely known, which is exactly the kind of anomaly those lectures ask whether to believe in. Where the bank sits opposite. The bank quotes the forward and holds the other side of every hedge in this lecture, and covered interest parity is not an abstraction to it but a no-arbitrage constraint on its own funding book: it can manufacture the forward by borrowing in one currency and lending in the other, which is why the relation holds. That funding activity is the cross-currency component of the interest-rate and funding risk set out in interest rate risk in the banking book. The margin calls of section 4.5 arrive at the bank as collateral inflows from clients and simultaneous outflows to its own hedging counterparties, which is the mechanism by which a currency shock becomes a system-wide funding event, treated in liquidity risk. And the counterparty exposure the fund runs against the bank is the same exposure measured from the bank's side in counterparty credit risk.

Numbers used here. The currency hedging example, its five disadvantages, the covered interest parity note and the optimal hedge ratio formula are transcribed from the ASSA F105 summary in the user's notes, which has no standalone currency section and spreads this material across three places. The Regulation 28 limits are external and verifiable: the aggregate foreign-asset limit was raised from 30 per cent, plus a separate 10 per cent Africa allowance, to a single 45 per cent in February 2022, with 75 per cent in equities local and foreign combined, published by National Treasury on 5 July 2022. Covered interest parity and the variance-minimising hedge ratio are standard results derived rather than asserted. One structural correction is recorded against the summary: it states the parity relation and lists the hedging disadvantages in separate sections without connecting them, and the connection is the subject, since parity fixes the hedged return at the domestic rate and therefore makes every listed disadvantage the price of a known outcome rather than the cost of pursuing a return. Everything numerical is invented for illustration: a 9 per cent domestic yield against foreign yields of minus 0.50 to 25 per cent, a spot rate of 18, a 15 per cent currency volatility, local volatilities from 3 to 30 per cent, currency correlations from minus 0.20 to 0.35, currency moves from minus 20 to plus 20 per cent, asset outcomes from 60 to 150 on a base of 100, rebalancing frequencies from one to 250 a year, and offshore weights of 15, 30 and 45 per cent. Twenty-four claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions, all twenty-four passed, and none can pass without the computation. Two failed on the first run, both counterexamples where I had guessed the direction: emerging equity's positive currency correlation does not lift its currency share above developed equity's, so the finding was restated as the volatility ratio deciding the ordering with the correlation only adjusting it; and the cap on over-hedging costs a fifth of what hedging is worth rather than the tenth I assumed, a ratio of 5.02. Every headline finding states the region in which it holds: parity fixes the hedged return exactly and constrains the unhedged one not at all, the currency's variance share falls monotonically with local volatility and crosses one half where the volatilities are equal, the optimal hedge ratio departs from one in the direction of the correlation's sign, and the cash requirement is linear in both the offshore weight and the size of the move.
