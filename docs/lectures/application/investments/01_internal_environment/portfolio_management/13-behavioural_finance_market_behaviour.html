"Enough investors are rational" is not the criterion, and no number of them removes a correlated bias

A hundred thousand investors with independent errors price a security to within 0.0632 per cent. The same hundred thousand, correlated at just 0.10, are still 6.3248 per cent wrong, and the limit is exactly the individual error times the square root of the correlation. So the question was never how many investors are rational: it is whether the errors move together, which is why every topic in the summary's market section is a correlating mechanism. Along the way: an arbitrageur who is right about the mispricing on every path is forced out on 82.25 per cent of them at eight times leverage, and the summary's four mood anomalies are exactly the number pure chance yields from eighty candidate variables.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Lecture 12 established that individuals are biased. That is not yet a statement about markets, and the summary's own efficient market hypothesis makes the distinction precisely: it requires that enough investors are rational, not all of them. This lecture computes what "enough" would have to mean, and the answer reorganises the whole subject.

Take investors who each misvalue a security by 20 per cent on average and let the market price be their average. With independent errors, a hundred thousand investors price it to within 0.0632 per cent, falling by exactly the square root of the number of them. Correlate those same errors at just 0.10 and the aggregate error settles at 6.3248 per cent and stays there however many investors arrive, because the limit is the individual error times the square root of the correlation. So "enough investors are rational" is the wrong criterion. No number of rational investors removes a correlated bias and almost any number removes an independent one.

That reframing explains the summary's own structure. Sentiment, herding, noise traders and mood anomalies are all in the market section rather than the individual one, and what they have in common is that each is a mechanism for correlating errors: everyone reads the same news, copies the same managers, reacts to the same headline, experiences the same weather. An individual's miscalculation correlates with nothing and washes out. The summary organises the material correctly and never says why.

The remaining gap is why a rational arbitrageur does not simply correct the mispricing. Simulating a short position that is right on every path shows it forced out on 82.25 per cent of them at eight times leverage against 21.12 per cent unleveraged, a factor of 4.44 on an identical view. What limits arbitrage is the funding structure, not the analysis, which is Shleifer and Vishny's point and the reason mispricings requiring patient capital are the ones that persist.

On the details the summary is mostly right and incomplete in three specific places. It lists five of Baker and Wurgler's six sentiment proxies and omits the first-day return on IPOs, which is the only demand measure among them. It defines cross-sectional momentum as "a poor performing stock will continue to perform poorly", which is the smaller leg at 42.86 per cent of the spread and the one a long-only fund cannot implement. And it states overreaction and underreaction as parallel bullets when they require different horizons: continuation at three to twelve months, reversal at three to five years, and reversal again at one month, which it does not mention at all.

Then the finding that should change how the mood anomalies are read. Testing eighty candidate variables at a 5 per cent significance level makes four false positives more likely than not, and the summary lists exactly four mood anomalies. That is not a claim that they are false. It is a claim that their number carries no evidence, because the same number arises from pure noise. It is the identical arithmetic to the performance measurement finding that the best of a hundred skill-free managers shows an apparent information ratio above one.

Step 2

Why this matters

This lecture decides whether anything in lecture 12 has investment consequences. A biased individual investor is a fact about psychology. A biased market is a fact about prices, and only the second creates an opportunity or a risk for a fund. The aggregation arithmetic in section 4.1 is therefore the hinge of the entire behavioural finance topic, and it gives a sharp test: a bias matters to a portfolio manager if and only if there is a mechanism correlating it across investors. Applying that test dismisses most of the bias catalogue as irrelevant to prices and promotes four specific mechanisms to central importance.

It also matters because the anomalies in this section are the empirical basis for a large part of the asset management industry. Momentum, value and size are the factors that underpin most quantitative equity strategies and every smart beta product, and the summary lists all three under behavioural headings. Whether they are behavioural in origin, compensation for risk, or artefacts of data mining is the live question, and section 4.6's multiple testing arithmetic is the tool for answering it. A fund allocating to a factor because a paper found it significant needs to know how many factors were tested.

The limits to arbitrage result has a direct governance consequence for institutional investors. If mispricings persist because arbitrageurs cannot hold positions long enough, then a fund with genuinely long-dated liabilities and stable funding holds a structural advantage over a hedge fund: it can be the patient capital. That advantage is real, rare, and destroyed by any governance arrangement that forces the fund to react to short-term performance, which is the same conclusion lectures 4 and 12 reached from statistical significance and myopic loss aversion respectively. Three separate arguments converge on the same recommendation.

Finally, the herding material is the one part of this subject that describes the reader's own industry rather than an anonymous market. Fund managers copying each other because career risk is relative is not a psychological failing; it is a rational response to being measured against a peer group, and it means the benchmark a fund chooses partly determines how much herding it will experience. That connects behavioural finance to mandate design, which is a mundane and controllable thing.

Step 3

The mental model

Averaging destroys what is independent and preserves what is shared. Every result in this lecture is that one sentence applied to a different quantity.

Ask a thousand people to guess the weight of an ox. Some guess high, some low, for a thousand unrelated reasons. Average the guesses and you get remarkably close to the truth, because the errors point in random directions and cancel. This is the famous result and it is the whole case for market efficiency.

Now show the same thousand people a photograph taken from an angle that makes the ox look thin. Every single one of them guesses low. Average the guesses and you are still wrong, by exactly as much as one person is wrong, because averaging cannot remove an error everybody shares.

That is the difference between the two halves of behavioural finance. Lecture 12's biases are mostly the first kind: your particular anchor, your particular mental accounts, your particular overconfidence. They cancel. The four topics in this lecture are the second kind, and they cancel not at all.

And the fraction of the error that survives has a formula. If each person is 20 per cent wrong and their errors are correlated at one in ten, then no matter how many people you ask, the average stays about 6 per cent wrong. That figure is 20 per cent times the square root of one tenth. The number of people has vanished from the answer entirely.

Now the second question, which is the one that took the profession twenty years to take seriously. Suppose you spot the thin-looking ox and know it is fat. Why do you not simply bet on it and get rich? Because you have to hold the bet until everyone else notices, and in the meantime the photograph gets published more widely and the price moves further against you. If you borrowed to place the bet, you are sold out before you are proved right. Being correct and being solvent are different achievements, and only the second pays.

Formally, if n investors each have a valuation error with standard deviation s and pairwise correlation rho, the standard deviation of their average is s x sqrt((1 + (n-1) x rho) / n). As n grows this tends to s x sqrt(rho), which is zero only when rho is zero. The same expression governs the volatility of aggregate demand in section 4.7, which is why herding and mispricing have identical arithmetic.

   herding                 investors copy each other        yes, by construction
   noise traders           all react to the same headline   yes

   miscalculation

This is why the summary has two behavioural sections rather than one long list. The dividing line is whether the error is shared.

4.1 Whether a biased individual makes a biased market

The summary's efficient market statement is that enough investors are rational and market anomalies disappear. Test what "enough" has to mean by letting each investor misvalue a security with a 20 per cent standard deviation and taking the market price as their average.

Aggregate mispricing at three correlations

   investors   error if the biases   error if they are   error if they are
             are independent       correlated at 0.10   correlated at 0.50
           1               20.0000%              20.0000%              20.0000%
          10                6.3246%               8.7178%              14.8324%
         100                2.0000%               6.6030%              14.2127%
        1000                0.6325%               6.3530%              14.1492%
      100000                0.0632%               6.3248%              14.1422%

The independent column falls by exactly the square root of the count. The correlated columns stop falling after about a hundred investors.

Independent biases fall by exactly the square root of the number of investors, from 20.0000 per cent to 0.0632 across a factor of a hundred thousand. Correlated biases do not: at a correlation of 0.10 the error settles at 6.3248 per cent, and the limit is exactly the individual error times the square root of the correlation, verified as an identity. The entire question is the correlation and not the number of investors.

So the summary's criterion needs restating. "Enough investors are rational" implies that a sufficiently large market is efficient, and the arithmetic says otherwise: no number of rational investors removes a correlated bias, and almost any number removes an independent one. The examinable position is the summary's, since it is the standard formulation and the mechanism it has in mind is arbitrage by the rational minority, which section 4.2 addresses. But a complete answer names correlation rather than headcount as the condition.

How quickly the correlated error reaches its floor

   correlation between   limiting error   investors needed before the error is
   investors' errors     as N grows       within a tenth of that limit
                  0.0001           0.2000%                                    65536
                  0.0010           0.6325%                                     8192
                  0.0100           2.0000%                                      512
                  0.1000           6.3246%                                       64

A correlation of one ten-thousandth still leaves a floor of 0.2000 per cent, reached by about sixty-five thousand investors.

The practical point is how low the threshold is. A correlation of one ten-thousandth between investors' errors still leaves a permanent floor, and at that level the correlation is undetectable in any individual investor's behaviour. Mispricing survives at correlations far too small to notice, which is why the empirical question is never "are investors biased" but "is there any shared influence on their judgement". There always is.

4.2 Limits to arbitrage, or why being right is not enough

Section 4.1 leaves the obvious objection. If a correlated bias makes a security 20 per cent too expensive, why does a rational arbitrageur not short it and collect? Shleifer and Vishny answered in 1997: because the mispricing can widen before it converges, and the capital is withdrawn at the worst moment. Simulate a short position that is right about the mispricing on every single path, and count how often it survives to be proved so.

A correct arbitrageur's survival, by leverage

   the arbitrageur's   loss tolerated before   chance of surviving   chance of being
   leverage            the capital is pulled   to convergence        forced out
                   1.0                   50.00%                 78.88%            21.12%
                   2.0                   50.00%                 52.40%            47.60%
                   4.0                   50.00%                 30.08%            69.92%
                   8.0                   50.00%                 17.75%            82.25%

The view is correct on all 6,000 paths. At eight times leverage it survives on 17.75 per cent of them.

At eight times leverage the arbitrageur is forced out on 82.25 per cent of paths despite being right on every one of them. Unleveraged, the same view survives 78.88 per cent of the time, a factor of 4.44. So what limits arbitrage is the funding structure and not the quality of the analysis, and the mispricings that persist in practice are precisely those requiring capital to be committed for longer than any investor will commit it.

Horizon against leverage: patience substitutes for capital

   horizon in years   chance of surviving at   chance of surviving at
                      4x leverage              1x leverage
                  1.0                    54.27%                    97.50%
                  3.0                    30.53%                    79.73%
                 10.0                    17.00%                    48.73%

A longer convergence horizon lowers survival at any leverage, because there is more time for the gap to widen first.

The result runs the opposite way to intuition and is worth pausing on. A longer horizon makes survival harder, not easier, because a slower convergence gives the mispricing more time to widen against the position before it closes. Patience helps only in the sense that an unleveraged investor survives 48.73 per cent of ten-year convergences against 17.00 for a leveraged one. The advantage of long-term capital is that it can be unleveraged, not that time is on its side.

That is the structural argument for a pension fund as an arbitrageur of last resort. It has liabilities measured in decades and no margin lender, so it can hold a correct position through a widening. What it must not have is a governance process that reacts to the widening, because the effect of such a process is to convert patient capital into leveraged capital in the only sense that matters here: capital that can be withdrawn at the wrong moment.

4.3 The summary's five sentiment measures against Baker and Wurgler's six

The summary lists five quantitative sentiment measures. Baker and Wurgler's index, which is where this list originates, combines six proxies by principal components.

The six proxies, and which the summary carries

   Baker and Wurgler's proxy        in the summary?   what high sentiment does
   the closed-end fund discount     yes               narrows the discount
   share turnover                   yes               raises turnover
   the number of IPOs               yes               raises IPO activity
   FIRST-DAY RETURNS ON IPOs        NO, OMITTED       raises the first-day pop
   the equity share in new issues   yes               raises equity issuance
   the dividend premium             yes               lowers the premium, so
                                                      growth shares are in demand

Five of six. The omitted proxy is the only one that measures demand rather than supply.

The summary has five of the six and omits the first-day return on IPOs, which is worth adding because it measures something the other five do not. IPO volume, equity issuance and turnover are all supply and activity measures: they record what companies and traders did. The first-day pop is a demand measure: it records how eagerly buyers took the stock at the offer price. Since sentiment is a statement about demand, that proxy is arguably the most direct of the six.

One further note for precision. Baker and Wurgler subsequently dropped share turnover from the index, so of the summary's five, one is a proxy its own authors have since abandoned and one of the six is missing. That does not make the summary's list wrong for examination purposes, and the examinable answer is its five, but a candidate who can name the sixth and note the revision is demonstrably reading the source rather than the summary.

Why the index combines proxies rather than picking one

   proxies combined   signal-to-noise ratio   improvement over one proxy
                    1                  0.6667                        1.00x
                    2                  0.9428                        1.41x
                    3                  1.1547                        1.73x
                    6                  1.6330                        2.45x
                   12                  2.3094                        3.46x

Six proxies improve the signal-to-noise ratio by exactly the square root of six, 2.4495 times.

The reason for using principal components is section 4.1's reason running in the useful direction. Each proxy contains sentiment plus something idiosyncratic to that proxy, so averaging across proxies destroys the idiosyncratic part and keeps the shared one, improving the signal-to-noise ratio by exactly the square root of the number of proxies. Averaging kills what is independent and keeps what is shared: in section 4.1 that was the reason mispricing survives, and here it is the reason the index works. Same arithmetic, opposite sign of usefulness.

4.4 Overreaction and underreaction need different horizons

The summary says the market overreacts in the long run and underreacts in the short run, then lists cross-sectional and time-series momentum beneath that heading as parallel bullets. Those cannot all describe the same horizon, and the summary does not say what the horizons are.

The three documented horizons, and the finding at each

   horizon           the documented pattern      autocorrelation   the named finding
   one month         reversal, from bid-ask      negative          short-term reversal
                     bounce and liquidity
   three to twelve   continuation                POSITIVE          Jegadeesh and Titman
   months                                                          (1993) momentum
   three to five     reversal                    negative          De Bondt and Thaler
   years                                                           (1985) overreaction

The sign of the autocorrelation changes twice. The summary's "short run" means three to twelve months and its "long run" means three to five years.

Both of the summary's clauses are right and they describe different lags. Underreaction means a move continues, which is positive autocorrelation and shows up at three to twelve months. Overreaction means a move goes too far and comes back, which is negative autocorrelation and shows up at three to five years. At one month the sign flips back to reversal again, driven largely by bid-ask bounce and liquidity provision, and the summary does not mention that horizon at all. A candidate who says "the market overreacts" without naming a horizon has said nothing testable.

Now a check on whether one mechanism could produce both. Build a price process with a slow fundamental and a mean-reverting sentiment component at a 0.90 monthly persistence, then measure the autocorrelation of non-overlapping returns at each holding period.

A sentiment-driven process, autocorrelation by holding period

   holding period   autocorrelation of   what it implies
   in months        successive periods
                  1               -0.0461   reversal
                  6               -0.2092   reversal
                 12               -0.3062   reversal
                 48               -0.2960   reversal

Reversal at every horizon, strongest at twelve months. The sentiment half-life is 6.58 months, so the reversal peaks at roughly twice the half-life.

A decaying sentiment component produces reversal at every horizon tested, strongest at twelve months, which is close to twice its 6.58-month half-life. That is not surprising in retrospect: what went up with sentiment must come back as sentiment fades, and the strongest measured reversal occurs over the period across which most of the decay happens. Overreaction therefore needs no separate bias at all: any transient component in prices produces it mechanically.

But the same process produces no momentum at any horizon, which is the useful negative result. Underreaction cannot be generated by a mean-reverting sentiment component, so overreaction and underreaction genuinely require two different mechanisms and the summary is right to list them separately even though it does not say they operate at different lags. Momentum needs something like gradual information diffusion or slow updating of beliefs, which is a different behavioural story from sentiment.

4.5 The loser leg is the smaller half, and the one you cannot trade

The summary defines cross-sectional momentum as "a poor performing stock will continue to perform poorly". Momentum is a long-short finding and the two legs behave differently, which matters for whether a fund can use it.

The loser leg the summary names contributes 42.86 per cent of the spread and the winner leg it omits contributes 57.14. So the summary names the smaller half, and worse, it names the half a long-only fund cannot implement. Shorting losers requires a short position; buying winners does not. For the great majority of institutional mandates the omitted leg is the only actionable one, which inverts the practical significance of the omission.

The region in which momentum is attractive is limited by its tail. A 3 per cent chance of a 25 per cent monthly loss takes the mean from 0.9700 to 0.2200 per cent, removing 77.32 per cent of it, and the 2009 momentum crash is the documented instance. The consequence is that momentum's Sharpe ratio overstates its attractiveness badly, because the risk sits in a tail that a standard deviation does not describe. That is the same objection lecture 7 of the performance measurement module raised against Sharpe ratios generally, arriving here with a specific and well-known casualty.

4.6 Mood anomalies and the multiple testing problem

The summary lists four mood-based anomalies: the calendar effect, the sunshine effect, sports results and aviation disasters. It presents them as findings. The question that decides how much weight they deserve is how many candidate variables were tested, because at a 5 per cent significance level one in twenty will look significant by chance.

False positives from testing candidate variables at 5 per cent

   candidate mood   expected number   chance of at least   chance of at least
   variables tested significant at 5%  one false positive   four false positives
                  4               0.20                 18.55%                  0.00%
                 20               1.00                 64.15%                  1.59%
                100               5.00                 99.41%                 74.22%
               1000              50.00                100.00%                100.00%

Eighty candidate variables make four false positives more likely than not. A hundred make at least one a near certainty at 99.41 per cent.

The summary's four anomalies are exactly the number chance yields from about eighty candidate variables, and from a hundred the probability of finding at least four is 74.22 per cent. That is not a claim that they are false. It is a claim that their number carries no evidence, because the identical number arises from pure noise. Weather, day of the week, month, lunar phase, sporting results, temperature, cloud cover, daylight hours and pollution are all cheap to test against returns, and the literature has tested many more than eighty things.

This is the same arithmetic as the performance measurement result that the best of a hundred skill-free managers shows an apparent information ratio above one. A maximum over many trials is not a measurement, whether the trials are managers or variables, and the correction is the same in both cases: adjust for the number of trials, or test out of sample.

   the anomaly            was a mechanism   is it testable out of   how much weight
                          specified first?  sample?                 does it deserve
   the January effect     no                yes, and it weakened    little
   the sunshine effect    yes, via mood     yes, across cities      some
   sports results         yes, via mood     yes, across countries   some
   aviation disasters     no                barely: too few events  almost none

The two with a pre-specified mechanism and an out-of-sample test survive the critique. The other two do not.

The region in which a mood anomaly deserves belief is where a mechanism was specified in advance and an out-of-sample test is possible. Sunshine and sports results qualify on both counts: mood is a stated mechanism and both can be tested across cities and countries not used to find the effect. The January effect had no mechanism specified first and weakened after publication, which is the signature of a data-mined result. The aviation disaster anomaly is the weakest of the four, because the events are too rare to support any test: a handful of observations cannot distinguish a real effect from a coincidence, whatever significance level is reported.

4.7 Herding raises volatility, and by how much

The summary states that herding increases volatility and stops. It is section 4.1's arithmetic applied to demand rather than to valuation errors, and it gives the same square-root-of-correlation answer.

The volatility of aggregate demand from a thousand investors

   correlation between   volatility of aggregate   as a multiple of the
   investors' demands    demand                    independent case
                    0.00                    0.6325%                   1.00x
                    0.05                    4.5144%                   7.14x
                    0.20                    8.9621%                  14.17x
                    0.50                   14.1492%                  22.37x
                    1.00                   20.0000%                  31.62x

At perfect correlation the aggregate volatility equals a single investor's own, because a thousand identical investors are one investor.

At perfect correlation aggregate volatility equals one investor's own, 20.0000 per cent, because a thousand investors acting identically are one investor. At zero correlation it is 0.6325 per cent, a factor of 31.62 lower, which is the square root of a thousand. So herding can multiply market volatility by up to the square root of the number of participants, and the summary's claim is not only right but understated: the effect is as large as the market is big.

The summary's causal chain for herding, made mechanical

   the summary's link                     what it means mechanically
   regret aversion                       being wrong alone is worse than being
                                         wrong with everybody
   thinking others are better informed   treating others' actions as data, which
                                         makes the herd self-confirming
   fund managers copy each other         career risk is relative, so tracking the
                                         peer group is rational for the manager
   fear of missing out                   an asymmetric penalty for absence
   the desire to conform                 a social cost independent of the money
   noise traders react to headlines      a shared trigger, which is the
                                         correlation of section 4.1

The summary's chain is correct and specific. The third link is the one with an institutional remedy.

The second link deserves attention because it is self-confirming rather than merely mistaken. Treating other investors' actions as evidence is rational if they might be informed, and it means the first few movers create the data that justifies the next few. That is an information cascade, and it can produce a large price move on almost no information, which is a stronger result than "people copy each other".

The third link is the one with a remedy. If a manager is judged against a peer group, then holding what the peer group holds minimises risk for the manager and not for the client. That is not a bias at all: it is a rational response to a badly chosen benchmark, and it means the choice of benchmark partly determines how much herding a fund will experience. Lecture 5 of the performance measurement module objected to peer-group benchmarks on measurement grounds, that the median is beaten exactly half the time at any skill level. This is the same objection arriving from the incentive side, and it is the more serious of the two.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Three things distinguish a practitioner's use of this material, and the first is a test that dismisses most of it.

Apply the correlation test before believing any behavioural story about prices. Section 4.1 gives a single question that separates the biases that move markets from the ones that do not: is there a mechanism making this error shared? Most of lecture 12's catalogue fails that test and is therefore irrelevant to a portfolio manager, however interesting it is about human beings. What passes are the four topics in this lecture plus anything transmitted by a common information source, a common incentive structure, or a common regulatory constraint. That last one is underrated: a rule requiring every insurer to hold a particular asset class correlates their demand perfectly and by statute, which is behavioural finance without any psychology at all.

The limits to arbitrage result tells you what kind of edge is available to you specifically. A mispricing that persists does so because correcting it requires something most investors lack. Section 4.2 shows the constraint is usually the ability to hold through a widening. So the honest question for a fund is not "is the market efficient" but "what can I hold that others cannot", and the answers are unglamorous: illiquidity, complexity, long horizons, and positions that look embarrassing in a quarterly report. A fund with genuinely stable long-term funding has a real and rare advantage, and every governance arrangement that shortens its effective horizon spends that advantage without recording the cost anywhere.

Treat published anomalies as hypotheses with a known false positive rate, not as findings. Section 4.6's arithmetic applies to the whole factor literature and not only to the mood anomalies, which are simply the most obviously fragile examples. Hundreds of factors have been published and the great majority fail out of sample. The practitioner's filter is the same three questions the credibility table asks: was a mechanism specified before the data was examined, does the effect survive in samples not used to find it, and is there enough independent data to distinguish it from chance. An anomaly that passes all three deserves capital. One that passes none deserves a footnote, and the aviation disaster effect deserves less than that.

On the summary itself, the three corrections in this lecture are all of the same type: it is right and incomplete in ways that matter for the follow-up question. The efficient market criterion should name correlation rather than headcount. The cross-sectional momentum definition should name both legs, and should note that the omitted one is the implementable one. And the overreaction and underreaction findings should name their horizons, because without them the two statements appear to contradict each other and a candidate cannot defend either. None of these is a factual error. All three are the difference between reproducing a list and understanding it, which is what the final question in a well-written examination tests.

Connections

What this assumes. The individual biases that this lecture asks about in aggregate are built in behavioural finance, individual biases, and the underlying decision theory is in behavioural economics. The efficient market hypothesis and the price patterns section 4.4 measures are in technical analysis, and the empirical return properties that make the momentum crash possible are in empirical characteristics of asset prices. The factor structure behind size, value and momentum is in asset pricing models and factors, and the multiple testing arithmetic of section 4.6 is the same result as the skill-free manager finding in measuring portfolio performance. The peer-group objection section 4.7 revisits is in benchmarks and comparators, and the styles the anomalies correspond to are in active equity styles.

Where this goes next. This lecture completes the portfolio management module and with it the internal environment's analytical core. What remains in the investments track is the six overview lectures, covering the investing institution's business model, objectives and constraints, governance and trustees, mandates and the statement of investment principles, vehicles and wrappers, and structure and financial planning, and then the external environment. Section 4.7's conclusion feeds directly into the mandate lecture, since the choice between a peer-group and an index benchmark determines how much herding a manager's incentives will produce, and section 4.2's conclusion feeds into the governance lecture, since a fund's ability to hold a correct position through a drawdown is a governance property rather than an investment one.

Where the bank sits opposite. Correlated errors are what turn a bank's idiosyncratic exposures into a systemic event, and section 4.1's arithmetic is the formal statement of why diversification fails in a crisis: correlations rise, so the floor rises, and a portfolio that was safe on independent assumptions is not. That is the substance of the correlation and concentration treatment in credit concentration risk. Herding among banks has its own name and its own regulatory machinery: if every institution holds the same assets and faces the same rules, they will all need to sell on the same day, which is the mechanism behind the countercyclical buffer and the whole macroprudential apparatus, and the funding consequence is in liquidity risk. The limits to arbitrage result is also a bank's business model seen from the other side: a dealer earns the spread precisely because it can warehouse a position others cannot, and its capital requirement is the price of that capacity, set out in market risk.

Numbers used here. The sentiment measures, the cross-sectional return behaviour with its four sub-items, the herding chain with its five stated causes, and the four mood-based anomalies are all transcribed from the "market behaviour" section of the ASSA F105 summary in the user's notes. The external sources are verifiable: the six sentiment proxies are from Baker and Wurgler (2006, 2007), "Investor sentiment in the stock market", Journal of Economic Perspectives 21(2), whose index combines the closed-end fund discount, share turnover, the number of IPOs, first-day returns on IPOs, the dividend premium and the equity share in new issues by principal components; long-run reversal is De Bondt and Thaler (1985), Journal of Finance 40(3); three to twelve month continuation is Jegadeesh and Titman (1993), Journal of Finance 48(1); and the arbitrage constraint is Shleifer and Vishny (1997), "The limits of arbitrage", Journal of Finance 52(1). Three corrections are recorded against the summary, with the examinable position stated alongside each. First, its efficient market criterion of "enough investors are rational" names headcount where the binding condition is correlation: no number of rational investors removes a correlated bias and almost any number removes an independent one. Second, its definition of cross-sectional momentum names only the loser leg, which is the smaller contribution at 42.86 per cent of the spread and the one a long-only fund cannot implement. Third, its overreaction and underreaction statements are listed as parallel bullets and require different horizons, three to twelve months for continuation and three to five years for reversal, with a further reversal at one month that the summary does not mention. One omission is also recorded: the summary carries five of Baker and Wurgler's six proxies and drops the first-day IPO return, the only demand measure among them. Everything numerical is invented for illustration: individual valuation errors of 20 per cent at correlations from zero to one, investor counts from one to a hundred thousand, a 20 per cent mispricing with 30 per cent volatility converging over one to ten years at leverage from one to eight times with a 50 per cent loss tolerance, sentiment signal and noise of 1.0 and 1.5, a monthly sentiment persistence of 0.90 over 120,000 simulated months, monthly momentum legs of plus 0.80 and minus 0.60 per cent with a 3 per cent chance of a 25 per cent crash, and candidate variable counts from four to a thousand at a 5 per cent significance level. The arbitrage simulation uses 6,000 paths over 252 steps and the horizon variant 3,000 paths, both from a fixed seed so the figures reproduce. Twenty-three claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions, all twenty-three passed, and none can pass without the computation. Three failed on the first run. Two were my thresholds: independent errors fall by a factor of 316 rather than the thousand I asserted, and the unleveraged arbitrageur survives 78.88 per cent of the time rather than the 90 I guessed, so the claim was restated as a ratio against the leveraged case. The third was more interesting: I expected the sentiment process's reversal to weaken with the holding period and it strengthens to a peak at twelve months, near twice the component's 6.58-month half-life, so the section was rewritten around the peak. Four further claims were flagged as unfalsifiable by a vacuity check and rewritten or removed. Every headline finding states the region in which it holds: the aggregation floor exists at any positive correlation however small, the arbitrage constraint binds through leverage and horizon jointly rather than either alone, the reversal peak tracks the sentiment half-life, the momentum crash removes most of the mean at a 3 per cent frequency, and a mood anomaly's credibility depends on a pre-specified mechanism and an out-of-sample test rather than on its reported significance.
