Rebalancing a delta hedge more often does not reduce its expected cost by a single basis point

The total gamma error over a year is 3.813878 whether you rebalance once or 250 times, identically, because the error per rebalance falls exactly as fast as the number of rebalances rises. So the summary's "continual adjustment" buys predictability rather than cheapness, and pays for it in transaction costs that rise linearly. Along the way: the summary is right that a naked put's loss is limited, though the bound is 27.7 times the premium received, and a straddle's break-even is a nearly constant 0.797 standard deviations at every expiry, so a short-dated one is cheap and needs a proportionally just as unlikely move.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Lecture 7 hedged with forwards and futures and found the "no upside" comparison reducing entirely to an option premium. This lecture prices the premium's side of that trade, and the summary's material divides cleanly: delta hedging, transition management, four speculative structures and three income-enhancement structures. Its one instruction, "continual adjustment", turns out to mean something different from what it appears to.

A static delta hedge is exact only for a zero move, and the error is the gamma term. On a one-year at-the-money call with a delta of 0.6179 and a gamma of 0.019069, a twenty-point fall leaves an error of 4.1387 against a quadratic approximation of 3.8139. The error is positive in both directions, so a delta-hedged long option makes money from movement and a delta-hedged short one loses it.

Then the result I did not expect. Rebalancing more often quarters the error per rebalance and doubles the number of rebalances, and the total is identical at every frequency: 3.813878, whether once a year or 250 times. The variance of the move is linear in time and the error is quadratic in the move, so the two cancel exactly. "Continual adjustment" therefore buys predictability rather than cheapness, and costs transaction charges that rise linearly with the number of trades.

Delta also moves with time and volatility while the price stands still, which is the part that catches people. An in-the-money delta rises from 0.7767 to 0.9860 as expiry approaches and an out-of-the-money one falls from 0.5206 to 0.0110, with the price unchanged throughout. The at-the-money delta is the most stable, ranging 0.1376 against the out-of-the-money option's 0.5096, so the hedge instability is concentrated near the strike close to expiry.

On income enhancement the summary is right in a way that runs against intuition: a naked call's loss is unlimited and a naked put's is limited, at exactly the strike less the premium. But limited is not small: the bound here is 96.5098 against a premium of 3.4902, 27.7 times the premium, which is why the summary calls both high risk. And a covered call's "better performance in a downturn" is a cushion of exactly the premium, 4.4852, and nothing more.

Feynman test You can cancel an option's price sensitivity by holding the right amount of the underlying, but the right amount changes the moment the price moves, so the hedge is only correct instantaneously. The surprise is that trading more often does not make it cheaper: the mistakes get smaller exactly as fast as they get more numerous, so you pay the same total either way and only the reliability improves. Selling a put looks more dangerous than selling a call and is actually the bounded one, because a share can rise forever and can only fall to zero, though the bound is nearly thirty times what you were paid.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the rebalancing result inverts the usual justification for frequent hedging. A manager told that hourly rebalancing reduces hedging cost is being told something false: it reduces the variance of the cost and raises the transaction charges. The decision is therefore a trade between predictability and commission, not between accuracy and laziness.

The second is that the gamma sign decides who wants movement. A delta-hedged long option position profits from realised volatility and a short one loses, so an income-enhancement programme that sells options and delta-hedges them is short volatility whether or not anybody described it that way. That is the mechanism behind most option-selling blow-ups and it follows from one sign.

The third is that delta drifts without any price movement at all, so a hedge left alone through a quiet week is no longer a hedge. The instability is concentrated near the strike close to expiry, which is precisely where a large book accumulates positions, so the operational burden is worst exactly when the position is largest.

And the fourth is that "limited loss" is a statement about whether a position can be sized, not about whether it is safe. A bound of 27.7 times the premium is limited and is not tolerable at scale, and the summary is right to call both naked positions high risk despite the asymmetry between them.

Step 3

The mental model

A hedge exact only instantaneously, a total cost independent of frequency, a delta that drifts in still air, and three structures whose bounds are computable.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to carry is the rebalancing result, because it contradicts the standard justification for frequent hedging. The total gamma error over a year is 3.813878 whether you rebalance once or 250 times, identically, because the variance of the move is linear in time and the error is quadratic in the move. SO "CONTINUAL ADJUSTMENT" CONTROLS THE VARIANCE OF THE HEDGING COST AND NOT ITS LEVEL, and it pays for that in commissions rising linearly with the number of trades. A manager told that hourly rebalancing is cheaper has been told something false.

WHY THAT RESULT IS THE REASON AN OPTION HAS A PRICE AT ALL. the expected cost of replicating an option by delta hedging is a FIXED quantity independent of how the replication is carried out WHICH IS WHAT MAKES THE BLACK-SCHOLES NUMBER A PRICE RATHER THAN AN ESTIMATE. if the cost depended on the hedger's diligence, there would be no single arbitrage-free value.

The second is the sign of gamma, which decides who wants movement and is worth stating as a rule. The static hedge error is positive in both directions, so A DELTA-HEDGED LONG OPTION MAKES MONEY FROM MOVEMENT AND A DELTA-HEDGED SHORT ONE LOSES IT. An income-enhancement programme that sells options and delta-hedges them is therefore short volatility whether or not anyone described it that way, and that single sign is the mechanism behind most option-selling failures.

The third is that delta drifts in still air, and the drift is concentrated where the book is largest. An in-the-money delta rises from 0.7767 to 0.9860 and an out-of-the-money one falls from 0.5206 to 0.0110 with the price unchanged, while the at-the-money delta moves least at 0.1376. AND A VOLATILITY SHOCK REHEDGES THE WHOLE BOOK AT ONCE, because high volatility pulls every delta towards a half. So the day a market becomes volatile is the day every position needs trading, in a market that has just become expensive to trade in, which is an operational point rather than a theoretical one.

WHERE THE HEDGING BURDEN ACTUALLY FALLS. near the strike, close to expiry delta swings hardest: the OTM delta ranged 0.5096 at the money most stable, ranging only 0.1376 deep in or out, near expiry pinned at one or zero, nothing moves it SO THE OPERATIONAL LOAD IS A NEAR-THE-STRIKE PHENOMENON, and it coincides with a volatility shock rehedging everything simultaneously.

And the fourth, on the three income structures, where the summary is right on a point most people get backwards. A NAKED CALL'S LOSS IS UNBOUNDED AND A NAKED PUT'S STOPS AT THE STRIKE LESS THE PREMIUM, because a price can rise forever and can only fall to zero. But the bound here is 96.5098 against a premium of 3.4902, 27.7 times what was received, so limited is not small and the summary is right to call both high risk. The distinction is about whether a position can be capitalised against, not about whether it is prudent. And a covered call's downturn cushion is exactly the premium and nothing more, which helps a small fall materially and a large one hardly at all.

And the thread that runs through the whole lecture, which is the thing worth carrying into lecture 9. Every option decision here reduced to a comparison against what the premium already implies: the straddle pays only if realised volatility exceeds implied, the spread pays only if the tail was never going to happen, and choosing an option over a future is a volatility view rather than a directional one. SO AN OPTION IS NEVER A WAY TO AVOID HAVING A VIEW, IT IS A WAY TO EXPRESS A VIEW ABOUT THE SECOND MOMENT INSTEAD OF THE FIRST. Lecture 6 found the long bond to be a bet against the market's own forward rate; an option is a bet against the market's own volatility forecast, and the structure is identical.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. Options and their payoffs are built in options, and the Black-Scholes model that produces the deltas and gammas used throughout is derived in bond options and the Merton model. The futures comparison and the "no upside" break-even that opened this lecture are from hedging with forwards and futures, and the transition cost framework is the same one. The volatility that a straddle bets on is characterised in empirical characteristics of asset prices, which found returns leptokurtic and volatility heteroscedastic, both of which matter for whether implied volatility is a fair forecast. The convexity that makes the gamma term positive is the same second-order effect measured in liability hedging and dynamic benchmarks.

Where this goes next. Lecture 9 covers swaps, which solve the problem lecture 7 identified and this lecture could not: a single swap replaces a long chain of rolled forwards without the premium an option charges. Lecture 10 applies the whole toolkit to currency, where the summary's own opening example sits, and the delta-hedging arithmetic here is what makes a currency overlay an operational commitment rather than a single trade. Lecture 11 is transition management in full, where the option and futures routes priced here are two of the available paths. And lectures 12 and 13 on behavioural finance bear on this lecture more than they appear to: whether implied volatility is a fair forecast of realised volatility is an empirical question about market pricing, and every structure here is a bet on the answer.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank runs delta hedging as a continuous operational function rather than an occasional technique, and the finding that rebalancing frequency does not change the expected cost is why a trading desk's gamma is a risk limit rather than a cost budget: what is controlled is the exposure to movement, not the hedging bill, which is set out in market risk. The naked-call asymmetry is why written options attract punitive capital treatment while purchased ones do not, since an unbounded loss cannot be capitalised against, and the volatility-shock rehedging problem is the mechanism that turns a market shock into a liquidity event, treated in liquidity risk: every dealer's book needs the same trade on the same day.
