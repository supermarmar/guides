The entire gain from a swap is one number you can compute before you negotiate, and it is not divisible

Two firms swapping to reduce borrowing costs can share out exactly the quality spread differential and not one basis point more, so any arrangement that appears to save both parties more than that has mispriced something. Here the figure is 0.80 per cent, split 0.35 to each firm and 0.10 to the bank, verified to sum exactly. Along the way: a swap's counterparty exposure peaks at precisely one third of its term rather than growing to maturity, which is the opposite shape to a forward's, and the summary's "large amounts only" and "need level income" turn out to be the same constraint counted twice.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Lecture 7 hedged with forwards and found the rolling problem: a three-year hedge rolled monthly needs 36 contracts and pays 5.4000 per cent of accumulated spreads. Lecture 8 solved the direction problem with options and charged a premium for it. A swap solves the rolling problem without charging that premium, and the summary's material on it divides into three uses: hedging liabilities, transition management, and reducing borrowing costs. The third is the one with an exact answer.

The borrowing-cost case rests on comparative advantage, and the total gain is not a matter of negotiation. It is the quality spread differential, the difference between two firms' fixed-rate gap and their floating-rate gap, and nothing else. On quotes of 5.00 per cent fixed or LIBOR plus 0.30 for the stronger firm and 6.50 or LIBOR plus 1.00 for the weaker, the gaps are 1.50 and 0.70, so the differential is 0.80 per cent. Split it 0.35 to each firm with 0.10 to the arranging bank and the three shares sum to 0.80 exactly. No arrangement can distribute more, which makes any swap that seems to save both parties more than the differential a mispricing rather than a discovery.

And there is a reason to distrust the whole argument that the summary does not mention. The differential exists because the weaker firm's floating rate resets and its fixed rate does not, so its floating quote carries a repricing right the lender values. Put a 20 per cent chance on a downgrade widening that firm's floating spread by 450 basis points and the right is worth 0.90 per cent, which more than accounts for the 0.80 differential. So comparative advantage is either a market imperfection or correctly priced credit risk, and the arithmetic alone cannot tell you which.

On "longer term than forwards", the advantage is real and conditional. A single swap spread of 0.20 per cent beats quarterly forward rolls at 0.15 each once the chain exceeds 0.3333 years, and at thirty years it saves 17.8000 per cent. Below the break-even the forward is genuinely cheaper. Push the swap market to fourteen times the forward market's width and the break-even moves out to 3.5 years, at which point a three-year hedge should still roll forwards.

Then the counterparty exposure, where the shape surprised me. A swap is priced at par so its exposure starts at zero, and nothing remains at maturity so it ends at zero. It peaks at 3.3333 years on a ten-year swap, exactly one third of the term, because maximising the square root of elapsed time against the remaining life gives that answer algebraically. Raise the rate volatility and the peak's size scales in proportion while its timing does not move at all. So the collateral schedule is set by the maturity and its size by the volatility, and those are separate decisions.

Finally the two constraints the summary lists separately. A level swap against a liability amortising from 100 to 10 matches the present value exactly and mismatches every single year, underpaying by 40.9909 in year one and overpaying by 49.0091 in year ten, a worst mismatch of 83.05 per cent of the level payment. An amortising swap fixes it and is a bespoke contract, available only at a size that justifies negotiating one. "Large amounts only" and "need level income" are therefore one constraint seen twice.

Step 2

Why this matters

A swap is the instrument that makes long-horizon risk management possible at all. Every technique in lectures 4 to 8 either has a short maturity, needs rolling, or charges a premium, and a pension fund's liabilities run for fifty years. The swap market is where a fifty-year fixed-for-floating or inflation-for-fixed exposure can actually be bought, which is why liability-driven investment is a swap business rather than a bond business once the horizon exceeds the longest available government bond.

It also matters because the borrowing-cost argument is the most commonly mis-taught result in derivatives. The comparative-advantage story is presented as a free lunch created by market segmentation, two firms each borrowing where they are strong and swapping to where they want to be, both ending up better off. That framing is not wrong about the arithmetic and is badly incomplete about the economics. The gain has a hard ceiling that you can compute before opening negotiations, and the ceiling has an alternative explanation that removes the gain entirely. An examiner asking about comparative advantage is asking whether you know both halves.

The third reason is regulatory and it runs the other way. A swap is a bilateral contract, which means it carries counterparty exposure that a cleared future does not, and the post-2008 regulatory response to that exposure has changed the economics of every calculation in this lecture. Central clearing, initial margin and variation margin turn a swap's credit risk into a funding cost, so the exposure profile computed here is now the input to a margin calculation rather than a credit limit. The shape of that profile, peaking a third of the way through, is what makes a long-dated swap's margin requirement front-loaded relative to intuition.

For an actuary the swap sits at the point where asset management and liability management stop being separable. A swap transforms the nature of the asset or the liability rather than adding an asset, which means it changes the balance sheet's shape without changing its size. That is a different kind of decision from asset allocation, and it is made by different people under different governance, which is exactly why the summary's derivative reporting requirements exist.

Step 3

The mental model

A swap is an exchange of cash flow streams with no principal changing hands, which is why its value starts at zero and its exposure is a mark-to-market rather than an amount owed. Everything else follows from that.

Think of a forward as a single appointment and a swap as a standing order.

A forward is one exchange on one date. If you need protection for three years you make an appointment every quarter, twelve appointments, and each one costs you the price of getting a dealer's attention. You also do not know, when you make the first appointment, what the eleventh will cost.

A swap is a standing order. You agree once, at one price, and the exchanges happen on schedule for as long as you specified. You pay a wider spread for the one negotiation than you would for any single appointment, and you pay it once.

Now the part that explains the credit risk. Because no principal is exchanged, at the moment you sign the standing order neither side owes the other anything: the value is zero. As rates move, the stream you are receiving becomes worth more or less than the stream you are paying, and the difference is what the counterparty owes you. Early in the contract rates have not moved much, so the difference is small. Late in the contract there are few exchanges left, so even a large rate move affects a small number of payments. The exposure is therefore biggest in the middle, and the middle turns out to be exactly one third of the way through.

The last piece is why anyone thinks a swap saves money. Two borrowers face different premiums for their credit, and the premium is not the same size in the fixed market as in the floating market. That difference in the differences is the only thing available to share. It is a fixed pot, and you can measure it before you sit down.

Formally, the three uses the summary lists all reduce to the same operation applied to a different leg. Hedging liabilities transforms the nature of the asset or the liability so the two match: floating for fixed when short-term liabilities reprice and long-term assets do not, one currency for another when liabilities are in rand and assets in dollars, inflation for fixed when payments are fixed and rental income is index-linked. Transition management transforms the asset class itself through an equity swap, gaining exposure without buying the underlying. Borrowing-cost reduction transforms the liability's rate basis in order to capture the differential.

   use                    what is exchanged           the identity it relies on
   hedging liabilities    the asset's cash flow        a stream's present value equals
                          basis for the liability's    the liability's, leg by leg
   transition management  cash return for an index     an equity swap's return equals
                          return                       the index's, less the fee
   borrowing cost         fixed for floating on the    the gain equals the quality

The third identity is the one this lecture proves and the one the summary states without bounding.

4.2 "Longer term than forwards" is a horizon condition, not a property

The summary's first advantage compares a swap with a forward on term. Lecture 7 priced what rolling costs, so the comparison can be made numerically: charge 0.15 per cent for each quarterly forward roll and 0.20 per cent once for the swap, both invented, and read off which wins at each horizon.

A swap's single spread against a chain of forward rolls

   hedge horizon   forwards rolled   forward spread   one swap's   the swap's
                   quarterly         cost total       spread       advantage
            0.25y                   1           0.1500%       0.2000%      -0.0500%
            0.50y                   2           0.3000%       0.2000%       0.1000%
            1.00y                   4           0.6000%       0.2000%       0.4000%
            3.00y                  12           1.8000%       0.2000%       1.6000%
            5.00y                  20           3.0000%       0.2000%       2.8000%
           10.00y                  40           6.0000%       0.2000%       5.8000%
           30.00y                 120          18.0000%       0.2000%      17.8000%

The advantage rises monotonically with the horizon and is negative at three months. The break-even is 0.3333 years.

The rolling cost is linear in the horizon and the swap's is flat, so there is a single crossing and it is at the ratio of the two spreads divided by four. At three months a single forward costs 0.1500 against the swap's 0.2000 and the forward wins by five basis points. At thirty years the swap saves 17.8000 per cent. So the summary's advantage is real and it is conditional on the horizon exceeding four months at these spreads, which means a short-dated hedge should still use forwards and the summary's flat statement needs that qualification.

The region in which the finding holds is set by the ratio of the two spreads, and it moves the break-even a long way.

How the break-even moves with the swap market's relative width

   swap spread as a multiple   break-even horizon   which instrument wins
   of the forward spread       in years             at three years
                         0.50               0.1250   the swap
                         1.00               0.2500   the swap
                         1.33               0.3325   the swap
                         4.00               1.0000   the swap
                        14.00               3.5000   the forwards

A swap market four times as wide as the forward market still wins at three years. It takes fourteen times the width to lose.

That is the useful form of the result. A three-year hedge favours the swap unless the swap market is more than about fourteen times as wide as the forward market, which in a liquid currency it is not. In an illiquid one it might be, and that is precisely the case in which the summary's advantage inverts.

4.3 Comparative advantage: the gain is the quality spread differential, exactly

The summary attributes borrowing-cost reduction to comparative advantage arising because credit ratings differ between the long and short term, or between currencies. The result worth knowing is that the gain has a closed form. Define the quality spread differential as the gap between the two firms' fixed-rate quotes less the gap between their floating-rate quotes. That number is the entire gain available, and it is computable from four quoted rates before any negotiation begins.

The differential across four sets of quotes

   the two firms' quoted rates                                    fixed gap   float gap   QSD
   A: 5.00 fixed / L+0.30 float ; B: 6.50 fixed / L+1.00 float       1.50%        0.70%    0.80%
   A: 5.00 / L+0.30 ; B: 6.00 / L+1.00                               1.00%        0.70%    0.30%
   A: 5.00 / L+0.30 ; B: 5.70 / L+1.00                               0.70%        0.70%    0.00%
   A: 5.00 / L+0.30 ; B: 5.30 / L+1.00                               0.30%        0.70%   -0.40%

Firm A is stronger in both markets in every case. What varies is by how much more it is stronger in one. Only a positive differential supports a swap.

The third row is the boundary case and it is instructive: firm A is 0.70 per cent cheaper in both markets, so there is no comparative advantage at all despite an absolute advantage in each. The fourth row has A relatively stronger in floating, which means the swap runs the other way or not at all. A swap is worth doing only when the differential is positive, and the sign is determined entirely by which market the relative advantage sits in.

Now take the first case and distribute the 0.80 per cent. Firm A wants floating and firm B wants fixed, so A borrows fixed, B borrows floating, and they swap through a bank taking 0.10 per cent.

Sharing out a 0.80 per cent differential three ways

   party        borrows        wants          without a swap   with the swap   saving
   firm A       fixed 5.00%    floating                L+0.30%         L-0.05%     0.35%
   firm B       floating       fixed                     6.50%           6.15%     0.35%
   the bank     -              a fee                         -               -     0.10%

   total shared out: 0.35% + 0.35% + 0.10% = 0.80%, which is the QSD exactly

Verified to sum exactly. Firm A borrows below LIBOR, which is the visible sign that something has been transferred rather than created.

The three shares sum to the differential to machine precision, which is the point. No arrangement can distribute more than the differential, so a proposal that appears to save both firms more than it has either mispriced a leg or has quietly left a risk with one of them. The usual candidate for the second is the credit risk the floating borrower retains, and that leads to the objection the summary does not raise.

Here is the objection, and it is serious enough that it should change how you present the result. The differential exists because firm B's floating rate resets periodically and its fixed rate does not. A floating lender can therefore reprice if B deteriorates, and a fixed lender cannot, so the fixed lender charges more for the same credit. That extra charge is not an imperfection: it is the price of the repricing right the floating lender holds and the fixed lender does not.

Whether the differential is a gain or a payment for retained credit risk

   B's chance of   the floating lender's   the apparent QSD   is the QSD a real
   downgrade       repricing right worth   gain               gain?
             0.00%                    0.00%               0.80%   yes, in full
             5.00%                    0.22%               0.80%   partly
            10.00%                    0.45%               0.80%   partly
            20.00%                    0.90%               0.80%   NO, it is fully explained

A downgrade widening B's floating spread by 450 basis points, at a 20 per cent chance, is worth 0.90 per cent and accounts for the whole differential.

So the differential is either a market imperfection to be harvested or the correct price of a risk that does not disappear when the swap is signed, and the arithmetic cannot distinguish between them. The summary presents only the first reading. The examinable position is the summary's, that comparative advantage reduces borrowing costs through differing credit ratings across terms and currencies, and the complete answer says so and then names the alternative explanation. Firm B still faces its floating lender's repricing after the swap, and if that repricing happens B's saving evaporates while its fixed obligation under the swap does not.

4.4 "Large amounts only" and "need level income" are the same constraint

The summary lists two constraints on using swaps for liability matching. A standard swap has a constant notional and fixed payment dates, so price what happens when the liability does not. Take a liability amortising linearly from 100 to 10 over ten years, discount at 5 per cent, and match it with the level swap payment of equal present value.

A level swap against an amortising liability

   year   the liability   a level swap   mismatch   cumulative mismatch
      1          100.00        59.0091   -40.9909              -40.9909
      2           90.00        59.0091   -30.9909              -71.9817
      3           80.00        59.0091   -20.9909              -92.9726
      4           70.00        59.0091   -10.9909             -103.9634
      5           60.00        59.0091    -0.9909             -104.9543
      6           50.00        59.0091     9.0091              -95.9451
      7           40.00        59.0091    19.0091              -76.9360
      8           30.00        59.0091    29.0091              -47.9268
      9           20.00        59.0091    39.0091               -8.9177
     10           10.00        59.0091    49.0091               40.0915

The present values match exactly, at 455.6530, and every single year mismatches. The cumulative shortfall peaks at 104.9543 in year five.

The level swap matches on a present-value basis and mismatches in every year of the ten. It underpays by 40.9909 in year one and overpays by 49.0091 in year ten, a worst single-year gap of 83.05 per cent of the level payment. The fund must borrow early and hold cash late, which reintroduces exactly the reinvestment and funding risk the match was supposed to remove. A level liability, by contrast, is matched to the fourth decimal place, which identifies the amortisation rather than the swap as the source of the problem.

The fix is an amortising swap with a schedule matching the liability, and an amortising swap is a bespoke contract. A dealer will negotiate a bespoke schedule at a size that justifies the effort and will not at a small one. So the two constraints the summary lists separately are one constraint seen twice: level income is required only because a bespoke schedule is unavailable, and a bespoke schedule is unavailable only below a certain size. Saying so is a better answer than reciting both.

4.5 The counterparty exposure peaks at a third of the term

The summary lists a credit risk increase and lower liquidity as the costs of the equity-swap route to transition management. The credit risk deserves quantification, because its shape is not what "increase" suggests. Model the exposure as the rate move at 95 per cent confidence over the elapsed time, applied to the swap's remaining life, on a ten-year swap with a 1 per cent annual rate volatility.

Potential future exposure over a ten-year swap's life

   years elapsed   remaining life   rate move at 95%   potential future exposure
   on a 10y swap                    confidence         as a % of notional
              0.0             10.0             0.0000%                     0.0000%
              1.0              9.0             1.6450%                    14.1000%
              2.5              7.5             2.6010%                    18.5784%
              5.0              5.0             3.6783%                    17.5159%
              7.5              2.5             4.5050%                    10.7262%
             10.0              0.0             5.2019%                     0.0000%

Zero at inception because the swap is priced at par, zero at maturity because nothing remains. The sampled maximum is 18.5784 per cent; a fine search puts the true peak at 3.3333 years.

Two effects work against each other. The possible rate move grows with the square root of elapsed time, and the number of payments it can affect falls linearly with the remaining life. Their product is maximised where the derivative vanishes, and for a term T that is at T/3 exactly. On the ten-year swap a fine search finds 3.3330 against the algebraic 3.3333. The exposure peaks at exactly one third of the term, which is a result worth carrying because it is independent of the volatility and of the notional.

That is the opposite shape to a forward's. A forward exchanges everything at settlement, so its exposure rises monotonically to the end. A swap exchanges continuously, so its exposure dies away. The summary's "credit risk increase" is a correct comparison with a cleared future, which has essentially none, and it is not a statement about the profile over time.

How the peak responds to rate volatility

   rate volatility   peak exposure as a   when it peaks   the exposure on a
                     % of notional                       100m notional
               0.50%               9.5341%          3.3 years           9.5341m
               1.00%              19.0681%          3.3 years          19.0681m
               2.00%              38.1363%          3.3 years          38.1363m
               4.00%              76.2726%          3.3 years          76.2726m

The peak's size scales in exact proportion to volatility. Its timing does not move at all.

The volatility multiplies the whole profile rather than reshaping it, so the size of the peak scales in proportion and its timing is fixed by the maturity alone. The collateral schedule is therefore a function of the term and the collateral amount a function of the volatility, and those are separate decisions taken with separate information. That is a cleaner conclusion than the one I expected, which was that higher volatility would pull the peak earlier.

4.6 The inflation swap as an alternative to index-linked bonds

The summary's inflation-matching case describes a fund with fixed-payment liabilities and index-linked assets such as rent, and says a swap is an alternative to index-linked bonds. Verify the equivalence: a nominal bond at 5.00 per cent plus an inflation swap paying fixed at 2.94 and receiving realised inflation should replicate an index-linked bond at 2.00 per cent real.

Two routes to the same real return

                                      return    leg         return   held          credit
   an index-linked bond                     -           -   2.0000%             1   the issuer's

The synthetic real return matches to within 0.0012 per cent, which is a rounding difference in the quoted inflation leg rather than an economic gap.

The equivalence holds to 0.0012 per cent, and the two positions are nonetheless not the same instrument. The bond's credit risk is the issuer's alone; the synthetic route carries the issuer's and the swap counterparty's, which section 4.5 measured peaking at 18.5784 per cent of notional. So the swap route adds an exposure the bond route does not have, and the summary's word "alternative" is doing more work than it appears to.

The reason the swap route exists at all is supply, and this is where the summary's point earns its place. An index-linked bond has to be issued by somebody, and the stock of long-dated index-linked government bonds is finite and in most markets much smaller than the aggregate demand from pension funds. An inflation swap is available in any size and at any maturity a counterparty will quote. The choice is therefore not usually a preference between two available instruments; it is a swap or nothing.

4.7 The derivative reporting requirements

The summary closes its derivatives material with reporting requirements, which have no lecture of their own and belong here because a swap is the hardest instrument to report honestly. Its notional overstates its risk, its market value understates its exposure, and it appears in no asset class unless someone puts it in one.

The Greeks requirement is the one lecture 8 makes concrete, and the first is the one that connects this whole module.

The Greeks requirement is easiest to satisfy badly. Lecture 8 showed a delta-hedged short option position losing money from movement in either direction, so a report showing zero delta without showing gamma describes a position as riskless when it is short volatility. That is exactly what the requirement exists to prevent and exactly what a delta-only report conceals.

The first requirement is the one that matters most for this module. A synthetic equity holding created by an equity swap is an equity holding for every purpose that matters: it earns the index return, it loses money when the index falls, and it consumes the fund's risk budget. If it does not appear in the asset allocation, then the strategic allocation monitoring of lecture 2 is measuring the wrong portfolio, and every attribution figure computed from that allocation is attributing returns to holdings the fund does not have.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Three things separate a practitioner's answer from a textbook one on swaps, and all three are about what the instrument does to the balance sheet rather than what it does to the numbers.

The comparative-advantage argument is a credit argument wearing a rates costume. Every presentation of it that stops at the arithmetic is incomplete, and the incompleteness is not a technicality. When firm B swaps into fixed, it has not fixed its cost of funds: it has fixed the rate on a floating loan whose spread still resets. If B is downgraded, its floating spread widens, its swap does not compensate it, and the 0.35 per cent saving is gone several times over. A practitioner asks first whose credit risk moved and finds that nobody's did, which is the honest reason the differential exists. Sit with the question of who is short what, and the free lunch resolves into a risk that was already there.

The exposure profile is now a funding calculation, not a credit limit. Before central clearing, a swap's potential future exposure fed a counterparty credit limit and an occasional collateral call. After it, that same profile determines initial margin, which must be posted in cash or high-quality collateral and funded. The finding that exposure peaks at a third of the term therefore says something operational: a long-dated swap's margin requirement is front-loaded relative to where you would expect, which means the liquidity cost of a liability hedge is heaviest in its early years, precisely when a scheme is least likely to have planned for it. The 2022 gilt episode in the United Kingdom is what this looks like when it goes wrong at scale.

The instrument's real advantage is that it separates the exposure from the funding. A physical bond purchase requires the cash. A swap requires the margin. For a fund whose liabilities need fifty years of duration and whose assets need to earn a return, that separation is the whole point: hedge the liability with a swap, invest the assets in return-seeking holdings, and the balance sheet does two jobs at once. That is leverage, described honestly. It works until a margin call arrives on a day when the return-seeking assets cannot be sold at a price you like, which is the same day everybody else needs to sell them.

The correction I would make to the summary's framing, beyond the two already made, is to its ordering. It lists "longer term than forwards" as the first advantage and "large amounts only" among the constraints, as though these were independent items. They are the same fact seen from two sides: a swap is a negotiated bilateral contract, which is why it can be long-dated and bespoke and why it needs a size that justifies the negotiation and carries the counterparty's credit. Every advantage and every drawback in the summary's list follows from the contract being bilateral rather than exchange-traded. Say that first, then enumerate.

What this assumes. The swap contract itself, its cash flow structure and its valuation as two offsetting streams are built in swaps, and the pricing of a swap as the level rate equating two streams is in swap and swaption valuation. The rolling cost this lecture prices a swap against comes from hedging with forwards and futures, and the gamma exposure that makes the derivative reporting requirement bite is from using options. The duration and cash-flow matching that liability swaps extend are in bond passive management and LDI, which found a cash-flow match immune to twists that a duration match is not, and the index-linked bonds the inflation swap substitutes for are in government and corporate bonds. The credit deterioration that undermines the comparative-advantage argument is characterised in credit risk measurement.

Where this goes next. Lecture 10 takes the currency case the summary raises here and treats it in full, where a swap and a rolled forward are genuine alternatives and the break-even computed in section 4.2 decides between them. Lecture 11 is transition management in full, of which the equity swap priced here is one of four available routes alongside physical trading, futures and options. Where the bank sits opposite. The bank is the counterparty on every swap in this lecture, and the 0.10 per cent it takes in section 4.3 is the visible half of its economics. The invisible half is the exposure profile of section 4.5 seen from the other side, which the bank capitalises and funds, and which is measured through the counterparty credit risk framework in counterparty credit risk. Numbers used here. The three swap uses, their eleven advantages and constraints, the three matching cases, the four transition points and the five derivative reporting requirements are transcribed from the "using swaps" and "derivative reporting" sections of the ASSA F105 summary in the user's notes. Two corrections are recorded against it, and the examinable position is stated alongside each. First, "longer term than forwards" is presented as a general advantage and is conditional on the horizon: below the break-even, four months at the spreads used here, the forward is genuinely cheaper. Second, the comparative-advantage argument is presented as a gain and may instead be the correct price of the repricing right a floating lender holds, which at a 20 per cent downgrade probability accounts for the entire differential. A third observation is that the summary's "large amounts only" and "need level income" are one constraint rather than two, both following from a bespoke amortising schedule needing a size that justifies negotiating it. Everything numerical is invented for illustration: forward spreads of 0.15 per cent and swap spreads of 0.20, horizons from three months to thirty years, quoted borrowing rates of 5.00 to 6.50 per cent fixed against LIBOR plus 0.30 to 1.00, a bank fee of 0.10 per cent, a downgrade widening of 450 basis points at probabilities from zero to 20 per cent, a liability amortising from 100 to 10 over ten years discounted at 5 per cent, rate volatilities from 0.5 to 4 per cent a year at 95 per cent confidence, and a nominal yield of 5.00 per cent against a 2.94 per cent inflation leg. Twenty-four claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions, all twenty-four passed, and none can pass without the computation. Four failed on the first run: the six-month horizon at which I expected forwards to win is already past the break-even, so the comparison was restated at three months; one set of quotes I intended to give a negative differential gave a positive one and was replaced; and the downgrade widening needed to be 450 rather than 150 basis points to account for the differential. A separate unit error was printing the exposure as 1857 per cent of notional, and a prose line claiming the exposure peak moves earlier with volatility was corrected to the computed result, which is that it does not move at all. Every headline finding states the region in which it holds: the swap's cost advantage holds above the break-even horizon and inverts below it, the differential identity is exact, the exposure peak at one third of the term is independent of both volatility and notional, and the level-income cost is a property of the liability's amortisation rather than of the swap.
