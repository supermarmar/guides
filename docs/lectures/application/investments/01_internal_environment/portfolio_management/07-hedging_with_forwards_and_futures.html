Step 1

The 30-second version

Lectures 4 to 6 chose bond positions. This lecture opens the implementation block: how to take or remove an exposure without trading the underlying. The summary's material is unusually practical, listing five costs of a physical transition and four uses of index futures, and its one silence is the important one: it says market risk is "reduced" and never says by how much.

The answer follows from the correlation between the exposure and the hedge, and it depends on its square. At the minimum-variance ratio the residual volatility is σ√(1−ρ²), so a correlation of 0.98 leaves 19.90 per cent of the risk and 0.90 leaves 43.59. Improving a hedge from 0.90 to 0.98 removes less than half the remaining risk. That is the missing quantification from lecture 2, which found synthetic replication substituting basis risk for name-selection risk without pricing it.

The summary also says to take a position "opposite to the cash market" without saying how large, and the naive one-for-one answer can be badly wrong. With a more volatile future the optimal ratio is 0.7364, leaving 43.59 per cent of the risk, while one-for-one leaves 54.21. And because hedged variance is a quadratic, at twice the optimal ratio the hedge is exactly as risky as doing nothing, which here is 1.4727.

On the "need to roll over short periods", the cost and the uncertainty have different shapes. Rolling a three-year hedge monthly needs 36 contracts costing 5.4000 per cent in spreads and accumulating 2.3664 per cent of uncertainty: the cost rises linearly with the number of rolls and the uncertainty only with its square root. So the binding problem is the spread rather than the risk, which is the opposite of what the phrase "roll risk" implies, and it makes the objection about liquidity rather than about rolling.

And on transition management the summary is right for the reason it gives. Trading the underlying costs 2.3300 per cent of the amount moved against 0.0900 by futures, but 1.8000 of the 2.2400 saving is capital gains tax. For a tax-exempt fund the physical cost falls to 0.5300 per cent and the futures advantage shrinks to 0.4400, real but no longer decisive. And the summary's split is exactly right: a futures overlay rolled quarterly for ten years costs 6.0000 per cent in spreads against a one-off saving of 2.2400, so the overlay is a bridge and not a destination.

Feynman test You can cancel an exposure by taking the opposite position in a future instead of selling the thing itself. Two catches. The future is never quite the same as what you hold, and because the leftover risk depends on the square of how well they move together, even a very close match leaves a fifth of the problem. And the size matters: matching pound for pound is right only by coincidence, and getting it twice as wrong as necessary leaves you exactly where you started. What futures are genuinely brilliant at is moving money between asset classes without triggering a tax bill, which here is most of the saving, so a tax-exempt fund gains far less than a taxed one.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that "hedged" is treated as a binary and is a matter of degree governed by a squared term. A manager reporting a position as hedged when the instrument correlates at 0.95 with the exposure has retained 31.22 per cent of the volatility, and no amount of care about the notional amount changes that. The correlation, not the size, is the first thing to establish.

The second is that the hedge ratio is a computation and is usually a convention. One-for-one is optimal only when the future's volatility equals the correlation times the exposure's, which is a coincidence. Over-hedging is symmetric with under-hedging about the minimum, so the penalty for guessing is two-sided and can reach the point where the hedge accomplishes nothing.

The third is that the roll cost is linear and the roll uncertainty is not, so the two should be managed differently. A manager worried about "roll risk" is worried about the smaller of the two problems, and the answer to the larger one is instrument liquidity rather than contract length.

And the fourth is that the futures advantage in transition is mostly a tax advantage, which determines who benefits. A taxed institution saves 2.2400 per cent of the amount moved and a tax-exempt one saves 0.4400, so the same technique is transformative for one and merely useful for the other. A mandate written on the taxed case will overstate the benefit for a pension fund.

Step 3

The mental model

A residual governed by a square, a ratio that is not one, a roll cost that is linear, and a saving that is mostly tax.

FIRST what is the correlation between the exposure and the contract? below about 0.90 the hedge removes less than half the volatility SECOND what is the minimum-variance ratio, rho x s_s / s_f? one-for-one is optimal only by coincidence THIRD what does the notional work out to? MOST ATTENTION GOES TO THE THIRD AND ALL THE LEVERAGE IS IN THE FIRST. a perfectly sized hedge on a poorly correlated contract is a poorly hedged position.

The second is the symmetry of the hedge ratio error, which is worth knowing because it bounds the damage in both directions. Hedged variance is a parabola in the ratio, so the penalty for over-hedging equals the penalty for under-hedging by the same margin, and AT EXACTLY TWICE THE OPTIMAL RATIO THE HEDGE ACCOMPLISHES NOTHING.
