The summary says full replication has "tracking error = 0". That is true of the sampling component and false of the shortfall an investor experiences, and it also runs together two different quantities: tracking difference is a mean and tracking error is a volatility. Decomposing a realistic full-replication fund, the largest single item is not the fee but the dividend withholding tax, at 0.4800 per cent against 0.1500. Step 1

The 30-second version

Lecture 1 costed the choice between active and passive and found the case for indexing to be an identity. This lecture builds the tracking machinery, and the first thing to establish is that the summary's central claim about it uses one phrase for two quantities.

Tracking difference is a mean and tracking error is a volatility. A management fee is perfectly predictable, so it creates a difference and no error at all. Full replication has the smallest tracking error by a factor of 303.5 against a 25-name sample, and it does not have the smallest tracking difference, because its fee is seven basis points higher. Those are different questions and "tracking error = 0" answers only one.

Decomposing what a full-replication fund actually loses: 0.6740 per cent a year, of which the dividend withholding tax is 0.4800 and the fee only 0.1500. The tax is 71.22 per cent of the whole shortfall and 3.20 times the fee, and it is the component nobody discloses, because a published index is usually a gross total return index that assumes no tax is paid. At a 4.50 per cent dividend yield the drag reaches 0.6750 per cent, 4.50 times a fifteen basis point fee, so a high-yielding market is much harder to track than a low-yielding one for reasons unconnected to the number of names held.

Then reconstitution, which is "buying high" in its defensible form. Every tracker of the same index must trade the same names on the same announced day, so the price moves first. The impact rises with the square root of the indexed demand, from 0.1581 per cent at a five per cent indexed share to 0.5000 at fifty. Unlike the capitalisation-weighting version of the argument in lecture 1, this one does not reduce to a claim about market efficiency: it is a mechanical consequence of the index rule.

Two results came out against expectation. The multi-factor model's advantage over naive largest-name sampling peaks at 100 names, at 1.2522 per cent, rather than being largest at the smallest sample. And commercial matching, which every validity argument condemns, has a tracking error of 1.1758 per cent at a six-month reporting lag, equivalent to a 266-name partial replication and better than a 100-name one. The case against it is not that it tracks badly.

Feynman test An index fund is supposed to just hold everything, so how does it fall behind? Mostly tax: the published index pretends dividends arrive untaxed and a real fund pays withholding on them, which here costs three times the management fee. Then the fund has to buy whatever the index adds, on the day everyone else is buying it, which moves the price against every tracker at once. Also worth separating two things the same phrase covers: falling behind by a predictable amount every year is not the same as wobbling around the index, and the fund that wobbles least is not the one that falls behind least.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the two quantities drive different decisions. A pension scheme comparing trackers on tracking error will choose full replication; one comparing on tracking difference may not, and only the second question is about what the fund receives. A fund quoted on one and selected on the other is being mis-sold, and the summary's phrasing invites exactly that confusion.

The second is that the withholding tax dominates and is invisible. It appears in no fee table, it varies by market and by the fund's domicile and treaty position, and at 71.22 per cent of the shortfall here it is the single most important thing about a tracker's expected performance. Two funds with identical fees tracking identical indices can differ by tens of basis points on treaty access alone.

The third is that reconstitution cost scales with how much of the market indexes, which makes it a growing problem rather than a fixed one. At a fifty per cent indexed share the impact is 0.5000 per cent per affected name, and the square-root scaling means it grows more slowly than the indexed share but never stops growing.

And the fourth is a caution about my own reasoning in the previous lecture. Commercial matching fails six of seven benchmark validity criteria and is still not dominated on tracking error. That means the argument against it has to be made on the criteria and on the incentive it creates, not on the measure it is usually attacked on, and I had assumed otherwise before computing it.

Reconstitution, and "buying high" in its defensible form

Every tracker of the same index must trade the same names on the same day when the index is reconstituted, and the change is announced in advance. That is a predictable demand shock. Taking a name entering at a 0.40 per cent index weight, whose daily volume is 0.80 per cent of its market capitalisation, and a square-root price impact law: share of the market demand as a multiple of price impact annual cost at 6% tracking this index the name's daily volume on the day index turnover 5.00% 2.50 0.1581% 0.0095% 15.00% 7.50 0.2739% 0.0164% 30.00% 15.00 0.3873% 0.0232% 50.00% 25.00 0.5000% 0.0300% THE IMPACT RISES WITH THE SQUARE ROOT OF THE DEMAND, so tripling the indexed share from five to fifteen per cent raises the impact by 73.21 per cent rather than tripling it. At a fifty per cent indexed share the impact is half a per cent on each affected name, which annualises to 0.0300 per cent at six per cent turnover: comparable to a fifth of a management fee, and invisible in the fee disclosure. AND THIS IS THE SUMMARY'S "BUYING HIGH" IN ITS DEFENSIBLE FORM. Lecture 1 showed that the capitalisation-weighting version of that argument reduces to a claim about market efficiency, since at zero pricing noise the drag was exactly zero. THE RECONSTITUTION VERSION DOES NOT REDUCE TO ANYTHING OF THE KIND: the tracker buys after the price has risen because the buying was announced, which is a mechanical consequence of the index rule and holds in a perfectly efficient market. WHICH MEANS THE SUMMARY'S PHRASE COVERS TWO ARGUMENTS OF VERY DIFFERENT STANDING. One needs markets to be inefficient and the other needs only that index changes be announced. A candidate asked to explain "buying high" should give the second, because it survives the objection to the first. THE MITIGATIONS ARE WORTH KNOWING and follow directly from the arithmetic: trade away from the reconstitution date and accept a tracking error instead of a cost, use an index with a lower turnover rule or with staggered reconstitution, or use a total-return swap and transfer the problem to the counterparty, which is the synthetic route and its own set of risks.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to carry is the distinction between the two quantities, because it is the one that changes a selection decision. Full replication minimises the volatility of the shortfall and not the shortfall itself, and the 25-name fund here has a tracking error 303.5 times larger and a smaller average loss. A SCHEME COMPARING TRACKERS ON TRACKING ERROR AND RECEIVING THE TRACKING DIFFERENCE IS SELECTING ON THE WRONG NUMBER, and the summary's single phrase for both is what invites that.

WHICH QUANTITY TO ASK FOR, AND WHY. tracking DIFFERENCE what the fund loses on average what you receive tracking ERROR how reliably it loses that amount how well you can predict it BOTH ARE NEEDED AND THEY ANSWER DIFFERENT QUESTIONS. and a fund with a large tracking error has a tracking difference that is hard to measure: the synthetic fund's twenty-year observed shortfall sits about one standard error from its fee, so even two decades of data does not pin it down.

The second is that the shortfall is mostly tax, which is the single most useful practical finding in the lecture. The dividend withholding tax is 0.4800 per cent against a 0.1500 per cent fee, 71.22 per cent of the total, and it appears in no fee disclosure. IT SCALES WITH THE MARKET'S DIVIDEND YIELD RATHER THAN WITH ANYTHING THE MANAGER CONTROLS, reaching 0.6750 per cent at a 4.50 per cent yield. So two funds with identical fees tracking identical indices can differ by tens of basis points on domicile and treaty access, which is a legal question that decides an investment outcome. The summary's list of index tracking characteristics does not mention tax at all.

The third is that reconstitution gives "buying high" a form that survives scrutiny. Lecture 1 found the capitalisation-weighting version collapses to a claim about market efficiency, since the drag was exactly zero at zero pricing noise. THE RECONSTITUTION VERSION NEEDS ONLY THAT INDEX CHANGES BE ANNOUNCED, which they are, so it holds in a perfectly efficient market. A candidate asked to explain the phrase should give this version. And the cost grows with the indexed share of the market, at a square root rather than linearly, which makes it a slowly worsening structural problem rather than a fixed charge.

THE TWO VERSIONS OF "BUYING HIGH", AND WHICH TO USE. cap-weighting version the index holds more of whatever is overvalued REQUIRES prices to be wrong and to revert. zero drag if not. reconstitution version every tracker must buy the same name on the same announced day REQUIRES only that the index rule be published. THE SECOND IS THE ONE TO ARGUE, and its mitigations follow from the arithmetic: trade away from the reconstitution date and accept tracking error instead of cost, choose a lower-turnover index rule, or use a swap and transfer the problem to a counterparty.

And the fourth, which is a correction to my own reasoning rather than to the summary's. I expected commercial matching to be dominated on tracking error and it is not: at a six-month reporting lag its 1.1758 per cent is equivalent to a 266-name partial replication, better than a 100-name one. SO THE OBJECTION HAS TO BE MADE WHERE IT ACTUALLY HOLDS, on the six validity criteria the peer group fails and on the incentive it creates, which performance measurement lecture 4 priced at 93.32 per cent survival odds for a manager who hugs. The honest verdict is that commercial matching tracks its target competently and the target is the wrong one, which is a stronger objection than the one I set out to make.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The tracking error definition and its two conventions are from risk-adjusted measures and the information ratio, where the root-mean-square variant was measured. The identity behind the case for indexing at all is active versus passive, which also established the replication trade-off this lecture decomposes. The seven benchmark validity criteria the peer group fails are in benchmarks and comparators, and the hugging incentive that explains commercial matching's survival is priced in measuring portfolio performance. The square-root price impact law used for reconstitution is the execution arithmetic from liquidity risk, and index construction including the cap-weighted default is in listed equity.

Where this goes next. Lecture 3 covers the five active equity styles the summary names, where lecture 1's breadth requirement is the test each has to pass. Lectures 4 to 6 do the bond equivalents, beginning with the liability-driven case. Lecture 7 measures the basis and roll risk that synthetic replication substitutes for name-selection risk, which this lecture identified and did not quantify. And lecture 11 on transition management picks up the reconstitution finding directly, since the mechanism is the same: a large, predictable, announced trade is the most expensive kind, and the mitigations are the same three.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank's version of the tracking difference decomposition is the reconciliation of a hedge's realised cost against its theoretical cost, and it contains the same categories: an explicit fee or spread, a tax or regulatory cost, a funding drag on collateral, and an execution cost on rebalancing. The withholding tax finding has a direct parallel in the treatment of tax in the banking book, where a gross-of-tax measure of return systematically overstates what reaches capital, treated in financial management. And the reconstitution problem is a crowded-trade problem, which is the mechanism behind the liquidity spirals in liquidity risk: a predictable forced trade by many participants at once is where market and liquidity risk stop being separable.
