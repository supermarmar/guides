Step 1

The 30-second version

The F105 summary's behavioural material is a well-organised list of named biases with no underlying research and no quantities. Its structure is good, opening with what rationality requires and then dismantling each requirement in turn, and this lecture attaches numbers to it from the papers the list is drawn from. Two of its statements turn out to need correction and one of its omissions is the ingredient that makes the whole theory work.

Start with what it gets right. Prospect theory does produce risk aversion when winning and risk seeking when losing: at Tversky and Kahneman's estimated exponent of 0.88, a fifty-fifty gamble between nothing and plus 100 has a certainty equivalent of 45.4905 against an expected value of 50, and the same gamble in losses has a certainty equivalent of minus 45.4905 against an expected minus 50. But that asymmetry comes from the curvature, not from loss aversion: the certainty equivalent in losses is 45.4905 at every loss aversion coefficient including one, because the coefficient cancels. The summary lists the two as consecutive bullets without saying they are independent parameters.

Then the correction that matters most. The summary says an investor is "less risk averse as frequency of choice / reporting period increases", and those two clauses point in opposite directions, since a higher frequency is a shorter period. Computing the prospect value of an equity holding at each evaluation horizon settles it: it rises from minus 0.030440 at a quarter to plus 1.277241 at twenty years, so a longer period makes the investor less risk averse and a higher frequency makes them more so. Only the second clause is right as written. The mechanism is visible in the loss column, where the chance of seeing a loss falls from 43.05 to 5.88 per cent.

Solving for indifference gives 1.2548 years, which is Benartzi and Thaler's account of the equity premium puzzle: equities are priced as they are because investors look once a year, and one year is the accounting cycle. That result is a claim about preferences rather than markets, since the indifference period moves from 0.3486 to 3.3364 years as loss aversion runs from 1.50 to 4.00.

The summary omits probability weighting entirely, and it is the ingredient that lets prospect theory do what no concave utility function can. A one per cent chance is felt as 5.53 per cent in gains and 3.97 in losses, so the same person rationally buys a lottery ticket and an insurance policy. The overweighting is confined to the tails: by an even chance the weight is already 0.4206, below the truth.

Two further quantifications. Overconfidence has a price and it is lecture 11's price: a manager whose true information coefficient is 0.05 optimally trades 25 times a year for a net 0.1250 per cent, and believing the coefficient is 0.10 means trading 100 times for exactly zero, while believing 0.40 means 1600 trades and minus 6.0000 per cent. The skill never changed. And under a 1/n rule, adding two equity funds to a menu moves a saver's equity weight from 50.00 to 66.67 per cent without anyone deciding anything, so the menu designer chooses the portfolio.

Step 2

Why this matters

Everything in lectures 1 to 11 assumed a decision-maker who maximises expected utility over outcomes. This lecture is about the decision-maker who actually exists, and the gap is not a rounding error. Myopic loss aversion alone is large enough to account for the equity risk premium, which is the single largest unexplained quantity in asset pricing. If the reason equities return more than bonds is that investors dislike opening their statements, then the premium is a fact about reporting cycles rather than about risk, and an institution that can change its reporting cycle can change what it is willing to hold.

The material matters practically because behavioural biases are the one investment problem that governance can actually fix. A fund cannot make its manager skilful, cannot lower transaction costs below the market's, and cannot know which way the currency will move. It can decide how often the trustees see performance figures, how the fund menu is laid out, whether options are presented as a list or as buckets, and what the default is. Those are design decisions with measurable consequences, and section 4.4 puts a number on one of them.

There is a professional dimension too. The biases in this lecture apply to the adviser as much as to the client, and several of them are specifically hazardous for actuaries. Anchoring on a previous valuation basis, confirmation bias in model validation, hindsight bias in reviewing an experience investigation, and the self-serving attribution of good outcomes to method and bad ones to circumstance are all occupational risks rather than abstractions. The summary's list reads as a description of retail investors and applies with equal force to the person writing the report.

Finally, this lecture is the necessary half of a pair. Individual biases matter to markets only if they survive aggregation, and the summary's own statement of the efficient market hypothesis is precisely that enough investors are rational rather than all of them. Whether biased individuals produce a biased market is lecture 13's question, and it cannot be asked without first establishing what the individual biases are and how large they are.

Step 3

The mental model

Prospect theory replaces expected utility with three separate changes, and keeping them apart is most of the understanding. Outcomes are measured as changes rather than levels, losses count more than gains, and probabilities are distorted before being applied.

Expected utility says: work out how wealthy each outcome leaves you, decide how much you like each level of wealth, and weight by the probabilities. That is one function and one set of probabilities.

Prospect theory says people do three different things instead, and it is worth separating them because they have different consequences.

First, they measure from where they are. Not "I would have three million", but "I would be up two hundred thousand". So the same final position is a gain or a loss depending on what you paid, which is why nobody can sell a share below the price they bought it at.

Second, losses hurt about two and a quarter times as much as equivalent gains please. That is a level effect: it shifts the whole loss side down without bending it.

Third, the curve bends. Going from zero to plus 100 feels like more than going from plus 100 to plus 200, and going from zero to minus 100 feels like more than minus 100 to minus 200. That bending is what makes people cautious when ahead and reckless when behind, and it is completely separate from the second point. You can switch off loss aversion entirely and the recklessness remains.

And fourth, which the summary leaves out. A one-in-a-hundred chance does not feel like one in a hundred. It feels like about one in twenty. That single distortion explains the two behaviours that no amount of curve-bending can: the same person buys a lottery ticket, where a tiny chance of a large gain is overweighted, and buys insurance, where a tiny chance of a large loss is overweighted. Take probability weighting away and prospect theory cannot explain either.

Now the trick that makes all of it matter for investing. Because outcomes are measured as changes, the frequency of measurement becomes an input. Look at a volatile asset once a quarter and you see a loss four times in ten. Look once a decade and you see one once in seven. The asset is identical. What differs is how many times you were made to apply a 2.25 multiplier.

Formally, the value function is v(x) = x^0.88 for gains and v(x) = -2.25 x (-x)^0.88 for losses, and the probability weighting function is w(p) = p^g / (p^g + (1-p)^g)^(1/g) with g = 0.61 for gains and 0.69 for losses. All four parameters are Tversky and Kahneman's 1992 estimates from experimental choices, not free parameters chosen here.

Step 4

The detail

4.1 Risk seeking in losses, and why it is not loss aversion

The summary claims that an investor who is winning is risk averse and one who is losing is risk seeking. Test it at Tversky and Kahneman's estimated parameters by computing the certainty equivalent of fifty-fifty gambles in each domain.

Certainty equivalents at an exponent of 0.88 and loss aversion of 2.25

   a 50/50 gamble on          expected   the prospect   certainty     is the chooser
                              value      value          equivalent    risk averse?
   0 or +100, in gains            50.00        28.7720      45.4905    yes
   +50 or +150, in gains         100.00        56.7421      98.4180    yes
   0 or -100, in losses          -50.00       -64.7370     -45.4905    NO, risk seeking
   -150 or -50, in losses       -100.00      -127.6698     -98.4180    NO, risk seeking

In gains the certainty equivalent sits below the expected value, so the chooser pays to avoid risk. In losses it sits above, so the chooser pays to take it.

The summary is right. In gains a chooser will accept 45.4905 rather than a fifty-fifty shot at 100, paying 4.5095 to avoid the risk. In losses the same chooser prefers a fifty-fifty shot at minus 100 to a certain minus 45.4905, paying 4.5095 to take the risk instead. That is the gambling-to-get-even behaviour the summary describes, and it is the reason a trader sitting on a loss doubles the position.

But the mechanism is the exponent, not loss aversion, and the two get conflated because the summary lists them as consecutive bullets under prospect theory. Verify by varying the loss aversion coefficient and watching what happens.

Risk seeking in losses at three loss aversion coefficients

   loss aversion   certainty equivalent   certainty equivalent   risk seeking
   coefficient     of 0 or +100           of 0 or -100           in losses?
              1.00                45.4905               -45.4905   yes
              2.25                45.4905               -45.4905   yes
              5.00                45.4905               -45.4905   yes

Identical at every coefficient, because it multiplies both the gamble and the certain equivalent and cancels.

The certainty equivalent in losses is minus 45.4905 whatever the loss aversion coefficient, including exactly one, where there is no loss aversion at all. The coefficient multiplies the prospect value of the gamble and of the certain outcome equally, so it cancels. Risk seeking in losses therefore comes from the curvature and loss aversion is an entirely separate fact. A candidate who explains gambling-to-get-even by saying "losses hurt more" has given the wrong reason for a right answer, and an examiner can expose that with one follow-up.

What the two parameters do differently is worth stating. Loss aversion makes a chooser refuse a fifty-fifty gamble straddling zero: winning 100 or losing 100 has a prospect value of 0.5 times 55 minus 0.5 times 127, which is negative, so it is declined. Curvature governs behaviour within a domain once the reference point is passed. One explains why people avoid risk; the other explains which direction they err in when they cannot.

4.2 Probability weighting, which the summary omits

The summary's prospect theory section has four bullets and none of them is probability weighting. That omission removes the part of the theory that does the most explanatory work, because a distorted value function alone cannot account for the two most common financial behaviours of ordinary households.

How probabilities are felt, at Tversky and Kahneman's estimated parameters

   the true       weighted for   weighted for   what it explains
   probability    gains          losses
           0.001         0.0145         0.0084   a small chance felt as large
           0.010         0.0553         0.0397   a small chance felt as large
           0.050         0.1316         0.1114   a small chance felt as large
           0.500         0.4206         0.4540   a large chance felt as smaller
           0.950         0.7932         0.8499   a large chance felt as smaller
           0.990         0.9116         0.9451   a large chance felt as smaller

A one per cent chance is felt as 5.53 per cent in gains, a factor of 5.53, and as 3.97 in losses. The distortion reverses before an even chance.

A one per cent chance is felt as 5.53 per cent when the outcome is a gain and 3.97 when it is a loss, factors of 5.53 and 3.97. Overweighting a small chance of a large gain is why lottery tickets sell, and overweighting a small chance of a large loss is why insurance sells. The same person buys both, which no concave utility function can explain, because concavity implies insurance-buying and rules out lottery-buying simultaneously. Probability weighting resolves the contradiction and the summary leaves it out.

The region matters: the effect is a tail phenomenon rather than general optimism or pessimism. By an even chance the weight is 0.4206, already below the truth, and it stays below all the way to certainty. So the distortion is not that people are bad at probabilities in general; it is that they are systematically wrong about rare events in a specific direction, which is precisely the direction that matters for insurance pricing, catastrophe risk, and the pricing of deep out-of-the-money options priced in lecture 8.

4.3 The myopic loss aversion line, and which half of it is right

The summary states that an investor is "less risk averse as frequency of choice / reporting period increases". A higher frequency is a shorter period, so the two clauses cannot both be true. Compute the prospect value of holding equity at a 7 per cent real return and 20 per cent volatility, evaluated at each horizon, against a safe asset at 1 per cent.

The same equity holding at seven evaluation periods

   evaluation period   chance of seeing   prospect value   prospect value   which is
   in years            a loss             of equity        of the safe      preferred
                                                           asset
                  0.25              43.05%        -0.030440         0.005131   the safe asset
                  0.50              40.23%        -0.022339         0.009443   the safe asset
                  1.00              36.32%         0.005045         0.017378   the safe asset
                  2.00              31.03%         0.071891         0.031982   equity
                  5.00              21.69%         0.286637         0.071630   equity
                 10.00              13.42%         0.634612         0.131826   equity
                 20.00               5.88%         1.277241         0.242609   equity

The asset is identical in every row. Only the frequency of looking at it changes, and the prospect value runs from minus 0.030440 to plus 1.277241.

A longer reporting period makes the investor less risk averse and a higher frequency of choice makes them more so. The summary joins those with a slash as though they agreed. Only the second clause is right as written, and the first is the opposite of the truth. The examinable position is the phenomenon rather than the sentence: an investor who evaluates more frequently behaves more conservatively, which is the standard statement of myopic loss aversion and is what the summary means.

The mechanism is in the loss column. The chance of seeing a loss falls from 43.05 per cent over a quarter to 5.88 over twenty years, because the mean grows linearly in time and the standard deviation only as its square root. A frequent evaluator meets more losses and applies a 2.25 multiplier to each one, so the same asset looks worse the more often it is looked at. Nothing about the asset changed.

Solving for the horizon at which the two are equally attractive gives 1.2548 years. Benartzi and Thaler found approximately one year in 1995 and pointed out that this is also the natural accounting and reporting cycle, which is their explanation of the equity premium puzzle: equities are priced to offer as much as they do because investors look once a year. That is a large claim from a small piece of arithmetic, and it is the single most useful result in behavioural finance for an institutional investor.

How the indifference period depends on the loss aversion coefficient

   loss aversion   indifference period   what it implies about the
   coefficient     in years              equity premium
              1.50                0.3486   a more loss-averse investor needs longer
              2.25                1.2548   a more loss-averse investor needs longer
              3.00                2.1862   a more loss-averse investor needs longer
              4.00                3.3364   a more loss-averse investor needs longer

The indifference period moves from 0.3486 to 3.3364 years as loss aversion runs from 1.50 to 4.00, so this is a claim about preferences rather than about markets.

The region in which the finding holds is important and limits how much weight the equity premium explanation can bear. The indifference period is not a market quantity: it moves nearly tenfold across the plausible range of loss aversion, so the theory says the premium is consistent with annual evaluation at a particular loss aversion coefficient rather than implied by it. That is weaker than it is often presented as being, and the honest statement is that myopic loss aversion is a sufficient explanation, not the demonstrated one.

The practical consequence is direct and unusual in this subject, because it is actionable. If frequent evaluation causes excessive caution, then a pension fund's reporting cycle is an investment decision. Reporting to trustees quarterly rather than triennially will, on this arithmetic, produce a more conservative portfolio for reasons entirely unconnected to the fund's actual horizon or liabilities. That is an argument for a long-horizon fund to report its funding position annually and its investment performance over rolling multi-year periods, which lecture 4 reached from the completely different direction of statistical significance.

4.4 The 1/n heuristic: the menu designer chooses the portfolio

The summary's "effect of options" lists primacy, recency and intermediate effects, notes that multiple options discourage decision-making, and stops. The sharpest finding in that literature is simpler and more troubling: Benartzi and Thaler found in 2001 that savers spread contributions roughly evenly over whatever funds are offered.

The equity weight under a 1/n rule, by menu composition

   the menu offered                        equity funds   bond funds   resulting
                                           on the menu    on the menu  equity weight
   one equity fund, one bond fund                     1            1         50.00%
   two equity funds, one bond fund                    2            1         66.67%
   four equity funds, one bond fund                   4            1         80.00%
   one equity fund, four bond funds                   1            4         20.00%

A swing of 60.00 percentage points in equity weight from menu design alone, with no saver making a different decision.

Adding two equity funds to a menu moves the saver's equity weight from 50.00 to 66.67 per cent, and four gets to 80.00. Reversing the menu gives 20.00, a swing of 60.00 percentage points from menu design alone. Nobody chose differently. This is the summary's "multiple options discourage decision making" taken one step further and it is worse than abstention: the saver does not abstain, the saver divides evenly, which looks like a decision and is not one.

The same behavioural rule under two menu designs

   how the menu is organised          equity funds   equity weight under a
                                      offered        1/n rule over the menu
   a flat list of funds                          1                   50.00%
   a flat list of funds                          2                   66.67%
   a flat list of funds                          4                   80.00%
   a flat list of funds                          8                   88.89%
   two asset class buckets                     any                   50.00%

Bucketing by asset class makes the allocation independent of the fund count, so the identical rule gives a defensible answer under one design and an arbitrary one under the other.

The practical lesson is the second table. Organising the menu by asset class rather than as a flat list of funds makes the 1/n rule produce 50.00 per cent equity regardless of how many funds sit inside each bucket, because dividing evenly across two asset classes is a defensible answer. The same behavioural rule gives a sensible outcome under one presentation and an arbitrary one under another, and choosing the presentation costs nothing. That is the strongest argument in this lecture for taking behavioural finance seriously as a design discipline rather than as a catalogue of human failings.

4.5 Overconfidence, priced

The summary defines overconfidence as overestimating outcomes and lists hindsight bias, confirmation bias and dislike of negative events beneath it. What it does not do is connect it to anything measurable. The connection is turnover, and lecture 11 priced turnover. A manager sizes activity to the information coefficient they believe they have and earns on the one they actually have.

A manager with a true information coefficient of 0.05, at four levels of self-belief

   the manager believes   trades a year   gross alpha   trading cost   net alpha
   the IC is              (its optimum    on the REAL
                          for that IC)    IC of 0.05
                     0.05              25       0.2500%         0.1250%     0.1250%
                     0.10             100       0.5000%         0.5000%     0.0000%
                     0.20             400       1.0000%         2.0000%    -1.0000%
                     0.40            1600       2.0000%         8.0000%    -6.0000%

The skill is 0.05 in every row. Doubling the self-belief takes the net alpha to exactly zero and quadrupling it takes it to minus 1.0000 per cent.

The calibrated manager trades 25 times a year and nets 0.1250 per cent. A manager who believes the coefficient is twice as large trades four times as often, because the optimal trade count goes with the square of the coefficient, and nets exactly zero. At eight times the belief the net is minus 6.0000 per cent. The skill was real and unchanged throughout, and the confidence destroyed it.

The structural reason is worth carrying because it is lecture 1's fundamental law meeting lecture 11's cost curve. Gross alpha rises with the number of bets, as the square root of breadth. Cost rises linearly with the number of trades. A square root and a straight line must cross, so there is an optimal activity level for any positive trading cost, and it is 25 trades a year here against an unbounded appetite. Overconfidence does not put the manager in the wrong place on a flat surface: it puts them to the right of a definite peak.

Barber and Odean found exactly this pattern in 2000 in 66,000 household brokerage accounts: the most active fifth of investors underperformed the least active fifth substantially, with gross returns nearly identical between the groups and the whole gap explained by trading costs. That is as close to a controlled test as this literature offers, because the two groups held similar portfolios and differed only in how often they changed them.

4.6 Mental accounting is a directionless bias, not a conservative one

The summary defines mental accounting as separating related decisions rather than aggregating them. Quantify what the separation costs by taking two holdings, each at 20 per cent volatility, correlated at minus 0.50.

Two holdings, judged separately and jointly

   how the decision is made        holding A   holding B   combined     risk seen
                                                           volatility   by the saver
   each pot judged separately         20.00%       20.00%      10.0000%   20.00% and 20.00%
   the two judged together            20.00%       20.00%      10.0000%   10.0000%

The combined volatility is 10.0000 per cent, half of either part. A saver judging each pot separately never sees that figure.

The true combined volatility is 10.0000 per cent, exactly half of either holding on its own, and a saver who judges each pot separately sees two 20 per cent risks and never sees the 10. The diversification is real and invisible, so the saver may reject a combination that is safer than either of its parts. That is the standard account of mental accounting and it is only half the story.

Change the correlation to positive 0.90 and the combined volatility becomes 19.4936 per cent, so separate accounting now understates the risk rather than overstating it. Mental accounting is therefore not a conservative bias but a directionless one, and which way it errs depends on a correlation the saver is not looking at. That distinction matters for advice: the remedy is not "be less cautious", it is "aggregate before deciding", and a practitioner who assumes the bias always runs one way will give the wrong correction half the time.

4.7 Ambiguity aversion, and information that cannot change a decision

The summary defines ambiguity aversion as paying for more information to reduce uncertainty. The sharp version is that the value of information is exactly zero unless it can change what you do, and people pay for it anyway.

When information has value and when it has none

   the decision                     best action   best action   can the news   value of
                                    if good news  if bad news   change it?     the news
   allocate 60% to equity, either way 60% equity    60% equity    NO             EXACTLY ZERO
   allocate 80% if good, 20% if bad   80% equity    20% equity    yes            positive

The first row is the common case. The report is read, and the allocation is what it was always going to be.

The first row is the ordinary case in institutional practice. A board commissions a report, reads it, discusses it, and allocates what it was always going to allocate. The report's value is exactly zero and its cost is not. So the correct test before buying information is whether any possible answer would change the decision, and if not, the money buys comfort rather than information. That is not always worthless, since comfort has a role in getting a decision made at all, but it should be recognised as what it is.

This test is also the only antidote in the entire lecture that costs nothing to apply. Debiasing programmes for anchoring, overconfidence and confirmation bias are difficult, slow and of contested effectiveness. Asking "would any answer change what we do" before commissioning the work is a single question, asked once, before the spend.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Three things separate useful behavioural finance from the catalogue of biases it is usually taught as, and the first is about what you can actually do with it.

Biases you cannot fix and structures you can change are two different subjects, and only one of them is useful. Nobody has reliably debiased anybody. Overconfidence survives being told about overconfidence, and the hindsight bias is not much reduced by knowing its name. What does work is changing the structure in which the decision gets made: the reporting frequency in section 4.3, the menu layout in section 4.4, the default option, the trade authorisation limit. The practitioner's version of this lecture is therefore not "watch out for these twenty biases" but "which three design decisions in this fund are quietly determining its behaviour". That reframing is the whole professional value of the material.

The reference point is the thing to look for, because it is the only free parameter and it is usually set by accident. Prospect theory says everything is measured as a change from somewhere, and it does not say from where. In practice the reference point is whatever the last report showed, or the purchase price, or the peak value, or the funding level at the last valuation, and it is set by an accounting convention nobody chose deliberately. A scheme whose reference point is its funding level three years ago behaves entirely differently from one whose reference point is the buy-out cost, and the difference is not an investment view: it is a choice of denominator. When a client's behaviour looks irrational, the first question is what they are measuring from, and the answer is nearly always in a document rather than in their head.

The biases apply to the adviser, and the actuarial ones are specific and identifiable. Anchoring on the previous valuation basis so that assumptions move in small steps from an arbitrary starting point is the summary's definition of anchoring applied verbatim to a professional process. Confirmation bias in model validation means testing whether the model works rather than whether it fails, which lecture 8's verification discipline exists to prevent. Hindsight bias in an experience investigation makes last cycle's outcome look predictable and this cycle's assumptions overconfident. The self-serving attribution of good outcomes to method and bad ones to markets is what an attribution analysis exists to test, and lecture 6 of the performance measurement module showed how easily attribution can be constructed to give the flattering answer. None of these is a retail investor's problem.

On the summary itself, the correction I would stress is section 4.3's, because it is a genuine error rather than an omission. "Less risk averse as frequency of choice increases" is backwards, and a candidate who reproduces the sentence has stated the phenomenon in reverse. The safe formulation is that more frequent evaluation produces more conservative behaviour, because each evaluation is another opportunity to apply the loss multiplier. And the omission I would add to any answer is probability weighting, because without it prospect theory cannot explain the two behaviours everyone recognises, and an examiner asking why the same person insures a house and buys a lottery ticket is asking for exactly that parameter.

Connections

What this assumes. The expected utility framework these biases depart from is built in indifference curves and the optimum, and the efficient market hypothesis the summary invokes is tested against price patterns in technical analysis. The fundamental law of active management that section 4.5 meets against a cost curve is in active versus passive, and the trading costs it collides with are priced in transition management and overlays. The statistical difficulty of demonstrating skill, which is the other reason to review infrequently, is in measuring portfolio performance, and the underlying decision theory is in behavioural economics. The tail probabilities that probability weighting distorts are what price the structures in using options.

Where this goes next. Lecture 13 asks the question this lecture cannot: whether individual biases survive aggregation into market prices. The summary's own efficient market assumption is that enough investors are rational, so a biased individual is not yet a biased market, and the mechanisms that let biases aggregate rather than cancel are herding, noise trading and the limits to arbitrage. The sentiment measures lecture 13 covers are attempts to observe in aggregate what section 4.1 describes in the individual. Beyond this module, the governance and mandate lectures pick up section 5's design argument, since the reporting frequency and the menu structure are mandate items rather than investment ones.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank designs the menus and sets the defaults, so section 4.4 is its product design discipline rather than an observation about savers: the equity weight of an entire cohort of retail customers is determined by a fund list somebody drew up, which is why conduct regulation now treats product design as a risk in its own right. The probability weighting of section 4.2 is also the bank's revenue: structured products that pay a large amount with small probability sell precisely because that probability is overweighted, and pricing them fairly while selling them profitably is the tension conduct rules police, treated alongside the operational and conduct exposures in operational risk. On the other side, myopic loss aversion is a bank's own problem too: a trading desk marked to market daily and a banking book carried at amortised cost hold the same risk and behave differently towards it, which is the accounting-driven wedge running through market risk.

Numbers used here. The rationality requirements, prospect theory and its four sub-items, the heuristics tree with its eleven named biases, and the seven option effects are all transcribed from the "individual behaviour" section of the ASSA F105 summary in the user's notes, which lists them without research citations or quantities. The parameters are external and verifiable: the value function exponent of 0.88, the loss aversion coefficient of 2.25, and the probability weighting parameters of 0.61 for gains and 0.69 for losses are all from Tversky and Kahneman (1992), "Advances in prospect theory: cumulative representation of uncertainty", Journal of Risk and Uncertainty 5, with the original framework in Kahneman and Tversky (1979), Econometrica 47(2). The myopic loss aversion argument and the roughly one-year evaluation period are from Benartzi and Thaler (1995), Quarterly Journal of Economics 110(1); the 1/n finding is from Benartzi and Thaler (2001), American Economic Review 91(1); and the turnover result is from Barber and Odean (2000), "Trading is hazardous to your wealth", Journal of Finance 55(2). One correction is recorded against the summary, with the examinable position stated alongside: its myopic loss aversion sentence, "less risk averse as frequency of choice / reporting period increases", contains two clauses pointing in opposite directions, and only the second is right as written, since the computed prospect value rises with the length of the evaluation period. Two further observations are recorded: risk seeking in losses comes from the curvature and not from loss aversion, surviving at a coefficient of exactly one, so the summary's consecutive bullets describe independent parameters; and the summary omits probability weighting entirely, which is the ingredient without which prospect theory cannot explain insurance and lottery purchases by the same person. Everything else numerical is invented for illustration: gambles of plus or minus 50 to 150, an equity real return of 7 per cent at 20 per cent volatility against a 1 per cent safe asset, evaluation periods from three months to twenty years, loss aversion coefficients from 1.50 to 5.00, a true information coefficient of 0.05 against beliefs up to 0.40 with a 0.50 per cent round-trip trading cost, menus of one to eight funds, and two holdings at 20 per cent volatility correlated at minus 0.50 and plus 0.90. Twenty-three claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions, all twenty-three passed, and none can pass without the computation. One failed on the first run: my baseline manager broke exactly even rather than making money, because 100 trades a year is already past the optimum at the assumed cost, so the section was restructured to make the baseline the algebraically optimal 25 trades and the grid search now checks that optimum against the closed form. Three further claims were flagged as unfalsifiable by a vacuity check and rewritten or removed. Every headline finding states the region in which it holds: risk seeking in losses holds for any exponent below one at any loss aversion coefficient, probability overweighting is confined to probabilities below roughly a third, the myopic loss aversion indifference period depends strongly on the loss aversion coefficient and is therefore a preference claim rather than a market one, the 1/n distortion disappears under a bucketed menu, and mental accounting errs in a direction set by a correlation the saver is not observing.
