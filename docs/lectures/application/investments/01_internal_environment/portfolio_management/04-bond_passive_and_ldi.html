On one curve every matching portfolio costs the same, so "the cheapest bonds that match" cannot mean what it says

Twenty-five bonds, two bonds and one bond all cost exactly 1341.3933 to match the same liability and all match its 10.2957-year duration, so cost cannot be the criterion. What separates them is the residual: the cash flow match has exactly zero surplus at every twist and the single zero swings 153.2724. And "low expected returns" understates the position badly, because matching does not merely lower the return, it converts an uncertain chance of full funding into a certain 9.07 per cent shortfall whenever the liability discount rate exceeds the asset yield by a point.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Lectures 2 and 3 covered the equity side. This lecture takes the passive bond techniques the summary names, liability-driven investment, index tracking and commercial tracking, and finds that two of its phrases carry more weight than they can bear.

"Identify the cheapest bonds that match the liabilities" describes a constrained optimisation, and on a single curve the constraint makes cost irrelevant: a 25-bond cash flow match, a two-bond barbell and a single zero at the duration all cost exactly 1341.3933 and all match the 10.2957-year duration. So "cheapest" only acquires content once bonds are priced off different curves, which is to say once credit or liquidity differences exist. What actually separates the three is the residual: the cash flow match has zero surplus at every twist and the single zero swings 153.2724.

"A matched position has low expected returns" is the second phrase, and it understates the position. If the liabilities are discounted at a rate above the matching assets' yield, matching locks in the shortfall permanently: at a one point spread the matched position is worth 90.93 per cent of the liability, a 9.07 per cent deficit no future return can close. That is not a market loss, since the assets are worth what they cost. It is the discount rate being higher than what gilts pay. The sign reverses cleanly: a scheme discounting below the asset yield locks in a surplus, 110.54 per cent at a 4.50 per cent rate.

On index tracking, bonds differ from equities in two opposite directions and the summary treats them as the same. Bonds mature, so a bond index turns over mechanically: 20.00 per cent a year for a five-to-ten-year index against 3.45 for a one-to-thirty, compared with six per cent for an equity index, costing 50.00 per cent of a ten basis point fee at the narrow end. But bonds have no specific risk to diversify, so five bonds matching five key rates leave exactly zero residual against a thirty-line index, where 25 stocks of 500 left a 5.4582 per cent tracking error.

And the inclusion rules the summary mentions turn out to matter more than tidiness: weighting by line count rather than debt outstanding gives the government 71.43 per cent against a true 57.14, and understates the smallest issuer at 1.79 against 11.43. The rule is what makes the index represent the market rather than the issuance calendar.

Feynman test If you owe a fixed stream of payments, the safest thing is to buy bonds paying exactly that stream. On one interest rate curve every way of doing it costs the same, so "find the cheapest" is not a real instruction until some bonds are riskier than others. What differs is how badly you are hurt if the curve bends rather than shifts, and matching the payments exactly means you are not hurt at all. The catch is that if your accountant assumes a higher return than bonds pay, buying the bonds makes the shortfall permanent instead of merely likely. And a bond index is odd in two ways: bonds keep falling out of it by growing old, but a handful of bonds can mimic hundreds, because bonds of the same maturity are nearly the same thing.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the cost equivalence redirects the whole LDI decision. If cost cannot discriminate, then the question is how many instruments to hold against how much residual risk, which is a risk budget rather than a shopping exercise. A trustee told that LDI means finding cheap matching bonds is being pointed at the wrong variable.

The second is that the locked-in deficit is the central LDI decision and the summary's phrase conceals it. Matching converts an uncertain chance of full funding into a certain shortfall, and whether that is right depends entirely on whether the discount rate was ever achievable. That is a question about the valuation basis, not about the assets, and the two cannot be decided separately.

The third is that bond index tracking has a cost floor set by the index rule. Mechanical turnover of twenty per cent a year on a narrow maturity band costs half a typical tracker fee before anything else happens, and no manager skill reduces it. The choice of index rule is therefore a cost decision made by the index provider on the investor's behalf.

And the fourth is that the equity intuition about sampling is actively misleading for government bonds. Five bonds tracking a thirty-line index perfectly is not a near miss, it is exact on the dimension that matters, because there is no specific risk to diversify away. A manager applying equity sampling logic to a gilt portfolio will hold far more lines than necessary.

Step 3

The mental model

Equal costs, unequal residuals, a locked-in deficit whose sign is the spread, and two opposite differences from equities.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The immunisation machinery, the key-rate durations and the instrument-against-residual-risk schedule are all built in liability hedging and dynamic benchmarks, which established that N sensitivity conditions plus the level need N plus one instruments. The discounting and duration arithmetic is from fixed income pricing, and the surplus framework that makes the locked-in deficit meaningful is from asset liability mismatching risk. The equity comparisons throughout are from equity index tracking, and the benchmark appropriateness point that inclusion rules raise is from benchmarks and comparators. The credit risk that "cheapest" imports is treated in credit analysis.

Where this goes next. Lectures 5 and 6 take the active bond techniques the summary names: anomaly switching, which trades price discrepancies between similar bonds, and policy switching and yield pick-up, which take views on the curve and on credit spreads. The breadth test from lecture 1 bites harder there than in equities, because a bond manager has fewer independent instruments than an equity manager has names, and this lecture has just shown why: five bonds span a thirty-line index, so a bond portfolio has about five independent dimensions to bet on rather than five hundred. Lecture 9 on swaps is where the synthetic route to matching is priced, since an interest rate swap matches a liability without holding a bond at all.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank runs the identical calculation as its interest rate risk in the banking book, and the locked-in-spread finding has a direct counterpart: matching the repricing profile of assets to liabilities removes the earnings volatility and fixes the margin at whatever the curve currently offers, which is the economic value against earnings trade-off in interest rate risk. The mechanical maturity turnover of a bond index is the bank's rollover problem in liquidity risk, where a short-dated funding book must be refinanced continually whether or not conditions are favourable. And "cheapest bonds that match" is the collateral optimisation problem, where the cheapest eligible asset to deliver is cheap because it is the least desirable, treated in liquidity management.
