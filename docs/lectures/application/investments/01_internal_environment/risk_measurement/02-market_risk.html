Five portfolios, six measures, and three different answers to which one is safest

The all-cash portfolio ranks first of five on absolute risk, fourth on actuarial risk and fifth on relative risk. Semi-variance is exactly half the variance under normality, so it adds nothing until the distribution is skewed, at which point it adds a great deal. And the 95 per cent value at risk of a balanced fund turns negative after 2.8282 years, which reads as no risk at all.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Market risk is the risk that the value of assets changes. Lecture 1 established that step one of the control process is choosing a definition, and offered two: actuarial risk, failing to meet liabilities as they fall due, and downside risk, failing to meet a target return. To those the source adds value at risk and the variance of returns, and requires a choice between absolute and relative and a choice of timescale.

Apply all six measures to the same five portfolios and they disagree. The all-cash portfolio is ranked first of five on absolute standard deviation, fourth on actuarial surplus volatility and fifth on relative tracking error. Across the six measures, three different portfolios are named safest: all cash, bond heavy and balanced. Nothing about the portfolios changed between columns.

Semi-variance deserves particular scrutiny because it is the measure most often recommended as an improvement on variance. Under normality, with the target at the mean, the semi-variance is exactly half the variance, to twelve decimal places, on every portfolio. So it cannot reorder anything. Construct two distributions with the same mean and variance but skew of minus 0.9877 against minus 0.0001, and it earns its place: the semi-deviation is 12.28 per cent higher, the 95 per cent value at risk 21.13 per cent higher and the 99 per cent 48.96 per cent higher. The shortfall probability moves the other way, 11.64 per cent lower, so probability-based and severity-based measures disagree in sign on the same pair.

And the timescale is not a presentational choice. The shortfall probability of a balanced fund falls from 45.07 to 13.39 per cent between three months and twenty years, while its 95 per cent value at risk rises, peaks at one year and then turns negative, crossing zero at exactly 2.8282 years because the drift grows with the horizon and the dispersion only with its square root.

Feynman test Ask six sensible questions about the same five funds and get three different winners. Is the cash fund safe? It cannot lose money, so yes. Will it pay the pensions? No, so no. Does it look like the benchmark? Not remotely, so no again. Then a warning about the fashionable fix. Measuring only the downside sounds obviously better than measuring both sides, and if returns are symmetric it is literally the same number halved, so it changes no decision at all. It starts to matter the moment losses come in rare large lumps, which is when everything else stops working too.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that syllabus objective (f) requires methods for monitoring and controlling market risk, and the honest answer is that the method is determined by the definition and the definition is a choice. A risk report showing one number for market risk has concealed a decision, and this lecture shows that the concealed decision changes which portfolio is judged safest.

The second is that semi-variance is widely recommended as a correction to variance and, under the assumptions in which portfolio theory is usually taught, it is variance divided by two. That is worth knowing before proposing it. It becomes a genuinely different measure only when the return distribution is asymmetric, which is precisely when the mean-variance machinery of the previous module was already in trouble.

The third is that probability-based and severity-based measures can disagree in direction, not merely in magnitude. On two distributions with matched mean and variance, the skewed one has a lower probability of falling short of seven per cent and a 48.96 per cent higher 99 per cent value at risk. A fund reporting shortfall probability would call it safer and a fund reporting tail loss would call it far riskier. Both would be right about what they measured.

And a fourth. The value at risk of a positively-drifting portfolio eventually becomes negative, which a report renders as no risk. The crossing point is computable, at 2.8282 years here, and it is well inside the horizon of any pension fund. Any long-horizon value at risk number should be treated as a statement about the drift assumption rather than about the risk.

Why three of the six measures agree

Absolute standard deviation, semi-deviation and value at risk gave the same ordering. Under normality they are all monotone functions of the standard deviation, so they must. Reporting both is not belt and braces, it is the minimum, because they can point in opposite directions.

The timescale

The source's fourth requirement at the measuring step is a timescale. The shortfall probability falls monotonically from 45.07 to 13.39 per cent. The value at risk rises to a peak at one year and then falls, turning negative between three and five years. WHY DOES THE VALUE AT RISK TURN OVER? Because the drift grows with the horizon T and the dispersion only with the square root of T, so the drift eventually dominates. Solving for the crossing: T* = (1.6449 x sigma / mu)^2 = 2.8282 years the VaR at T* is -0.000e+00, confirming the algebra A NEGATIVE VALUE AT RISK MEANS THE FIFTH PERCENTILE OF THE OUTCOME IS A GAIN. A risk report renders that as no risk, or as a negative number nobody knows how to read, and at twenty years the figure is minus 117 per cent. THAT IS NOT REALLY A DEFECT OF VALUE AT RISK. It is a demonstration that a long-horizon value at risk is dominated by the assumed drift, which is the least reliable input available, as portfolio construction lecture 6 measured: a twenty-year mean estimate carries a 4.25 per cent standard error. SO A TWENTY-YEAR VALUE AT RISK IS A STATEMENT ABOUT THE EXPECTED RETURN ASSUMPTION WEARING THE CLOTHES OF A RISK MEASURE. AND IT CONNECTS TO STRATEGIC ASSET ALLOCATION. Lecture 7 of portfolio construction found that the terminal dispersion grows with the horizon while the annualised dispersion shrinks, and that both are correct. This is the same phenomenon a third time: the horizon is a substantive choice and different measures respond to it in different directions.

The four steps, applied

STEP 1, MEASURING. Choose from actuarial risk, downside risk, value at risk or the variance of returns; choose absolute or relative; choose a timescale. This lecture has shown that all three choices change the ranking of the same portfolios. STEP 2, MODELLING. Modern portfolio theory if returns can be treated as normal, in which case semi-variance collapses to half the variance and adds nothing. Asset-liability modelling if the actuarial definition was chosen at step 1. Scenario analysis if neither, which is lecture 8. STEP 3, REPORTING. The requirement that bites here is that the risk factors be understood: a single market risk number conceals three choices, and a report that does not name them cannot be interrogated. STEP 4, DEPARTURE LIMITS. The load ratios and load differences of lecture 1, which control relative risk whether or not the fund measures it. Note the consequence: a fund that has chosen ABSOLUTE measurement at step 1 and writes departure limits at step 4 is controlling a different quantity from the one it reports. THAT LAST POINT IS THE MOST LIKELY REAL-WORLD FAILURE IN THE WHOLE FRAMEWORK. Departure limits are inherently relative, since they are expressed against a benchmark allocation. If the measured and reported risk is absolute, the control and the measurement are addressing different risks, and nothing in the four steps forces them to be reconciled.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to carry is that "market risk" is not a quantity, it is a family of quantities that disagree. Three different portfolios are named safest across six measures on one set of holdings, and the all-cash portfolio is simultaneously safest under three of them and riskiest under two. ANY SINGLE NUMBER PRESENTED AS THE MARKET RISK OF A FUND HAS CONCEALED THREE CHOICES: the definition, the basis and the horizon. The useful professional habit is to ask which three were made before reading the number.

THE THREE CHOICES HIDDEN INSIDE ONE NUMBER. choice options changes the ranking? the definition actuarial, downside, VaR, variance YES the basis absolute or relative YES the horizon any YES, and in opposite directions ALL THREE ARE MADE AT STEP 1 OF THE FRAMEWORK, USUALLY IN A SENTENCE, AND NONE APPEARS IN THE OUTPUT.

The second is that semi-variance is the clearest example in this module of a fix that fixes nothing under the assumptions in which it is usually proposed. Under normality with the target at the mean it is exactly half the variance, to twelve decimal places, on every portfolio tested. Moving the target changes the level and not the order. It becomes a genuinely different measure only when the distribution is asymmetric, and the mean-variance framework that recommended it had already assumed away the asymmetry. THE MEASURE AND THE ASSUMPTION ARE INCOMPATIBLE: adopt one and the other is redundant.

The third is the sign disagreement, which is the finding I would most want a candidate to remember. On two distributions with mean and variance matched to within three basis points, the skewed one has a 48.96 per cent higher 99 per cent value at risk and an 11.64 per cent LOWER probability of missing the target. A shortfall-probability report calls it safer and a tail-loss report calls it half again as risky, and both are correct. Probability of failure and severity of failure are not substitutes and they are not even correlated in sign.

WHY THE TWO MEASURES DISAGREE, MECHANICALLY. negative skew means: many modest gains, rare large losses consequence for P(miss): the target is missed LESS often consequence for severity: when missed, missed by MUCH more BOTH FOLLOW FROM THE SAME SHAPE. a measure that counts occurrences and a measure that weighs magnitudes must diverge on any asymmetric distribution, and the more asymmetric it is the further they diverge.

The fourth is that every risk basis makes something riskless by definition, and the choice of basis is a choice of what to treat as safe rather than a discovery. Absolute measurement makes cash riskless, relative measurement makes the benchmark riskless whatever it contains, and actuarial measurement makes the liability hedge riskless. A fund measuring relative risk against a 40 per cent equity benchmark has declared 40 per cent equities to be the zero point, and if the benchmark is wrong for the fund then the measurement system cannot detect it.

And the fifth, which is the one most likely to matter in practice. Departure limits are inherently relative, because they are written against a benchmark allocation. So a fund that measures and reports absolute risk while controlling through load ratios is controlling a different quantity from the one it reports, and nothing in the four-step framework forces the two to be reconciled. THAT MISMATCH IS INVISIBLE IN EVERY DOCUMENT INVOLVED, because each document is internally consistent.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The four-step control framework, the actuarial and downside definitions, and the load ratios that make departure limits inherently relative are all from the risk control framework. The surplus volatility used as the actuarial measure is constructed in asset-liability modelling, and the tracking error used as the relative measure in risk budgets, value at risk and tracking error, which also establishes the coherence properties that expected shortfall satisfies and value at risk does not. The claim that cash is riskless is the capital market line and CAPM. The horizon behaviour reproduces strategic asset allocation, and the 4.25 per cent standard error on a twenty-year mean estimate is from limitations of mean-variance. The fat tails and skew that make the asymmetric measures earn their place are documented in the empirical characteristics of asset prices, and the moments, mixture distributions and quantiles used throughout are from statistics.

Where this goes next. Lecture 3 takes the actuarial definition seriously and treats asset-liability mismatching as a risk type in its own right. Lectures 4 to 7 walk the same four steps for credit, liquidity, operational and relative performance risk, where the measurement problem is progressively less quantitative and the controls progressively more organisational. Lecture 8 is stress testing, which exists precisely because of the limitation this lecture documented: every measure here is a summary of an assumed distribution, and a scenario is a way of asking what happens outside it.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank's market risk framework makes the three choices this lecture identifies explicitly and in regulation rather than in a sentence: the measure is expected shortfall, the basis is absolute, and the horizon is ten days scaled by liquidity. That specificity is the direct response to the disagreement measured here. The distinction between probability and severity of failure is the same one separating a bank's probability of default from its loss given default in credit risk, where the two are estimated by different models precisely because they behave differently. And the observation that a relative basis makes the benchmark riskless has a supervisory analogue in the standardised approach, where prescribed risk weights make the prescribed portfolio the zero point whether or not it is safe.

That is deliberate: the summary is a list of trigger phrases rather than an exposition, one of its quantitative rules was found to be wrong in lecture 1, and its non-quantitative claims cannot be checked the same way.
