The naive stress test everyone runs is an 8.28 sigma event, and reverse stress has a closed form

Stressing every factor adversely at three sigma sounds conservative and describes a state of the world the correlation matrix says is 8.28 standard deviations away. Score scenarios by Mahalanobis distance instead and they become comparable. And the most plausible scenario producing a given loss has an exact solution: its severity is the loss divided by the portfolio volatility, and no factor moves as far as three sigma.

Step 1

The 30-second version

This lecture closes the module, and it exists because of a limitation running through the seven before it: every measure so far summarises an assumed distribution. A scenario does the opposite. It names an outcome and asks what it would cost, which is why the source introduces stress testing with the observation that value at risk does not consider extreme events.

Make that precise. Build two loss distributions with the same mean and the same 99 per cent value at risk, matching to 0.05 percentage points, and their 99.9 per cent value at risk differs by a factor of 2.08 and their 99 per cent expected shortfall by 1.61. A value at risk limit cannot distinguish them at all. Expected shortfall can, which is why the market risk framework moved to it, and it is still an average over the tail, so it cannot distinguish a tail that is deep from one that is merely long.

The source's two test types are localised, stressing weak areas, and major market, stressing all parameters. The naive version of the second applies an adverse three-sigma move to every factor and sums the losses, giving 56.40 per cent against a portfolio three-sigma loss of 33.78. That is not conservatism, it is incoherence: measured by Mahalanobis distance, the number of standard deviations a scenario sits from the centre once correlations are counted, that scenario is 8.2796 sigma away, and two of the five factor correlations are negative, so it describes a world the matrix says cannot happen.

And reverse stress testing, which asks what scenario would break you rather than what your chosen scenarios cost, has an exact solution on a linear portfolio. Minimising the Mahalanobis distance subject to a fixed loss gives a scenario proportional to the covariance matrix times the exposures, at a distance of the loss divided by the portfolio volatility. A 30 per cent loss sits at 2.6639 sigma, verified against the closed form to 8.9 parts in ten thousand trillion, and no factor in it moves as much as three sigma.

Feynman test Every number so far has been a summary of a bell curve you assumed. A scenario is different: you describe a specific bad day and add up what it costs. Two traps. Pushing every variable to its worst at once sounds cautious and is actually a wilder assumption than anything in your model, because some of those variables move in opposite directions. And rather than guessing at bad days, you can solve for the likeliest one that ruins you, which turns out to be a broad shallow move in everything rather than a dramatic move in one thing.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the source's justification for stress testing is exactly right and is usually stated too weakly. It is not that value at risk underestimates extreme events; it is that value at risk contains no information whatever about them. Two portfolios can share a value at risk to two decimal places and differ by a factor of two in the worst year in a thousand, and no amount of care in estimating the quantile helps.

The second is that the standard major market test is not conservative, which is worth knowing before defending one to a board. Stressing everything adversely at once assumes a correlation of one between all factors, and the portfolio here has two negative correlations. The result is a scenario 8.28 standard deviations from the centre presented as a three-sigma test, and a loss 1.67 times the portfolio's own three-sigma figure. The number is not cautious, it is unanchored.

The third is that Mahalanobis distance solves the comparison problem for any scenario expressible as a factor vector. Without it, a stress programme produces a list of losses with no way to rank their plausibility, so the scenario that generates the largest number gets the attention rather than the one that matters. With it, a 29.70 per cent loss at 4.12 sigma and an 8.40 per cent loss at 3.78 sigma can be compared honestly.

And a fourth. Reverse stress testing is the one technique here with a closed form, and it is the one most often omitted. On a linear portfolio the answer is the z-score, and the scenario direction is the covariance matrix applied to the exposures, which typically involves modest moves in many factors rather than a dramatic move in one. That is precisely the class of scenario a committee does not write down.

THAT IS WHAT A SCENARIO IS FOR. It names a specific outcome and prices it, rather than summarising a distribution and reporting a statistic of the summary.

Localised tests

The source's first test type is LOCALISED, aimed at weak areas of the portfolio. Take a portfolio with exposures to five factors and a total volatility of 11.2616 per cent, and stress each factor by three of its own standard deviations: factor 3-sigma move loss from that factor alone equity 54.0000% 29.7000% credit spread 2.4000% 8.4000% rates 3.0000% 6.0000% currency 30.0000% 4.5000% property 39.0000% 7.8000% SUM of the separate losses 56.4000% THE LOCALISED TESTS DO EXACTLY WHAT THEY SHOULD. Each isolates one exposure, and the ranking tells the risk function where the concentrations are: equity dominates at 29.70 per cent, and currency contributes 4.50 despite a 30 per cent move, because the exposure is small. WHAT A LOCALISED TEST CANNOT FIND is anything requiring two factors to move together, and the portfolio's largest single-factor loss, 29.70, is well below its total volatility multiplied by three, 33.78. So the localised suite systematically understates, and it understates by an amount that depends on the correlations it ignores.

The major market test, done wrongly

The source's second type stresses ALL PARAMETERS. The naive implementation applies an adverse three-sigma move to every factor simultaneously, which is the sum above: naive all-factor 3-sigma stress 56.4000% portfolio 3-sigma loss 33.7848% the naive test overstates by 1.67 times THAT IS NOT CONSERVATISM, IT IS INCOHERENCE. Summing adverse three-sigma moves assumes every factor moves against you together, which is a correlation of one. This portfolio's correlations are not one, and two of them are NEGATIVE: rates correlate at minus 0.25 with equity and minus 0.35 with credit spreads. The naive scenario therefore describes a state of the world the covariance matrix says does not occur. MEASURE HOW FAR OUT IT IS. It is not a severe scenario, it is an uninterpretable one, and a board told it is a three-sigma stress has been misinformed about the only thing that would let it judge the result. AND NOTE THE SINGLE-FACTOR ROWS. A three-sigma move in one factor with the others held at zero has a distance ABOVE three, because holding correlated factors at zero is itself an unusual configuration. The excess measures how correlated the factor is with the rest: equity crash alone 4.1228 most correlated with the others credit blowout alone 3.7805 rates spike alone 3.2146 least correlated, and negatively With all correlations set to zero, a single-factor three-sigma move has a distance of exactly 3.000000, which confirms that the excess is entirely a correlation effect. SO THE MAHALANOBIS DISTANCE FIXES BOTH TEST TYPES AT ONCE. It tells you that your localised tests are more severe than three sigma and your major market test is very much more severe, and it puts them on one scale so they can be compared.

Reverse stress testing, solved

Rather than choosing scenarios and computing losses, choose a loss and find the MOST PLAUSIBLE scenario that produces it. That is a constrained minimisation: minimise x' V^-1 x subject to -b' x = L The Lagrangian gives x* proportional to minus V times b, and substituting back: d* = L / sqrt( b' V b ) SO THE SEVERITY OF THE MOST PLAUSIBLE SCENARIO CAUSING A LOSS L IS THE LOSS DIVIDED BY THE PORTFOLIO VOLATILITY. THE FOUR STEPS OF THE FRAMEWORK, APPLIED TO STRESS TESTING ITSELF. STEP 1, MEASURING. A scenario is not a measure and has no distribution, so this step is replaced by a choice of scenario set. The factor model and its covariance matrix, which is what makes the severity computable. Note the circularity: the covariance matrix used to score scenarios is the same object whose failure the scenarios are meant to cover, so a scenario scored at eight sigma is eight sigma ACCORDING TO THE MODEL THAT MISSED IT. STEP 3, REPORTING. The distinctive requirement is that the severity be reported alongside the loss, since a loss without a severity cannot be acted on. STEP 4, DEPARTURE LIMITS. A stress limit is a maximum loss under a named scenario, which is the one limit form in this module that binds on a state of the world rather than on a position.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to carry is that the standard major market test is not a severe scenario, it is an uninterpretable one. Stressing every factor adversely at three sigma produces a point 8.2796 standard deviations from the centre, on a covariance matrix containing two negative correlations, so it describes a world the model says does not occur. A BOARD TOLD THIS IS A THREE-SIGMA STRESS HAS BEEN DEPRIVED OF THE ONLY INFORMATION THAT WOULD LET IT JUDGE THE ANSWER, and the fix is one line of arithmetic per scenario.

WHAT TO ASK OF ANY STRESS RESULT. question why what is its Mahalanobis distance? so it can be compared with other scenarios is that distance the one we intended? the naive 3-sigma test is 8.28 does the direction respect the correlations? two of ours are negative what does the REVERSE test say? it has a closed form and is usually skipped NONE OF THE FOUR IS CONVENTIONALLY ASKED AND ALL FOUR ARE CHEAP.

The second is the reverse stress result, which is the most useful thing in the lecture because it is exact and because its answer is counterintuitive. The most plausible scenario producing a given loss sits at the loss divided by the portfolio volatility, and its direction is the covariance matrix applied to the exposures. For a 30 per cent loss that is 2.6639 sigma with no factor moving as much as three, so THE LIKELIEST ROUTE TO A LARGE LOSS IS A BROAD SHALLOW MOVE IN EVERYTHING. That scenario has no narrative, which is exactly why nobody proposes it.

The third is that a value at risk limit cannot distinguish two portfolios differing by a factor of two in the worst year in a thousand. That is not an estimation problem to be improved with better data; it is what a quantile is. Expected shortfall improves matters by a factor of 1.61 here and remains an average over the tail, so it too has a blind spot, just a smaller one. THERE IS NO SUMMARY STATISTIC THAT DOES NOT HAVE ONE, which is the argument for scenarios rather than an argument about which statistic to prefer.

THE CIRCULARITY WORTH BEING HONEST ABOUT. the covariance matrix scores the scenarios the scenarios exist because the covariance matrix misses things so a scenario scored at 8 sigma is 8 sigma ACCORDING TO THE MODEL THAT MISSED IT MAHALANOBIS SCORING IS STILL WORTH DOING, because a wrong common scale is more useful than no common scale. but it cannot validate a scenario that the model does not represent, and two of the six blind spots identified in step 4 are of that kind.

The fourth is the scenario set. Deriving it from the blind spots of lectures 2 to 7 gives six scenarios, and not one of them is an extreme version of a measured quantity. A regulatory change in the discount basis moves the liability with no market variable moving at all; an operational loss larger than any recorded is not a function of the factor vector; every peer losing together is a statement about other institutions. STRESSING THE INPUTS OF THE EXISTING MEASURES HARDER WOULD NEVER PRODUCE ANY OF THEM, which is the case for building the set this way rather than from history.

And the fifth, closing the module. Eight lectures have produced a framework whose every measure is internally correct and each of which is blind to something specific. The four-step process of lecture 1 applies cleanly to five of the seven risk types, does not reach operational risk at all, and is applied to stress testing itself only by analogy. THE HONEST SUMMARY IS THAT RISK MEASUREMENT IS A SET OF PARTIAL VIEWS RATHER THAN A SYSTEM, and the professional skill is knowing which view is blind to what, which is the list in step 4.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. This lecture depends on all seven before it, because its scenario set is derived from their blind spots. The four-step framework is the risk control framework; the tail beyond the quantile is market risk, which also supplies the moment-matched construction reused in step 4; the discount-basis change that moves a liability with no market move is asset-liability mismatching risk; correlated default and the sector scenario are credit and counterparty risk; the collateral call and the stressed gap it is priced against are liquidity risk; the unobservable operational tail is operational risk; and industry-wide correlation is relative performance and basis risk. The failure of value at risk to be coherent, and the move to expected shortfall, are established in risk budgets, value at risk and tracking error, whose Euler attribution the reverse-stress direction inverts. Quadratic forms, matrix inversion and the Lagrangian are from mathematics, and the Mahalanobis distance from statistics.

Where this goes next. This completes the risk measurement module and the internal environment's treatment of risk. The performance measurement module turns from what could happen to what did, and inherits the benchmark problem from lecture 4 of portfolio construction and the survivorship bias from lecture 7 here. The portfolio management module implements the mandates these eight lectures constrain. The external environment module supplies the regulatory regime that mandates reverse stress testing for some institution types and prescribes the scenarios for others, which is the governance answer to the judgement problem identified in step 4.

Where the bank sits opposite. Stress testing is more developed and more prescribed in banking than anywhere in investments, and the wiki covers it: stress testing treats the Pillar 2B framework, and risk management the ICAAP within which it sits. Three correspondences are exact. Reverse stress testing is a supervisory requirement rather than an option, and the closed form in step 4 is why it is answerable. Supervisory scenarios are prescribed centrally precisely because scenario design is the governance judgement identified here, and a firm choosing its own would choose ones it survives. And the circularity noted in step 5, that the model scoring the scenario is the model that missed it, is the reason supervisory stress tests specify the scenario rather than the severity, leaving the firm to compute the loss and the supervisor to own the judgement.

Numbers used here. The F105 summary gives stress testing three lines: that value at risk does not consider extreme market events, that localised tests examine weak areas of the portfolio, and that major market tests stress all parameters. Those three statements are quoted and used, and everything else is built externally. The Mahalanobis distance as a scenario severity measure follows the plausibility-constrained stress testing literature, principally Breuer, Jandacka, Rheinberger and Summer (2009), "How to Find Plausible, Severe and Useful Stress Scenarios", International Journal of Central Banking 5(3); the closed-form reverse stress solution derived in step 4 is a standard Lagrangian result for a linear portfolio. Everything numerical is invented for illustration: five factors at 18, 0.8, 1.0, 10 and 13 per cent volatilities with the stated correlation matrix including two negative entries, portfolio exposures of 0.55, minus 3.50, minus 2.00, 0.15 and 0.20, and a normal distribution at 10 per cent volatility against a mixture with a 0.5 per cent chance of a 60 per cent loss. Simulations use 4,000,000 draws with a seeded generator. Twelve claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions and every table was emitted by the computation and pasted without retyping. Four claims failed on the first run and one was a genuine construction error rather than a threshold problem: the fat-tailed distribution had been calibrated by searching over its bad-state volatility, which drove it towards a point mass and produced a tail FLATTER than the normal's, the opposite of the intended demonstration. The parameters were then solved algebraically so that both the mean and the 99 per cent quantile match by construction. The remaining three were mis-set thresholds, including an assertion that a single-factor three-sigma move would have a Mahalanobis distance near three when it is 4.1228, and correcting that produced the finding that the excess measures the factor's correlation with the others.
