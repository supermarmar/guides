Step 1

The 30-second version

Operational risk is the one risk type in this module with almost no quantitative machinery, and the source treats it as a list: one definition, four controls and thirteen sources. All three lists repay closer reading, and the four controls turn out to be one control and three enablers of it.

Start with an inconsistency inside the source. It defines operational risk as loss due to fraud or external events, and then lists thirteen sources of which nine fall outside that definition: IT problems, human error, internal control issues and the rest are failures of processes, people and systems. The list is right and the definition is too narrow. Basel's definition covers exactly what the list contains, and every one of the thirteen sources maps onto one of Basel's seven event types.

Then the control the index singles out. Segregating front and back office converts a one-person fraud into a two-person one, so at a 0.20 per cent individual propensity the probability falls from 0.2000 to 0.000400 per cent, a factor of 500. Nothing else on the list comes close. But the factor is exactly one over the propensity only if the two people are independent, and at a 0.30 correlation in willingness the joint probability is 11.0 times higher and the reduction factor falls to 45, which is 9.1 per cent of the ideal.

And the measurement problem is worse than for any other risk in the module. Modelled as a compound Poisson with lognormal severity, the 99.9 per cent annual loss is 11.89 times the mean, and in a tail year the largest single event is 92.29 per cent of the total. Ten years of data at twenty events a year reaches the 99.50 per cent severity quantile; the capital number needs the 99.99, which is 7.8 times larger. The number depends on the assumed shape beyond the data rather than on the data, which is why the Basel advanced measurement approach was withdrawn.

Feynman test Needing two signatures instead of one is worth more than every other safeguard put together, because two dishonest people are far rarer than one. That reasoning only holds if the two are genuinely unconnected, and if they share an office, a boss and a bonus pool then most of the benefit evaporates. Then the hard part: the losses that matter are single enormous rare events, so a decade of records tells you almost nothing about the one you are trying to hold capital against. You end up with a number that reflects your assumption about the unseen tail, not your experience of it.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that a definition narrower than the risk it names will exclude the exposures that matter. Nine of the source's own thirteen sources fall outside its own definition, and the four that do not are fraud, theft, natural disasters and third-party dependencies. A firm applying the definition literally would have no framework for IT failure or human error, which is where most operational losses actually arise.

The second is that segregation of duties is the only high-leverage control available, and its leverage is entirely conditional on something nobody measures. The reduction factor is one over the individual propensity when the two holders are independent and collapses to nine per cent of that at a modest correlation. So the useful question about a segregated process is not whether it is segregated but whether the two people share incentives, management or premises.

The third is that controls and capital address different quantities and are routinely confused. Halving the event frequency halves the expected loss and cuts the 99.9 per cent quantile by only 30.9 per cent, because a tail year is one large event and halving the arrival rate barely changes how large the largest one is. Every control on the source's list reduces frequency or detection time; none reduces severity.

And a fourth, which is the honest limit of the whole subject. A 99.9 per cent operational capital number requires extrapolating a severity distribution well beyond every loss ever observed, and the extrapolation factor is driven by the assumed tail thickness rather than by the length of the data history. That is not a criticism of practitioners; it is why the supervisory approach abandoned internal modelling in favour of a business indicator.

The loss distribution

Operational losses are conventionally described as high-frequency-low-severity plus low-frequency-high-severity. Model that as a compound Poisson with lognormal severity, at twenty events a year: statistic value as a multiple of the mean expected annual loss 5.0457 1.00 50.0% quantile 3.7683 0.75 90.0% quantile 9.3308 1.85 99.0% quantile 23.8795 4.73 99.9% quantile 59.9984 11.89 THE 99.9 PER CENT QUANTILE IS 11.89 TIMES THE MEAN AND 15.92 TIMES THE MEDIAN. For comparison, a normal distribution's 99.9 per cent point is about three standard deviations from its mean. AND IN A TAIL YEAR IT IS ONE EVENT. Averaging over the years in the worst 0.1 per cent, the total loss is 97.21 and THE LARGEST SINGLE EVENT IS 92.29 PER CENT OF IT. So the capital number is not a statement about a bad year's accumulation of small losses; it is a statement about the largest single loss the firm might suffer.

Why it cannot be estimated

To observe a 99.9 per cent ANNUAL quantile directly requires a thousand years of history. The practical alternative is to model the severity distribution from event data and combine it with a frequency, so the question becomes how far into the severity tail the data reaches. Halving the lognormal sigma: sigma = 1.80 -> extrapolation factor 7.80 sigma = 1.20 -> extrapolation factor 3.93 sigma = 0.60 -> extrapolation factor 1.98 So a firm with thin-tailed losses could estimate its capital from ten years of data, and a firm with fat-tailed losses cannot do it from any realistic history. THE FIRMS THAT MOST NEED AN OPERATIONAL CAPITAL NUMBER ARE THE ONES LEAST ABLE TO ESTIMATE ONE. THAT IS WHY THE BASEL ADVANCED MEASUREMENT APPROACH WAS WITHDRAWN. Banks were permitted to model operational capital internally, the models were dominated by an unobservable tail assumption, and the results were not comparable between institutions holding similar risks. The replacement, the standardised measurement approach in BCBS d424, uses a business indicator scaled by a loss multiplier, which abandons the attempt to estimate the tail and accepts a cruder but comparable number instead.

The reason is the composition of a tail year: it is one large event, and halving the arrival rate does not make the largest event much smaller, it makes it slightly less likely to occur. THAT IS NOT AN ARGUMENT AGAINST CONTROLS. It is an argument for being clear which quantity a control is bought to move. A control programme is justified by the expected loss it prevents, which it halves, and not by the capital relief it produces, which is far smaller. A business case built on the second will disappoint. THE FOUR STEPS, APPLIED TO OPERATIONAL RISK. STEP 1, MEASURING. The definition is where this lecture began, and the source's is too narrow. Use Basel's, and note that the choice is not cosmetic: nine of thirteen sources are in or out depending on which is adopted. STEP 2, MODELLING. A frequency and a severity, combined. The modelling is honest only about the body of the distribution, and the tail is an assumption, which is what the withdrawal of the advanced measurement approach conceded. STEP 3, REPORTING. The distinctive requirement here is a LOSS DATABASE, including near misses, because the events are rare enough that a firm cannot afford to learn only from its own realised losses. Consortium data and scenario workshops exist for the same reason. STEP 4, DEPARTURE LIMITS. There is nothing to set a limit against, because operational risk is not a position. The control is the process design of section 4, and its measurement is an audit finding rather than a number. THIS IS THE ONE RISK IN THE MODULE WHERE THE FOURTH STEP DOES NOT APPLY.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to carry is that the source's four controls are not four controls. Segregation reduces the fraud probability by a factor of one over the individual propensity, 500 on these figures, and the other three reduce it by nothing at all on their own. What they do is keep the correlation in willingness low, and at a 0.30 correlation the segregation benefit falls to 9.1 per cent of its ideal. SO REPORTING LINES, QUALIFICATIONS AND EXPERIENCE ARE NOT ALTERNATIVES TO SEGREGATION, THEY ARE WHAT MAKES SEGREGATION WORTH HAVING, and a firm that has the second signature without the independence has bought the form and not the substance.

THE TEST OF A SEGREGATED PROCESS, IN THREE QUESTIONS. do the two signatories report to different people? are they paid on different measures? do they sit in different places? NONE OF THOSE IS "ARE TWO SIGNATURES REQUIRED", which is the question actually asked. the three above are what determine whether the factor is 500 or 45.

The second is the inconsistency inside the source, which is worth noticing because the definition is the examinable part and the list is the useful part. Nine of the thirteen sources fall outside a definition covering only fraud and external events. The list describes what operational risk is; the definition describes a subset of it. Basel's definition, covering processes, people and systems as well as external events, is exactly the one the list requires, and every source maps onto one of its seven event types.

The third is that the summary's taxonomy mixes causes, events and consequences at one level, which matters for a reason beyond tidiness. Human error is a cause, theft is an event, delivery failure is a consequence, and a taxonomy that puts them side by side cannot attribute a loss, because a single incident belongs in three categories at once. Basel's seven are organised by event type alone, which is why one of them absorbs four of the summary's entries. LEARN THE THIRTEEN FOR THE EXAM AND USE THE SEVEN FOR WORK.

WHY THE MIXING MATTERS OPERATIONALLY. a payment goes to the wrong account because a clerk mistyped it and no one checked under the summary's list human error, internal control issues, delivery failures under Basel's seven execution, delivery and process management ONE INCIDENT, THREE CATEGORIES UNDER THE FIRST AND ONE UNDER THE SECOND. a loss database built on the first double-counts and cannot be aggregated.

The fourth is the measurement result, which is the most honest thing in the lecture. Ten years of data at twenty events a year reaches the 99.50 per cent severity quantile, and a 99.9 per cent capital number needs the 99.99, which is 7.8 times larger. The extrapolation factor is driven by the assumed tail thickness rather than the length of the history: at a sigma of 0.60 it is 1.98 and at 1.80 it is 7.80. SO THE FIRMS WHOSE LOSSES ARE MOST DANGEROUS ARE THE ONES LEAST ABLE TO QUANTIFY THEM, and the withdrawal of the advanced measurement approach was a supervisory concession to exactly that.

And the fifth. Controls buy frequency and capital buys severity, and confusing the two produces bad business cases. Halving the event rate halves the expected loss and cuts the 99.9 per cent quantile by 30.9 per cent, because a tail year is one event and halving the arrival rate makes it slightly less likely rather than much smaller. A CONTROL PROGRAMME IS JUSTIFIED BY THE EXPECTED LOSS IT PREVENTS AND NOT BY THE CAPITAL RELIEF IT PRODUCES. And note that this is the one risk in the module where step four of the framework does not apply at all, because operational risk is not a position and there is nothing to set a departure limit against.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The four-step control framework, whose fourth step this risk type does not reach, is from the risk control framework. The one-factor copula used to model correlated willingness is the same construction as the joint default probability in credit and counterparty risk, where correlation also destroys a control's value and the parallel is exact. The observation that a measure's answer depends on which quantile is chosen is market risk, and the distinction between the probability and the severity of a failure is developed there on a moment-matched pair. The failure of a tail measure to be estimable from a short history is the backtesting result in risk budgets, value at risk and tracking error, where a 99 per cent model wrong by a factor of two passes a 250-day test 76 per cent of the time. Compound distributions, the Poisson and lognormal, and order statistics are from statistics.

Where this goes next. Lecture 7 covers relative performance and basis risk, the last two of the source's risk types and the two with the least machinery. Lecture 8 is stress testing and scenario analysis, which is the only honest response to the estimation problem set out in step 4: if the tail cannot be estimated it can at least be described, and a scenario workshop is a structured way of describing it. In the portfolio management module, the segregation argument reappears as the role of the custodian, which is segregation of duties applied to an entire function rather than to a process.

Where the bank sits opposite. Almost everything quantitative in this lecture is banking material. The seven event types are the Basel II taxonomy still used for loss data collection, and operational risk in the wiki covers the capital approaches in full: the basic indicator and standardised approaches, the advanced measurement approach and its withdrawal, and the standardised measurement approach in BCBS d424 that replaced it. The estimation result in step 4 is the technical reason for that sequence of policy changes. Segregation of duties is the first line of the three-lines-of-defence model in risk management, and the correlation result explains why the second line must report independently of the first rather than merely separately from it. Anti-money-laundering and know-your-customer controls, treated under operational risk in the banking track, are the clearest case of a control whose value is entirely in reducing frequency.

Numbers used here. The definition, the four controls and all thirteen sources are from chapter 12 of the ASSA F105 summary in the user's notes, quoted in full and in the source's own words. The seven level-one event types are the Basel II operational risk taxonomy, and the withdrawal of the advanced measurement approach in favour of the standardised measurement approach is BCBS d424, Basel III: Finalising post-crisis reforms (2017). Everything numerical is invented for illustration: individual fraud propensities from 0.05 to 1.00 per cent, correlations in willingness from zero to 0.80, a Poisson frequency of twenty events a year and a lognormal severity with a median of 0.05 and a sigma of 1.80. Simulations use 400,000 years for the loss distribution and 200,000 for the frequency comparison, with seeded generators. Thirteen claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions and every table was emitted by the computation and pasted without retyping. One claim failed on the first run, asserting that the segregation benefit would vanish entirely at a correlation of 0.9999; the reduction factor there is 1.0182 rather than 1.0000, which is convergence limited by the integration grid rather than a substantive result, and the corrected version reports the convergence across five correlations instead. The classification of the thirteen sources into Basel categories in step 4 is my own judgement, not a published mapping, and two of the assignments are arguable: third-party dependencies could sit under external fraud or under business disruption, and confidentiality breaches could sit under clients and products or under internal fraud depending on the cause.
