Step 1

The 30-second version

Liquidity risk is two risks in one name: cash flows insufficient to meet liabilities, and an inability to raise funds in time. The source gives two techniques, gap analysis and duration analysis, and attaches three caveats to the first. This lecture takes all three seriously, because each of them changes the answer.

The gap is defined on a six-month criterion, which means one bucket. Build a maturity ladder instead and the defect is immediate: a portfolio with a six-month net gap of plus 8.0 has a cumulative gap of minus 29.0 in the one-to-three-month bucket, and is negative in three of the four buckets inside the window. A net figure is a sum and liquidity is a sequence, so the correct statistic is the minimum cumulative gap, not the total.

The source says to allow for dealing costs, and they are not small. Liquidation cost is a half-spread plus a market impact that grows as the square root of the participation rate, so it is a property of the position relative to the market rather than of the asset: the same small-cap holding costs 1.0906 per cent to sell at a tenth of daily volume and 2.8000 at three times it. Selling faster costs impact and slower costs price risk, and the optimum is interior. For the property position it sits at 53.7348 days, where the two cost components are exactly equal.

And the source states plainly that gap analysis has no impact of stress. Fix it the Basel way, haircutting assets and applying runoff rates, and this fund passes comfortably at plus 98.50. That is the honest result, and it relocates the question: what would make it fail is not the balance sheet but the leverage of its own hedge. At an overlay notional equal to its liabilities the fund survives a 492 basis point move; at four times that, 123.

Feynman test Adding up six months of money in and money out tells you nothing about whether you can pay next week's bills, because a total hides the order things happen in. Track the running balance and take the worst point. Then two corrections. What you own is worth less than the statement says if you have to sell it quickly, and how much less depends on how big your holding is compared with what trades in a normal day. And a healthy-looking fund can still be caught out, not by its own weakness but by the size of the hedge it has put on, because the hedge asks for cash exactly when it is working.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the single-bucket gap is not a conservative approximation, it is an unsound one. A positive six-month figure and a minus 29.0 cumulative position two months in are perfectly consistent, and the fund fails while the measure passes. Replacing the net gap with the minimum cumulative gap costs nothing and is the only version that answers the question asked.

The second is that the horizon the source chooses is an order of magnitude longer than the one supervisors settled on. Six months against thirty days is not a detail: the 2007 to 2009 experience was that funding disappears in days, and the cumulative gap at thirty days here is minus 27.0 against plus 8.0 at six months. A measure calibrated to the wrong horizon can be satisfied throughout a failure.

The third is that dealing costs make liquidity a property of the position rather than the asset, which changes what a liquidity policy can sensibly say. "Hold liquid assets" is not actionable; "hold no position exceeding a given multiple of daily volume" is. A property holding at twenty times daily volume takes 100 days to sell at a twenty per cent participation rate, so it is not a liquid asset on any six-month criterion and certainly not on a thirty-day one.

And a fourth. The stressed gap test on this balance sheet passes with a wide margin, and I had expected it to fail. Reporting that honestly matters more than manufacturing a failure, because it locates the real vulnerability: the funds caught out in September 2022 did not have weak balance sheets, they had large overlays, and the surviving yield move falls inversely with the overlay notional.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to carry is that the single-bucket gap is unsound rather than approximate, and the fix is free. A net figure of plus 8.0 coexists with a cumulative position of minus 29.0 two months in, because netting across a period discards the order in which things happen. THE MINIMUM CUMULATIVE GAP COSTS NOTHING EXTRA TO COMPUTE AND IS THE ONLY VERSION THAT ANSWERS THE QUESTION, and it comes with two useful by-products: the bucket in which the minimum occurs tells you when the cash is needed, and the number of negative buckets tells you how long the squeeze lasts.

WHY NETTING FAILS HERE AND NOT ELSEWHERE. quantity netting across the period is market risk exposure fine: the position is the position credit exposure fine: the counterparty owes what it owes LIQUIDITY WRONG: a surplus in month four does not pay month two LIQUIDITY IS THE ONE RISK WHERE THE ORDER OF EVENTS IS THE RISK. that is why it needs a ladder and the others do not.

The second is that dealing costs relocate liquidity from the asset to the position, and that changes what a policy can say. There is no such thing as a liquid asset: the same small-cap holding costs 1.0906 per cent to sell at a tenth of daily volume and 2.8000 at three times it, and the property position at twenty times daily volume takes 100 days at a twenty per cent participation rate. "HOLD LIQUID ASSETS" IS NOT A CONTROL. A limit expressed in multiples of daily volume is.

The third is the execution optimum, which is worth knowing because it gives a check anybody can apply. Total cost of the form A over root T plus B times root T is minimised where the two halves are equal, which happens at T equal to A over B, here 53.7348 days with both components at 9.151210 per cent. So IF A LIQUIDATION PLAN'S IMPACT COST AND PRICE RISK ARE NOT ROUGHLY EQUAL, THE HORIZON IS WRONG, and you can tell without solving anything.

THE CHECK, IN ONE LINE. impact much larger than price risk selling too fast price risk much larger than impact selling too slowly roughly equal about right TRUE FOR ANY COST OF THE FORM A.T^-0.5 + B.T^0.5, whatever A and B are, so it does not depend on the impact model's calibration.

The fourth is that the stressed gap passed and I had expected it to fail. Reporting that honestly is worth more than adjusting the balance sheet until it broke, because the pass is the realistic case and it moves the question to the right place. A fund with 240 of assets against 61 of stressed thirty-day outflows is not fragile. What made funds fragile in September 2022 was the size of their overlay: the surviving yield move falls inversely with the notional, from 1970 basis points at a small overlay to 123 at a large one. THE VULNERABILITY WAS IN THE HEDGE, NOT THE BOOK.

And the fifth. The four measures form a hierarchy rather than a set of alternatives, which distinguishes this lecture from lecture 2. There, six measures of market risk disagreed and none dominated. Here each row adds an assumption the row above omitted, so a fund that survives the fourth necessarily survives the others. WHEN MEASURES NEST LIKE THAT THE ONLY DEFENSIBLE CHOICE IS THE STRICTEST, and the argument for reporting a weaker one is administrative rather than analytical.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The liquidity gap, the six-month criterion and the liquidity risk elasticity are introduced in the risk control framework, where the source's sign rule for the elasticity is also corrected. The observation that measures of the same fund can disagree, and the discipline of naming what each assumes, is market risk. The collateral call that relocates the vulnerability is measured from the poster's side in liability hedging and dynamic liability benchmarks and from the receiver's in credit and counterparty risk, which is the same operation seen from the two ends of the contract. The free-asset logic that determines how much illiquidity a buffer can support is asset-liability mismatching risk. Trading costs and market microstructure are treated in asset classes, and the square-root impact law and the optimisation over the execution horizon follow the framework in mathematics.

Where this goes next. Lecture 6 turns to operational risk, where the measurement problem is qualitative and the controls are organisational rather than quantitative. Lecture 7 covers relative performance and basis risk, and lecture 8 is stress testing, which generalises the reverse-stress framing used in step 4: the surviving yield move is a single-factor reverse stress, and a full scenario imposes several at once. In the portfolio management module, the participation-rate arithmetic of step 4 becomes transition management, where the whole problem is executing a large reallocation without paying the impact computed here.

Where the bank sits opposite. The stressed gap constructed in step 4 is the liquidity coverage ratio in miniature, with the same two ingredients, haircuts on assets and runoff rates on liabilities, and the thirty-day horizon it uses is the direct supervisory response to the six-month criterion the source applies. The maturity ladder is the bank's contractual maturity mismatch disclosure, and the requirement to report the minimum cumulative position rather than the net is why that disclosure is bucketed. The net stable funding ratio addresses the longer horizon the ladder's later buckets cover, and liquidity management treats intraday liquidity, which is a bucket shorter than anything in this lecture. Collateral outflows appear in the LCR as a specific line precisely because of the mechanism in step 4.

Numbers used here. The two definitions of liquidity risk, the gap formula, the six-month criterion and all three caveats, dealing costs, no impact of stress and the desirability of a positive gap, are from chapter 12 of the ASSA F105 summary in the user's notes, quoted as written. The square-root market impact law and the trade-off between impact and price risk follow Almgren and Chriss (2000), "Optimal Execution of Portfolio Transactions", Journal of Risk 3(2). The haircut and runoff construction follows the Basel III liquidity coverage ratio, where thirty days is the stress horizon. Everything numerical is invented for illustration: a seven-bucket ladder, five asset classes with spreads from 2 to 150 basis points and impact coefficients from 0.0010 to 0.1500, daily volumes and positions chosen so that participation spans four orders of magnitude, a 12 per cent property volatility, and a balance sheet of 240 assets against 230 liabilities. Fourteen claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions and every table was emitted by the computation and pasted without retyping. Five claims failed on the first run and all five were caused by my choice of illustrative figures rather than by the analysis. The ladder had been calibrated to a negative six-month gap, which made the central point unprovable; the impact tests clipped at full participation, which suspended the square-root law; the execution optimum sat at a boundary rather than an interior point; and the stressed gap test I had expected to fail passed by a wide margin. The last of those is reported as a pass in step 4 rather than re-engineered, and it led to the overlay-threshold analysis that replaced it.
