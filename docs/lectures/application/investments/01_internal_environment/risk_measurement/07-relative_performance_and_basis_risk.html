A hedge with a 0.95 correlation removes 90 per cent of the variance and leaves 31 per cent of the volatility

Hedge effectiveness is the squared correlation, and risk is measured in standard deviations, so the residual is the square root of one minus that. It is the most misread number in hedging. Then relative performance risk, where a manager one point ahead of the median needs 43 years to prove it, and where the control that removes the risk leaves every real exposure untouched and makes the industry fail together.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Two risk types share this lecture because both are about a comparison rather than a position. Basis risk is the risk that a hedge and the thing it hedges move differently. Relative performance risk is the risk of underperforming competitors. Only the second appears in syllabus objective (f); basis risk is examinable under the derivatives objectives instead.

Basis risk has a clean formula and a widely misread consequence. The minimum-variance hedge ratio is the correlation times the ratio of volatilities, and the residual variance is the spot variance times one minus the squared correlation. So a 0.95 correlation removes 90.25 per cent of the variance, which sounds excellent, and leaves 31.22 per cent of the volatility, which does not. At 0.90 the residual is 43.59 per cent. Risk is quoted in standard deviations and effectiveness in variances, and the two differ by a square root.

The source's three sources of basis risk each map to one term. Different pricing assumptions give a correlation below one, which is irreducible. An uncertain sale date means an early unwind at a non-zero basis, which is zero if the hedge is held to expiry and scales as the square root of the remaining term. And the need to close out means rolling, where forty quarterly rolls over ten years accumulate 6.32 times the basis risk of one.

Relative performance risk is harder to measure than anything else in the module. A manager genuinely one percentage point ahead of the peer median, with a four point tracking error against peers, needs 43 years to demonstrate it at 95 per cent confidence, and the peer median itself drifts upward as failing funds close: culling the worst 30 per cent lifts it by 0.79 percentage points, which is 79 per cent of the edge being sought. The source's control is to track the peers, which removes the measured risk, leaves the absolute risk at 15 per cent throughout, and raises the probability that all ten funds lose more than 10 per cent from 0.000105 to 25.25 per cent.

Feynman test A hedge that moves 95 per cent in step with your holding sounds nearly perfect and still leaves you a third as exposed as before, because the reassuring number is squared and the number you care about is not. Then the odder risk: losing to your competitors. You cannot measure it, since decades of results are needed to tell skill from luck and the comparison group quietly improves as the failures shut down. And the cure is to hold what everyone else holds, which works, changes nothing about how risky your fund actually is, and means that when things go wrong they go wrong for everybody at once.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the gap between variance effectiveness and volatility residual is a live source of overconfidence in hedging. A committee told a hedge is 90 per cent effective will not expect a third of the original risk to remain, and the two statements are the same statement. Quoting the residual volatility rather than the variance reduction costs nothing and removes the misunderstanding.

The second is that the three sources the summary lists are not three separate problems. Only the first, an imperfect correlation between hedge and exposure, is irreducible. The second and third are both consequences of unwinding before expiry, and both can be reduced by matching the contract to the exposure. Knowing which of the three you face determines whether anything can be done.

The third is that relative performance risk is close to unmeasurable, and the reasons compound. The signal-to-noise ratio requires decades, and the benchmark is biased upward by survivorship at roughly the same magnitude as the edge being measured. A peer-group comparison over any realistic period is therefore mostly measuring luck against a moving target.

And a fourth, which is the interesting one. The rational response to relative performance risk is to track the peer group, and it works: the tracking error falls to zero. It reduces no real exposure, because the absolute volatility is unchanged, and if every fund does it the industry's outcomes become perfectly correlated. Each fund is behaving correctly and the aggregate result is that a bad year is bad for everyone simultaneously.

Relative performance risk: the noise

Relative performance risk: the bias

The competitor data is not merely noisy, it is biased. Funds that perform badly close and are removed from the peer group, so the SURVIVING median sits above the median of all funds that ever existed. Simulate two hundred funds with random skill, observe five years, and remove the worst performers: funds closed surviving median minus true median as % of a 1% edge 0% 0.0000% 0.0% 10% 0.2561% 25.6% 20% 0.5182% 51.8% 30% 0.7912% 79.1% 50% 1.3883% 138.8% CULLING THE WORST 30 PER CENT LIFTS THE SURVIVING MEDIAN BY 0.79 PERCENTAGE POINTS, WHICH IS 79 PER CENT OF THE ONE-POINT EDGE THE MANAGER IS TRYING TO DEMONSTRATE. At a 50 per cent cull the bias exceeds the edge entirely. SO THE TWO PROBLEMS COMPOUND IN THE SAME DIRECTION. The signal needs decades to emerge from the noise, and over those decades the benchmark drifts upward by an amount comparable to the signal. A peer-group comparison over any realistic period is measuring luck against a moving target, which is what the source means by "competitor data unreliable" and is worse than that phrase suggests. THE THIRD DIFFICULTY THE SOURCE LISTS, ALLOWING FOR RISK, IS ALSO REAL AND IS LECTURE 2's PROBLEM. Peers running different absolute risk are not comparable, and adjusting for it requires choosing one of the six measures that lecture found to disagree.

Syllabus objective (f) lists six risk types and basis risk is not among them: asset-liability mismatching, market, credit, operational, liquidity and relative performance. Basis risk appears in the summary's risk control section and is examinable under the derivatives objectives, (o)(iv) to (o)(vi), where the uses of futures, options, forwards and swaps to an institutional investor are covered. THE LAST ROW IS THE DIFFERENCE THAT MATTERS ANALYTICALLY. Basis risk is a private problem: one fund's imperfect hedge affects that fund. Relative performance risk is not, because the rational individual response makes the system less diversified, and the cost of that falls on clients who were never party to the decision. THE FOUR STEPS, FOR BOTH RISKS. STEP 1, MEASURING. Basis risk is measured as the residual volatility after an optimal hedge, and should be quoted as a standard deviation. Relative performance risk is measured as a tracking error against a peer group, and the peer group is the problem. STEP 2, MODELLING. For basis risk, a correlation and two volatilities, which are estimable. For relative performance, peer returns, which are noisy, biased and lagged. STEP 3, REPORTING. The distinctive requirement for relative performance is that the comparison be fixed in advance, because a peer group chosen after the fact can be constructed to produce any answer. STEP 4, DEPARTURE LIMITS. For relative performance these are lecture 1's load ratios and load differences, which is the one risk type where they apply naturally, since they are defined against a benchmark. For basis risk the limit is on the residual, or more usually on the permitted instruments.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first thing to carry is the square root, because it is the difference between a hedge that sounds excellent and one that leaves a third of the exposure. Effectiveness is reported as a variance reduction, because that is what a regression R-squared produces, and exposure is experienced as a standard deviation. A 0.95 correlation removes 90.25 per cent of the first and leaves 31.22 per cent of the second. THE FIX IS TO QUOTE THE RESIDUAL VOLATILITY AND NEVER THE VARIANCE REDUCTION, which costs nothing and removes the misunderstanding permanently.

THE SAME HEDGE, DESCRIBED TWO WAYS. "this hedge is 90 per cent effective" true, in variance "a third of the risk remains" true, in volatility BOTH DESCRIBE rho = 0.95. the first will be approved and the second will be questioned, and the second is the one a committee needs.

The second is that the source's three sources of basis risk collapse into two. An imperfect correlation is irreducible and is the one thing a hedger cannot design away. The uncertain unwind date and the need to roll are the same problem, exposure to a non-zero basis before convergence, and both shrink to nothing if the contract can be held to expiry. So the diagnostic question about a basis exposure is whether the mismatch is in the INSTRUMENT or in the TIMING, because only the second is fixable.

The third is that relative performance risk is the least measurable quantity in the module, and the two obstacles push the same way. A one-point edge against a four-point peer tracking error needs 43 years to establish, and over any such period the peer median drifts upward through survivorship by an amount comparable to the edge: 0.79 points when the worst 30 per cent close. THE SIGNAL TAKES DECADES TO EMERGE AND THE TARGET MOVES AT THE SAME SPEED.

WHY THE TWO OBSTACLES COMPOUND RATHER THAN OFFSET. noise requires a long observation period to see a 1% edge bias accumulates over that same period, in the same direction A LONGER RECORD FIXES THE FIRST AND WORSENS THE SECOND. there is no observation length at which both are small, which is a stronger statement than "peer data is unreliable".

The fourth is the control, and I want to be careful to state it fairly. Tracking the peer group takes the relative risk to zero and leaves the absolute risk at 15 per cent, so it reduces the measured risk and no real exposure. THAT IS NOT AN OBJECTION. If a fund is judged against its peers then peer divergence is a genuine business risk, clients do leave when it runs the wrong way, and the control addresses precisely the risk it is aimed at. A fund that ignored relative performance risk on the grounds that it is not a real exposure would be making a category error about whose risk is being managed.

And the fifth, which is the reason the two risks belong in one lecture. Basis risk is private: one fund's imperfect hedge affects that fund. Relative performance risk is not, because the rational individual response is to converge on the peer group, and if everybody converges the probability that all ten funds lose more than ten per cent rises from 0.000105 to 25.25 per cent, a factor of 240,000. NO INDIVIDUAL FUND CAN ADDRESS THAT, because unilaterally diverging is exactly the risk the control exists to avoid. It is the clearest example in the module of a risk whose correct management at the level of the firm produces a worse outcome at the level of the system.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The four-step control framework and the load ratios and load differences that find their natural home in relative performance risk are from the risk control framework. The choice between absolute and relative measurement, and the finding that a relative basis makes the benchmark riskless by definition, is market risk, and the third of the source's difficulties, allowing for risk, is that lecture's problem of six disagreeing measures. Tracking error and its decomposition are from risk budgets, value at risk and tracking error, and the arithmetic relating an edge to the record length needed to demonstrate it is the fundamental law of arbitrage pricing theory and factor construction and the backtesting power calculation of lecture 10. Futures pricing, the convergence of the basis at expiry and the mechanics of rolling are from derivatives, and the minimum-variance hedge ratio is the regression slope from statistics.

Where this goes next. Lecture 8 completes the module with stress testing and scenario analysis, which is the response to a limitation running through all seven preceding lectures: every measure so far summarises an assumed distribution, and a scenario asks what happens outside it. The correlation result in step 4 is a natural stress scenario in itself, since a stress test that assumes funds behave independently will understate the industry-wide case. In the performance measurement module, the peer-group comparison analysed here is treated as one of the three benchmark choices, and the survivorship bias computed in step 4 is why the other two are usually preferred.

Where the bank sits opposite. Basis risk is the substance of a bank's market risk hedging, where the residual after hedging is explicitly capitalised rather than assumed away, and the standardised approach's prescribed correlations exist because an internally estimated correlation is the input this lecture shows the whole answer depends on. The rolling result explains why a bank funding a long asset with short liabilities faces the same accumulation, treated under IRRBB. The herding result has a direct supervisory analogue: a system in which every bank holds the same assets and runs the same models is the concern behind macroprudential policy, and it is why supervisors resist prescribing a single model as firmly as they resist permitting none. Peer comparison itself appears in supervisory benchmarking exercises, with the same survivorship caveat.
