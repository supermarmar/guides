Step 1

The 30-second version

The five earlier overview lectures looked inward at the investing institution. This one looks outward at the issuer, because the assets everything in this track buys are created by somebody's capital budgeting decision. The F105 summary covers it in chapter 4: four roles, five planning steps, three working-capital activities, three merger types and twelve merger reasons. Three of those statements are testable and all three fail.

Start with the seventh merger reason, "possible EPS increase". Take a firm rated at twenty times earnings buying one rated at ten, paying exactly market value in shares, with no synergy whatever. Earnings per share rises from 1.0000 to 1.2000, a gain of 20.00 per cent, while the combined earnings are exactly the sum of the parts and the shares issued are worth exactly the target's market value. Nothing was created. The rise happens if and only if the acquirer's rating exceeds the target's, is exactly unchanged when they are equal, and falls when the acquirer is rated lower. It is a statement about two ratings and not about two businesses, and the same deal reversed would cut EPS while creating exactly as much value, namely none.

The trick also exhausts itself, which explains a pattern usually attributed to management hubris. The merged rating is the value-weighted average of the two, so the available gain falls from 20.00 per cent on the first deal to 8.25 per cent on the fourth as the acquirer's rating converges towards its targets'.

Second, the summary's sharpest line: shareholders could achieve a similar result by adjusting their holdings. For a conglomerate merger that is not merely similar but identical. A conglomerate's volatility is 26.773121 per cent and a portfolio of the two firms in the same weights is 26.773121 per cent, a difference of 0.000000e+00, because a conglomerate is a portfolio. So the diversification benefit to a diversified shareholder is exactly zero, and worse than zero once a 25 per cent premium costs 125.0000, which is 6.25 per cent of the acquirer's own market value, for an exposure the shareholder could have assembled at market prices.

Applying that test to all twelve reasons, four fail: diversification and surplus funds because a shareholder can replicate them, the EPS increase because it is an artefact, and takeover protection because it benefits management rather than shareholders. The other eight are genuine candidates. The summary lists all twelve without distinguishing them.

Third, a plain factual error. The summary defines the principal-agent problem as "agent appoints principal". It is the other way round: the principal appoints the agent. And the summary is right that a merger has the greatest scope for it, because management pay rises 7.50 per cent on every deal since it depends on size alone, while shareholders gain only if the synergy exceeds the premium. The break-even synergy is exactly the premium as a fraction of the target, 25.00 per cent, verified as an identity. The agent's payoff is positive on every deal and the principal's on some, which is the structural reason mergers destroy value on average.

Step 2

Why this matters

Every asset in this track is a claim on something a company decided to build. The bonds priced in the valuation module fund somebody's capital budgeting decision, the equities analysed in the asset classes module are residual claims on those projects, and the returns the whole performance measurement module attributes are ultimately produced by them. So this lecture is where the supply side of the investment universe comes from, and an investor who cannot read a capital budgeting decision cannot assess the security that funds it.

It matters more directly because sections 4.1 to 4.3 give an investor three tests to apply to any announced acquisition. Does the claimed EPS improvement survive the observation that it follows mechanically from the two ratings? Could a shareholder replicate the stated benefit by adjusting holdings? Does the premium exceed the synergy? Those three questions dispose of most acquisition announcements, and they can be asked from the announcement itself without any private information.

The working capital arithmetic in section 4.4 matters for credit analysis rather than equity analysis. A firm's cash conversion cycle determines how much financing it needs before it earns anything, and section 4.4 shows the three policy levers being unequal by exactly the ratio of sales to cost of goods. That means a high-margin firm gains most from chasing its debtors and a low-margin one is nearly indifferent, which is a testable prediction about where management attention should sit and a diagnostic when it does not.

And section 4.6's covenant result is the one place in the whole overview sequence where a good project is correctly rejected. A positive net present value is not sufficient, because the borrowing required may breach an existing agreement, and the borrowing headroom is computable as the covenant multiple times EBITDA less existing debt. That is the financing decision constraining the investment decision, which is precisely what Modigliani and Miller's assumptions rule out and real balance sheets do not.

Step 3

The mental model

A firm is a collection of projects, and financial management is two decisions about them: which to own, and how to pay for them. The summary assigns the first to the CFO and the second to the treasurer, and that split is the whole organising idea.

A company is not a thing. It is a bundle of projects, each of which needs real assets and produces cash. Two questions follow, and they are genuinely separate.

Which projects? That is capital budgeting, and it is the CFO's. You forecast the cash, discount it, and keep the ones worth more than they cost. Long-term work, three to five years out, about fixed assets.

How do we pay for them? That is financing, and it is the treasurer's. Debt, equity, retained earnings. Shorter-term work, and in a famous idealisation it does not affect the answer to the first question at all, which is why the summary calls the treasurer subordinate.

Then a third thing that nobody owns and everybody needs: the cash to run the business between paying for stock and collecting from customers. Buy goods, hold them for six weeks, sell them, wait two months to be paid, and meanwhile pay your own suppliers in one month. The gap is money you must find before you have earned anything, and it is bigger than people expect.

Now mergers, and here is the trick to keep in your head. If a company trading at twenty times earnings buys one trading at ten, and pays with its own shares at market value, its earnings per share goes up. Every time. Even if the two businesses have nothing to offer each other, even if not a single cost is saved. The reason is that it is buying earnings for fewer shares than it already has per unit of earnings. That is not a benefit, it is division.

And the deepest point in the summary is one line: a shareholder could have done it themselves. If the only benefit claimed is diversification, then the shareholder simply buys both companies and gets exactly the same risk reduction, to the last decimal place, without paying a premium. A conglomerate is a portfolio whose manager charges you to assemble it and will not let you rebalance.

Formally, if firm A has earnings E_A on N_A shares at a rating P_A, and buys firm B by issuing shares at its own price, the merged earnings per share is (E_A + E_B) / (N_A + P_B E_B / (P_A E_A / N_A)), which exceeds E_A / N_A exactly when P_A > P_B. And the merged firm's rating is the value-weighted average of the two, which is why the effect erodes with repetition.

The detail

4.1 "Possible EPS increase" is an accounting artefact

The summary lists twelve reasons for a merger and the seventh is a possible increase in earnings per share. Take a firm rated at twenty times buying one rated at ten, paying exactly market value in its own shares, with no synergy of any kind.

   price-earnings ratio           20.0000    10.0000            see below
   market value                 2000.0000   500.0000            2500.0000

Earnings per share rises 20.00 per cent. The combined earnings and the combined market value are both exactly the sum of the parts.

Earnings per share rises from 1.0000 to 1.2000, a gain of 20.00 per cent, with the combined earnings exactly the sum of the parts and the 25.0000 shares issued worth exactly the target's 500.0000 market value. Nothing was created. The only thing that happened is that a high-rated firm bought a low-rated one's earnings cheaply in share terms.

   acquirer's P/E   target's P/E   merged EPS   change in EPS   did EPS rise?

Exactly unchanged when the ratings are equal, which identifies the mechanism precisely.

Earnings per share rises exactly when the acquirer's rating exceeds the target's, is exactly unchanged when they are equal, and falls when the acquirer is rated lower. It is a statement about two ratings and not about two businesses. So the summary lists as a reason for a merger a quantity that can be manufactured at will by choosing a lower-rated target, and which says nothing about whether the deal creates value: the same transaction run in reverse would reduce earnings per share while creating exactly as much value, namely none. The examinable position is the summary's twelve reasons as given; a complete answer flags the seventh as an artefact.

The gain shrinks from 20.00 per cent on the first deal to 8.25 on the fourth, because the merged rating is the value-weighted average of the two and converges towards the target's. That is why serial acquirers run out of road, and it is a better explanation of that pattern than any story about management overreach: the arithmetic that made the strategy work destroys its own precondition.

4.2 "Shareholders could achieve a similar result" is an exact identity

The summary's sharpest line on mergers is that shareholders could achieve a similar result by adjusting their holdings. For a conglomerate merger, whose stated purpose is diversification, that is not merely similar but identical.

A conglomerate against a portfolio of its parts

   what the shareholder does                        resulting volatility
   holds the merged conglomerate                              26.773121%
   holds both firms separately in the same weights            26.773121%
   the difference                                    0.000000e+00

Identical to machine precision, because a conglomerate is a portfolio and a portfolio is a portfolio.

The two are identical to machine precision at 26.773121 per cent, because a conglomerate is a portfolio of its divisions. The diversification benefit of a conglomerate merger to a diversified shareholder is exactly zero. And it is worse than zero once a premium is paid: a 25 per cent premium on a target worth 500.0000 costs 125.0000, which is 6.25 per cent of the acquirer's own market value, and buys an exposure the shareholder could have assembled at market prices.

The identity has a boundary worth stating, because the argument is often overextended. It applies to any benefit a shareholder can replicate by trading and to nothing else. Apply that test to all twelve of the summary's reasons.

Diversification and surplus funds fail because the shareholder can replicate them, the earnings per share increase fails because it is an artefact, and takeover protection fails because it benefits management rather than shareholders. The other eight are genuine candidates requiring evidence rather than assertion. Presenting all twelve as a flat list is the summary's characteristic compression, and here it puts four items that settle nothing alongside eight that could.

4.3 The summary states the principal-agent problem backwards

The summary defines the principal-agent problem as "agent appoints principal, conflict of interest". That is the wrong way round. The principal appoints the agent, and the problem is that the appointed party acts in its own interest with the appointer's money.

Four principal-agent relationships in this course

   the relationship        the principal    the agent      what the agent controls
   a company               shareholders     management     the whole firm's assets
   a pension fund          beneficiaries    trustees       the whole fund
   an investment mandate   the trustees     the manager    the portfolio
   a merger                shareholders     the acquiring  whether the deal happens
                                            board          at all

In every row the principal appoints the agent and not the reverse. The summary's wording should be read as a transcription slip.

The examinable definition is that a principal delegates a decision to an agent whose interests differ, and the summary's wording is best read as a transcription slip rather than a conceptual error, since everything else it says about the problem is correct. It is worth correcting because a candidate who reproduces the phrase has said something false about the direction of the relationship, which an examiner will notice.

The summary is right that a merger has the greatest scope for the problem, and the reason is quantifiable. Management pay rises with size and shareholder value rises with synergy, and those are different quantities.

Management pay rises 7.50 per cent on every deal because it depends on size alone, and shareholders lose unless the synergy exceeds the premium. The break-even synergy is exactly the premium as a fraction of the target, 25.00 per cent, verified as an identity. So the agent's payoff is positive on every deal and the principal's on some, which is the structural reason mergers destroy shareholder value on average. That is not a claim about any particular management's competence, which is what makes it a governance argument rather than a criticism.

The summary's short-term planning section lists three activities and no arithmetic. The arithmetic is the cash conversion cycle, which turns three operating policies into one financing number.

The cycle is 75 days and the funding need is 193.1507, which is 19.32 per cent of annual sales. That is cash the firm must raise before it earns anything, and it is the summary's own phrase "capital is needed for the project and the income only comes later" made numerical. It is also why a fast-growing profitable firm can fail: growth increases the cycle's absolute size before it increases the cash coming back.

The ratio is exactly sales over cost of goods, 1.4286, verified as an identity. So a high-margin firm gains most from chasing its debtors and a low-margin one is nearly indifferent between the three levers, which is a testable statement about where management attention should sit.

4.5 The inventory trade-off the summary lists without resolving

The summary's stock management item names considerations pulling in opposite directions: buffer stock, unforeseen demand and bulk buying being cheaper, against the opportunity cost, storage costs, spoilage and obsolescence. Resolve it, because the resolution is a single formula.

Total cost against order size, on 12,000 units a year

   order size   orders a year   ordering cost   holding cost   total cost
          200           60.00       9000.0000       400.0000    9400.0000
          500           24.00       3600.0000      1000.0000    4600.0000
          949           12.64       1896.7334      1898.0000    3794.7334
         1500            8.00       1200.0000      3000.0000    4200.0000
         3000            4.00        600.0000      6000.0000    6600.0000

Not monotone. The minimum is at 948.6833 units, where the ordering and holding costs are exactly equal.

The total cost falls and then rises, so there is an interior optimum, and it is the square root of twice demand times the order cost over the holding cost: 948.6833 units. At that point the ordering cost and the holding cost are exactly equal, at 1897.3666 each. That equality is the optimum's signature and a useful check: a firm whose ordering cost differs from its holding cost is not at the optimum, whichever way the difference runs, and it does not need to know the formula to notice.

Seven considerations, two terms. Six belong to the holding cost and one to the ordering cost.

Seven considerations reduce to two terms, which is a better way to hold the material than a list and makes the direction of each effect unambiguous.

Inventory therefore does not scale with sales, so a growing firm's stock falls as a percentage of turnover with no improvement in management at all. That square-root economy is one of the few items on the summary's merger list, "economies of scale", with an actual formula behind it, and it is worth knowing that the formula gives a weaker effect than the phrase suggests: quadrupling on a sixteenfold increase is a real saving and not a transformative one.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Three habits for reading a corporate announcement, and the first takes about a minute.

Ask what a shareholder could have done alone. Section 4.2's identity is the most useful single test in corporate finance because it is cheap and it disposes of a whole category of claim. Every announced benefit falls into two boxes: things requiring the two businesses to be under one roof, and things a shareholder could arrange by trading. The first box needs evidence and the second needs no discussion. Diversification, surplus cash deployment and earnings per share arithmetic all fall in the second box, and between them they account for a large fraction of what gets said in acquisition announcements. Applying the test does not require an opinion on the businesses.

Read the earnings per share claim as information about the ratings, not the deal. Section 4.1 makes the arithmetic mechanical, and the professional use of that is diagnostic rather than sceptical. If an acquirer emphasises earnings accretion, it is telling you that it is rated above its target, which is a fact about how the market views the two businesses and is often the most informative thing in the announcement. It also tells you the strategy has a limited life, since section 4.1's erosion table shows the available gain falling from 20.00 to 8.25 per cent across four deals. A serial acquirer whose rating has converged on its targets' has nothing left, and that is visible in advance.

For a credit view, compute the covenant headroom before the net present value. Section 4.6 inverts the usual order and the inversion is the practitioner's version. A firm with 100.0000 of headroom cannot undertake a project needing 200.0000 however attractive it is, so the headroom determines the opportunity set and the appraisal only ranks within it. That is also where the working capital arithmetic of section 4.4 becomes a credit question rather than an operational one: a lengthening cash conversion cycle consumes the same headroom the project needed, so a firm can lose its investment capacity without making any investment decision at all.

On the summary, three things are recorded. Its definition of the principal-agent problem is stated backwards and should read that the principal appoints the agent. Its twelve merger reasons include four that do not justify a merger, and it presents all twelve alike. And its "possible EPS increase" is an accounting consequence of the two ratings rather than a reason to do anything, which the summary could hardly be expected to develop in a bullet but which a candidate must not reproduce as a benefit. None of the twelve items is misdescribed. The list is simply flat where the material is not.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The chain that connects the issuer to the investor is in the business model, whose investment bank material is the other half of the issuer's relationship with the market, and the principal-agent problem quantified here is the same one priced in governance, trustees and delegation. The diversification identity in section 4.2 rests on the portfolio arithmetic in portfolio risk and return, and the appraisal measures in step 4 are built in fixed income pricing and the wider valuation module. The ratio analysis that reads a firm's working capital position is in fundamental analysis and ratios, and the covenant constraint of section 4.6 is the borrower's side of the credit assessment in private debt.

Where this goes next. This lecture completes the six overview lectures and with them the whole internal environment, so the investments track's remaining work is the external environment: the regulators, tax authorities, market infrastructure, ratings agencies and economic conditions the investing institution must react to rather than control. Section 4.1's bootstrap result feeds into the external environment's treatment of accounting standards, since the earnings figure the arithmetic operates on is defined by a standard rather than by nature. And section 4.3's agency finding connects to the market conduct and competition material, since merger regulation exists largely because the private incentive and the public interest diverge in the way this lecture computes.

Where the bank sits opposite. The bank appears on both sides of this lecture. As an adviser it earns a fee for the merger whose value section 4.3 shows to be negative for shareholders on most deals, which is the clearest conflict in investment banking and is why advisory conduct is regulated separately from lending, treated alongside the conduct exposures in operational risk. As a lender it writes the covenant of section 4.6 and monitors the working capital of section 4.4, which is the substance of the credit assessment set out in credit risk measurement, where the leverage ratio and the cash conversion cycle are both standard inputs. And a bank runs the same front, middle and back office separation the summary requires, where the middle office it does not mention is the risk function that grew out of exactly the failures that separation was meant to prevent, covered in risk management.

Three findings are recorded against it, with the examinable position stated alongside each. First, the summary defines the principal-agent problem as "agent appoints principal", which is the wrong way round: the principal appoints the agent, and the examinable definition is that a principal delegates a decision to an agent whose interests differ. Second, its seventh merger reason, a possible increase in earnings per share, is an accounting consequence of the two price-earnings ratios rather than a reason to transact, since it appears with no synergy at all, is exactly unchanged when the ratings are equal, and reverses when the acquirer is rated lower. Third, four of its twelve merger reasons do not justify a merger: diversification and surplus funds because a shareholder can replicate them exactly, the earnings per share increase because it is an artefact, and takeover protection because it benefits management. The other eight are genuine candidates and the summary lists all twelve alike. Everything numerical is invented for illustration: two firms with earnings of 100 and 50 on 100 shares each at ratings from 8 to 20 times, a 25 per cent acquisition premium, volatilities of 30 and 40 per cent at a 0.20 correlation, a management pay elasticity of 0.30 to size, sales of 1000 against cost of goods of 700 with debtor, stock and creditor days of 60, 45 and 30, annual demand from 3,000 to 192,000 units with an order cost of 150 and a holding cost of 4, and EBITDA of 200 against debt of 500 with a three times covenant. Twenty-six claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions, all twenty-six passed, and none can pass without the computation. One claim was flagged by a vacuity check as comparing a quantity with itself and was split into two claims that each test something, and one was flagged as unfalsifiable and removed. Three display defects were corrected before publication: an earnings per share change of exactly zero was being reported as a fall rather than as unchanged, a market value column was too narrow for its values, and one table header carried a literal double percentage sign. One prose figure was also wrong and was corrected against the script, since the ordering and holding costs at the exact optimum are 1897.3666 rather than the 1896.7334 visible in the sampled row. Every headline finding states the region in which it holds: the bootstrap effect requires the acquirer to be rated above the target and erodes with repetition, the diversification identity holds exactly for any benefit a shareholder can replicate by trading and not otherwise, the agency result holds whenever management pay depends on size, the debtor lever's advantage is exactly the ratio of sales to cost of goods, and the inventory optimum scales with the square root of demand.
