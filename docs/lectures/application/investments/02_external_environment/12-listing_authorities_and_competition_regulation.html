The over-subscription rule looks administrative and it sets the issue price

Under pro-rata allocation an uninformed investor receives 40 shares when the issue is good and 100 when it is bad, so the break-even price falls to 95.714286 and the issue must be discounted 4.2857 per cent. Change the allocation rule and the discount ranges from nothing to the whole gap. Along the way: the summary's product definition clause has the sign reversed, its import defence is true of the merger's contribution and false of the concentration level, and a rate-of-return cap makes profit exactly proportional to the asset base with no turning point anywhere.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Lecture 11 found that only about 2 per cent of investment flows are primary issuance, and that primary issuance is the only route by which an investor's money reaches a company. This lecture covers where that 2 per cent happens and how it is priced, plus the competition rules that decide whether a company may buy another one.

The summary lists "process if over-subscribed" as one of nine prospectus items, which reads as an administrative detail. It sets the price. Split investors into informed, who bid only for good issues, and uninformed, who bid always. Under pro-rata allocation the uninformed are crowded out of good issues and filled in full on bad ones: with informed demand of 150 against 100 shares, they receive 40 shares when the issue is good and 100 when it is bad. Their break-even price is therefore 95.714286 against a fair value of 100, so the issue must be discounted 4.2857 per cent. Underpricing a new issue is not a mistake and not a favour, it is the price of keeping uninformed money in the market. And it is entirely set by the allocation rule: a rule allocating the same number of shares in both states removes the discount completely, and a rule filling the informed first prices the issue at the bad-state value of 90.0000.

On competition, the summary says narrow product areas create a lower share of concentration. That has the sign reversed for the level. Narrowing the market from eight firms to three raises the Herfindahl index from 1800.0000 to 3609.4675, and raises the merger's own effect from 1200.0000 to 2840.2367. The direction the summary describes can hold for a merging pair's own share within a segment where they are minor players, and even there the level rises. The examinable position is that a narrow definition raises measured concentration, which is why merging parties argue for the broadest market they can defend.

The import defence splits the same way. The merger's own contribution to concentration falls monotonically with imports, from 2840.2367 to 27.1355, and is identical whether the imports come from two large firms or ten small ones, because the change depends only on the merging pair's shares. The level does not: with two large importers it falls to 2825.2551 and then rises to 4131.9464, because the importers become the concentration. Only fragmented imports reduce concentration in the sense a regulator cares about.

Finally, monopoly price control. Under a rate-of-return cap the firm's profit is exactly the allowed margin times its capital base, 2.0000 per cent of it here, so ten times the capital earns ten times the profit with no turning point anywhere. Under a price cap the firm keeps the whole of any cost saving and the consumer keeps exactly one less the sharing factor, so the two objectives are in strict opposition.

Step 2

Why this matters

Two of this lecture's three results overturn a plain reading of the summary, and in both cases the plain reading is the one a candidate would give under exam pressure. The product definition clause states a direction that is wrong for the quantity regulators actually measure, and the over-subscription item is buried in a list of nine as though its position there were arbitrary. Getting either wrong in an exam is a lost mark; getting the first wrong in practice is a lost merger case.

The IPO result also has direct investment content. It says a new issue's discount is a function of who else is bidding and how the book is allocated, not of the issuer's generosity or the bank's incompetence. An investor deciding whether to subscribe should therefore ask what the allocation rule is before asking what the company is worth, because the rule determines whether the discount on offer compensates them for the adverse selection they face. A fixed retail tranche removes the adverse selection entirely and, on section 4.1's arithmetic, is the arrangement that lets the company sell at the highest price.

The price control material matters for anybody valuing a regulated utility, which in a South African context means most of the large infrastructure exposures available. Section 4.3 shows the two regimes have opposite incentive properties, so the same company under the same demand is a different investment depending on which regime it faces, and the regime is a policy variable rather than a fact about the business.

The Feynman version. A company is selling a hundred shares and you cannot tell whether they are worth 110 or 90. Some other bidders can tell. When the shares are worth 110 those clever bidders pile in, so there are 250 bids for 100 shares and you get 40. When the shares are worth 90 they stay at home, so you are the only bidder and you get all 100.

Count your money. You get a small helping of the good stuff and a full plate of the bad. Averaged out you are worse off than the shares are worth, so you would not bid at all unless the price came down. How far down? Exactly far enough that your small good helpings and your large bad ones break even, which here is 95.71 instead of 100. That is the whole explanation of why new issues are cheap, and it says the discount is decided by how the shares are handed out rather than by anybody's kindness.

Step 3

The mental model

Both halves of this lecture are about the same thing: a regulator choosing a rule, and the rule determining a number that everybody then argues about as though it were a fact. The listing authority chooses an allocation rule, and that rule determines the issue discount. The competition authority chooses a market definition, and that choice determines the concentration measure. The price regulator chooses a cap formula, and that formula determines whether the firm over-invests or under-invests.

Hold each as a rule-to-number map and the three become one topic. The important consequence is that arguing about the number is arguing about the rule, which is why merger litigation is almost entirely about market definition and almost never about arithmetic. Once the market is defined, the Herfindahl index follows mechanically and both sides can compute it.

The second organising idea is a distinction between a level and a change. Competition tests use both, and section 4.2 shows they move differently: the level depends on the whole structure of the market and the change depends only on the merging parties. That distinction resolves both of the summary's competition clauses, because each turns out to be true of one and false of the other.

The detail

4.1 The over-subscription rule sets the issue price

The summary lists the process if over-subscribed among nine prospectus items. Split investors into informed, who bid only for issues worth more than the price, and uninformed, who bid always. Under pro-rata allocation the uninformed are crowded out of good issues and filled in full on bad ones.

   informed demand   uninformed get   uninformed get   break-even    discount to
   when good          when good        when bad         issue price   fair value
   0.0000                     100.0000         100.0000    100.000000       0.0000%
   50.0000                     66.6667         100.0000     98.000000       2.0000%
   150.0000                    40.0000         100.0000     95.714286       4.2857%
   400.0000                    20.0000         100.0000     93.333333       6.6667%

With no informed demand the issue prices at fair value and needs no discount. With informed demand of 150 the uninformed receive 40.0000 shares when the issue is good and 100.0000 when it is bad, so the break-even price falls to 95.714286 and the issue must be discounted 4.2857 per cent. The required discount rises with informed demand, reaching 6.6667 per cent at 400.

So the underpricing of a new issue is not a mistake by the investment bank and not a gift to favoured clients. It is the price of keeping uninformed money in the market, and it is set by the allocation rule the summary lists as an administrative item. Which is why a listing authority requires the rule to be published in advance rather than merely applied fairly: an investor cannot compute a fair price without it, so the disclosure is a pricing input rather than a procedural safeguard.

The region is the part that makes the result useful, because the discount depends on the rule itself.

   the allocation rule            uninformed get   uninformed get   break-even
                                  when good        when bad         price
   pro rata across all bids                40.0000         100.0000     95.714286
   uninformed given priority              100.0000         100.0000    100.000000
   a fixed retail tranche of 40            40.0000          40.0000    100.000000
   informed given priority                  0.0000         100.0000     90.000000

A rule that allocates the same number of shares in both states removes the discount entirely, pricing at 100.000000, whether that is achieved by giving the uninformed priority or by a fixed tranche. A rule that fills the informed first prices the issue at 90.000000, the bad-state value. The entire range from no discount to a total one is set by one prospectus line, and the fixed retail tranche common in real offers is the rule that makes the issue cheapest for the company to sell. That is a striking result about a device usually presented as a fairness measure.

4.2 The product definition clause has the sign reversed for the level

The summary says narrow product areas create a lower share of concentration. Market definition is the single most consequential discretionary choice in competition law, so check the direction. Take two specialists merging, each selling only in a narrow segment.

   the definition           firms   HHI before   HHI after   the change   would a
                            counted the merger   the merger   in HHI       regulator
                                                                          intervene?
   broad: the whole market        8    1800.0000   3000.0000    1200.0000   YES
   narrow: the segment only       3    3609.4675   6449.7041    2840.2367   YES

For two specialists the narrow definition gives an HHI of 3609.4675 against 1800.0000 broad, and the merger's own effect rises from 1200.0000 to 2840.2367. So narrowing raises concentration, which is the opposite of what the summary's clause says. Now the case where the summary's direction might hold: two diversified firms merge, and we look at one segment where both are minor and a local specialist dominates.

   the definition           HHI before   HHI after   the change   would a regulator
                            the merger   the merger   in HHI       intervene?
   broad: all five segments    1800.0000   3000.0000    1200.0000   YES
   narrow: this segment only    6962.5000   7112.5000     150.0000   no

The narrow HHI is 6962.5000, higher still because a local specialist dominates, and yet the merger's own effect is only 150.0000 against 2840.2367 in the first case. So the two quantities move differently: narrowing always raises the level and can either raise or lower the change, depending on whether the merging firms are the segment's leaders.

The summary's clause is therefore false about the level of concentration and can be true about a merging pair's own share. The examinable position is that a narrow definition raises measured concentration, which is why merging parties argue for the broadest market they can defend and the regulator argues for the narrowest. That dispute is the whole of most merger cases, and the summary points at it with the sign reversed.

The summary's multinational clause is the same arithmetic in geography rather than product. Widen the market to include importers and see what happens.

   0.0000             6449.7041   2840.2367    6449.7041   2840.2367

Two things fall out. The merger's own contribution falls monotonically from 2840.2367 to 27.1355 and is identical under both import structures, because the change depends only on the merging pair's own shares and not on how the rest of the market is arranged. The level does not: with two large importers it falls to 2825.2551 and then rises back to 4131.9464, because the importers become the concentration, while with ten fragmented importers it falls all the way to 875.6855.

So the import defence is a defence about the merger's own contribution and not about the level of concentration. Opening a market to two large foreign firms replaces domestic concentration with foreign concentration. The summary's clause is true of the change and false of the level, which is the same split its product definition clause turned out to have, and that recurrence is the real lesson: every competition claim has to name which of the two measures it is about.

And the change test is demanding. Clearing a 200-point threshold takes 1799.4897 of imports against a domestic market of 650.0000, which is 2.7684 times as large, because the change is twice the product of the two merging shares and so falls as the square of any dilution while the level falls only linearly.

4.3 A rate-of-return cap rewards over-investment exactly

The summary's monopoly regulation item is controlling pricing policies for newly privatised utilities and telecommunications. There are two ways to do it with opposite incentive properties. Take the older one: allow the firm its operating costs plus a return on its capital.

   the firm's capital   allowed revenue   the firm's true   the firm's       return on
   base                 at cost plus 8%   cost of capital  economic profit  extra capital
   500.0000                      240.0000         230.0000          10.0000        2.0000%
   1000.0000                     280.0000         260.0000          20.0000        2.0000%
   2000.0000                     360.0000         320.0000          40.0000        2.0000%
   5000.0000                     600.0000         500.0000         100.0000        2.0000%

The firm's profit is exactly the allowed margin of 2.0000 per cent times its capital base. Ten times the capital earns ten times the profit, 100.0000 against 10.0000, with no turning point anywhere. So a regulated firm allowed a return above its cost of capital will build as much as the regulator permits, which is gold plating and is the exact reason the rate-of-return approach was abandoned.

   allowed return   true cost of   the margin   profit on 2000   the firm wants to
           5.0000%         6.0000%     -1.0000%         -20.0000   shrink the base
           6.0000%         6.0000%      0.0000%           0.0000   do nothing, exactly
           8.0000%         6.0000%      2.0000%          40.0000   EXPAND the base

Below the true cost the firm wants to shrink its asset base and stops investing, which is the opposite failure and worse for the consumer. A search locates the zero exactly at the true cost of capital, so the regulator is aiming at a point rather than a range and misses in one direction or the other by construction. That is the whole of regulatory practice: the allowed return is contested precisely because being slightly wrong either way produces a systematic distortion.

The modern alternative caps the price and lets the firm keep what it saves.

   the regime                  sharing   the firm keeps   the consumer   the firm's
                               factor s  of a 50 saving   receives       incentive
   pure cost plus                 0.0000           0.0000        50.0000   none at all
   sliding scale, quarter         0.2500          12.5000        37.5000   partial
   sliding scale, half            0.5000          25.0000        25.0000   partial
   a fixed price cap              1.0000          50.0000         0.0000   total

Under pure cost plus the firm keeps 0.0000 of a 50 saving and has no reason to make one. Under a fixed cap it keeps all 50.0000 and every reason. The consumer's share is exactly one less the sharing factor at every setting, so the two objectives are in strict opposition and a sliding scale is a chosen point on that line rather than a compromise that escapes it.

And a fixed cap transfers all cost risk to the firm, so a firm facing an unexpected cost rise has the same incentive to cut quality that it had to cut cost. The sharing factor buys cost discipline with quality risk, one for one, which is why price control always arrives with service standards attached.

That connects two prospectus items the summary lists separately, and it is the mechanism behind most of what is criticised about IPO allocation.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The single most useful habit from this lecture is to ask, of any concentration figure, whether it is a level or a change and what market it was computed on. Section 4.2 shows both of the summary's competition clauses are true of one measure and false of the other, and that is not a peculiarity of the summary: it is the general condition of competition analysis. A merger cleared in one jurisdiction and blocked in another usually reflects a different market definition rather than different economics, and the arithmetic is identical once the definition is fixed.

On new issues, the practitioner's version of section 4.1 is that you should read the allocation section of a prospectus before the financials. If the offer has a guaranteed retail tranche, the adverse selection problem has been engineered away and you should expect little discount, which means you are being asked to pay approximately fair value for a company you know less about than the institutions do. If allocation is discretionary and pro rata, expect a discount and treat it as compensation rather than as an opportunity. Either way the discount is not free money, and the persistent finding that IPOs underperform over the following years is consistent with the discount being exactly what the arithmetic says: compensation for a known disadvantage, not an anomaly.

On price control, the professional judgement is that section 4.3's sharing factor is the number that determines whether a regulated utility is an equity investment or a bond substitute. At a low sharing factor the firm's cash flows are almost contractual and its equity behaves like debt. At a high one the firm bears cost and volume risk and its equity behaves like equity. Anybody valuing a utility on a peer multiple without checking the sharing factor is comparing two different instruments.

One limit worth stating on section 4.1. The two-state model with a fixed informed demand gives the clean 4.2857 per cent figure, and the real world has a continuum of quality and endogenous informed demand that itself responds to the discount. The structure survives: any allocation rule that gives the uninformed more of the bad issues than the good ones requires a discount, and the required discount rises with the informed presence. The specific number does not, and the model also ignores the underwriter's ability to withdraw an issue entirely, which is the other instrument in the same toolbox.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The 2 per cent primary share of flows that makes this lecture's subject matter the only route to real-world effect is established in ESG and responsible investment. The asymmetry result that underlies the winner's curse is derived in the principles of financial regulation, where the collapse threshold was exactly 2, and the same adverse selection appears there as the reason a market can fail to clear at all. The merger arithmetic this lecture regulates is in structure and financial planning, where four of twelve merger reasons justified nothing and EPS rose with no synergy. The equity issuance mechanics are in the asset class framework, the sector characteristics behind the utility and telecommunications examples are in sector classification, and the disclosure obligations that partially relieve a non-executive's information cap are the other side of corporate governance.

Where this goes next. Lecture 13 covers accounting standards, which determine whether the audited financial statements of section 4.4 mean the same thing across jurisdictions, and the summary's own IFRS aims claim that reliable data reduces information asymmetry and therefore the cost of capital, which is section 4.1's mechanism running in reverse. Lecture 14 covers market participants and infrastructure, where the investment banks the summary names as leading the issuing process are described directly, and the six indices lectures then close the module. Section 4.2's concentration arithmetic reappears there as the reason a cap-weighted index in a concentrated market is itself a concentration decision.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank meets all three of this lecture's mechanisms from the other side. It is the underwriter of section 4.4, so the put option it writes is a real exposure on its own balance sheet and is capitalised as such: an unsold underwritten position is a market risk exposure, treated in market risk, and the fee it charges is the option premium this lecture prices. It also holds the allocation discretion of section 4.1, which is why the conflict of interest machinery of lecture 9 applies to it with particular force: the same institution advises the issuer on price and decides which investors receive the discount. Bank mergers additionally face a second regulator beyond the competition authority, because concentration in banking is a financial stability question as well as a pricing one, which is the systemic surcharge framework in G-SIBs and TLAC: a bank can be too concentrated to permit even where the competition arithmetic of section 4.2 clears it.

Everything numerical is invented for illustration: an issue of 100 shares worth 110 or 90 with informed demand from zero to 400, markets of eight, three and five firms with sales from 30 to 400 and import volumes to 6000, allowed returns of 5 to 8 per cent against a 6 per cent cost of capital on capital bases of 500 to 5000, sharing factors from zero to one on a 50 saving, and failure probabilities of 2 to 20 per cent against shortfalls of 100 to 300 on a 1000 issue. Twenty-seven claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions, all twenty-seven passed, and none can pass without the computation. Four claims failed during checking. One asserted that adding importers clears the merger, which it does not on the change test. Two asserted the concentration level falls monotonically with imports; it does not when the importers are themselves few, and pursuing that produced the finding that the level is U-shaped while the change is not. The fourth was the counterexample built on the same wrong premise. One claim was also flagged as a self-comparison by a vacuity check and was rewritten as a bisection search that locates the zero at the true cost of capital. Every headline finding states the region in which it holds: the IPO discount assumes two states and exogenous informed demand, and generalises in structure rather than in magnitude; the concentration results hold for any share distribution, with the change depending only on the merging pair and the level on the whole market; and the price control results are exact for any allowed return, with the incentive vanishing precisely at the true cost of capital.
