Step 1

The 30-second version

This lecture opens the module's regulation arc, and it takes the summary's regulation section as its whole subject: four aims, a list of direct and indirect costs, four types ordered by degree, and three forms. The list is complete and the arithmetic underneath it is not stated, so this lecture supplies the arithmetic. Three results and one warning about the ordering.

The summary calls information asymmetry the biggest cause of market failure. That is Akerlof's result and it has an exact condition. When a seller knows quality and a buyer does not, the sellers who offer at any price are those who value their goods below that price, so the average quality offered is half the price whatever the price is. The market therefore survives only if the buyer values the goods at more than twice the seller's valuation. The threshold is exactly 2, it is the same at every price, and below it the loss is the entire available gain from trade rather than a fraction of it. Worse for a regulator: the absolute loss is largest just below the cliff, at 45000.0000 against 10000.0000 at the bottom of the range, so the markets with most to lose are the ones closest to surviving unaided.

The summary lists protecting consumers as its first aim and lists moral hazard, meaning changes in behaviour and less care in choices, among the costs of the same activity. Those cancel, and the point at which they cancel is computable. For a depositor with 1000 of wealth facing a possible loss of 100 at a 5 per cent failure rate, protection pays only if it raises that rate by less than 0.2542 percentage points, which is barely a twentieth in relative terms. A doubling of the failure rate destroys the case on a loss of 100 and preserves it on a loss of 900, so the same policy is right for one exposure and wrong for another under identical behaviour.

The summary lists barriers to entry and reduced competition among the indirect costs. Compliance cost is largely fixed while revenue is proportional to assets, so the minimum viable firm size is the compliance cost divided by the fee rate, exactly. At a 0.75 per cent fee, three million of compliance cost excludes every firm below four hundred million of assets. Across ten firms a five million cost removes six of them and leaves 92.2156 per cent of the assets still served, which is the whole mechanism of the summary's clause in two numbers: the money is fine, the clients of the small firms are not.

Finally, the summary presents its four types in order of increasing degree of regulation, which reads like a ranking and is not one. Add the direct cost to the expected cost of failures and the total is minimised at neither end. Where a failure costs 10 the unregulated market wins outright and where it costs 500 statutory regulation does, so the four types are a menu matched to the stakes.

Step 2

Why this matters

Everything in this module so far has treated the rules as fixed. Lecture 4 of the internal environment worked inside Regulation 28's limits and found that only the 2.5 per cent bucket binds, and that the caps interact so every point of offshore equity removes a point of local capacity. Lecture 3 worked inside the trustee's duties. Lecture 6 worked inside the tax code. In each case the question was what an investor should do given the rules.

This lecture asks where the rules come from and what they cost. That matters for a practical reason and an examinable one. The practical reason is that the compliance cost of section 4.3 is one of the six links in the fee chain that lecture 1 of the internal environment found takes 52.08 per cent of a forty-year outcome, and it is the only link nobody in the chain chose. The examinable reason is that the summary's list is the standard framework and its own internal tensions are the obvious source of a discussion question: it wants consumer protection and lists reduced consumer protection mechanisms as a cost, and it presents four types in an order that is not a ranking.

The asymmetry result also has direct investment content that outruns the regulation topic. It is the reason a market can have no price at which anybody trades even though every participant would gain from trading, which is the sharpest available statement of what a liquidity crisis is. When section 4.1's multiple falls below 2 in a market that was previously above it, the market does not become expensive. It stops.

The Feynman version. Imagine a hundred people each selling a used car, and each knows exactly what their own car is worth to them. You are a buyer who cannot tell one car from another, so you can only offer one price. Whatever price you name, the only people willing to sell are those whose cars are worth less than that to them, which means the cars you are offered average about half your price. So your offer is always about twice what you are getting.

The only escape is that a car is worth more to you than to its owner, because you need one and they do not. The question is how much more. If a car is worth 2.5 times to you what it is worth to them, then averaging half is still fine, and you trade happily. If it is worth 1.5 times, then half of 1.5 is 0.75, you are paying 1 for 0.75, and you walk away. Everybody walks away. Nothing is sold, even though every single pairing would have made both people better off. That is the failure the summary is pointing at, and the dividing line is not a matter of degree: it is the number 2.

Step 3

The mental model

Hold the whole regulation topic as a single sum with two terms that move in opposite directions. One term is what the machinery costs, which is the summary's direct costs plus its barriers to entry. The other term is what failures cost, which is what the aims are trying to reduce. Every question in this lecture is which of the two terms dominates in a particular market, and the answer is different in every market because the size of a failure is different in every market.

That framing does three things at once. It makes the summary's four types comparable, because each is a point on the trade-off rather than a rung on a ladder. It explains why the aims and the costs overlap so awkwardly, because moral hazard is the machinery term raising the failure term. And it tells you what question to ask about any real rule: not whether it achieves its aim, but whether the reduction in failures exceeds what the machinery costs, which is an empirical question with a computable break-even.

Asymmetry sits underneath all of it as the reason the failure term exists at all. If information were symmetric the buyer would price each product on its merits and no market would unravel. Every one of the summary's four aims is downstream of asymmetry: a consumer needs protecting because they cannot assess the product, crime is possible because the counterparty cannot see it, and confidence collapses because nobody can tell a solvent bank from an insolvent one during a run.

Step 4

The detail

4.1 Asymmetry, and the threshold of exactly 2

The summary's fourth aim is to correct inefficiencies, and it names information asymmetry as the biggest cause of market failure. Take a market in which each seller knows their own product's quality and no buyer does. A seller values their product at v, sells only above v, and the product is worth m x v to a buyer. At any price p, the sellers who offer are those with v below p, so the average quality offered is p / 2.

   the buyer's   value of the   average quality   what it is   does the market
   multiple m    offer at p=60  offered, p/2      worth: m p/2  clear at 60?
   1.20                 60.0000           30.0000       36.0000  NO, it unravels
   1.50                 60.0000           30.0000       45.0000  NO, it unravels
   2.00                 60.0000           30.0000       60.0000  exactly, at the margin
   2.50                 60.0000           30.0000       75.0000  yes
   3.00                 60.0000           30.0000       90.0000  yes

A search for the root confirms the threshold to twelve places at 2.000000000000, and it is the same threshold at prices of 10, 60, 250 and 1000, so the condition is scale free. The reason is that the average quality offered is half the price whatever the price is, so the price cancels out of the comparison entirely. Asymmetry therefore destroys any market in which the buyer's valuation is less than twice the seller's, which is most markets, and leaves untouched the markets where the gain from trade is enormous.

Now the size of the loss, which is the quantity that would justify a regulator.

   the buyer's   gain from trade   gain realised     the welfare loss   as a % of the
   multiple m    if fully known    under asymmetry   from asymmetry     available gain
   1.20                 10000.0000            0.0000         10000.0000       100.0000%
   1.50                 25000.0000            0.0000         25000.0000       100.0000%
   1.90                 45000.0000            0.0000         45000.0000       100.0000%
   2.00                 50000.0000        50000.0000             0.0000         0.0000%
   2.50                 75000.0000        75000.0000             0.0000         0.0000%

The loss is the entire available gain below the threshold and zero above it, which makes it a cliff rather than a slope, and it is largest in absolute terms at 45000.0000 just below the threshold. That is the uncomfortable part: the markets with the most to lose from asymmetry are the ones closest to surviving without intervention.

And the discontinuity is the practical objection to the summary's fourth aim. A regulator cannot tell from observed prices whether a market sits just below the cliff or well above it, because in one case it sees no trades and in the other it sees trades at a fair price, and neither observation identifies the multiple. The aim names a real failure and supplies no test for its presence, which is why disclosure requirements are imposed across the board rather than where the asymmetry actually binds. An examiner asking why regulators favour blanket disclosure over targeted intervention is asking for exactly this.

4.2 Consumer protection and moral hazard are one mechanism, and they cancel quickly

The summary lists protecting consumers as its first aim. It then lists moral hazard, "changes in behaviour, less care in choices", among the indirect costs, and separately lists reduced consumer protection mechanisms as another. Those items are the same mechanism seen from two sides, and the point at which they cancel can be computed.

Take a depositor with wealth 1000 facing a possible loss of 100, who screens their bank when unprotected. Unprotected they bear the loss with probability p0. Protected they bear nothing but fund the guarantee through a levy or higher charges, and screening stops, so the probability rises to p1. Compare certainty equivalents under log utility.

   the loss   unprotected CE   the break-even p1   as a multiple of   the moral hazard
   at risk    at p0 = 5%       for protection      p0 = 5%            room available
   100.0000           994.7458            0.052542             1.0508x            0.2542%
   300.0000           982.3243            0.058919             1.1784x            0.8919%
   500.0000           965.9363            0.068127             1.3625x            1.8127%
   900.0000           891.2509            0.120832             2.4166x            7.0832%

On a loss of 100 the break-even is 0.052542 against a starting 0.0500, so protection pays only if it raises the failure probability by less than 0.2542 percentage points, a relative increase of about a twentieth. On a loss of 900 the room is 7.0832 points, or 2.4166 times the starting rate. The room widens with the loss at risk because risk aversion bites harder, which is the whole source of the benefit: the depositor is not gaining money, they are exchanging a variable loss for a certain levy.

Test a plausible behavioural response. Suppose removing the need to screen doubles the failure rate.

   the loss   p1 = 2 x p0   protected CE   unprotected CE   is protection worth it?
   100.0000          0.1000       990.0000         994.7458   NO
   300.0000          0.1000       970.0000         982.3243   NO
   500.0000          0.1000       950.0000         965.9363   NO
   900.0000          0.1000       910.0000         891.2509   yes

A doubling destroys the case on a loss of 100 and preserves it on a loss of 900. So the same protection is right for one exposure and wrong for another under identical behaviour, which is the argument for a capped guarantee rather than an unlimited one, and for the cap to be chosen by reference to the moral hazard response rather than to fairness.

Except that the arithmetic cuts the opposite way to every real scheme. The pure risk-aversion case for protection is strongest where the loss is largest, meaning the wholesale depositor, and real schemes cover small balances in full and large ones not at all. The reconciliation matters and is examinable: the large depositor can screen and the small one cannot, so the case for covering small balances is an asymmetry argument from section 4.1 rather than a risk-aversion argument from this section. The summary's first aim and its fourth are doing different work, and only one of them supports the schemes that actually exist.

4.3 A fixed compliance cost is not a deterrent, it is an exclusion

The summary lists barriers to entry, reduced competition and "less banking accessibility" among the indirect costs. The mechanism is that compliance cost is largely fixed, since a rulebook must be read once whatever the firm's size, while revenue is proportional to assets.

   annual compliance   the fee earned    minimum viable   as a multiple of the
   cost, millions      rate              assets, millions 100m starting firm
   0.0000                         0.7500%           0.0000               0.0000x
   1.0000                         0.7500%         133.3333               1.3333x
   3.0000                         0.7500%         400.0000               4.0000x
   5.0000                         0.7500%         666.6667               6.6667x

The minimum viable size is the compliance cost divided by the fee rate, exactly. A three million compliance cost at a 0.75 per cent fee excludes every firm below four hundred million of assets, four times a hundred million boutique. That is the summary's barrier to entry with a number attached, and the number changes the character of the claim: this is not a cost that deters marginal entrants, it is a threshold that removes an entire size class.

Now the consequence for the consumer the rules were written to protect. Take ten firms spread across sizes from 50 million to 9 billion.

   compliance cost   firms surviving   share of assets   the smallest firm
   millions          of ten            still served      left standing, m
   0.0000                           10         100.0000%            50.0000
   0.5000                            9          99.7006%            80.0000
   1.0000                            7          98.5030%           200.0000
   3.0000                            5          95.2096%           500.0000
   5.0000                            4          92.2156%           900.0000

A five million compliance cost removes six of the ten firms and leaves 92.2156 per cent of the assets still served. So the rules barely dent the industry's size while removing most of its firms, which is the summary's reduced competition and reduced accessibility in two numbers. The money is fine. The clients of the small firms are not, and they are the clients least able to go elsewhere.

The shape is the one lecture 6 found in bed and breakfasting: a fixed quantity meets a proportional one, so the break-even is a size rather than a rule, and the burden lands on the small participant. One measure was aimed at the taxpayer and the other at the consumer, and both land on the same person. That recurrence is worth carrying forward, because it predicts the incidence of any rule with a fixed cost of compliance before you know anything about the rule's content.

4.4 The four types are a menu matched to the stakes, not a ladder

The summary lists four types "in order of increasing degree of regulation": unregulated, voluntary codes of conduct, self-regulation and statutory regulation, with advantages and disadvantages for each. It says self-regulation is "the cheapest option" and statutory is "the most costly", and separately that statutory carries "a higher degree of public confidence". A total cost has both terms in it, so add them.

   the summary's type   direct cost   failure rate   expected failure   TOTAL societal
                        each year     each year      loss each year     cost
   unregulated                 0.0000        24.0000%            48.0000        48.0000
   voluntary codes             2.0000        18.0000%            36.0000        38.0000
   self-regulation             5.0000        10.0000%            20.0000        25.0000
   statutory                  20.0000         4.0000%             8.0000        28.0000

The total is minimised at self-regulation at 25.0000, which is interior to the summary's own ordering. The unregulated end costs 48.0000 because its failures are expensive and the statutory end costs 28.0000 because its machinery is. The summary's cheapest-option remark happens to survive here, and it survives for a reason the summary does not give: not because the direct cost is lowest, but because the sum of both terms is.

The ordering is not robust, and that is the point worth taking away. Vary the loss a failure causes and the winner moves.

   loss per failure   unregulated   voluntary   self-reg   statutory   which wins
   10.0000                   2.4000      3.8000     6.0000     20.4000   unregulated
   30.0000                   7.2000      7.4000     8.0000     21.2000   unregulated
   60.0000                  14.4000     12.8000    11.0000     22.4000   self-regulation
   200.0000                 48.0000     38.0000    25.0000     28.0000   self-regulation
   500.0000                120.0000     92.0000    55.0000     40.0000   statutory

Where a failure costs 10 the unregulated market wins outright and where it costs 500 statutory regulation does. So the four types are matched to the stakes rather than ranked, and the summary's own first row supports this: a market with only professionals is useful, and good decisions are made at the wholesale level. Notice also that voluntary codes never win at any loss size in this range, which fits the summary's own list of their disadvantages, chiefly that not all firms subscribe and the penalties are mild.

The same trade-off explains the summary's three forms. Prescriptive regulation writes detailed rules, each covering some named set of cases, so the uncovered residual falls geometrically while cost rises linearly.

   rules written   share of cases   the residual gap   cost of the rules   total
                   covered          uncovered          at 0.9 each
   0                         0.0000%           100.0000%              0.0000  30.0000
   2                        36.0000%            64.0000%              1.8000  21.0000
   5                        67.2320%            32.7680%              4.5000  14.3304
   10                       89.2626%            10.7374%              9.0000  12.2212
   20                       98.8471%             1.1529%             18.0000  18.3459
   40                       99.9867%             0.0133%             36.0000  36.0040

The total is minimised at ten rules at 12.2212, an interior optimum, and the residual gap never reaches zero: at forty rules it is still 0.013292 per cent, and it shrinks without bound while remaining positive. So a purely prescriptive regime cannot close the gap by writing more rules, and past the optimum each further rule makes things worse. That is the entire argument for the summary's third form: prescribe the outcome and the uncovered cases are caught by construction.

Outcomes-based regulation is not free of the problem, though. It relocates it, because the residual gap becomes disagreement about whether an outcome was achieved, which is expensive to resolve and resolves after the harm. That is why the summary's middle form, freedom of action, consists entirely of disclosure obligations, publicity rules, informing third parties and publishing information, rather than conduct rules: disclosure does not require anybody to agree in advance what good conduct is.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The summary's four aims are not four. They are one aim, correcting information asymmetry, plus three of its consequences. A consumer needs protecting because they cannot assess the product, financial crime is possible because the counterparty cannot observe it, and confidence collapses in a run because no depositor can distinguish a solvent bank from an insolvent one. Recognising that turns a list to memorise into a structure to reason from, and in an exam it turns four separate paragraphs into one argument with three applications.

The habit worth forming is to ask, of any rule, which of the two terms in section 3's sum it moves and by how much. Most published defences of most rules argue that the rule advances an aim, which is the easy half. The hard half is the size of the machinery cost, and section 4.3 shows why it is usually understated: the direct cost is visible and quantified in a regulator's annual report, while the barrier-to-entry cost appears as a slow reduction in the number of firms and is attributed to consolidation.

On section 4.2, the professional judgement is that the moral hazard response is nearly always larger than the models assume, and the reason is that it operates on the firm as well as the consumer. A guaranteed depositor stops screening, which is the effect the section models. A bank that knows its depositors are guaranteed can also fund riskier assets at an unchanged cost, which is a second and larger channel the section does not model. Both push p1 up, and the break-even of 0.2542 percentage points on a small exposure leaves almost no room for either.

The uncomfortable conclusion, and one worth stating plainly because the summary does not: on the arithmetic above, a good deal of retail financial regulation is net negative for the retail consumer, not because the aims are wrong but because the machinery cost falls on the same person the aims protect, twice over, once as a higher fee and once as a narrower choice of provider. The defensible version of the case for it rests on the asymmetry result of section 4.1 rather than on the protection argument of 4.2, because a consumer who cannot assess a product also cannot assess whether the regulation protecting them is worth its cost.

One caution about how far to push section 4.1. Akerlof's setup has a uniform quality distribution and a single price, and both matter to the exact figure 2. The structure of the result, that a threshold exists and that below it the loss is total rather than partial, survives any distribution. The number does not. Quote the mechanism in an exam and quote the number only with its assumptions attached.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The fee chain that section 4.3's compliance cost enters, and the finding that six links take 52.08 per cent of a forty-year outcome, is in the business model. The Regulation 28 limits this lecture stands behind are worked in mandates and the statement of investment principles, and the trustee duties in governance, trustees and delegation, where sixteen decisions at a 5 per cent error rate gave a 55.99 per cent chance of a wrongful dismissal. The fixed-versus-proportional shape section 4.3 shares with bed and breakfasting is in taxation of investment returns, regulation is the seventh of the nine policies listed in government objectives and the policy toolkit, and the utility comparison section 4.2 uses is set up in externalities, public goods and market failure, which also derives the asymmetry result at greater length.

Where this goes next. Lectures 8 to 13 apply this framework to named bodies of law and practice: trust law and fiduciary duty, market conduct and the regulation of financial services providers, corporate governance, responsible investment, listing authorities and competition regulation, and accounting standards. Each is an instance of section 3's sum, and the useful question in each case is which of the two terms the rules move. Section 4.4's finding that voluntary codes never win is worth carrying into lecture 11, where much of responsible investment is exactly that. Lecture 14 covers market participants and infrastructure, where section 4.1's threshold reappears as the reason exchanges exist at all, and the six indices lectures close the module.

Where the bank sits opposite. Every result here is sharper for a bank than for an asset manager, because a bank's failure is the summary's own example of a confidence collapse. Section 4.2's moral hazard is the explicit design problem of a deposit guarantee scheme and of the resolution regime built to replace it, which is why the loss-absorbing requirements in capital management exist: the point of making creditors bear losses is to restore the screening incentive section 4.2 shows protection destroys. Section 4.3's fixed compliance cost is far larger for a bank, since the model approval, validation and reporting machinery in A-IRB capital modelling is available only above a size threshold, which is why small banks use the standardised approach and pay more capital for the same assets, an exclusion of exactly the kind section 4.3 describes. And section 4.4's prescriptive-versus-outcomes tension is the Basel framework's own history, moving from prescribed risk weights to internal models and partly back again, traced in the Basel IV finalisation.

Numbers used here. The four aims, the direct and indirect costs, the four types with their advantages and disadvantages, and the three forms are all transcribed from chapter 2 of the ASSA F105 summary in the user's notes, including its own wording that the types are listed in order of increasing degree of regulation and that self-regulation is the cheapest option. The asymmetry result is Akerlof (1970), and the threshold of 2 follows from a uniform quality distribution and a single market price; the structure of the result survives other distributions and the number 2 does not, which is stated in section 5. One observation is recorded against the summary: it presents its four types in order of increasing degree of regulation, which reads as a ranking, while the total of direct and failure costs is minimised at neither end and the preferred type moves with the size of the loss at stake, so the examinable answer gives the summary's order and its advantages and disadvantages as written, and adds that the order is a spectrum of intensity rather than of desirability. Everything numerical is invented for illustration: valuation multiples from 1.2 to 3.0 across a thousand trades with sellers averaging 50, wealth of 1000 with losses at risk of 100 to 900 at a 5 per cent starting failure rate, a 0.75 per cent fee against compliance costs of 0.5 to 5 million, ten firms from 50 million to 9 billion, direct costs of 0 to 20 against failure rates of 24 to 4 per cent with losses per failure from 10 to 500, and rules covering a fifth of the residual each at a cost of 0.9. Twenty-four claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions, all twenty-four passed, and none can pass without the computation. Two claims failed on the first run, both asserting that the total-cost minimum sat at the statutory end, and the correction is section 4.4's finding that it is interior. Two further claims were flagged as unfalsifiable by a vacuity check, one asserting the threshold by literal arithmetic and one asserting a positive power of a fraction, and both were rewritten, the first as a bisection search reported to twelve places. Every headline finding states the region in which it holds: the asymmetry threshold is exact under a uniform distribution and scale free in the price, the protection break-even widens with the loss at risk so a doubling of the failure rate is fatal on a small exposure and tolerable on a large one, the minimum viable firm size is exact for any fixed cost and fee rate, and the preferred regulation type depends on the loss a failure causes rather than on the type itself.
