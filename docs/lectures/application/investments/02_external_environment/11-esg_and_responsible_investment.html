An exclusion is free on the measure nobody uses and expensive on the measure everybody uses

Excluding one sector of ten costs 0.5013 per cent of the Sharpe ratio and creates 1.4907 per cent of tracking error, a ratio of 2.9740 to one. Above an excluded weight of two over the number of sectors the Sharpe ratio actually improves, because a cap-weighted market is itself the concentrated portfolio, and that threshold is the same at every volatility and correlation. The tracking error never turns negative, so no exclusion is free on both measures. Along the way: divestment does reach the company, and 98 per cent of labelled flows fund nothing at all.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Lecture 3 of the internal environment established two things about the summary's ESG material: an exclusion costs exactly zero in expected return absent an expected edge, and all six of the summary's performance examples are ordinary financial analysis rather than anything distinctively ESG. Both stand. This lecture supplies what those results left out, which is the cost in variance, the mechanism by which anything reaches a company, and the one label among the summary's four that is different in kind.

Start with the cost. The expected return is unchanged, but the excluded portfolio is less diversified and it differs from the market. Excluding one sector of ten costs 0.5013 per cent of the Sharpe ratio, which is negligible, and creates 1.4907 per cent of tracking error, which is not, a ratio of 2.9740 to one. The summary insists ESG must not compromise the financial objective, and that clause is satisfied on one measure and violated on the other. The measure it is violated on is the one lecture 3 found trustees are actually judged against.

The region contains a sign change worth knowing. A real market is not equally weighted, so excluding a large sector and equal-weighting the remainder can move the portfolio towards minimum variance. Above an excluded weight of exactly 2/n, twice the equal weight, the Sharpe ratio improves, by 0.6231 per cent at a 25 per cent exclusion. That threshold falls out at 0.200000 for ten sectors and 0.100000 for twenty, identically at every volatility and correlation, because both parameters cancel out of the condition. The tracking error, however, never turns negative: it is 2.9814 per cent at the flip point and rises monotonically. There is no excluded weight at which an exclusion is free on both measures.

Then the mechanism. If a fraction of the market refuses to hold a sector, the remainder must hold 1/(1-f) times as much and must be paid for it, so the sector's required return rises by that factor: 26.3158 basis points at a 5 per cent divestment and 125.0000 at 20 per cent. The divesting holder forgoes exactly what they impose, so every basis point charged to the company is a basis point handed to whoever keeps holding it. And it does bite: a 20 per cent divestment lifts a 7 per cent hurdle to 8.2500 and kills every project in a band exactly as wide as the rise.

Finally, additionality. A secondary market purchase hands the company 0.0000 to spend and a primary issue hands it the whole amount. At a 2 per cent primary share of turnover, 980.0000 of every 1000 of labelled flow acts only through the price. So the summary's four labels split one and three rather than four ways.

Step 2

Why this matters

The summary contains two clauses about ESG that cannot both do work. It says the primary focus is financial and ESG must not compromise it, and it says ESG factors increase returns and trustees have a fiduciary duty to consider them. If the second is true, the first is unnecessary: nobody needs a rule protecting them from an action that improves returns. If the first is needed, the second is false for the cases where it is needed. Section 4.4 sets that out as a dilemma with two exhaustive branches, and neither branch leaves room for a distinctively ESG decision that is both permitted and consequential.

That is not an argument against responsible investment. It is an argument about which instrument to use. Lecture 9 gave the free-rider threshold for engagement, 15 per cent of a company, and lecture 10 gave the structural reasons a board resists outside pressure. This lecture gives the arithmetic of the two alternatives an investor actually has: screening, which costs tracking error and moves the price by tens of basis points, and primary capital, which funds projects directly. The field's shift from screening towards engagement and impact over the past decade is exactly what these numbers predict.

For an examiner the useful content is the sort in section 4.4: fifteen named factors, seven of which are already fully inside an ordinary cash flow forecast, four partly, three weak or contested and one with no financial transmission at all. A candidate who can say which is which has understood the topic. A candidate who lists all fifteen under three headings has memorised it.

The Feynman version. Suppose you refuse to own tobacco shares. Three things might happen. Your returns might suffer: they do not much, because one sector out of many barely moves the average. Your returns might wobble differently from everyone else's: they do, quite a lot, and that is what your trustees will notice at the next quarterly meeting. Or the tobacco company might notice: it does not, because you sold to somebody who was happy to buy, and the company never saw the transaction.

The one thing that would make the company notice is if enough people refused that the remaining buyers had to be bribed with a higher return to hold all those shares. Then the company's cost of raising money goes up, and some project it was about to fund no longer pays. That works. But the bribe you refused to collect is exactly the bribe somebody else collects, so you paid for the whole effect and split the credit with nobody. And it only kills the projects that were nearly failing anyway.

Step 3

The mental model

Separate three questions that ESG discussion routinely merges. What does this cost me? What does it do to the company? And is it something I would want even if it did nothing? The three have different answers and different units, and most disputes about responsible investment are two people answering different ones.

The first question has two answers depending on the measure, which is section 4.1. The second has one answer and it runs entirely through price, which is section 4.2, except in the single case where money reaches the company directly, which is section 4.3. The third is not a financial question at all, and the honest position is that a private investor may answer it however they like while a trustee acting for others may not, which is where the summary's fiduciary clause belongs.

The organising insight is that a screen operates on a portfolio and an investment operates on a company. Buying and selling existing shares rearranges who owns what without changing what exists. Only primary issuance, private capital and engagement change what a company does, and of those three the first two require money to change hands with the company and the third requires a stake large enough to clear lecture 9's threshold. Everything else is a preference expressed through a portfolio, which is a legitimate thing to want and not a mechanism.

The detail

4.1 The variance cost of an exclusion, and the measure that never turns free

Overview lecture 3 showed an exclusion costs exactly zero in expected return absent an expected edge. That is true and it is not the whole cost. Compute both remaining costs on a market of ten equally weighted sectors at 20 per cent volatility and 0.5 pairwise correlation.

Excluding one sector of ten costs 0.5013 per cent of the Sharpe ratio, which is negligible, and creates 1.4907 per cent of tracking error, which is not. The ratio between the two is 2.9740 to one. So the summary's clause that ESG must not compromise the financial objective is satisfied on one measure and violated on the other, and the measure it is violated on is the one a trustee is judged against. An exclusion is free in theory and expensive in a board meeting.

The sign flips at an excluded weight of 0.200000, which is exactly two over the number of sectors, twice the equal weight. A search confirms the same threshold at every parameter combination tested: 0.200000 at ten sectors whether volatility is 0.20 or 0.40 and whether correlation is 0.10 or 0.50, and 0.100000 at twenty sectors. Both parameters cancel out of the condition, so 2/n is the whole answer.

Below that weight, excluding the sector moves the portfolio away from equal weighting and raises variance. Above it, the market was the concentrated portfolio, and excluding its largest constituent moves towards minimum variance, so the Sharpe ratio improves by 0.6231 per cent at a 25 per cent exclusion. The tracking error, though, never turns negative. It is 2.9814 per cent at the flip point and rises monotonically with the excluded weight. There is no excluded weight at which an exclusion is free on both measures, and for a large sector the two measures disagree about the sign rather than merely the size.

4.2 Divestment does reach the company, and the reach is a narrow band

The summary lists four labels and says nothing about mechanism, so ask the question that matters: when an investor sells a holding on ethical grounds, what reaches the company? The answer runs through the cost of capital. If a fraction f of the market refuses to hold a sector, the remainder must hold 1/(1-f) times as much of it and must be paid for the extra risk, so the sector's required return rises by the same factor.

   share of the market   required return   rise in the      the divesting holder's
   that divests, f       on the sector     cost of capital  forgone return
   0.0000                          5.0000%           0.0000%                 0.0000%
   0.0500                          5.2632%           0.2632%                 0.2632%
   0.2000                          6.2500%           1.2500%                 1.2500%
   0.5000                         10.0000%           5.0000%                 5.0000%
   0.8000                         25.0000%          20.0000%                20.0000%

A 5 per cent divestment raises the sector's cost of capital by 26.3158 basis points and a 20 per cent divestment by 125.0000. The divesting holder forgoes exactly the same amount, so the effect is symmetric: every basis point imposed on the company is a basis point handed to whoever keeps holding it. That symmetry is worth stating plainly, because it means a divestment campaign transfers return from the ethically motivated to the indifferent, in exact proportion to its success.

Does it change anything? A company invests where the project return clears its cost of capital, so ask which projects die.

   project return   hurdle at f=0   hurdle at f=0.20   does the project survive
                                                       before / after divestment
          12.0000%         7.0000%             8.2500%   yes / yes
           9.0000%         7.0000%             8.2500%   yes / yes
           8.2500%         7.0000%             8.2500%   yes / NO, killed
           7.5000%         7.0000%             8.2500%   yes / NO, killed
           7.1000%         7.0000%             8.2500%   yes / NO, killed

A 20 per cent divestment lifts the hurdle from 7.0000 to 8.2500 per cent and kills every project in that band. The band is exactly as wide as the rise, 1.2500 per cent, so divestment works and its reach is precisely the density of marginal projects inside a narrow band of returns.

Which is why the empirical finding that divestment campaigns have small effects is not a refutation of the mechanism. The mechanism is exact and the band is narrow. A campaign that moves the divesting share from zero to 5 per cent shifts the hurdle by 26.3158 basis points and kills only projects already within a quarter of a point of failing. To kill anything substantial you need a large f, and a large f means the divesting investors have handed a correspondingly large return to those who stayed.

4.3 Additionality separates one of the summary's four labels from the other three

The summary lists sustainable investing, impact investing, socially responsible investing and ethical investing as if they were variants of one thing. One of the four is different in kind and the difference is computable: ask what the company receives.

The indirect share is at least nine times the direct one across the whole range and ninety-nine times at the lowest. So the summary's four labels split one and three rather than four ways: impact investing claims additionality, and the other three are screening rules applied to a secondary market.

That reframes what the other three labels are for. If they cannot fund a project and their price effect is tens of basis points, their function is to express a preference rather than to change an outcome. That is a legitimate objective for a private investor and a hard one to reconcile with the summary's insistence that the primary focus is financial, because expressing a preference is precisely a non-financial objective. The summary's own two clauses are what create the difficulty, not the labels.

If a factor is financially material, considering it is ordinary analysis and the summary's fiduciary clause adds nothing. If it is not material, excluding on it costs the tracking error of section 4.1 for no return, which breaks the summary's own must-not-compromise clause. The two branches are exhaustive, so there is no room for a distinctively ESG decision that is both permitted and consequential. That is the honest state of the argument, it is why the field has moved towards impact and engagement rather than screening, and it is a defensible answer to an exam question asking whether ESG and fiduciary duty conflict: they do not conflict, they collapse into each other.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The most useful thing to carry from this lecture is the habit of asking which measure a cost is quoted on. Almost every published defence of exclusionary screening quotes a Sharpe ratio or a long-run return comparison, and section 4.1 shows those are the measures on which the cost is smallest and can even be negative. Almost every trustee objection is really a tracking error objection, and that is the measure on which the cost is largest and never vanishes. Both sides are quoting real numbers and neither is answering the other.

The 2/n result has a practical consequence beyond the argument. It says that excluding a genuinely dominant sector can lower a portfolio's variance, which means that in a market with one very large sector, an exclusion and a diversification improvement are the same trade. For a South African investor that is not hypothetical: a market with a handful of very large constituents sits in exactly the region where dropping the largest one moves towards minimum variance. The exclusion still costs tracking error against the local index, so a fund benchmarked domestically pays for it and a fund benchmarked globally may not.

On divestment, the symmetry in section 4.2 is the fact most campaigns do not acknowledge and the one that decides whether they are coherent. A successful campaign raises the excluded sector's return, which means the sector's remaining owners are paid more, which means the campaign's success is measured in money transferred to people who disagree with it. That is not a reason not to run one. It is a reason to be clear that divestment is a costly signal rather than a profitable strategy, and anybody who claims it is both has not done the arithmetic.

The professional judgement on additionality: the 2 per cent primary share of section 4.3 is the single most important number in responsible investment and the least often quoted. It says that the overwhelming majority of money labelled sustainable is a claim about a portfolio's composition rather than about anything happening in the world. An investor who genuinely wants effect should look at primary issuance, private capital and engagement above the 15 per cent threshold, in that order, and should treat a screened secondary portfolio as what it is: a statement of preference with a small price effect attached.

One limit. Section 4.1's model has identical volatilities and a single correlation, which is what makes the equal-weighted portfolio the minimum-variance one and gives the clean 2/n threshold. With realistic dispersion in volatilities and correlations the threshold moves and the flip may not occur at all for a sector that is both large and low-volatility. The structure survives, which is that the two cost measures need not agree in sign; the number does not, and it should be quoted with the assumption attached.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The zero expected-return cost of an exclusion and the finding that all six of the summary's performance examples are ordinary analysis are both established in governance, trustees and delegation, which also supplied the 16.8760 per cent tracking error that section 4.1 measures against. The portfolio variance and tracking error arithmetic is derived in portfolio risk and return. The 15 per cent engagement threshold that makes screening the default instrument is in market conduct and financial services provider regulation, the executive pay ratchet behind one of the fifteen factors is in corporate governance, and the three corporate tax systems behind another are in taxation of investment returns. The fiduciary framework the whole dilemma sits inside is in trust law and fiduciary duty.

Where this goes next. Lecture 12 covers listing authorities and competition regulation, which is where the primary issuance of section 4.3 actually happens, so the 2 per cent figure and the prospectus requirements belong together. Lecture 13 covers accounting standards, which determine whether any of the fifteen factors is measurable at all, and the answer bears directly on section 4.4's sort: a factor with a transmission channel and no disclosure is not investable. Lecture 14 covers market participants and infrastructure, and the six indices lectures close the module, where section 4.1's exclusion problem reappears as the construction choice behind every screened index.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank meets the environmental pillar as a credit problem rather than a portfolio one, which makes its version of this lecture quantitative in a way an equity screen never is. Climate risk enters a bank's loan book through the borrower's ability to repay, so the physical and transition channels of section 4.4 become PD and LGD inputs: see climate risk, where the scenario approach replaces screening entirely. That difference is instructive. A bank cannot divest a twenty-year mortgage, so it has no screening instrument and must price the risk instead, which is why bank climate work is about measurement and asset manager climate work is about exclusion. The governance pillar also lands differently: executive pay at a bank is constrained by remuneration rules with deferral and clawback requirements rather than by the peer benchmarking of lecture 10, and the capital consequences of a conduct failure are in operational risk. And section 4.2's cost of capital channel is exactly how a bank transmits pressure to a borrower, through the loan spread, which is a far more direct instrument than a share sale.

Two observations are recorded against the summary. First, its clause that ESG must not compromise the financial objective is satisfied on a Sharpe ratio measure and violated on a tracking error measure by every exclusion, and the second measure is the one trustees are judged against, so the clause is not self-executing; the examinable answer gives the clause as written and adds that the cost depends on the measure. Everything numerical is invented for illustration: ten sectors at 20 per cent volatility and 0.5 correlation with a 5 per cent premium, exclusions of one to five sectors and excluded weights of 1 to 25 per cent, divesting shares from zero to 80 per cent, a 7 per cent hurdle against project returns of 7.1 to 12 per cent, and primary issuance shares of 1 to 10 per cent of a 1000 flow. Eighteen claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions, all eighteen passed, and none can pass without the computation. Four claims failed on the first run. One asserted the tracking error was many times the Sharpe loss, where the ratio is 2.9740, so the claim was narrowed to roughly three times. Two asserted the exclusion cost was monotone in the excluded weight; it is not, and locating the turning point produced the lecture's 2/n result, which a further search showed to be independent of volatility, correlation and sector count. The fourth was a floating-point tie at a 10 per cent primary share, where the indirect share is exactly nine times the direct one. Three further claims were flagged as unfalsifiable by a vacuity check, all three asserting arithmetic on literal constants, and all three were rewritten against computed values. Every headline finding states the region in which it holds: the 2/n threshold assumes identical volatilities and a single correlation and moves under realistic dispersion, while the disagreement in sign between the two cost measures survives; the divestment identity is exact for any divesting share and its practical reach is the density of projects inside a narrow band; and the additionality result holds for any primary share, with the indirect portion at least nine times the direct one across the range tested.
