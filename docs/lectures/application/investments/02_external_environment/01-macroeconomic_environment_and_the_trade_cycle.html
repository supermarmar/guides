Lectures / Investments / External environment

By the time a reliable GDP print confirms the turn, 100.00 per cent of the market move has happened

A market that leads the real economy by six months has completed half its move when the economy turns, three quarters when the first GDP figure appears, and all of it by the time that figure is reliable. So an economist must be right about the economy and an investor must be right about it earlier than the price, which is a strictly harder test the first does not imply. Along the way: cycle timing turns out to be an exactly symmetric bet, since the midpoint of a perfect rotation and a backwards one equals the midpoint of the two static holdings to 0.0e+00, and a thirty-year project is 26.33 times as rate-sensitive as a one-year one.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The economics in this lecture is built elsewhere. Twenty-five lectures in the fundamentals economics module cover the trade cycle, monetary transmission and the open economy properly. What this module adds is the one thing an economist does not need and an investor cannot do without: a forecast is worthless unless it is earlier than the price, and that changes which questions are worth asking.

Price the delay. Take a market that turns six months before the real economy, a GDP figure published three months after the quarter it measures, and a further three months before revisions make it reliable. Acting on the market's own turn captures 100.00 per cent of the move. Acting when the real economy turns captures 50.00. Acting on the first print captures 25.00. Acting on the revised, reliable print captures 0.00 per cent: the move is entirely over by the time the data confirming it exists.

The region runs opposite to intuition. A market that leads by twelve months leaves 25.00 per cent to capture and one that leads by a single month leaves 0.00, because a long lead means a slow repricing while the data lag is fixed. So cyclical strategies work best in markets that anticipate furthest ahead, which is not where anyone looks for them.

Second, the summary lists "viability of capital projects" and "NPVs" among the business channels without saying that a rate change hits projects in proportion to their duration. A one-point rise cuts a one-year project's value by 0.92 per cent and a thirty-year project's by 24.16 per cent, a factor of 26.33. That connects two of the summary's own lists it never joins: utilities are described as capital and infrastructure intensive in one place and as defensive value stocks in another, and the reason they behave like bonds is this duration effect on their assets rather than anything about their customers.

Third, sector rotation turns out to be an exactly symmetric bet. Perfect rotation earns 10.5000 per cent a cycle and backwards rotation minus 5.0000, against a midpoint of the two static holdings at 2.7500. The prize is 7.7500 and the penalty is 7.7500, equal to 0.0e+00, and that is an identity rather than a parameter coincidence: the two rotations between them use each sector once in each phase, so their sum must equal the sum of the two static strategies. Cycle timing therefore needs a hit rate above one half to break even before costs, and section 4.1 showed the confirming signal arriving systematically late.

Finally the gearing channel, which the summary details more than the others. A one-point rise costs an ungeared firm 0.00 per cent of profit and a firm with 800 of debt against 100 of operating profit 22.22 per cent, which is 22.2 times the rate change. That explains two of the summary's three financial sub-sectors and not the third: general insurers are described as having no gearing and volatile profits, which gearing cannot account for, so its three-row table quietly contains two different mechanisms.

Step 2

Why this matters

This module exists because the same facts mean different things to an economist and an investor, and the difference is almost always about timing. An economist who correctly calls a recession two months after it began has done useful work. An investor who does the same has lost money, because the market moved first. Section 4.1 puts a number on that gap and the number is total: nothing at all is left by the time a reliable figure exists. Every subsequent lecture in this module rests on that observation, which is why it comes first.

It also matters as a discipline on how macroeconomic views are used. A fund that hears a persuasive economic forecast has three questions to ask before acting, and only the third is about the economy: is this forecast already in the price, will the confirming evidence arrive before or after the repricing, and is the forecast right? Most macroeconomic debate in investment committees answers the third and skips the first two, which is how a correct view becomes a losing position.

The duration result in section 4.2 matters because it explains a persistent puzzle in equity analysis. Sectors described as defensive on business grounds, utilities most obviously, behave like long bonds in rate cycles, and analysts routinely treat that as anomalous. It is not: their assets are long-lived, so their present values carry long duration, and that dominates whatever their revenue stability contributes. Recognising it turns an anomaly into an arithmetic consequence and makes the exposure hedgeable.

And the symmetry result in section 4.3 has a governance use. A fund considering tactical sector rotation is considering a bet whose payoff is exactly symmetric before costs, which means it requires demonstrable skill rather than a plausible narrative. That is a much higher bar than "we have a view on the cycle", and stating it in those terms is often enough to settle the discussion without any forecast being contested.

Step 3

The mental model

There are three clocks: the market's, the economy's, and the statistician's. They run in that order, and everything in this lecture follows from the gaps between them.

Three things happen in sequence when a downturn arrives, and they are months apart.

First the market falls. Not because anyone has data, but because enough people suspect. That is the market's clock, and it is the earliest.

Then the economy turns. Orders drop, hiring stops, output falls. That is the economy's clock, and by the time it strikes, the market has already moved a long way.

Then the statistics arrive. A quarter's output is measured, published a few months later, and revised for a few months after that. That is the statistician's clock, and it is the latest of the three.

Now ask what an investor can do with the third clock. Nothing at all, if the market's whole move is complete before that clock strikes. And that is the ordinary case: six months of market lead, three months to publish, three more to be sure, and the repricing finished while you were waiting to be sure.

Which leaves a strange conclusion. The better the market is at anticipating, the more time you have. A market that moves the day before the economy turns gives you nothing, because there is nothing left to move into. A market that starts repricing a year ahead is still repricing when the data arrive. So slow, thoughtful markets are the ones where macroeconomic analysis pays, and quick ones are where it does not, which is the reverse of how people usually put it.

And one thing about interest rates worth holding separately. A rate change is a discounting change, so it hits whatever has the longest cash flows hardest. That is why a water company and a thirty-year bond move together. Their businesses have nothing in common and their durations have everything in common.

Formally, if the market's move is spread over a window of 2L months centred on the economy's turn, where L is the lead, then a signal arriving m months after the market turned leaves max(0, 1 - m / 2L) of the move available. With m = L + D + R for a publication lag D and revision lag R, the remainder is positive only when L > D + R.

The three clocks, and what each one tells you

   the clock          when it strikes            what it is good for
   the market's       first, on suspicion        nothing: it IS the price
   the economy's      second, on activity        confirming what the price said
   the statistician's third, on measurement      history, and revising history

Only the first is actionable and it is not a forecast. The other two describe what already happened.

The detail

4.2 The NPV channel, and why long projects feel rates most

The summary lists "viability of capital projects" and "NPVs" among the business channels and stops. The missing quantity is that a rate change does not hit projects equally: it hits them in proportion to their duration, exactly as it hits bonds.

The thirty-year project loses 24.16 per cent of its value against the one-year project's 0.92, a factor of 26.33.

A one-point rise cuts the one-year project's value by 0.92 per cent and the thirty-year project's by 24.16, a factor of 26.33. So "capital investment levels affected" is not a uniform statement: it is a statement about long-lived projects, and short-payback ones are nearly immune. A firm whose projects pay back in two years is barely troubled by a rate cycle and a firm building infrastructure is transformed by it.

That connects two of the summary's own lists it never joins. It describes utilities as capital intensive and infrastructure intensive in the sector taxonomy, and separately as defensive and value in the cycle classification. The reason they behave like bonds is this duration effect on their assets, not anything about their customers' loyalty. The summary supplies both facts and leaves the link to the reader, and the link is what makes the exposure hedgeable: a utility's rate sensitivity can be offset with an interest rate swap in a way its business risk cannot.

4.3 Sector rotation is an exactly symmetric bet

The summary splits sectors into cyclical and defensive and implies a rotation strategy. Price all four possibilities across an equally weighted upturn and downturn.

Four strategies over a full cycle

   the strategy                        in an upturn   in a downturn   average over
                                                                      a full cycle
   hold cyclicals throughout                  25.00%          -20.00%         2.5000%
   hold defensives throughout                 10.00%           -4.00%         3.0000%
   rotate perfectly                           25.00%           -4.00%        10.5000%
   rotate exactly backwards                   10.00%          -20.00%        -5.0000%

Perfect rotation earns 10.5000 per cent and backwards rotation minus 5.0000, against a static midpoint of 2.7500.

The prize for perfect rotation over the midpoint of the two static holdings is 7.7500 per cent and the penalty for rotating backwards is 7.7500, equal to 0.0e+00. And that is an identity rather than a coincidence of these parameters: the two rotations between them use each sector exactly once in each phase, so their sum equals the sum of the two static strategies whatever the four returns are, and the midpoints must coincide.

The consequence is a hurdle rather than a prohibition. Cycle timing needs a hit rate above one half to break even before costs, and rather more than that after them. Combine that with section 4.1, where the confirming data arrive after the move is over, and a disciplined data-driven rotation is a symmetric bet played with a systematically late signal. That is not an argument against sector analysis, which is the summary's point about analyst specialisation and is about understanding businesses rather than timing them. It is an argument against timing the cycle with published statistics.

When both sectors fall equally the prize is exactly zero, because there is nowhere to rotate to.

The prize is entirely a property of how differently the two sectors behave in a downturn. When both fall equally it is exactly 0.0000 per cent, because there is nowhere to rotate to. That matters because sector dispersion in downturns is not constant: it widens in a sector-specific shock and narrows in a broad liquidity event, so the strategy's value is highest in the crises where its signal is most confused and lowest in the ordinary recessions where the signal is clearest.

But it explains only two of the summary's three financial sub-sectors. A candidate who explains all three by gearing has explained two correctly and one by accident.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Three habits that separate a usable macroeconomic view from an interesting one.

Ask what the price already implies before asking what will happen. Section 4.1's arithmetic makes this the first question rather than a refinement of the last one. In practice that means reading the forward curve, the implied inflation from index-linked bonds, and the cyclical sector spread, and treating those as the consensus forecast against which any view must differ. A view identical to the implied one is a description of the market, not a reason to trade, and stating it as a house view is how committees convince themselves they have done analysis.

Separate the duration exposure from the business exposure in every sector view. Section 4.2 shows a utility's rate sensitivity coming from the length of its asset cash flows rather than from anything about its customers. That separation is practically valuable because the two halves are managed differently: the duration can be hedged with swaps at low cost, as lecture 9 of the portfolio management module priced, and the business exposure cannot be hedged at all. A fund that likes a utility's business and dislikes its rate sensitivity has a solvable problem, and one that has not separated the two has an unanalysed position.

Treat cycle timing as requiring evidence of skill rather than a plausible story, because section 4.3 makes it a symmetric bet. The identity there is useful precisely because it is parameter-free: whatever the four sector returns, getting the rotation right earns exactly what getting it wrong loses. So the only question is the hit rate, and the honest answer for most institutions is that they have never measured theirs. Measuring it is straightforward, requires only a record of past tactical decisions, and usually ends the conversation. That is the same discipline the performance measurement module applied to manager skill, arriving here as a constraint on the fund's own decisions rather than its managers'.

On the summary, two observations are recorded, both of connection rather than of fact. It gives the duration nature of long-lived assets and the defensive nature of utilities in separate lists without joining them, when the first explains the second's rate behaviour. And its three-row financial sector table uses one format for two mechanisms, since gearing explains banks and life insurers and underwriting risk explains general insurers. Neither is an error. Both are places where the compression that makes a summary useful removes the link that makes it explanatory.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The economics is built in the fundamentals economics module, and specifically the trade cycle in Keynesian macroeconomics, the transmission mechanism in monetary economics, the exchange rate and the two accounts in open economy macroeconomics, and the measurement lags in national accounts and macro measurement. The duration arithmetic in section 4.2 is the same as in fixed income pricing, the swap that hedges it is priced in using swaps, and the hit-rate discipline in section 4.3 is the one applied to managers in measuring portfolio performance. The market efficiency question underneath section 4.1 is in technical analysis.

Where this goes next. Lecture 2 covers central banks and monetary policy, which section 4.4 showed to be the single origin of all eleven transmission channels, so it does most of the work in this arc. Lecture 3 follows the transmission into asset prices in detail, and lecture 4 develops the sector taxonomy this lecture only sampled, including the summary's own FTSE classification and its problems with conglomerates and heterogeneous sectors. Lecture 5 turns from the central bank to the government, covering the balance of payments, demand management and fiscal policy, and lecture 6 covers taxation, which the internal environment already found to be the largest controllable effect on an investor's outcome.

Where the bank sits opposite. The prime rate that begins the summary's transmission list is set by the bank rather than experienced by it, and the margin between the policy rate and the prime rate is the bank's own pricing decision, set out in bank financial management. The summary's own note that a repo rate rise "increases loan interest more than deposit interest" is the bank's margin expanding, and its note that "recession can wipe out profits" is the expected credit loss offsetting it, which is the tension quantified in interest rate risk in the banking book and credit risk measurement. And the gearing arithmetic of section 4.4 is why bank equity is the most rate-sensitive equity in any market: a bank is the most highly geared firm in the summary's own taxonomy.

Seventeen claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions, all seventeen passed, and none can pass without the computation. Two failed on the first run and both concerned the same guess: I asserted that the penalty for rotating backwards exceeds the prize for rotating perfectly, and they are exactly equal, which turned out to be a parameter-free identity worth stating in its own right, since the two rotations between them use each sector once in each phase. A vacuity check found no unfalsifiable claims. Two label defects were corrected before publication, one reading "1 years" and one a column too narrow for its values. Every headline finding states the region in which it holds: the data-lag result depends on the market's lead exceeding the publication and revision lags combined, the duration result holds for any single-payment project and approximately for real ones, the rotation symmetry is exact for any four sector returns, and the gearing effect scales with debt against operating profit rather than with debt alone.
