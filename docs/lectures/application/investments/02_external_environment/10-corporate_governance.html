Lectures / Investments / External environment

Solvent trading is not a legal nicety, it is the exact point where shareholders and creditors stop wanting the same thing

Below a debt equal to the bad-state asset value, shareholders are indifferent to a fair gamble to twelve decimal places. Above it they prefer one, and every unit they gain is a unit creditors lose, summing to exactly zero. At a debt of 90 against a safe value of 100 they prefer a gamble that destroys 30 per cent of the firm. Along the way: a board that nominates its own successors converges to the nomination process's bias whatever it started as, and setting pay by peer comparison has no fixed point below the distribution's own upper tail.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Lecture 9 established that a shareholder below a 15 per cent stake will not intervene in a company however bad its governance, because the cost is borne by one holder and the benefit shared by all. That result is the reason this lecture exists: if the owners will not police the company, the policing has to be internal, and the summary's board structure is what internal policing looks like.

The summary makes the board responsible for governance, supervising the firm's affairs and solvent trading, and leaves the last of those as a phrase. It is the most precise item on the whole list. Take a firm worth 100 if management plays safe, or 140 and 60 with equal probability if it gambles, so the gamble creates nothing. Below a debt of 60, which is the bad-state asset value, shareholders are exactly indifferent to the gamble, to twelve decimal places and at every debt level. Above 60 they strictly prefer it, and every unit they gain is a unit the creditors lose: at a debt of 90 the shareholders gain 15.0000 and the creditors lose 15.0000, summing to exactly zero. So the threshold is precisely the point at which default becomes possible, and it is a cliff rather than a slope.

Worse, past the threshold shareholders prefer gambles that destroy value. At a debt of 90 they prefer one destroying 30.0000 per cent of the firm. So a board that simply maximises shareholder value near insolvency is acting against the company, which is why the duty is owed to the company rather than to the shareholders, and why the summary's fourth mark of good governance, considering all stakeholders and not only personal interest, is a statement about creditors with a precise trigger point rather than a general aspiration.

Two structural results follow. The summary requires non-executives to have no relation to the firm and separately to nominate new board members, and those are incompatible in steady state: with a quarter of the board replaced each year and a nomination process biased 0.6000 towards management, a perfectly independent board reaches 0.598097 after twenty years, covering 99.6829 per cent of the distance. Every starting point converges to the same place, so a board's original composition is irrelevant in the long run and only the nomination process matters. And setting executive salaries by peer comparison has no equilibrium: aiming at the upper quartile lifts the median 30.1693 per cent in five rounds with no firm ever paying above its peers' upper quartile, terminating at the second-highest original value. Aiming at the median still ratchets, because half the firms are below it and none moves down.

Step 2

Why this matters

Corporate governance is usually taught as a list of good practices, and the summary's version is a good list. What a list cannot show is which practices are load-bearing. Three of the results below identify load-bearing items, and in each case the reason the item matters is arithmetic that the list does not contain.

For an investor the solvent trading result has direct application. It says that a company's leverage determines whose interests its board is actually serving, and it gives the threshold: as soon as the debt exceeds what the assets are worth in a bad outcome, shareholder value and firm value diverge, and the divergence is a one-for-one transfer from lenders. That is why a bondholder reads a highly geared company's strategy announcements differently from a shareholder, and why covenants exist. Lecture 6 of the internal environment found the shield from that same debt worth exactly the tax rate times the debt. This lecture supplies the offsetting cost, and the two together are the whole capital structure trade-off.

The nomination result matters because it reframes what governance reform can achieve. Appointing better directors is a one-off improvement that decays with a half-life this lecture computes. Changing who nominates them moves the destination. Anybody assessing a company's governance should therefore look at the nomination committee's composition before looking at the board's, and the arithmetic says how much more that matters rather than merely asserting that it does.

The Feynman version. You owe the bank 90 and your business is worth 100. You have 10 to your name. Somebody offers you a coin flip: heads the business is worth 140, tails it is worth 60. On average nothing changes. Should you take it?

Take it. On heads you owe 90 and keep 50. On tails you owe 90, have 60, and lose everything you had, which was 10. So you are risking 10 to win 40, on a coin flip. Of course you take it. The bank hates it, because on tails it gets 60 instead of 90, and the 30 it lost is where most of your 40 came from.

Now notice when this stops being true. If you only owed 40, then on tails you would still have 20 left, and your gains and losses would cancel exactly. It is the possibility of being wiped out that makes the gamble attractive, because being wiped out costs you the same whether the business is worth 59 or nothing at all. The bank pays for everything below the line, and you keep everything above it. That line is the whole subject.

Step 3

The mental model

A company is a set of claims on one pool of assets, ordered by seniority, and every governance question is about whose claim the decision-makers are serving. The board sits above all the claims and owes its duty to the company, meaning to the pool rather than to any claimant. That is not a piece of legal decoration. Section 4.1 shows it is the only formulation that gives the right answer when the claims disagree.

The disagreement has a precise structure. A junior claim is an option: it pays the excess above the senior claims and nothing below, so it gains from volatility. A senior claim is short that option, so it loses from volatility. When the senior claims are small relative to the assets, the junior claim is deep in the money and behaves almost like the assets themselves, so the two agree. When the senior claims approach the assets, the option's convexity dominates, and the two disagree. The threshold is where the option first has a chance of expiring worthless.

The board structure then follows from a second observation. The people who know most about the company are the people running it, and the people whose interests need protecting are outside it. Every non-executive role in the summary's list is an attempt to bridge that gap, and section 4.4 shows that five of the five non-executive roles depend on information or processes the non-executives do not control, in three cases on the very people they are monitoring. That dependency, not any lack of diligence, is what limits board oversight.

Step 4

The detail

4.1 Solvent trading, and the threshold at the bad-state asset value

The summary makes the board responsible for solvent trading alongside governance and supervising the firm's affairs. Take a firm whose assets are worth 100 if management does the safe thing, or 140 and 60 with equal probability if it gambles. The gamble has the same expected value, so it creates nothing. Ask who prefers it.

   debt due   equity if   equity if   the shareholders'   the creditors'   who
             safe        it gambles  gain from gambling  loss             prefers
   20.0000       80.0000     80.0000              0.0000           0.0000   neither, exactly
   40.0000       60.0000     60.0000              0.0000           0.0000   neither, exactly
   60.0000       40.0000     40.0000              0.0000           0.0000   neither, exactly
   75.0000       25.0000     32.5000              7.5000          -7.5000   the gamble
   90.0000       10.0000     25.0000             15.0000         -15.0000   the gamble

Below a debt of 60.0000 the shareholders are exactly indifferent, to twelve decimal places, and above it they strictly prefer the gamble. The threshold is precisely the bad-state asset value, which is to say precisely the point at which default becomes possible. And where they prefer it, every unit they gain is a unit the creditors lose: at a debt of 75.0000 the two are +7.5000 and −7.5000, and at 90.0000 they are +15.0000 and −15.0000, summing to exactly zero in both cases. So it is a pure transfer that creates nothing, and the summary's phrase names the exact point at which the board's two constituencies stop agreeing.

Now the part that makes this a governance problem rather than a financing one. Past the threshold, shareholders prefer gambles that destroy value.

   the gamble        expected     value created   equity if   do shareholders
   up / down state   asset value  or destroyed    it gambles  still prefer it?
   140 / 60              100.0000          0.0000     25.0000   YES
   135 / 55               95.0000         -5.0000     22.5000   YES
   130 / 40               85.0000        -15.0000     20.0000   YES
   120 / 20               70.0000        -30.0000     15.0000   YES

A gamble destroying 30.0000 of value, which is 30.0000 per cent of the firm, is still preferred by the shareholders at a debt of 90.0000. So a board that simply maximises shareholder value near insolvency is acting against the firm, and the summary's solvent trading duty is what stops it. That is the reason the duty is owed to the company and not to the shareholders, and it is a sharper reason than the usual appeal to fairness.

Which re-reads the summary's fourth mark of good governance. Considering all stakeholders and not only personal interest is not a statement about social responsibility when it sits next to solvent trading. It is a statement about creditors, and it has a trigger point. Below the threshold a board can serve shareholders exclusively and harm nobody. Above it, it cannot.

4.2 A self-nominating board converges to the nomination bias

The summary requires non-executives to have no relation to the firm and to be an independent voice for shareholder interest. It separately lists nominating new board members among their roles. Those two are incompatible in steady state, and the arithmetic says how fast. Model it: each year a fraction of the board is replaced, and a replacement chosen through a process with bias phi is management-aligned with probability phi. Start from a perfectly independent board with a quarter replaced each year and a bias of 0.6000.

   year   share of the board   distance from the   distance as a share
          aligned with mgmt    steady state        of the original gap
   0                  0.000000            0.600000             1.000000
   1                  0.150000            0.450000             0.750000
   2                  0.262500            0.337500             0.562500
   3                  0.346875            0.253125             0.421875
   5                  0.457617            0.142383             0.237305
   10                 0.566212            0.033788             0.056314
   20                 0.598097            0.001903             0.003171

A board starting perfectly independent reaches 0.598097 after twenty years, which is 99.6829 per cent of the way to the nomination process's own bias. The half-life of the drift is 2.409421 years and depends only on the turnover rate, not on the bias, so a slower-turning board decays more slowly to the same place. The destination, not the speed, is the point.

   starting share   after 40 years   the steady state   does the start matter?
   0.0000                   0.599994             0.6000   no
   0.3000                   0.599997             0.6000   no
   0.6000                   0.600000             0.6000   no
   0.9000                   0.600003             0.6000   no
   1.0000                   0.600004             0.6000   no

Every starting point converges to 0.6000 and the spread after forty years is 1.01e-05. So a board's original composition is irrelevant in the long run and only the nomination process matters. Appointing good directors achieves nothing durable; changing who nominates them achieves everything. That is a strong claim and it follows from one line of arithmetic rather than from any view about directors.

So what lowers the bias? The summary supplies the answer in its own list without connecting it: a nomination committee led by non-executives and separate from the chief executive.

   who nominates                        bias phi   steady-state aligned   independence
                                                   share of the board     retained
   the chief executive alone                0.9000               0.900000       10.0000%
   the board as a whole, CEO present        0.6000               0.600000       40.0000%
   a non-executive nomination committee     0.3000               0.300000       70.0000%
   shareholders directly                    0.1000               0.100000       90.0000%

Retained independence converges to exactly one less the bias at every bias, so moving nomination from the chief executive to a non-executive committee raises it from 10.0000 per cent to 70.0000, a factor of 7.0000. That is the whole case for a nomination committee, and it is an arithmetic case rather than a procedural one.

4.3 Setting executive salaries by peer comparison has no equilibrium

The summary lists setting executive salaries among the non-executives' roles and managers' remuneration among the marks of good governance. Neither says how a salary is set, and in practice it is set by reference to peers. That procedure has no fixed point at the level anybody intends. Take eight firms paying 40 to 120 and have every board move to the upper quartile of the current distribution.

   round   median pay   upper quartile   the level every    growth in the
                        of the peers     firm moves up to   median, %
   0            75.0000          92.5000                 -               -
   1            92.5000          94.3750           92.5000         23.3333%
   2            94.3750          95.7812           94.3750          2.0270%
   3            95.7812          96.8359           95.7812          1.4901%
   4            96.8359          97.6270           96.8359          1.1011%
   5            97.6270          98.2202           97.6270          0.8169%

The median rises from 75.0000 to 97.6270 over five rounds, a gain of 30.1693 per cent, and no firm ever paid above the upper quartile of its peers. Every board behaved reasonably and the median rose in every single round.

It does terminate, and where it terminates locates the problem precisely. After two hundred rounds every firm but one pays 100.0000, so the terminal median is 100.0000 against an original maximum of 120.0000. The limit is the second-highest original value, not the highest, because the best-paid firm sits above the target percentile and never binds on anybody. The total rise is the ratio of that value to the original median, 1.3333 times. So the ratchet is bounded rather than explosive, and it still collapses an entire distribution onto its own upper tail without any board making an unreasonable decision.

The obvious fix is to aim lower, and it does not work.

   the target percentile   median after 5 rounds   median after 60   does it ratchet?
   0.5000                                  79.8438           80.0000   YES
   0.6000                                  86.7232           90.0000   YES
   0.7500                                  97.6270          100.0000   YES
   0.9000                                 116.6386          120.0000   YES

Targeting the median still ratchets, to 80.0000, because half the firms are below the median and move up to it while none moves down. Notice the pattern in the terminal column: in each case the limit is the original value sitting just above the target position, so a modest target lands lower and still lands above where it started. The ratchet is caused by the one-way adjustment rather than by the choice of percentile, and no percentile fixes it.

Which means the summary's remuneration item cannot be satisfied by benchmarking at all, however modest the target. The only procedures with a fixed point are ones that let pay fall, so an examiner asking how executive pay is controlled is asking for a mechanism that is not comparative: an absolute cap, a ratio to the workforce, or a formula tied to performance in units rather than to percentiles of a peer group.

So the binding constraint on a non-executive is information rather than diligence, which is why the summary gives them a leading role in the audit committee: the external auditor is the only channel in the whole structure that does not run through the executives. That is also why a board's most consequential decision is often the auditor's appointment rather than any strategic choice, and it is the one decision the summary's list assigns to non-executives with no executive dependency at all.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The solvent trading threshold is exact and unobservable, and holding both facts at once is what separates a useful application from a textbook recitation. The threshold sits at the bad-state asset value, which is a forward-looking quantity nobody reports. What is reported is a balance sheet at current values, and a firm can be comfortably solvent on that measure while sitting well past the threshold, because the threshold asks what the assets would fetch in the bad state rather than what they are carried at. In practice the signal is the credit spread: a widening spread is the market pricing the option that section 4.1 says the shareholders own, and it moves before any accounting measure does.

For an equity investor the practical use is inverted from the usual reading. A highly geared company's management is structurally biased towards risk, so its announced strategy should be discounted for that bias rather than taken as a view about opportunity. When such a firm announces an acquisition, an expansion or a change of business mix, section 4.1 says the announcement is consistent with both a good opportunity and a value-destroying gamble, and the two are indistinguishable from the outside. The signal that distinguishes them is whether the board has creditor representation or covenants that bind, which is a governance fact rather than a strategic one.

On section 4.2, the habit worth forming is to read a nomination committee's membership before reading a board's biography page. The arithmetic says a board's composition is a lagging indicator of a process that is itself observable, so the process is the better place to look. A nomination committee containing the chief executive is, on this model, a board whose independence has a known destination regardless of who currently sits on it.

On section 4.3, the honest position is that the ratchet is real, bounded and widely misdescribed. It is usually presented as an explosion, and it is not: it converges, and the total rise is the ratio of the distribution's upper tail to its middle. That is smaller than the rhetoric suggests and larger than any board intends, and the mechanism is a one-way adjustment rather than greed. A board that genuinely wanted to control pay would have to be willing to cut it, which is the one action no comparative procedure can generate.

One limit on section 4.1. The model has two states and one period, and the exact threshold at the bad-state value depends on that. With a continuous distribution of outcomes the sharp cliff becomes a smooth increase in the shareholders' preference for volatility, beginning as soon as default has any positive probability. The direction and the mechanism survive; the exact flat section does not, and the practical version of the result is that the bias exists at any positive default probability and grows with it.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The free-rider threshold that makes internal governance necessary is derived in market conduct and financial services provider regulation, where no holder below 15 per cent intervenes. The tax shield that section 4.1 supplies the offsetting cost to is in taxation of investment returns, where the capitalised shield was exactly the corporate rate times the debt. The merger and gearing arithmetic this lecture's threshold applies to is in structure and financial planning, which also found the summary states the principal-agent relation backwards, and the credit spread that section 5 names as the observable signal is priced in government and corporate bonds. The option structure of the equity claim is set up in derivatives, and the two-term regulatory framework is in the principles of financial regulation.

Where this goes next. Lecture 11 covers responsible investment, whose governance leg is the third of the summary's ESG factors and includes executive pay and board diversity, so section 4.3's ratchet is the quantitative content behind one of those items. Lecture 12 covers listing authorities and competition regulation, where the disclosure obligations are the mechanism that partially relieves section 4.4's information cap. Lecture 13 covers accounting standards, which is where the executives' monopoly on producing the financial statements is constrained from outside, and lecture 14 covers market participants and infrastructure. The six indices lectures then close the module.

Where the bank sits opposite. Section 4.1 is the single most important result in this lecture for a bank, because a bank is the most highly geared institution in the economy and therefore permanently near the threshold. Its equity is a thin sliver above a very large deposit and debt claim, so its shareholders have a structural preference for asset volatility that its depositors do not share, which is the entire reason capital is regulated rather than left to the market: see capital management, where the minimum ratios and buffers exist precisely to keep the bank far enough from the threshold that the preference does not bite. The risk-shifting incentive is also why supervisors intervene before insolvency rather than at it, which is the resolution trigger, and why the loss-absorbing requirements deliberately put creditors in the firing line: a creditor who expects to bear losses prices the gamble and constrains it. Bank board governance is covered directly in the BCBS governance principles, whose separate risk committee and chief risk officer requirements are the banking answer to section 4.4's information cap: a reporting line to the board that does not run through the chief executive.

Numbers used here. The board's three responsibilities, the two director types with five roles each, and the six marks of good corporate governance are transcribed from chapter 3 of the ASSA F105 summary in the user's notes. The risk-shifting result is Jensen and Meckling (1976), sometimes called asset substitution. One observation is recorded in the summary's favour: its fourth mark of good governance, considering all stakeholders and not only personal interest, reads as a general aspiration and turns out to have a precise trigger, since below the threshold a board may serve shareholders exclusively and harm nobody while above it every shareholder gain is a creditor loss; the examinable answer gives the six marks as written and adds that the fourth is a statement about creditors when read alongside solvent trading. A second point is recorded against the summary's structure rather than its content: it lists non-executive independence and non-executive nomination of new board members as separate items without noting that the second determines the first in steady state. Everything numerical is invented for illustration: assets worth 100 safe against gambles of 140 and 60 down to 120 and 20 with debt from 20 to 90, a board turnover of a quarter a year with nomination biases from 0.1 to 0.9 over horizons to forty years, eight firms paying 40 to 120 with target percentiles from the median to the ninetieth, and information shares from 40 to 100 per cent against 90 per cent diligence. Twenty-three claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions, all twenty-three passed, and none can pass without the computation. Three claims failed on the first run, all three asserting that the pay ratchet converges on the original maximum; it converges on the second-highest original value, because the best-paid firm sits above the target percentile and never binds on anybody, and the corrected version generalises to the value sitting just above the target position at any percentile. Two further claims were flagged as unfalsifiable by a vacuity check, both asserting arithmetic on literal constants, and both were rewritten to run the convergence and read the answer off it. Section 4.2's three tables were also replaced during checking after being drafted from memory rather than from the script's output. Every headline finding states the region in which it holds: the solvent trading threshold is exact in a two-state one-period model and becomes a smooth increase in the preference for volatility under a continuous distribution, beginning at any positive default probability; the board drift result depends on the turnover rate for its speed and not at all for its destination; and the pay ratchet is bounded by the original distribution's upper tail rather than explosive, and is caused by the one-way adjustment rather than by the target.
