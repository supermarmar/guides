Step 1

The 30-second version

The summary gives a ten-sector taxonomy with detailed characteristics, two reasons analysts specialise, two problems with the scheme, a cyclical and defensive sort, and five alternative classifications. Counting the entries in the cyclical and defensive lists reveals that the scheme fails on its own terms, and the summary itself says why.

Ten sectors, seven cyclical entries and six defensive ones. Thirteen entries against ten sectors means three are listed twice: consumer goods, consumer services and telecommunications. No sector is missing from both lists, so this is a double count rather than a gap, and the classification is ambiguous for 30.00 per cent of its own units.

The summary supplies the explanation two lines earlier under "problems": heterogeneous firms within a sector, by size or niche operations. It even writes "goods (non-durable)" in the defensive list, which is an admission that the sector splits. So the diagnosis and the defect are on the same page and never joined. The ambiguity is a property of aggregation, since any classification with fewer units than distinct behaviours must contain ambiguous units, and ten sectors against thirteen behavioural slots is exactly that.

Second, lecture 3 showed an equity's duration is one over the discount rate net of growth, which is a different property from cyclicality. Building the two-by-two, a defensive utility has a duration of 33.3333 years and a cyclical bank 12.5000, so the defensive sector is the rate-sensitive one. "Defensive" is therefore not a synonym for "safe": a utility loses 33.33 per cent on a one-point rate rise against 0.90 from a growth shock, so an investor rotating to defensives in a downturn caused by rate rises has rotated into the damage. The summary's own sector notes contain eight phrases describing both axes, four on each, and never separate them.

Third, whether analyst specialisation is efficient reduces to one number. The sector explains exactly the intra-sector correlation of a stock's variance, so at a correlation of 0.05 it explains 5.00 per cent and the firm explains 95.00. And the summary's own "problems" item predicts where that correlation is low, since a heterogeneous sector has a low intra-sector correlation by construction. The sectors where specialisation is least useful are the same three that appear in both lists.

Finally the conglomerate, which the summary names and does not price. Step 2

Why this matters

Sector classification looks like bookkeeping and it decides what a great deal of investment analysis measures. Every sector index, every attribution analysis of the kind the performance measurement module built, every risk model's sector factors and every analyst's coverage list rests on the taxonomy. If it is ambiguous for three of ten units, then an attribution that assigns return to a sector effect is assigning it to a category that does not have a single behaviour, and the resulting number is not wrong so much as undefined.

The two-axis finding matters more directly. An investment committee rotating towards defensives in a downturn is making an implicit bet that the downturn is growth-driven, because section 4.2 shows the same rotation losing money if it is rate-driven. That is precisely the distinction lecture 3 made exact, and putting the two lectures together gives a testable procedure: identify the shock, then rotate on the axis it acts on. Rotating on the summary's single axis is right half the time.

The specialisation result matters for how research is organised. A firm that allocates analysts by sector has implicitly assumed that sector explains most of what moves a stock, and the intra-sector correlation is measurable from the data every firm already holds. Where it is low, the sector analyst is an expert in a category rather than in the companies, and the coverage would be better organised by something else, which is what the summary's five alternatives offer.

And the conglomerate result is a warning about measurement generally. The failure mode there is not a wrong answer but a plausible one: a sector beta of 0.68 looks entirely reasonable and is 70 per cent above the truth. That pattern, where contamination degrades a number without breaching any threshold that would flag it, recurs throughout applied finance, and the only defence is knowing which units are contaminated rather than checking whether the outputs look odd.

Step 3

The mental model

A classification is a claim that the things inside a box behave alike. It fails when the box contains two kinds of thing, and it fails silently when one of them is large.

Sorting shares into industries is meant to save work. If everything in a box moves together, you can study the box instead of the contents.

So test the boxes. The summary sorts ten sectors into cyclical and defensive, and if you count the entries there are thirteen. Three sectors are in both lists. Consumer goods is cyclical when you think of cars and defensive when you think of bread, and it is one box containing both.

That is not carelessness. It is arithmetic: you cannot put thirteen behaviours into ten boxes without doubling up somewhere. Finer boxes fix it and cost you the thing you wanted, which was fewer boxes to study.

Now the second problem, which is subtler. Being defensive means your earnings hold up when the economy weakens. Having a long duration means your value falls a long way when interest rates rise. Those are two different properties and a utility has both: steady earnings and very distant cash flows.

So a "defensive" utility is the safest thing you can own in a recession caused by weak demand and among the worst in one caused by rising rates. One word, two opposite answers, depending on something the word does not mention.

And then the conglomerate. Put a firm doing five things into one sector and that sector's measured behaviour shifts towards the firm. Make it big enough and the sector's beta rises 70 per cent, and the number still looks perfectly ordinary. Nothing breaks. You simply have a wrong figure that passes every sanity check, which is the most dangerous kind of wrong figure there is.

Formally, if a sector's true beta is b and a conglomerate of weight w and beta c sits inside it, the measured beta is (1-w)b + wc, so the error is w(c - b): proportional to the weight and to the difference in behaviour, and zero when either is zero. And where the intra-sector correlation is rho, the sector explains exactly rho of a constituent's variance, leaving 1 - rho to the individual firm.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Three habits for using a sector scheme without being misled by it.

Identify the shock before rotating, because section 4.2 shows the rotation depends on which axis it acts on. The practical procedure is two steps: read whether rates or growth expectations moved more, which lecture 3 made an exact condition, then rotate on the corresponding axis. Rotating on the cyclical-defensive axis when the shock is a rate shock is not a marginal error: it is the wrong direction, and the 2022 experience of defensive sectors underperforming in a downturn was not an anomaly but the arithmetic working correctly against a one-dimensional heuristic.

Know which of your sectors are contaminated, and by how much. Section 4.4's failure mode is a plausible wrong number rather than an implausible one, so it cannot be caught by inspecting outputs. What can be done is cheap: list the largest constituent of each sector index, note whether it is a conglomerate, and compute w(c - b). Two or three sectors in most markets will show a material figure, and knowing which they are is worth more than any adjustment, because it tells you which sector-level conclusions to distrust rather than requiring you to distrust all of them.

Test whether your research is organised on the right axis by measuring the intra-sector correlation. Section 4.3 makes this a single calculation on data every firm holds, and the answer varies enormously across sectors: mining companies move together and conglomerates do not. Where the correlation is low, a sector analyst is an expert in a label, and the coverage would be better organised by size, by gearing, or by geography. That is an uncomfortable conclusion for a research department structured by sector, which is why the measurement is rarely made.

On the summary, two observations are recorded and they have the same shape. Its cyclical and defensive lists contain thirteen entries across ten sectors, so three sectors are classified as both, and its own "problems" item two lines earlier explains exactly why. And its sector notes contain eight phrases describing two independent axes, four on each, before it sorts on one of them. In both cases the summary holds the material to identify its own limitation and stops just short of joining it up, which is the characteristic failure mode of a compressed list rather than an error in its contents.
