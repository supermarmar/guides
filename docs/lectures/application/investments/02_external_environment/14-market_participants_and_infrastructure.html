The investment bank's three objectives are one objective and two constraints

The summary wants the best possible price, the lowest issue cost and the funding requirement met. Those order the price three different ways: 50.6544 for a 95 per cent chance of placement, 114.8345 for maximum expected proceeds and 140.0000 for maximum proceeds if it clears. And the second is not independent, because the underwriting premium rises with the very price the first objective wants. Along the way: intermediation stops paying at a wealth of exactly 80,000 over the holding period, and stock lending buys 0.2000 per cent of income with the vote.

Step 1

The 30-second version

This closes the environment module and the summary's own chapter 4. Much of that chapter was priced earlier: overview lecture 6 handled mergers, the cash conversion cycle and the economic order quantity, and overview lecture 1 handled the intermediation cost chain and valued the custodian's withholding tax reclaim at 150 per cent of the manager's fee. Three things were left, and all three turn out to be exact.

The summary gives an investment bank three objectives: the best possible price, the lowest possible issue cost and meeting the funding requirement. Read together they conflict, because a higher price makes full subscription less likely. On an issue of 100 shares with demand falling one for one and a standard deviation of 30, the three objectives order the price three different ways: 50.6544 for a 95 per cent chance of placement, 114.8345 for maximum expected proceeds and 140.0000 for maximum proceeds if the issue clears. The summary lists them as three objectives and they are one objective and two constraints. Worse, the second is not independent either: underwriting is the put option lecture 12 priced, its premium rises with the chance of undersubscription, and that chance rises with the price, so all three are the same dial.

The summary says households need intermediaries and lists lower dealing costs among the advantages. True up to a point, and the point is computable. A direct investor pays a minimum charge on every trade, so thirty stocks costs thirty minimums, and a fee is paid every year while a dealing cost is paid once. The crossover wealth is therefore exactly 80,000 divided by the holding period: 80,000 at one year and 8,000 at ten. So the households that need an intermediary on cost grounds are the small and frequently trading ones, which is the reverse of how the advantage is usually claimed.

But the cost comparison is not the real case for intermediation. One stock carries 35.0000 per cent volatility against a floor of 19.1707 set by the correlation, and thirty stocks reaches 19.9018, leaving only 2.0902 per cent of the diversifiable risk. Thirty trades needs 60,000.0000 of wealth at a 1 per cent minimum, so an investor who cannot afford thirty trades cannot reach diversification at any price. That is the summary's first advantage and it is much larger than its fourth.

Finally, the summary lists stock lending and the exercise of voting rights among the same eight custodian services without noting that the first destroys the second. Half a holding on loan yields 0.2000 per cent of the whole and costs half the voting power. Applying lecture 9's 15 per cent intervention threshold, a 25 per cent holder lending out 40 per cent of the stake reaches an effective 0.150000, exactly at the threshold, where acting gains nothing.

Step 2

Why this matters

The module has spent thirteen lectures on the environment an investor reacts to. This one names the people in it, and the useful discovery is that most of the summary's participants are intermediaries being paid by one of three parties who pay: the household, the business and the foreign investor. Section 4.4 sets that out, and it is the frame that makes the whole chapter legible rather than a list.

The pricing result in section 4.1 has direct practical content for reading any new issue. A bank pursuing the issuer's best price is not pursuing the issuer's expected proceeds, and the gap between those two prices is large: 114.8345 against 140.0000 in the worked case. A bank pursuing certainty of placement prices lower still. So an investor asking why an issue was priced where it was should ask which of the three objectives the bank was actually serving, and the answer is usually the third, because a failed issue damages the bank's franchise more than a cheap one damages the issuer's proceeds.

The stock lending result matters because it is a live governance problem that most funds handle badly. The revenue is small, visible and easy to attribute to the lending programme. The cost is a lost vote, invisible unless a resolution happens to fall while the stock is out, and attributable to nobody. That asymmetry in visibility is why lending programmes grow, and section 4.3 gives the number that should bound them.

The Feynman version. You are selling a hundred shares and you want three things: a high price, cheap help, and to actually sell them all. Push the price up and fewer people want them, so you might not sell them all. Push it down and you sell out instantly but you left money behind. And the person underwriting the leftovers charges you more the higher you price, because they are the one stuck with what does not sell.

So you cannot have all three. You have one dial and three things you would like it to do. The honest way to describe the job is that you are maximising something, subject to two things that must not go wrong, and the summary's list of three objectives hides which is which.

Step 3

The mental model

Every participant in this lecture exists because somebody cannot do something themselves. A household cannot afford thirty trades, so an intermediary pools. A business cannot certify its own prospectus credibly, so a bank certifies. An investor cannot settle a foreign trade, so a custodian settles. Ask what each participant is a substitute for and the list becomes a set of make-or-buy decisions, each with a crossover.

The crossovers all have the same shape, and it is the shape lecture 13 found in the spread: a fixed cost of doing it yourself against a proportional cost of paying somebody, which means the answer depends on size and on how often you do it. Section 4.2's crossover wealth of 80,000 over the holding period is that shape stated exactly, and it recurs because it is the general form of every intermediation question.

The second organising idea is that a service can destroy another service sold by the same provider. Section 4.3's lending and voting are the clear case, and it generalises: a custodian's cash management can conflict with its settlement obligation, and a bank's underwriting can conflict with its advice on price. The summary lists services as menus, and the useful question about a menu is which items cannot be ordered together.

4.1 The three objectives order the price three different ways

The summary gives an investment bank three objectives: the best possible price, the lowest possible issue cost and meeting the funding requirement. Take an issue of 100 shares where expected demand falls one for one with the price and has a standard deviation of 30 shares.

Expected proceeds peak at a price of 114.834490 at 9092.6393, located by a search, and fall away on both sides. The proceeds if fully placed rise all the way to 14000.0000 at a price of 140.0000 and have no maximum in range. And a 95 per cent chance of full subscription requires a price of 50.654391.

So the three objectives order the price three different ways: 50.6544 for confidence of placement, 114.8345 for expected proceeds and 140.0000 for proceeds if it clears. The summary lists them as three objectives and they are one objective and two constraints. An examiner asking a bank to reconcile them is asking a candidate to notice that they cannot be reconciled, only ranked.

And the second objective is not independent either. Underwriting is the put option lecture 12 priced, its premium rises with the chance of undersubscription, and that chance rises with the price: the probability of full subscription is 0.6306 at a price of 90 against 0.3694 at 110. So pursuing the best price raises the issue cost through the very fee the second objective wants to minimise, and all three of the summary's objectives are the same dial.

That means the summary's advantage clause is right about the mechanism and wrong about the population. The households that need an intermediary on cost grounds are the small and frequently trading ones, and a household saving 200,000 for thirty years is above every crossover in the table. The usual claim, that ordinary savers need pooled vehicles because dealing is expensive, holds for small balances and inverts for large ones.

The region is why intermediaries exist anyway, because the comparison above counts only dealing costs and the summary's first advantage is diversification.

4.3 Stock lending sells one custodian service by destroying another

The summary lists stock lending and the exercise of voting rights among the same eight custodian services, without noting that a lent share carries its vote to the borrower. Price both sides.

That is an unattractive exchange if the vote is worth anything, and lecture 9 says exactly when it is. A 25 per cent holder can intervene, and the same holder with 40 per cent of the stake on loan has an effective 0.150000, landing exactly at the threshold where acting gains nothing. An 18 per cent holder is pushed below it by lending only 20 per cent. So lending revenue of 0.8000 on a 1,000 holding buys the loss of the only instrument that changes a company's behaviour, which is a bad trade at almost any fee rate.

Which is why stewardship codes require a recall policy rather than a lending ban. The revenue is genuine and the vote only matters on the days a resolution is put, so recalling lent stock before a vote captures both. A custodian offering lending without a recall process is selling one of the summary's eight services by destroying another, and the asymmetry that lets this persist is that the revenue appears in a report and the forgone vote does not.

The summary separates the capital budgeting decision from the financing decision and gives them to different people, which is correct and has a consequence it does not draw: the person choosing projects does not set the cost of capital those projects are discounted at, so the hurdle rate is an internal transfer price, and every problem with transfer pricing applies to it.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The habit worth taking from this lecture is to ask, of any participant charging a fee, what the client would have to do instead and what that would cost. Section 4.2 does it for the intermediary and gets an exact crossover. The same question applied to a custodian gives a different answer, because self-custody is not merely expensive but unavailable to most investors, which is why custody fees survive competition better than management fees do. The general rule is that a service whose alternative is impossible prices differently from one whose alternative is merely dear.

On section 4.1, the professional judgement is that banks serve the third objective and describe the first. A failed issue is a visible failure attributed to the bank; a cheap issue is an invisible cost borne by the issuer. So the equilibrium is a price near the placement-confidence end of the range rather than near the expected-proceeds optimum, and the persistent first-day pop in new issues is that choice showing up in the data. Lecture 12's winner's curse explains part of the discount and this asymmetry of blame explains the rest.

On stock lending, the number to remember is 0.2000 per cent for half a holding. Lending programmes are almost always defended as free money, and they are not free: they are 20 basis points of income against a contingent loss of governance influence and a counterparty exposure that is small but not zero. A fund with a genuine stewardship policy and an unrestricted lending programme holds two positions that cannot both be sincere.

One limit on section 4.2. The crossover assumes the direct investor achieves the same portfolio as the intermediary, which the diversification table shows is false below 60,000 of wealth. So the two halves of that section do not compose into a single rule: below the diversification threshold the intermediary is the only option, between it and the crossover the intermediary is cheaper, and above the crossover direct holding wins on cost while still requiring the investor to do the work. The summary's four advantages and four disadvantages are the right list; what it lacks is the observation that they bind in different wealth ranges.

And a limit on section 4.1. The demand curve is linear with normal noise, which gives the clean hump and the single optimum. Real demand for a new issue is neither, and it responds to the price the bank sets in ways the model treats as exogenous, since a low price attracts momentum demand that a high price does not. The structure survives, which is that the three objectives imply three different prices and the gap is large. The specific figures do not.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The intermediation cost chain and the custodian's withholding tax reclaim are priced in the business model, whose minimum dealing charge finding section 4.2 develops into an exact crossover. The underwriting put and the issue discount that section 4.1 builds on are in listing authorities and competition regulation, and the 15 per cent intervention threshold section 4.3 applies is in market conduct and financial services provider regulation. The amortisation logic shared with the crossover is in accounting standards and the investor, the diversification arithmetic is in portfolio risk and return, the mergers and working capital material the summary places in this chapter is in structure and financial planning, and the solvent trading threshold the board answers to past a certain gearing is in corporate governance.

Where this goes next. This completes the fourteen environment lectures and the whole of the summary's chapters 2 to 4. The six indices lectures follow, and two results here feed straight into them: section 4.2's diversification table is the same computation an index performs when it reports an effective number of constituents, and section 4.3's voting question is why index providers publish separate policies on securities lending inside index-tracking funds. The indices sub-module then closes the investments track, leaving the life insurance and actuarial risk management tracks.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank appears three times in the summary's own list and each appearance is a different business. As an investment bank it faces section 4.1's pricing problem and carries the underwriting exposure on its trading book, treated in market risk. As a custodian it earns the fee income of section 4.3 while holding client assets off its own balance sheet, which is why custody is a fee business with operational rather than credit risk, quantified in operational risk. And as the counterparty to a stock loan it is exposed to the borrower against collateral, which is the counterparty credit exposure computed in counterparty credit risk: a securities financing transaction is the textbook case there, and the haircut on the collateral is what section 4.3's fee income is really being paid for. The intermediation logic of section 4.2 is also a bank's own definition, since a bank is the intermediary the summary describes, selling deposits to buy loans, as set out in the bank business model.

Two observations are recorded against the summary. First, it presents the investment bank's three objectives as jointly achievable when they order the price three different ways, at 50.6544, 114.8345 and 140.0000, and the second is not independent of the first because the underwriting premium rises with the price; the examinable answer gives the three as written and adds that they are one objective and two constraints. Second, its intermediation advantage clause names lower dealing costs as though that were the operative benefit, when the crossover wealth is 80,000 over the holding period and the binding advantage below that is diversification, which a small investor cannot reach at any price; the examinable answer gives the four advantages and four disadvantages as written and adds that they bind in different wealth ranges. A third, milder point: it lists stock lending and the exercise of voting rights as parallel services without noting that the first consumes the second. Everything numerical is invented for illustration: an issue of 100 shares with demand of 200 less the price and a standard deviation of 30 across prices from 85 to 140, a minimum dealing charge of 20 with a 0.5 per cent rate and a 0.75 per cent annual fee over wealths to a million and horizons to ten years, single-stock volatility of 35 per cent at a 0.3 correlation across one to thirty holdings, a lending fee of 0.4 per cent on a holding of 1,000 with up to half on loan, and lecture 9's intervention cost of 12 against an uplift of 80. Eighteen claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions, all eighteen passed, and none can pass without the computation. One claim failed on the first run, asserting an interior optimum for expected proceeds within a price range that turned out to be entirely on the rising side; extending the range and adding a ternary search located the peak at 114.834490. Two table labels were also corrected during checking: one row reported a value as below a threshold when it sits exactly on it, and two column headers printed a doubled percent sign. Every headline finding states the region in which it holds: the pricing result assumes linear demand with normal noise and exogenous demand, so the three-way ordering survives while the figures do not; the crossover is exact for any minimum charge, rate, fee and horizon, but composes with the diversification result only as three wealth ranges rather than one rule; and the lending arithmetic is linear in the share on loan, with the threshold effect depending on lecture 9's cost-to-uplift ratio rather than on the lending fee.
