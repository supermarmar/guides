A geometric index falls 13.3975 per cent in a market where the average stock did not move

One stock up 50 per cent and one down 50 leaves an arithmetic index at exactly 100.000000 and takes a geometric index to 86.602540, the square root of three quarters. The gap is half the variance of the price relatives, confirmed to 1.000025 times at a small spread, and it compounds: at a constant 20 per cent annual spread the geometric index loses 39.9669 per cent over twenty-five years while the market is flat throughout. Along the way: without a divisor rebase a rights issue makes the index jump 15.6250 per cent though not one price moved.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Lecture 1 took the eight construction factors and the five purposes and left the algebra aside. This lecture takes the algebra: three formulas for combining prices, and one formula with a divisor in it whose whole purpose is contained in a sentence the summary states and does not demonstrate. Both halves turn out to be exactly computable.

The summary calls both the unweighted and the geometric index unsuitable. Lecture 1 dealt with the unweighted one. The geometric fails differently and worse, and two stocks are enough to show it: one up 50 per cent and one down 50 leaves an arithmetic index at exactly 100.000000 and takes a geometric index to 86.602540, which is exactly the square root of three quarters. So a geometric index reports a 13.3975 per cent fall in a market where the average stock did not move.

The size of the gap is not arbitrary. It is half the variance of the price relatives, and the approximation is second order: at a spread of 1 per cent the gap is 1.000025 times half the variance, at 5 per cent it is 1.000626, and at 10 per cent 1.002513. So the bias grows with volatility, which is the worst possible property for an index used to estimate long-run returns.

And it compounds rather than wobbling. At a constant 20 per cent annual spread the arithmetic index is flat at 100.0000 throughout, and the geometric index falls to 97.9796 after one year, 81.5373 after ten and 60.0331 after twenty-five, a shortfall of 39.9669 per cent of the market that is entirely bias.

The second half is the divisor. The summary's chain-linking formula divides by a quantity and says the index must change only on price. Put a three-stock index through a rights issue at the market price, where the company gains cash and its shareholders gain nothing: leaving the divisor alone makes the index jump from 100.000000 to 115.625000, a spurious 15.6250 per cent though not one price moved, and the jump is exactly the proportional rise in market capitalisation. Rebasing the divisor to 37.0000 leaves the index at exactly 100.000000 while a subsequent 10 per cent rise in every price still moves it by exactly 10 per cent. So the divisor removes the corporate action and leaves the price response intact, which makes it a running record of every corporate action the index has ever absorbed.

Step 2

Why this matters

Two practical consequences follow, and both are the kind of thing that goes wrong in real work rather than in exams.

The first is that the geometric index is not a historical curiosity. It was the construction of the FT30 for decades, which means any long-run study using that series is reading a downward-biased number, and the bias is larger in exactly the volatile decades people most want to study. Section 4.1 gives the correction: add back half the cross-sectional variance per period. That the summary calls the construction unsuitable is right, and knowing by how much is what lets you use an old series rather than discard it.

The second is that an index cannot be rebuilt from a price history. Section 4.2 shows the divisor moves on every corporate action, so a researcher reconstructing an index from prices and share counts will get a different series from the published one unless they have the divisor file too. Anybody who has tried to replicate a published index level from raw data has met this, usually as an unexplained level shift on a date that turns out to be a rights issue.

For the exam the useful content is that the summary's sentence about the index changing only due to price is a design requirement rather than an observation. Five of its six chain-link triggers are things the divisor exists to suppress, and one is the signal. Section 4.3 sorts them.

The Feynman version. You own two shares, one at 100 and one at 100. One doubles to 150 and the other halves to 50. On average you are exactly where you started: 200 in, 200 out.

Now average them the other way. Multiply the two changes together instead of adding them: 1.5 times 0.5 is 0.75, and the square root of that is 0.866. The geometric average says you lost 13 per cent. You did not. Multiplying punishes spread, because a halving takes away more than a doubling gives back when you multiply. Do that every year and it eats the number alive, which is exactly what happened to the old thirty-share index for fifty years.

The divisor is a different sort of trick. If a company issues more shares, the total value of the company goes up without anybody being richer. So you make the number you divide by go up by the same proportion, and the index does not move. It is a fudge factor that carries the memory of every share issued since the index began.

Step 3

The mental model

Hold an index as two separate machines. One combines many price changes into one number, and the choice of how to combine them is section 4.1. The other keeps that number comparable with yesterday's when the constituents themselves change, and that is section 4.2. The first is a statistical choice and the second is a bookkeeping requirement, and they fail in different ways.

The statistical choice comes down to whether you add or multiply. Adding gives the arithmetic mean, which answers the question "what did the average pound do", and that is the question an investor is asking. Multiplying gives the geometric mean, which answers "what was the typical growth rate", and the two differ by half the variance whenever the returns are not all identical. Because the arithmetic mean is what a portfolio actually earns, an index built by multiplying cannot be held: no portfolio produces the geometric average of its constituents' returns.

The bookkeeping requirement is simpler and easier to get wrong. Market capitalisation changes for two reasons, prices and share counts, and an index wants only the first. The divisor absorbs the second. That gives the design rule: whenever a corporate action changes market capitalisation without changing what a shareholder holds, the divisor changes by the same proportion so the level does not move.

Step 4

The detail

4.1 The geometric index and its exact bias

The summary gives three formulas. Take two stocks, one up 50 per cent and one down 50, and apply all three.

   the index type        value after the   change from   what it says about
                         two moves         100          the average stock
   unweighted arithmetic          100.000000       0.0000%   unchanged, correctly
   geometric                       86.602540     -13.3975%   DOWN, though nothing fell on average
   cap-weighted, equal caps        100.000000       0.0000%   unchanged, correctly

The arithmetic index is exactly unchanged and the geometric one falls to 86.602540, which is exactly the square root of three quarters. So a geometric index reports a fall in a market where the average stock did not move, and the error is 13.3975 per cent rather than a rounding difference. Now vary the spread.

   return spread   arithmetic   geometric   the gap in   half the variance
   plus or minus   index        index       per cent     of the relatives
   0.0000              1.000000    1.000000     0.000000          0.000000
   0.1000              1.000000    0.994987     0.005013          0.005000
   0.2000              1.000000    0.979796     0.020204          0.020000
   0.3000              1.000000    0.953939     0.046061          0.045000
   0.5000              1.000000    0.866025     0.133975          0.125000

The gap is exactly zero when every stock moves identically, and it tracks the last column closely. The relationship is exact to second order: the gap is 1.000025 times half the variance at a spread of 1 per cent, 1.000626 at 5 per cent and 1.002513 at 10 per cent, converging on one as the moves shrink. So the understatement is half the variance of the price relatives, and it grows with volatility.

And the bias compounds rather than wobbling, which is what makes it fatal.

   years at a 20%   arithmetic index   geometric index   the shortfall   as a % of
   annual spread    level              level             accumulated     the market
   1                          100.0000           97.9796          2.0204       2.0204%
   5                          100.0000           90.2980          9.7020       9.7020%
   10                         100.0000           81.5373         18.4627      18.4627%
   25                         100.0000           60.0331         39.9669      39.9669%

The arithmetic index is exactly flat throughout, so the entire shortfall is construction bias rather than market movement. After twenty-five years the geometric index stands at 60.0331 against a market at 100.0000, a shortfall of 39.9669 per cent. That rules out four of lecture 1's five purposes, since anything involving more than one period is corrupted, and it leaves only the narrow use the summary itself concedes: an indication of short-term price movements.

There is a deeper reason a geometric index cannot be used as a benchmark, and it is worth stating separately from the bias. No portfolio produces the geometric average of its constituents' returns. A portfolio earns the weighted arithmetic mean by construction, so a geometric index is not the performance of anything anybody could have held, which disqualifies it as a benchmark regardless of the size of the bias.

4.2 What the divisor is for

The summary's chain-linking formula divides by a quantity and requires that the index change only on price. Take three stocks with 100, 200 and 300 shares at prices of 10, 5 and 4, giving a market capitalisation of 3200.0000, and set the divisor to 32.0000 so the index starts at 100. Now stock 1 issues 50 new shares at the market price of 10: the company receives 500 of cash and its shareholders are neither better nor worse off, so no index should move.

   treatment                   market cap   divisor    index level   correct?
   before the issue               3200.0000    32.0000    100.000000   n/a
   no divisor change              3700.0000    32.0000    115.625000   NO: a spurious jump
   divisor rebased                3700.0000    37.0000    100.000000   yes, unchanged

Without a divisor change the index jumps to 115.625000, a spurious rise of 15.6250 per cent though not one price moved, and that jump is exactly the proportional rise in market capitalisation. With the divisor rebased to 37.0000 the index stays at exactly 100.000000. So the error scales with the size of the corporate action rather than with anything about the market, which is why a large rights issue in a large constituent is where reconstruction attempts break.

The other half of the requirement is that the fix must not damp the real signal.

   what happens next          market cap   index level   change   is that right?
   nothing                       3700.0000    100.000000   0.0000%   yes, no move
   stock 1 rises 10%             3850.0000    104.054054   4.0541%   yes
   all prices rise 10%           4070.0000    110.000000  10.0000%   yes

A 10 per cent rise in stock 1 moves the index by 4.054054 per cent, which is exactly that stock's post-issue weight of 0.405405 times ten per cent, and a 10 per cent rise in every price moves it by exactly 10 per cent. The divisor removes the corporate action and leaves the price response intact, which is precisely what the summary's sentence means.

Which tells you what the divisor is: a running record of every corporate action the index has ever absorbed. It moved from 32.0000 to 37.0000 on one rights issue. An index level is therefore not reconstructible from prices alone, which is why providers publish divisor files and why a hand-built index from a price history will disagree with the published series at every corporate action.

The rights issue row deserves a second look, because it is the one case where a price genuinely moves for a non-market reason. A rights issue priced below the market dilutes the existing price mechanically.

   rights terms          new shares   issue   theoretical   the fall is a
                         per 100      price   ex-rights     real price move?
   25 for 100 at 10           25.0000 10.0000     10.000000   no move at all
   25 for 100 at 8            25.0000  8.0000      9.600000   no, it is dilution
   50 for 100 at 6            50.0000  6.0000      8.666667   no, it is dilution

A rights issue at the market price leaves the theoretical price at exactly 10.000000, so there is nothing to adjust beyond the share count. One at 8 takes it to 9.600000, and the shareholder is no worse off: they lose 40.0000 on a hundred old shares and gain exactly 40.0000 on twenty-five new ones. So the index must absorb both the share count and the price fall, which makes a rights issue the hardest of the six triggers and the one most often got wrong.

Notice the general form of that last identity, because it is what makes the adjustment computable at all: the loss on the existing holding and the gain on the subscription are equal and opposite, which is the same conservation structure that appeared in lecture 8's duration transfer between beneficiaries and lecture 10's shareholder-creditor transfer. Whenever a corporate action leaves total value unchanged, an index adjustment exists that leaves the level unchanged, and the adjustment is found by writing down what cancels.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The habit worth forming is to ask, of any index series you are handed, what happened at each level discontinuity. Section 4.2 shows the divisor absorbs corporate actions so the level should be continuous through them, which means a genuine discontinuity is either a real market event or a data error, and the two are distinguishable only by checking the corporate action calendar. A great deal of spurious empirical finance comes from treating an unadjusted level shift as a return.

On section 4.1, the practical point for anybody using long historical series is that the correction is available. Add back half the cross-sectional variance per period and a geometric series becomes comparable with an arithmetic one, to second order. That is a real repair rather than a caveat, and it matters because the pre-1960 data for several markets exists only in geometric form. Discarding the series loses the history; correcting it loses only the third-order term.

The professional judgement on the arithmetic-versus-geometric question more broadly: the two answer different questions and the confusion is perennial. An arithmetic mean of returns is the right input to a forward-looking expected return, and a geometric mean is the right description of a realised outcome over a period. Both are correct in their place, and the error the summary is condemning is not using the geometric mean but using it cross-sectionally, across stocks at a point in time, where it has no interpretation at all. A geometric mean across time is meaningful; across stocks it is a number no portfolio earns.

One limit on section 4.1. The half-variance relation is a second-order Taylor result, so it degrades as the spread grows: at a 50 per cent spread the gap is 0.133975 against half-variance of 0.125000, an error of about 7 per cent in the correction itself. For monthly or daily data that is immaterial and for annual data in a volatile market it is not, so the correction should be applied at the highest frequency available and then compounded, rather than applied to annual figures directly.

And a limit on section 4.2. The worked example rebases the divisor for an issue at the market price, which is the clean case. Real corporate actions are messier: a scrip dividend, a demerger or a partial tender all change share counts and prices together in ways that require a judgement about what the shareholder actually holds afterwards. The principle is unchanged, which is that the divisor absorbs whatever leaves shareholder value untouched, but applying it requires knowing the economics of the action rather than only its arithmetic.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The eight construction factors and the five purposes this lecture's formulas serve are in why indices exist and the eight construction choices, which also showed that a cap-weighted holding self-rebalances while an equally weighted one does not, and that the summary's verdict on the unweighted index rests on that rather than on performance. The arithmetic and geometric mean distinction is developed in statistics, and the conservation structure section 4.3 identifies recurs from trust law and fiduciary duty, where liability-weighted duration mismatches summed to zero, and corporate governance, where a shareholder gain was exactly a creditor loss. The rights issue mechanics are set up in listing authorities and competition regulation.

Where this goes next. Lecture 3 covers total return indices, taking the two construction factors deferred from lecture 1: dividend reinvestment and the gross or net tax basis. That is the same divisor machinery applied to income rather than to share counts, and the arithmetic is closely related, since a dividend also reduces a price without making the holder poorer. Lecture 4 covers the equity index families, where the constituent-change trigger of section 4.3 becomes the quarterly review calendar and the SWIX adjustment becomes a share-count question rather than a price one. Lectures 5 and 6 cover bond and money market indices, where chain-linking is harder because constituents mature rather than merely leave, and then unlisted indices, where there are no prices to chain-link at all.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank meets the divisor as a settlement problem rather than a measurement one. Every index derivative it writes settles against a published level, so a divisor error is a direct cash loss on expiry rather than a reporting inaccuracy, which is why index futures reference a specific published series with a named calculation agent rather than a construction rule. The exposure sits in market risk. More directly, the arithmetic of section 4.1 is the same distinction a bank must handle in reporting portfolio returns over multiple periods, where a time-weighted return is geometric and a money-weighted return is not, and the choice determines whether the number describes the manager or the client: the treatment sits alongside the performance attribution in performance measurement. And the rights issue adjustment of section 4.3 is the same calculation a bank performs on its own collateral positions, since a corporate action on pledged stock changes what is held without changing its value, covered in counterparty credit risk.
