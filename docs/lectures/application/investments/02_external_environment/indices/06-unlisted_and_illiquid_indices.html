The summary's one word, subjectivity, is a systematic halving of a reported risk number

A valuer anchoring 60 per cent on the previous valuation reports 9.0000 per cent volatility against a true 18.0000, exactly half, at every level of true volatility. A simulation confirms it at 0.498925 with an autocorrelation of 0.597702 against the predicted 0.6. Fed to an optimiser, the understated risk lifts the property weight from 0.3864 to 0.8254 and lets the investor believe a Sharpe ratio of 0.477552 for a portfolio delivering 0.267645. So the summary's three words are one mechanism, and four of the five mechanisms this sub-module built fail outright here.

Step 1

The 30-second version

This closes the indices sub-module and the investments track. The summary devotes one line to unlisted indices: surveyors' valuations are an alternative to prices, with subjectivity, cost and circularity. Three words, no arithmetic, and all three turn out to be the same mechanism seen from different angles, with a consequence that reaches into asset allocation.

Subjectivity means a valuer anchors on the previous valuation, so the reported return is a blend of this period's truth and last period's report. That is an exact filter. At a weight of 0.4 on current truth the reported volatility is 9.0000 per cent against a true 18.0000, exactly half, and the ratio is 0.500000 at every level of true volatility, so it is a property of the filter rather than of the asset. A simulation over two hundred thousand periods confirms it at 0.498925, with a first-order autocorrelation of 0.597702 against the predicted 0.6, which is both the signature of the smoothing and the means of undoing it.

The consequence the summary does not reach is what happens when those figures meet an optimiser. On the true volatility the optimal property weight is 0.3864; on the reported figure at a smoothing weight of 0.4 it is 0.8254, and at 0.2 it is 0.9505. Worse, the investor believes a Sharpe ratio of 0.477552 for a portfolio that actually delivers 0.267645, a shortfall of 0.209907, and which underperforms the correctly chosen portfolio's 0.330472. The smoothing costs twice: once in the wrong allocation and once in the false confidence.

Cost is not a separate problem. A valuation is a professional's day rather than a screen price, so it is done rarely, and rarity is a heavier weight on the previous figure. A triennial cycle shows 0.1302 of the true volatility where a monthly one shows all of it. Cost determines frequency, frequency determines the weight, and the weight determines the understatement.

Circularity is different in kind. A valuer prices from comparable transactions whose parties took advice from valuations, so a fraction of every valuation is an echo. At an echo share of 0.6 a genuine 20 per cent shock shows as only 0.080000 immediately and reaches 0.184448 after five rounds, arriving in full only in the limit. Circularity delays a movement rather than destroying it, which is why an unlisted index is still falling long after the market has stopped.

Step 2

Why this matters

The optimiser result in section 4.2 is the most consequential number in this sub-module. Property and other unlisted assets are routinely allocated using reported risk figures, and section 4.2 shows that doing so more than doubles the allocation relative to what the true risk justifies. That is not a modelling nicety: it is the mechanism by which illiquid assets came to occupy the share of institutional portfolios they now occupy, and it operates entirely through a construction detail the summary covers in one word.

The circularity result matters at a different moment. Section 4.3 shows a shock arriving over several periods, which means a valuation-based index lags a turning point in both directions. An investor rebalancing mechanically against such an index buys into a fall that has not finished appearing and sells into a recovery that has not yet appeared. Lecture 8's warning about a benchmark carrying decisions nobody made applies here in its most damaging form, because the benchmark is not merely wrong about the level but wrong about the timing.

For the exam, the useful content is that the summary's three words are not three independent caveats. They are one mechanism with two amplifiers, and section 4.3 shows cost feeding directly into subjectivity. A candidate who can say why an infrequent valuation cycle produces a lower reported volatility has understood the whole line.

The Feynman version. Ask a surveyor what a building is worth and they will look at what they said last year and nudge it. If the market moved ten per cent they might report four, because moving further feels like admitting the old number was wrong. Next year they nudge again from the new figure. So the reported series wanders gently while the real thing lurches.

Now the mistake. Somebody measures how much that gentle series wobbles, decides property is half as risky as shares, and puts twice as much in it. The building did not become safer. The person describing it became cautious. And because each report is anchored on the last, the series is still drifting down months after the market has turned, so anybody who rebalances against it is always a beat behind.

Step 3

The mental model

Every previous lecture in this sub-module built on a transaction price. Lecture 1's self-rebalancing needed weights that move with prices. Lecture 2's divisor needed a price to hold constant through a corporate action. Lecture 3's income question needed a price separable from a dividend. Lecture 4's test needed a share count. Lecture 5's roll-off needed a maturity. An unlisted index has none of those, which section 4.4 tabulates, and that is the real content of the summary's phrase "alternative to prices".

What replaces the price is an opinion, and an opinion has a property a price does not: it is anchored. A price is set afresh by whoever is willing to transact today; a valuation is set by somebody who knows what they said last time and is professionally reluctant to contradict themselves by much. That single asymmetry generates all three of the summary's problems.

Hold the mechanism as a filter with one parameter. The parameter is how much weight the current truth gets, and everything follows: the reported volatility is the true volatility scaled by a factor that depends only on that parameter, the reported autocorrelation is one less the parameter, and the frequency of valuation is what sets the parameter in the first place. One number describes the whole defect, and because it is recoverable from the reported autocorrelation, the defect is correctable.

Step 4

The detail

4.1 Subjectivity is an exact filter, and it halves the reported risk

A valuer anchors on the previous valuation, so the reported return is a blend: a weight on this period's truth and the remainder on last period's report. On a true volatility of 18 per cent:

   weight a on this   reported     as a share of   autocorrelation   the smoothing is
   period's truth     volatility   the true 18%    of the reports    detectable how?
   1.0000                18.0000%        100.0000%            0.0000   no smoothing
   0.7000                13.2084%         73.3799%            0.3000   from the autocorrelation
   0.4000                 9.0000%         50.0000%            0.6000   from the autocorrelation
   0.2000                 6.0000%         33.3333%            0.8000   from the autocorrelation

At a weight of 0.4 the reported volatility is exactly half the truth, and at 0.2 it is a third. The formula is a closed form, so it is worth checking rather than trusting: a simulation over two hundred thousand periods gives a volatility ratio of 0.498925 against the formula's 0.500000 at a weight of 0.4, and 0.332461 against 0.333333 at 0.2, with first-order autocorrelations of 0.597702 and 0.798819 against the predicted 0.6 and 0.8.

And the halving is a property of the filter rather than of the asset. At true volatilities of 8, 18 and 30 per cent the ratio at a weight of 0.4 is 0.500000 in every case. So the summary's one word, subjectivity, is not a caveat about precision. It is a systematic halving of a reported risk number.

The direction matters more than the size. Anchoring can only damp a movement and never amplify one, so a valuation index understates risk and never overstates it. The error is a bias rather than noise, and it does not average out across periods or across properties, which is what separates it from ordinary measurement error.

4.2 The understated risk propagates into the allocation

Feed the reported figures to a mean-variance optimiser. Take property at a 9 per cent expected return against equities at 11, equity volatility 20 per cent, correlation 0.3 and a 5 per cent risk-free rate.

On the true volatility the optimiser holds 0.3864 of property. On the reported figure at a smoothing weight of 0.4 it holds 0.8254, an overweight of 0.4390, and at 0.2 it holds 0.9505, which is almost the whole portfolio. So the summary's subjectivity problem does not stop at the index: it propagates into the asset allocation, and it does so in one direction only.

It is worse than a misallocation, because the reported Sharpe ratio also looks better.

   reported, a = 0.4              0.477552           0.267645         0.209907

The investor believes a Sharpe ratio of 0.477552 and the portfolio actually delivers 0.267645, a shortfall of 0.209907. And it underperforms the correctly chosen portfolio's 0.330472. So the smoothing costs twice: once in the wrong allocation and once in the false confidence, and the second is the more dangerous because it suppresses the impulse to check.

Which is the strongest available argument for unsmoothing a valuation series before using it, and section 4.1 gives the recipe. The reported autocorrelation identifies the weight, the weight identifies the variance ratio, and dividing the reported volatility by that ratio recovers the figure the optimiser should have been given. The defect is correctable and the correction requires only the published series.

4.3 Cost feeds subjectivity, and circularity delays rather than destroys

The summary's second problem is cost. A valuation is a professional's day rather than a screen price, so it is done rarely, and a stale valuation is a full weight on the previous one.

   valuation cycle   valuations a   effective weight   reported volatility   share of the
                     year          on fresh truth     that follows          truth shown
   monthly                  12.0000             1.0000               18.0000%         1.0000
   quarterly                 4.0000             0.4000                9.0000%         0.5000
   annual                    1.0000             0.1000                4.1295%         0.2294
   triennial                 0.3333             0.0333                2.3433%         0.1302

A triennial cycle shows 0.1302 of the true volatility and a monthly one shows all of it. So the summary's second problem is not independent of its first: cost determines frequency, frequency determines the smoothing weight, and the weight determines the understatement. The three words are one mechanism, and that is the connection worth being able to state.

Circularity is different in kind. A valuer prices a property from comparable transactions, and the parties to those transactions took advice from valuations, so a fraction of every valuation is an echo of earlier valuations. Trace a genuine 20 per cent shock through it.

With no echo the shock shows in full at once. At an echo share of 0.6 only 0.080000 of the 0.2 appears immediately, 0.128000 after two rounds and 0.184448 after five, with the full shock arriving only in the limit. So circularity delays a movement rather than destroying it, which is a different defect from subjectivity: the first shrinks the measured variance permanently and the second only postpones it.

That distinction is what makes an unlisted index dangerous in a crisis rather than merely imprecise. A shock arrives in the reported series over several periods, so the index is still falling long after the market has stopped, and an investor rebalancing against it sells into a recovery. The level error is correctable by unsmoothing; the timing error is not, because it is not knowable in real time which part of a reported fall is news and which is echo.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The habit worth forming is to check the autocorrelation of any return series before using its volatility. A listed index's returns are close to serially uncorrelated; a valuation series' are not, and section 4.1 shows the autocorrelation is precisely the smoothing weight complement. That single diagnostic tells you whether the risk figure in front of you is the asset's or the valuer's, and it takes one line of code on data anybody already has.

On section 4.2, the professional judgement is that this is the single largest unexamined bias in institutional asset allocation. Illiquid allocations have grown for many reasons, some of them good, and one of them is that the inputs make the assets look better than they are by a factor section 4.2 quantifies. The correction is available and rarely applied, partly because applying it reduces the apparent attractiveness of a decision already taken.

On circularity, the practical implication is that a property portfolio should not be rebalanced on a valuation signal. Section 4.3 shows the signal lags, so mechanical rebalancing systematically trades against the market. A discipline based on transactions where they exist, or on listed property as a timing proxy, avoids the lag at the cost of a basis risk that is at least visible.

One limit on section 4.1. The filter is a first-order model with one parameter, and real valuation behaviour includes thresholds: a valuer may leave a figure entirely unchanged for several periods and then move it substantially. That produces a series with the same broad understatement and a different autocorrelation structure, so the single-parameter unsmoothing recovers most of the bias and not all of it. The direction and the approximate magnitude survive; the precision does not.

And a limit on section 4.2. The optimiser is a two-asset mean-variance problem with fixed expected returns, so the allocation figures are illustrative of the mechanism rather than of any real portfolio. What generalises is that understating one asset's variance raises its optimal weight monotonically, so the direction is certain and the magnitude depends on the correlation and on how many other assets compete for the space. With more assets the overweight is spread across the portfolio rather than concentrated, which makes it harder to see and no smaller.

That closes the indices sub-module and the investments track. The arc of the six lectures runs from indices whose inputs are certain and frequent, through bonds where the inputs are certain and the construction intricate, to property where the inputs are themselves estimates, and the lesson accumulates in one direction: an index is a construction before it is a measurement, and every result in this track that used one depended on a choice somebody made rather than on a fact about a market.

What this assumes. Where this goes next. This closes the indices sub-module, the external environment module and the investments track: ninety-nine lectures sequenced and all ninety-nine now built. What remains in the wider course is the life insurance track, sequenced at sixty-four lectures and not yet started, and then actuarial risk management, which is deliberately left last because it references banking, life insurance and investments material throughout and is best written once all three exist. This lecture's smoothing result will reappear there, because an actuarial valuation of any illiquid liability has the same anchoring property as a surveyor's valuation of a building.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank meets section 4.1's smoothing as a regulatory problem rather than an allocation one. It holds property directly and as collateral, and both are valued rather than priced, so the reported volatility of its property exposure understates the true figure by the factor this lecture computes, which is why supervisors impose valuation haircuts and revaluation frequency requirements rather than accepting reported volatility: the treatment sits in A-IRB capital modelling, where a collateral value feeds loss given default directly and a smoothed value understates downturn loss. Section 4.3's circularity is also the mechanism behind the property cycle's role in bank failures, since a lending decision based on a valuation that echoes previous lending decisions is self-reinforcing in both directions, which is the procyclicality problem treated in climate risk for physical assets and in risk management as a general stress testing concern.

All three of its named problems are correctly identified and correctly named as problems; what the line lacks is any indication of size, and the sizes turn out to be large: a halving of reported risk at a plausible anchoring weight, a doubling of an optimiser's allocation, and a shock that takes five periods to show four fifths of itself. The line is adequate as a warning and inadequate as a specification, which is the fair verdict on it. Everything numerical is invented for illustration: a true volatility of 18 per cent with anchoring weights from 0.2 to 1.0, checked at true volatilities of 8, 18 and 30 per cent, a two-asset optimisation with property at a 9 per cent expected return against equities at 11 with 20 per cent volatility, a 0.3 correlation and a 5 per cent risk-free rate, valuation cycles from monthly to triennial, and a 20 per cent shock traced through echo shares from zero to 0.6. Seventeen claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions, all seventeen passed, and none can pass without the computation. Two claims were flagged as unfalsifiable by a vacuity check, both asserting the autocorrelation relation on literal constants, and both were replaced by a deterministic two-hundred-thousand-period simulation which confirms the closed-form variance ratio to within one per cent and the autocorrelation to within two, at every weight tested. Every headline finding states the region in which it holds: the variance ratio is exact for a first-order filter and recovers most but not all of the bias where valuers hold figures unchanged for several periods before moving them substantially; the optimiser result is illustrative in magnitude and certain in direction, since understating one asset's variance raises its optimal weight monotonically whatever the rest of the portfolio contains; and the circularity result shows the full shock arriving in the limit at every echo share, so the defect is a delay rather than a loss.
