A broad index is easy to replicate, because the tail is cheap to hold precisely because it is small

The hundredth stock's spread is exactly ten times the largest's and its weighted cost contribution is exactly a tenth, because weight falls as one over rank while spread rises only as the square root. So on trading costs alone full replication wins at 0.1433 per cent a year against 1.4754 for the top ten. Sampling must therefore be driven by a per-holding fixed cost rather than by the spread, and at 40,000 a year per line a hundred-million fund holds twenty stocks covering 69.3557 per cent of the market.

Step 1

The 30-second version

This opens the last sub-module of the investments track. The summary gives index construction eight factors and five purposes, and asserts that an unweighted index is unsuitable. Two of those assertions carry arithmetic it does not supply, and in both cases the arithmetic points somewhere more interesting than the assertion.

Start with the purposes. The summary lists five and treats them as compatible uses of one index. Two are not: tracking movements wants the broadest possible coverage and index fund creation wants constituents that can be bought. Take a market of a hundred stocks whose capitalisations fall as one over rank. The top ten are 56.4634 per cent of the market, and reaching the whole market means holding every stock. So the first purpose and the fifth want different indices, and every real index family publishes both.

But then the replication arithmetic goes the other way from the intuition. On spread costs alone, full replication wins: 0.1433 per cent a year against 1.4754 for the top ten. The reason is exact. Weight falls as one over rank and spread rises only as the square root of rank, so each stock's weighted trading cost falls as one over the root. The hundredth stock's spread is exactly ten times the largest's and its weighted cost contribution is exactly a tenth. The tail is cheap to include precisely because it is small.

Which tells us what really drives sampling: a cost that does not shrink with the position. Add a fixed administrative charge per holding for custody, corporate actions and minimum lot sizes. At zero and at 2,000 a year a hundred-million fund still replicates fully; at 10,000 it holds fifty and at 40,000 it holds twenty, covering 69.3557 per cent. Large funds replicate and small ones sample because the fixed cost is the same and the fund it is divided by is not.

Second, the summary calls an unweighted index unsuitable because a portfolio's performance reflects weights. True, and the larger objection is that an equally weighted index cannot be held without trading every period while a cap-weighted one holds itself. At an 8 per cent monthly cross-sectional dispersion the rebalancing drag is 0.1149 per cent a year, exactly proportional to dispersion, and a cap-weighted holding left alone ends at exactly the new cap weights to fifteen decimal places. The same objection condemns every non-cap weighting sold as an improvement.

Step 2

Why this matters

An index is the single most consequential object in this whole track and it is usually treated as a given. Lecture 3 of the internal environment measured a manager against one. Lecture 4 wrote one into a mandate. Lecture 11 of the environment module found that excluding a sector costs tracking error against one. Every one of those results depends on construction choices the summary lists in eight words each, and this sub-module is where those choices get examined.

The replication result matters because it inverts a widely held belief. Broad-market index funds are commonly assumed to sample because holding thousands of tiny positions would be prohibitively expensive to trade. Section 4.1 shows that is false on spread costs: the tiny positions are tiny, so their trading cost contribution is tiny too. The real constraint is per-line administration, which means the decision to sample is a function of fund size rather than of index breadth, and a large fund tracking a broad index should replicate it while a small fund tracking a narrow one may not.

The rebalancing result matters for reading any claim about smart beta. Cap weighting is the only scheme that requires no maintenance, because it is the only one whose weights move with prices by construction. Every alternative pays a drag that section 4.2 computes and that scales with dispersion, which is precisely the environment in which alternative weightings are supposed to add most value. That does not make them wrong. It sets the hurdle they have to clear.

The Feynman version. Suppose you want to own the whole market. The biggest company is easy to buy and the hundredth is fiddly, costing you ten times as much per pound traded. So you might think owning all hundred is expensive. But you only need a hundredth as much of the hundredth company, because it is a hundredth of the size. Ten times dearer on a hundredth as much is a tenth of the cost. The awkward companies are the ones you barely have to buy.

So why does anybody skip them? Not the trading. It is the paperwork. Every holding needs custody, a dividend collected, a rights issue answered, a proxy voted, and that costs the same whether you hold a pound or a million. If your fund is big enough that the paperwork is a rounding error, hold everything. If it is not, hold the big ones and accept the mismatch.

Step 3

The mental model

An index is a rule for turning many prices into one number, and every construction choice is a decision about whose question the number answers. The summary's eight factors are that decision taken eight times, and its first factor is not parallel with the other seven: the purpose determines the rest. Section 4.3 sets that out, and it is why the summary puts purpose first without saying why.

The organising distinction is between measuring and holding. A measuring index wants completeness, because omitting anything biases the measurement. A holding index wants investability, because a constituent you cannot buy makes the index unusable as a benchmark. Those pull in opposite directions on the basis for inclusion, and no single choice satisfies both, which is why the JSE publishes an All Share and a Top 40, and FTSE publishes an All-World and a series of tradeable subsets.

The second idea, and the one section 4.2 turns on, is that cap weighting is a fixed point. A portfolio bought at cap weights and never touched remains at cap weights, because both the holding and the index scale with the same prices. No other weighting has that property, so no other weighting is free to maintain. That is a structural fact about the arithmetic rather than a claim about which weighting performs better.

A candidate asked why index funds do not hold every constituent should name the administrative cost rather than the spread.

4.2 An unweighted index is unsuitable, and not for the reason given

The summary calls an unweighted index unsuitable and explains that a portfolio's performance reflects weights. True, and the smaller objection. The larger one is that an equally weighted index cannot be held without trading every period.

   dispersion        to rebalance       to rebalance      0.30% round trip
   0.0000                         0.0000%            0.0000%             0.0000%
   0.0400                         1.5958%           19.1492%             0.0574%

At an 8 per cent monthly cross-sectional dispersion the rebalancing drag is 0.1149 per cent a year, and it is exactly proportional to dispersion: doubling the dispersion doubles the drag and quadrupling it quadruples it. A cap-weighted index incurs none of it, and the reason is worth verifying directly rather than asserting.

   1         0.2000     100.0000    120.0000        0.333333       0.387097   no

A portfolio bought at cap weights and left alone ends at exactly the new cap weights, to fifteen decimal places, because both the holding and the index are the same arithmetic. An equally weighted portfolio facing the same returns needs 0.053763 of one-way turnover after a single period. So the summary's verdict is right and its reason is the second-best one: the decisive objection is not that weights matter for performance but that non-cap weights cannot be maintained for nothing.

And the same argument condemns every non-cap weighting, including the fundamental and equal-risk schemes sold as improvements on cap weighting. Each requires trading to maintain, so each carries a drag proportional to dispersion, and any claimed outperformance must clear 0.1149 per cent a year before it is real. Note the awkward feature of that hurdle: it is largest exactly when dispersion is largest, which is the environment in which alternative weightings are supposed to earn their keep.

Read the columns. The first and third purposes want maximum coverage and the fourth and fifth actively do not, which is section 4.1's conflict set out across the whole list rather than for one pair. The derivative row is the sharpest case and the one most often missed. A futures contract needs a basket small enough to arbitrage and continuous enough to settle intraday, which is why every market's futures contract references a narrow index rather than its broad one. The JSE Top 40 exists because the All Share cannot be arbitraged, not because forty is an interesting number.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The habit worth forming is to ask, of any index, which of the five purposes it was built for, because that single answer predicts almost every other feature of it. A benchmark index will have a free-float adjustment and a periodic review. A derivative index will have a fixed constituent count and a continuous calculation. A measurement index will have neither and will include names nobody can buy. Reading a factsheet with the purpose in mind turns an arbitrary rulebook into a set of consequences.

On section 4.1, the practitioner's point is that the sampling decision is a fund-size decision and should be revisited as the fund grows. A fund that sampled sensibly at fifty million is leaving tracking error on the table at five hundred million, and the fixed cost per holding that justified the original decision has not changed while the base it divides into has grown tenfold. That is a straightforward operational improvement that goes unmade because the replication policy is set once at launch.

On section 4.2, the honest position on alternative weightings is that the drag is real, modest and routinely omitted from the backtests used to sell them. A backtest applies the weighting scheme to historical prices without paying to rebalance, so it overstates the strategy by roughly the figure section 4.2 computes. Whether the strategy still works after the drag is an empirical question; whether the published evidence has answered it is usually a matter of reading the methodology footnotes.

One limit on section 4.1. The one-over-rank capitalisation distribution and the inverse-root spread are both stylised, and the exact factor of ten and tenth depend on the exponents being minus one and plus a half. Real markets are close to that but not exactly on it, and a market with a much fatter tail would give a different ratio. What survives generalisation is the direction, which is that weighted trading cost falls with rank whenever the weight exponent exceeds the spread exponent in magnitude, and that condition holds comfortably in every equity market.

And a limit on section 4.2. The turnover formula assumes returns are drawn from one distribution each period with no persistence, so it ignores the reason equal weighting is sometimes defended: if returns mean-revert, the rebalancing trade is buying low and selling high, and the drag buys a genuine return rather than merely paying for administration. Whether that reversal exists is the whole argument, and this lecture computes only its cost.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The tracking error concept that section 4.1 trades against cost is derived in portfolio risk and return, and applied to exclusions in ESG and responsible investment, whose 2/n threshold is the same squared-weight arithmetic section 4.1 uses for the omitted tail. The transaction cost model with its square-root impact is in transition management and overlays. The amortisation of a one-off cost over a holding period recurs from accounting standards and the investor and market participants and infrastructure, where it set the disclosure benefit and the intermediation crossover. Benchmarks as mandate terms are in mandates and the statement of investment principles, and manager measurement against an index is in governance, trustees and delegation.

Where this goes next. Lecture 2 takes the three construction formulas and the chain-linking mechanics this lecture deliberately left aside, which is where the summary's own algebra sits. Lecture 3 covers total return indices, picking up the two deferred factors, dividend reinvestment and the gross or net tax basis, which belong together because both answer the question of whose after-tax return the index represents. Lecture 4 covers the equity index families including the JSE ALSI, Top 40 and SWIX, where section 4.3's purpose-driven design becomes concrete and the SWIX adjustment turns out to be a basis-for-inclusion choice rather than a weighting one. Lectures 5 and 6 cover bond and money market indices and then unlisted and illiquid ones, where the replication arithmetic of section 4.1 breaks down because the tail cannot be bought at any price.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank meets indices as a derivative writer rather than as an investor, and section 4.3's derivative row is why: a bank selling an index future or a structured note must hedge it, and hedging requires a basket it can trade continuously, which is the narrow index rather than the broad one. The market risk that residual basis creates is capitalised under the framework in market risk, where an imperfectly hedged index position attracts capital for exactly the tracking error section 4.1 computes. Banks also construct their own indices for benchmark rates, and the construction choices there are the same eight factors with far higher stakes, since a reference rate feeds every floating-rate contract priced off it: the governance requirements that followed the manipulation scandals are the summary's price data and frequency factors turned into regulation, covered alongside the yield curve construction in interest rate risk.

Numbers used here. Two observations are recorded against the summary. First, it presents its five purposes as five uses of one index when the first and third want maximum coverage and the fourth and fifth actively do not, so no single set of the remaining seven factors satisfies all five; the examinable answer gives the five purposes as written and adds that a market needs at least a measurement index and an investable one. Second, its verdict that an unweighted index is unsuitable is right for the second-best reason: the decisive objection is not that a portfolio's performance reflects weights but that non-cap weights cannot be maintained without trading, at a drag exactly proportional to cross-sectional dispersion, while a cap-weighted holding left alone tracks its index to fifteen decimal places. Everything numerical is invented for illustration: a hundred stocks with capitalisation falling as one over rank, a base spread of 0.20 per cent scaling as the inverse root of capitalisation, idiosyncratic volatility of 25 per cent, a five-year holding period, fixed costs per holding from zero to 40,000 on a fund of a hundred million, cross-sectional dispersions from zero to 16 per cent monthly at a 0.30 per cent round-trip cost, and a three-stock verification with returns of plus 20, zero and minus 10 per cent. Fourteen claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions, all fourteen passed, and none can pass without the computation. Three claims failed during checking, all three asserting an interior optimum for the number of holdings; on spread costs alone the optimum is full replication, and pursuing that failure produced the lecture's headline result and the fixed-cost extension that explains real sampling behaviour. A fourth claim failed on a floating-point tie, since the hundredth stock's spread is exactly ten times the largest's rather than more than ten, and was rewritten to assert the weight-times-spread mechanism directly. Every headline finding states the region in which it holds: the cheapness of the tail requires the weight exponent to exceed the spread exponent in magnitude, which holds in every equity market but is stated as a condition rather than assumed; the sampling optimum depends on fund size through the fixed cost per holding, so it is a fund-size decision rather than an index-breadth one; and the rebalancing drag assumes independent returns each period, which ignores the mean-reversion defence of equal weighting whose existence this lecture does not test.
