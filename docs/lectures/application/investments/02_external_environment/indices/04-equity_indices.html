SWIX adjusts the share count and RAFI replaces the rule, which decides between them

A SWIX holding drifts to exactly its new target weights after prices move, to fifteen decimal places, needing no trade. A RAFI holding drifts away from targets that prices do not touch, at 0.052024 of one-way turnover after a single period. So an adjustment to the share count inherits cap weighting's self-rebalancing property and an adjustment to the weighting rule destroys it. Along the way: the summary calls the Dow unweighted arithmetic, and a two-for-one split cuts a constituent's price weight by 0.170455 on an event that affected nobody.

Step 1

The 30-second version

This is the concrete lecture of the sub-module: the named index families of South Africa, the UK and the US. Most of it is a taxonomy to learn, and three items in it are checkable. One of the summary's descriptions turns out to be wrong, one pair of its items turns out not to be a pair, and one of its stated figures turns out to be informative in a way it does not intend.

The summary describes the Dow Jones as "unweighted arithmetic" and calls it unsuitable as a benchmark. The verdict is right and the description is not: the Dow is price weighted, which is a third construction the summary does not list. Three stocks priced 100, 50 and 10 give stock 1 a weight of 0.625000 price-weighted, 0.333333 unweighted and 0.166667 cap-weighted, so the three are genuinely different. And a two-for-one split in stock 1 cuts its price weight from 0.625000 to 0.454545, a fall of 0.170455, while leaving every market capitalisation and every cap weight unchanged to fifteen decimal places. A price-weighted index reweights itself on the corporate action lecture 2's divisor was invented to suppress.

The summary lists SWIX and RAFI side by side as specialist indices, and they are structurally different in a way that decides between them. SWIX adjusts the share count to locally held shares only; RAFI replaces market capitalisation with fundamental factors. Lecture 1 showed that a cap-weighted holding self-rebalances. A SWIX holding does too, drifting to exactly its new target weights after prices move, to fifteen decimal places, needing no trade. A RAFI holding drifts away from targets that prices never touch, at 0.052024 of one-way turnover after a single period. An adjustment to the share count inherits the self-rebalancing property and an adjustment to the weighting rule destroys it, which is the general test for any index innovation.

Finally the summary's stated coverage figures, the FTSE All Share at 99 per cent and the FTSE 100 at 80. A one-over-rank distribution gives the top hundred only 74.3712 per cent, so the real market is more concentrated. Fitting the 80 needs an exponent of 1.090298, and at that exponent the top 350 covers 94.3122 per cent against the stated 99, while fitting the 99 needs 1.499221. So no single power law fits both, and the real tail beyond 350 carries 1 per cent where the fitted model puts 5.6878. The real tail is lighter than any power law matching the top, which strengthens lecture 1's replication result.

Step 2

Why this matters

Three practical reasons, one per result.

The Dow correction matters because the Dow is the most quoted index in the world and almost nobody can say how it is built. Section 4.1 shows the consequence is not academic: a price-weighted index's constituent weights change on splits, so its history is a record partly of corporate housekeeping. Anybody using the Dow as a data series for anything other than headline narrative is using a series whose composition was reshuffled by events with no economic content.

The SWIX and RAFI distinction matters for South African practice specifically. SWIX exists because the ALSI's largest constituents are dual-listed with most of their shares held offshore, so a local fund tracking the ALSI is forced to hold more of them than the local market can supply. Section 4.2 shows SWIX solves that at no maintenance cost, which is why it became the default local benchmark. RAFI addresses a different question and pays lecture 1's drag to do it, and the two are routinely discussed as though they were alternatives of the same kind.

The calibration in section 4.3 matters because it is the only place in this whole sub-module where a model meets a stated fact. Lecture 1's replication result rested on a one-over-rank assumption, and section 4.3 shows the real distribution is more concentrated at the top and lighter in the tail than that assumption. Both differences push the same way: full replication is easier in a real market than in the model, so lecture 1's conclusion holds with room to spare rather than marginally.

The Feynman version. Suppose an index adds up share prices and divides by thirty. A company trading at 300 counts ten times as much as one trading at 30, even if the second company is twice the size. Now the expensive one splits its shares four ways: same company, same value, price now 75. It has just gone from being the biggest thing in the index to being an also-ran, and nothing happened. That is the Dow.

The second idea is subtler and better. If you want an index that only counts the shares available to local buyers, you have two ways to do it. You can change how many shares you count, or you can change how you turn shares into weights. Changing the count is free: when a price moves, everything still works out on its own. Changing the rule is not free, because your portfolio drifts one way while your target sits still, and you have to keep trading to catch up. Same-looking idea, completely different bill.

Step 3

The mental model

Every index in this lecture is a choice of two things: which shares count, and how a share becomes a weight. Sorting the families that way collapses a long taxonomy into a short one, and it produces section 4.2's test.

The which shares count question includes every tier in the summary's lists. The ALSI takes the top 99 per cent, the Top 40 takes forty, the Fledgling takes the last 1 per cent, and SWIX takes only locally held shares of whatever is included. All of those are inclusion decisions, and all of them leave the weighting formula alone.

The how a share becomes a weight question has only three real answers in practice: by capitalisation, by price, or by something else. Cap weighting is the fixed point lecture 1 identified. Price weighting is the Dow, and it is a fixed point for nothing. Anything else, RAFI included, requires maintenance.

That gives the rule worth carrying out of the lecture: changes to the numerator are free and changes to the formula are not. Free-float adjustment, SWIX, foreign-exclusion screens and liquidity screens all change which shares count and cost nothing to track. Fundamental weighting, equal weighting and risk weighting all change the formula and pay a drag proportional to dispersion.

Step 4

The detail

4.1 The Dow is price weighted, and a split proves it

Take three stocks priced 100, 50 and 10 with 10, 40 and 300 shares in issue, giving capitalisations of 1000, 2000 and 3000.

   the construction        stock 1 weight   stock 2 weight   stock 3 weight   what sets it
   price weighted (the Dow)         0.625000         0.312500         0.062500   the PRICE alone
   unweighted arithmetic           0.333333         0.333333         0.333333   nothing: all equal
   cap weighted                    0.166667         0.333333         0.500000   capitalisation

The three constructions put stock 1's weight at 0.625000, 0.333333 and 0.166667. So price weighting is a third construction, not one of the summary's three, and the Dow is the only major index that uses it. Note how badly it misranks: stock 1 is the smallest company in the set and carries the largest weight, purely because its shares are expensive.

Now the demonstration. Stock 1 splits two for one: its price halves, its share count doubles, its capitalisation is unchanged and no holder is affected in any way.

   construction            stock 1 weight   stock 1 weight   the change   is that
                            before           after            in weight    defensible?
   price weighted                  0.625000         0.454545    -0.170455   NO: nothing happened
   cap weighted                    0.166667         0.166667     0.000000   yes: unchanged

The split leaves every capitalisation and every cap weight exactly unchanged, to fifteen decimal places. It cuts stock 1's price weight by 0.170455, on an event that affected nobody. So a price-weighted index reweights itself on share splits, which is the real reason the Dow is unsuitable and is sharper than the summary's.

Sharper in a specific way, and the specificity is the point. Lecture 1 condemned the unweighted index for its rebalancing drag. A price-weighted index fails for an additional reason with no analogue there: its weights respond to exactly the corporate action lecture 2's divisor was invented to suppress, and no divisor can help, because the divisor sits outside the weighting while the price sits inside it.

4.2 SWIX and RAFI are not a pair

The summary lists them side by side. SWIX adjusts the share count to locally held shares only; RAFI replaces capitalisation with fundamental factors. Take four companies with different local holdings.

   the company              full cap   locally   SWIX cap   ALSI weight   SWIX weight
                                       held
   a local retailer          20000.0000    1.0000 20000.0000      0.084034      0.225479
   a dual-listed miner       160000.0000    0.2500 40000.0000      0.672269      0.450958
   a foreign-parented bank   48000.0000    0.4000 19200.0000      0.201681      0.216460
   a local insurer           10000.0000    0.9500  9500.0000      0.042017      0.107103

The dual-listed miner's weight falls from 0.672269 to 0.450958 and the local retailer's rises from 0.084034 to 0.225479. The changes sum to exactly zero, so SWIX is a redistribution rather than a different measurement. That is the whole purpose: the ALSI's largest constituents are mostly held offshore, so a local fund tracking the ALSI is asked to buy more of them than the local market holds.

Now the structural point. Lecture 1 showed a cap-weighted holding self-rebalances and any other weighting does not. Move every price by a different amount and see whose weights follow for free.

   the index          weight after prices move   weight a buy-and-hold   trading
                      on the new data            portfolio drifts to     needed?
   ALSI, cap weighted                   0.104393                0.104393   no
   SWIX                                 0.263490                0.263490   no
   RAFI, fundamental                    0.092784                0.111226   YES

A cap-weighted holding and a SWIX holding both drift to exactly their new target weights, to fifteen decimal places, needing no trade. A RAFI holding drifts away from its targets, because fundamental weights do not move with prices, and closing the gap takes 0.052024 of one-way turnover after a single period of these returns. So SWIX is free to maintain and RAFI carries lecture 1's drag.

Which gives the general test for any index innovation, and it is worth more than the taxonomy. An adjustment to the share count keeps cap weighting's self-rebalancing property, and an adjustment to the weighting rule destroys it. Free-float, SWIX and foreign-exclusion indices are therefore free to track, and fundamental, equal and risk-weighted ones are not. A candidate asked to compare SWIX and RAFI should say that first and the fundamentals second.

4.3 The stated coverage figures, checked

The summary gives the FTSE All Share at 99 per cent of the market and the FTSE 100 at 80 per cent. Those are checkable against lecture 1's one-over-rank distribution on a market of 600 stocks.

   index             stocks   the summary   a one-over-rank   does the model
                     covered  says          model gives       match?
   FTSE 100                100       80.0000%           74.3712% NO, badly
   FTSE 350                350       99.0000%           92.2810% NO, badly

A plain one-over-rank distribution gives the top hundred 74.3712 per cent against the stated 80, so the real market is more concentrated than one over rank. The exponent reproducing 80 per cent for the top hundred is 1.090298. At that exponent the top 350 covers 94.3122 per cent against a stated 99, and fitting the 99 instead needs 1.499221.

So the two stated figures are not consistent with any single power law. That is not an error in the summary; it is evidence about the shape of a real market. The real tail beyond 350 carries 1 per cent where the fitted model puts 5.6878, so the real tail is lighter than any power law matching the top. And that matters for lecture 1's replication result in the helpful direction: a lighter tail means an even smaller weighted trading cost, so full replication is easier in a real market than in the model, and lecture 1's conclusion holds with room to spare.

The tiers are not a size taxonomy, they are lecture 1's five purposes made concrete. The top tier exists for derivatives and index funds, the broad one for measurement, and the middle ones to give a mandate something to name. The summary's own note that the FTSE 100 is the derivatives underlying and the All Share is not confirms it, and lecture 1's derivative row explains why: a futures contract needs a basket small enough to arbitrage.

Two further items in the summary's lists deserve a word. The style indices, value and growth groupings, are inclusion decisions rather than weighting ones on the section 4.2 test, so they are free to track, but they require a periodic reclassification that moves stocks between them, and that reclassification is a constituent change under lecture 2's sixth trigger. And the VIX is described as a weighted arithmetic index of 30-day put and call prices, which understates it: it is a replication of a variance swap rate, so its level is a volatility rather than a price average, and it is the one entry in the summary's equity list that is not an index of anything holdable.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The habit worth forming is section 4.2's test, applied to any new index somebody offers you: does it change which shares count, or how a share becomes a weight? The first is free and the second is not, and the answer is usually visible in the methodology's first paragraph. That single question sorts the entire universe of index innovation into two piles with very different cost profiles, and it does so before reading any performance claim.

On SWIX specifically, the practitioner's point is that it solves a capacity problem rather than a measurement one. The ALSI is the better measure of the South African market's value; SWIX is the better description of what a local investor can actually own. Those are different questions and the local industry's near-universal adoption of SWIX as a benchmark is an answer to the second. Lecture 3's warning applies alongside: whichever is chosen, the tax basis must match too, and a SWIX benchmark on the wrong tax basis carries both errors at once.

On the Dow, the professional judgement is that its survival is a lesson in how little construction matters for a number's influence. It is the worst-constructed major index in the world, it has been for a century, and it remains the one most people can quote. That is worth remembering when evaluating any argument that a better methodology will displace an incumbent benchmark: methodology loses to familiarity, and the Dow is the proof.

One limit on section 4.3. The calibration uses a market of 600 stocks and the FTSE All Share has varied considerably around that, so the fitted exponents are illustrative rather than measured. What survives is the structural finding, which is that two stated coverage figures at different ranks over-determine a single-parameter model and therefore contain information about the distribution's shape. The direction of the mismatch, a lighter tail than any fitted power law, is robust to the assumed universe size, because it follows from the 99 per cent figure being higher than a top-heavy fit predicts rather than from the absolute numbers.

And a limit on section 4.2. The self-rebalancing property holds for SWIX only so long as the local holding proportions are unchanged. In reality those proportions drift as foreign investors buy and sell, so a SWIX index does require periodic rebalancing when the local-holding data is refreshed. The distinction from RAFI survives and softens: SWIX rebalances on a data update, which is occasional and exogenous, while RAFI rebalances on every price move, which is continuous and unavoidable.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The self-rebalancing property that section 4.2's test turns on, and the replication result section 4.3 calibrates, are both in why indices exist and the eight construction choices, whose five purposes section 4.3's tier table maps the families onto. The divisor that cannot fix price weighting, and the constituent-change trigger that style reclassification falls under, are in index arithmetic and chain-linking. The tax basis that must match alongside the SWIX choice is in total return indices. The dual-listed constituents that make SWIX necessary are characterised in sector classification, the Regulation 28 offshore limits that interact with them are in mandates and the statement of investment principles, and the variance swap the VIX replicates is in derivatives.

Where this goes next. Lecture 5 covers bond and money market indices, where section 4.2's test still applies but the inclusion question is harder: a bond matures, so a bond index's constituent list turns over by construction rather than by review, and there is no share count to adjust. Lecture 6 closes the sub-module with unlisted and illiquid indices, where the surveyors' valuations the summary mentions replace prices entirely, and both the self-rebalancing property and the divisor lose their meaning because there is no continuous price to rebalance against.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank writing an index derivative cares about exactly section 4.1's problem, because a price-weighted underlying reweights itself on corporate actions and the hedge must be adjusted accordingly, which is an operational risk rather than a market one. The exposure and its capital treatment sit in market risk. The SWIX construction also matters to a bank directly, because it is the benchmark most South African institutional mandates name, so it is the basket a bank must be able to price and hedge for its structured product business, and its concentration in a few dual-listed names is a single-name concentration issue of the kind treated in credit concentration risk. And section 4.3's finding that a real size distribution is more top-heavy than a power law is the same observation that drives a bank's own portfolio concentration measures, where an effective number of exposures is computed from squared weights exactly as an index's effective constituent count is.

Nineteen claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions, all nineteen passed, and none can pass without the computation. One claim failed on the first run with its direction inverted: I asserted that the exponent fitting the 80 per cent figure would overshoot the 99 per cent one, and it undershoots, at 94.3122 against 99. Correcting that produced the better finding, which is that the real tail is lighter than any power law matching the top and therefore strengthens lecture 1's replication conclusion rather than qualifying it.
