A price index ranks four identical investments by their payout policy

Four companies with the same 9 per cent total return all reach 560.4411 over twenty years, and their price indices range from 560.4411 down to exactly 100.0000 for the one that pays everything out. Applied to sectors, technology accounts for 49.4449 per cent of the market's price-index gain and utilities 9.6930, a ratio of 5.1011 to one, when every sector returned the same. That is a composition bias rather than a level error. Along the way: a gross benchmark leaves a taxed manager 80.0000 basis points behind before any decision.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Lecture 1 listed eight construction factors and deferred two of them here: reinvesting dividends and the gross or net tax basis. The summary gives each three words. The first turns out to change what an index measures rather than only its level, and the second turns out to make the index specific to an investor rather than to a market, which is a stronger claim than "a choice".

Start with the level, which is the familiar part. On 5 per cent price growth and a 4 per cent yield, the price index reaches 338.6355 after twenty-five years against a total return index at 862.3081, a ratio of 2.546420 that is exactly the compounded yield ratio. And the omitted part grows: the price index captures 45.9932 per cent of the total return over ten years, 31.3043 over twenty-five and 19.8622 over forty.

The part that matters more is the composition. Four companies with an identical 9 per cent total return all reach 560.4411 over twenty years, and their price indices range from 560.4411 for the one that pays no dividend down to exactly 100.0000 for the one that pays everything out, whose price index never moves at all. So a price index ranks identical investments by their payout policy.

Applied to sectors that becomes an attribution error. Give utilities a 6 per cent yield and 3 per cent growth, technology 0.5 and 8.5, and every sector the same 9 per cent total return. Technology then accounts for 49.4449 per cent of the market's price-index gain and utilities for 9.6930, a ratio of 5.1011 to one. A price index tells an analyst technology drove the market and utilities did nothing, when every sector delivered the same return.

Then the tax basis. Four investors in the same market reach four different levels after twenty-five years, from 862.3081 for an exempt pension fund to 653.8363 for a non-treaty foreigner, a shortfall of 24.1760 per cent. So a gross index is right for one of the four and a net index at any single rate is right for one of the four: there is no index that is right for the market. A manager taxed at 20 per cent and benchmarked against a gross index starts 80.0000 basis points behind every year before making a single decision, compounding to 3.9365 per cent over five years.

Step 2

Why this matters

The level result is well known and the composition result is not, and the second does more damage in practice. Any statement of the form "sector X led the market this decade" is a statement about a price index unless it says otherwise, and section 4.2 shows that such a statement partly measures payout policy. The error is largest in exactly the comparison people most want to make, between a high-yielding defensive sector and a low-yielding growth one, which is the comparison that drives most narratives about market leadership.

The tax result has a direct consequence for anybody setting a mandate. Lecture 4 of the internal environment established that a benchmark is the mandate stated as a portfolio. Section 4.3 adds that a benchmark on the wrong tax basis is a mandate the manager cannot fulfil, and gives the size: 80 basis points a year for a 20 per cent withholding rate on a 4 per cent yield. That is larger than most active management targets, so a mismatched tax basis can consume the entire alpha budget before anybody trades.

For the exam, both of the summary's deferred factors turn out to generate an index rather than to adjust one. Section 4.4 sets out the three indices a market needs and shows that these two factors specify all three. That is the same conclusion lecture 1 reached from the five purposes: the eight factors are the specification of a family rather than eight dials on one index.

The Feynman version. Two companies each make you 9 per cent a year. One hands you the whole 9 as a cheque and its share price never budges. The other hands you nothing and its share price climbs 9 per cent a year. You are equally rich either way.

Now write down only the share prices. The first company looks completely flat for twenty years and the second looks like it multiplied five and a half times. If you then ask which parts of the stock market did well, the answer you get is not about which businesses did well. It is about which businesses posted cheques.

The tax point is simpler. A dividend gets taxed on the way to you, and how much depends on who you are. A pension fund keeps all of it and a foreigner without a treaty keeps 70 per cent. So there is no such thing as "the market's return" once dividends are involved. There is only your return, and somebody has to decide whose return the index is going to be.

Step 3

The mental model

A dividend is lecture 2's corporate action in its purest form. It reduces the share price by roughly its own amount on the ex-dividend date, and it makes nobody poorer, because the holder receives the cash. Lecture 2's rule said that whenever a corporate action leaves shareholder value unchanged, an offsetting adjustment exists that leaves the index level unchanged. A total return index applies that rule. A price index deliberately does not, which is why its level is not the value of any portfolio.

That framing explains why the price index survives despite being wrong for most purposes. It answers a different question: not "what did an investor earn" but "what is a share worth now", and for the newspaper, the technical analyst and the option writer that is the question. Lecture 1's rule applies, which is that the purpose determines the construction.

The tax basis is different in kind from the other seven factors, and the difference is worth being precise about. Every other factor is a statement about the market: which stocks, weighted how, calculated when. The tax basis is a statement about the holder. Two indices differing only in tax basis describe the same securities and the same prices, and the gap between them is not a fact about the market at all.

Step 4

The detail

4.1 The omission compounds, and reaches four fifths of the answer

Take a market growing its prices at 5 per cent and paying a 4 per cent yield.

   years   price index   total return   the gap        the gap as a share
           level         index level    accumulated    of total return
   0            100.0000       100.0000         0.0000              0.0000%
   1            105.0000       109.0000         4.0000             44.4444%
   5            127.6282       153.8624        26.2342             48.7060%
   10           162.8895       236.7364        73.8469             54.0068%
   25           338.6355       862.3081       523.6726             68.6957%
   40           703.9989      3140.9420      2436.9431             80.1378%

The two indices are equal at outset and diverge thereafter, reaching a ratio of 2.546420 at twenty-five years, which is exactly the compounded yield ratio: one plus growth plus yield, over one plus growth, raised to the number of years. Read the last column the other way and the price index captures 45.9932 per cent of the total return over ten years, 31.3043 over twenty-five and 19.8622 over forty. So the price index captures a falling share of the answer and under a fifth of it over a working lifetime.

The reason a price index does this is lecture 2's reason for the divisor. A dividend lowers the price without making the holder poorer, so a price index records a fall that happened to nobody. That makes it the one index whose level is not the value of any portfolio, which is exactly the objection lecture 2 raised against the geometric construction. Both are measures of something no investor holds.

4.2 The composition bias, which is the part that misleads

Now the more consequential result. Take four companies with an identical 9 per cent total return and different payout ratios.

   the company's   dividend   capital   price index   total return   which does the
   payout ratio    yield      growth    after 20 yrs  after 20 yrs   price index favour?
   0.0000               0.0000    0.0900      560.4411       560.4411   this one
   0.3000               0.0270    0.0630      339.3636       560.4411
   0.6000               0.0540    0.0360      202.8594       560.4411
   1.0000               0.0900    0.0000      100.0000       560.4411

All four end at exactly 560.4411 on a total return basis. Their price indices range from 560.4411 for the zero-payout company down to exactly 100.0000 for the full-payout one, whose price index never moves at all. The ranking is strictly monotone in payout. So a price index ranks identical investments by their payout policy, which is a composition bias rather than a level error, and the distinction matters because a level error can be corrected by adding a constant while a composition bias cannot.

Apply that to sectors, which is where it does damage. Lecture 4 of the environment module found utilities defensive and high-yielding and technology the opposite, so give them yields and growth rates that sum to the same 9 per cent.

   sector             yield   capital   price index   share of the market's
                              growth    after 20 yrs  price-index gain
   utilities           0.0600    0.0300      180.6111                9.6930%
   banks               0.0500    0.0400      219.1123               14.3226%
   industrials         0.0300    0.0600      320.7135               26.5395%
   technology          0.0050    0.0850      511.2046               49.4449%

Every sector returns exactly 9 per cent. Yet technology accounts for 49.4449 per cent of the market's price-index gain and utilities for 9.6930, a ratio of 5.1011 to one. A price index tells an analyst that technology drove the market and utilities did nothing, when every sector delivered the same return. That is an attribution error created entirely by the construction.

Which is why serious sector attribution uses total return indices. The error is largest in exactly the comparison people most want to make, between a high-yielding defensive sector and a low-yielding growth one, and it is monotone rather than random, so it does not average out over a longer sample. It gets worse, because the compounding of section 4.1 applies to the gap between sectors as well as to the market's level.

4.3 A net-of-tax index is right for exactly one investor

The summary's eighth factor is gross or net of tax, and that choice does something the other seven do not: it makes the index specific to an investor rather than to a market.

So a gross index is right for one of the four and a net index at any single rate is right for one of the four. There is no index that is right for the market, because after dividends there is no such thing as the market's return.

The only zero gap in the table is the one where the manager's rate matches the benchmark's, and the summary lists the choice without saying that matching it is the point.

That closes a loop from the internal environment. Its lecture 2 found the same bond worth 4.36 per cent more to a tax-exempt holder than to a taxed one, and its lecture 5 put the wrapper crossover at the investor's own marginal rate. The benchmark is the third place the investor's tax position enters, and unlike the other two it is usually chosen by somebody who does not know what that position is.

4.4 The reinvestment convention, and the three indices a market needs

One more choice hides inside reinvesting dividends: when. An index reinvests on the ex-dividend date at the closing price. A fund receives the cash on the payment date, weeks later, and reinvests then.

   payment            over the gap  reinvests at reinvests at difference
   0                        0.000000     100.0000   100.000000       0.000000%
   14                       0.001873     100.0000   100.187316       0.007493%

A sixty-day gap costs the fund 3.2210 basis points a year in a rising market and earns it the same in a falling one. So the convention is a small systematic tracking difference whose sign follows the market, which makes it indistinguishable from skill over short periods and identifiable over long ones. A fund that persistently lags its index in rising markets and leads in falling ones has a settlement-lag signature rather than a strategy.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The habit worth forming is to check the basis of every index number before comparing it with another. A price index against a total return index, or a gross against a net, produces a difference that looks like performance and is arithmetic. Section 4.1 puts the first at a factor of 2.546420 over twenty-five years and section 4.3 puts the second at 24.1760 per cent, and both are large enough to reverse any ranking of two managers or two markets.

On section 4.2, the practitioner's version is that market commentary systematically overstates the contribution of low-yielding sectors and has done so for as long as price indices have been quoted. That is worth holding when reading any historical account of which sectors drove a market, and it is worth being specific about the direction: the bias favours growth over value, technology over utilities, and it grows with the horizon of the comparison. A twenty-year sector attribution on a price basis is not a slightly distorted picture but a substantially different one.

On the tax basis, the professional judgement is that this is the single most common technical error in South African mandate documentation. A local pension fund is tax-exempt on dividends and should be benchmarked gross; a life fund inside a taxed portfolio should not; and the two are routinely given the same benchmark because it is the one the index provider publishes most prominently. Section 4.3 gives the cost of getting it wrong, and 80 basis points a year is comparable with the entire fee being charged for active management.

One limit on section 4.2. The four sectors are constructed to have identical total returns, which isolates the bias perfectly and does not happen in reality. Real sectors differ in both yield and return, so an observed price-index difference is part composition bias and part genuine performance, and the two can only be separated by looking at total return series. What survives generalisation is the direction and the fact that the bias is monotone in yield, which means it can be signed even when it cannot be sized.

And a limit on section 4.3. The withholding rates are illustrative and the real position is more complicated, since a domestic taxpayer may face a different rate on dividends than on interest, treaty rates vary by counterparty country, and reclaim mechanics mean the cash timing differs from the accrual. The structure is unaffected: any tax basis is right for one investor and wrong for the rest, and the size of the wrongness is the yield times the rate difference.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The eight construction factors that defer these two, and the finding that the purpose determines the rest, are in why indices exist and the eight construction choices. The divisor rule this lecture applies to dividends is in index arithmetic and chain-linking, which also condemned the geometric construction for the same reason section 4.1 condemns the price index. The sector yields section 4.2 uses are characterised in sector classification, the benchmark as a mandate term is in mandates and the statement of investment principles, and the tax positions section 4.3 closes the loop on are in objectives, liabilities and constraints and vehicles and wrappers. The withholding tax reclaim that makes the net basis recoverable in part is valued in the business model.

Where this goes next. Lecture 4 covers the equity index families, where all three of section 4.4's index types appear as published series and the SWIX adjustment turns out to be a share-count question. Lecture 5 covers bond and money market indices, where the reinvestment question is sharper because most of a bond's return is income rather than capital, so the gap section 4.1 computes for equities is proportionally far larger for bonds and a bond price index is nearly meaningless. Lecture 6 closes the sub-module with unlisted and illiquid indices, where there is no reliable price to separate from income at all.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank meets section 4.3 as its own problem rather than a client's, because a bank holds securities on its own book and its tax position determines what it earns. The dividend received on a trading position is taxed differently from one on a long-term holding, which is one reason the trading and banking book distinction matters beyond capital treatment: see market risk. The reinvestment timing of section 4.4 is also a bank's exposure when it writes a total return swap on an index, since the swap pays the index's reinvestment convention while the bank's hedge pays the real settlement dates, leaving exactly the basis this lecture computes; the counterparty side sits in counterparty credit risk. And the composition bias of section 4.2 has a banking analogue worth noticing: a bank measuring business unit performance on capital growth rather than total contribution will systematically favour units that retain earnings over those that distribute them, which is the same arithmetic applied to internal reporting, alongside the transfer pricing in bank financial management.

Numbers used here. The two construction factors, reinvesting dividends and the gross or net tax basis, are transcribed from chapter 5 of the ASSA F105 summary in the user's notes, which names each in three words and draws no consequence. No observation is recorded against the summary in this lecture, for the same reason as lecture 2: both factors are correctly identified and what they lack is consequence rather than accuracy. What this lecture adds is that the first changes what the index measures rather than only its level, producing a composition bias monotone in payout ratio, and the second makes the index specific to an investor rather than to a market, so that no index is right for the market at all. Everything numerical is invented for illustration: 5 per cent price growth with a 4 per cent yield over horizons to forty years, a 9 per cent total return split across payout ratios from zero to one hundred per cent, four sectors with yields from 0.5 to 6 per cent summing to the same 9 per cent total return, withholding rates of zero, 15, 20 and 30 per cent, and settlement gaps from zero to sixty days. Twenty claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions, all twenty passed, and none can pass without the computation. One claim failed on the first run, asserting that technology accounts for over half the market's price-index gain where it reaches 49.4449 per cent, and was corrected to almost half; the ratio against utilities, 5.1011 to one, carries the point better and was already asserted separately. Two further claims were flagged as self-comparisons by a vacuity check, one comparing an expression with itself and one taking the difference of a rate with itself, and both were rewritten, the second as a search for which row of the benchmarking table has a zero gap. Every headline finding states the region in which it holds: the compounding gap is exact for any growth and yield, with the ratio equal to the compounded yield ratio; the composition bias is isolated here by construction and in reality mixes with genuine performance, so it can be signed from yields even when it cannot be sized; and the tax result holds for any set of rates, with the size of a mismatch equal to the yield times the rate difference.
