The summary says avoid a conflict of interest, not disclose one, and the arithmetic says why

A disclosed conflict can cost the client 7.2000 where the undisclosed one cost 5.0000, because the adviser moves further than the client discounts. Disclosure breaks even only if the client shades the advice by more than 58.3333 per cent, and a client shading by 40 is worse off than one never told. Along the way: separating front and back office loses exactly half its value at a correlation of one half, at every individual dishonesty rate, and activism has an exact minimum stake of 15 per cent that no diversified fund reaches.

Step 1

The 30-second version

This lecture covers the summary's two lists for financial services providers: seven customer legislation principles and eleven institutional investment practices. Six of the eleven practices were priced earlier in this track, so the list functions as an index to the whole course rather than a topic of its own. Three items have not been priced anywhere, and all three turn out to have exact answers.

The summary's third customer principle says to avoid a conflict of interest. Note the verb, because the usual remedy is to disclose one. Set up three regimes: a clean adviser recommends the suitable product and the client gets 100.0000; a conflicted adviser who says nothing recommends the commission product and the client gets 95.0000; a conflicted adviser who discloses feels licensed to recommend the high commission product, and a client who shades the advice by 40 per cent gets 92.8000. Disclosure made it worse. The break-even shading is 58.3333 per cent, so only a client who corrects by more than half the gap gains anything, and the correction has to be made by the least informed party in the transaction.

The summary's effective operations item is one phrase: separation of front office and back office. Without it, one dishonest person completes a fraud at a rate of 0.020000. With it, two must agree, so the rate falls to 0.000400, an improvement factor of exactly 1/p, and a third pair of eyes applies the same factor again. Introduce collusion and the benefit falls linearly: it is exactly halved at a correlation of one half, and that halving point is parameter free, holding at every individual dishonesty rate because the correlation term and the benefit both scale with p(1-p). But the improvement factor collapses much faster, from 50.0000 times to 1.9608 times at the same correlation, so the two natural measures of the benefit disagree sharply and only one of them is linear.

The summary's activism item asks a manager to define the situations in which it can intervene. The arithmetic defines them: intervention costing 12.0000 and creating 80.0000 of value pays only for a holder above 15.0000 per cent, which is the cost divided by the value created and does not depend on the size of the company. A broad index fund holding 0.50 per cent gains 0.4000 against a cost of 12.0000, a ratio of 30.0000 to one against acting. So the holders who own the most of the market in aggregate have the least reason to intervene in any one company, and it takes exactly thirty co-operating holders to bring such a fund to the threshold.

Step 2

Why this matters

Lecture 7 established the framework: every rule is a machinery cost set against a reduction in failures, and the question about any rule is which term it moves and by how much. Lecture 8 applied that to the trustee. This lecture applies it to the firm that takes the trustee's money, and it produces the module's clearest case of a rule that moves the wrong term.

Disclosure of conflicts is the default remedy in almost every conduct regime, it is cheap to impose, easy to evidence and universally popular. Section 4.1 shows it can leave the client worse off than silence, and the reason is not that disclosure is useless but that it shifts the corrective work onto the person least equipped to do it while relaxing the constraint on the person best equipped to exploit it. The summary's own wording, avoid rather than disclose, gets this right in one word, and it is worth noticing that the wording is deliberate.

The activism result matters for a different reason. Stewardship codes ask managers to engage, and section 4.3 shows the request runs against arithmetic rather than against willingness. A fund holding half a per cent of a company that would gain 80.0000 from intervention captures 0.4000 of it and pays the whole 12.0000, so engagement is a transfer from the engaging fund's clients to everybody else's. That is not an argument against stewardship. It is an argument that stewardship has to be organised collectively or not at all, and section 4.3 computes exactly how collectively.

The Feynman version. Imagine your builder tells you, honestly, that he earns more if you choose the expensive kitchen. You now know he is not neutral, so you take his recommendation with a pinch of salt and shade your choice a little towards the cheaper one. Good. But something else happened when he told you. He no longer feels he is hiding anything, so he pushes harder than he would have done if he had kept quiet, and recommends something more expensive still. Whether you end up better off depends on a race: did he move more than you shaded?

The arithmetic says he only has to move a bit further than you shade, and you have to shade by more than half the gap to break even. Most people shade by much less, partly because shading feels rude and partly because they have no idea how far to shade. So the honest warning left you poorer than the silent conflict would have. That is why the rule says get rid of the conflict rather than mention it.

Step 3

The mental model

Every item on both of the summary's lists is an answer to one question: what stops the firm holding the money from taking some of it? There are only three mechanisms available, and each of the three has a characteristic failure.

The first is to align the firm's interest with the client's, which is what avoiding conflicts does, and its failure is that alignment is expensive to arrange and easy to arrange only apparently. The second is to require more than one person to act, which is what segregation does, and its failure is collusion. The third is to inform the client and let them defend themselves, which is what transparency and reporting do, and its failure is section 4.1's: the client may not defend themselves competently, and telling them may relax the firm.

Hold the two lists that way and the ranking falls out. Alignment is the only mechanism whose success does not depend on somebody else's competence, which is why the summary's principle is worded as avoidance. Requiring two people is next best, because section 4.2 shows it degrades gracefully rather than failing suddenly. Informing the client is the weakest, and it is the one every regime relies on most, because it is the cheapest to legislate and the easiest to audit.

Step 4

The detail

4.1 Avoiding a conflict, and why disclosing one is not the same thing

The summary's seven customer legislation principles include avoiding a conflict of interest. Take a client who can be sold one of three products, where the adviser earns more on the worse ones, and add the finding of Cain, Loewenstein and Moore that an adviser who has disclosed a conflict feels licensed to push harder because the client has been warned.

   the regime            what is recommended   the adviser   the client   the client
                                               earns         discounts    ends up with
   no conflict at all      the suitable                 1.0000       0.0000%     100.0000
   conflict, undisclosed   the commission               3.0000       0.0000%      95.0000
   conflict, disclosed     the high commission          5.0000      40.0000%      92.8000

The undisclosed conflict costs the client 5.0000 against a clean adviser. The disclosed conflict costs 7.2000, which is more, because the adviser moved further than the client discounted. That is not a general result about disclosure and it is not a small effect either, so the break-even is worth locating.

   the client's   what disclosure lets   the client   better than saying
   discount, %     the adviser do        ends up with nothing?
   0.0000         recommend the worst        88.0000 NO
   20.0000        recommend the worst        90.4000 NO
   58.3333        recommend the worst        95.0000 exactly level
   70.0000        recommend the worst        96.4000 yes
   100.0000       recommend the worst       100.0000 yes

Disclosure helps only if the client discounts the advice by more than 58.3333 per cent, which is the ratio of the two product gaps rather than a behavioural estimate. So only a client who corrects by more than half the gap gains anything, and the client at 40 per cent is worse off than one who was never told.

That is why the summary says avoid rather than disclose. A disclosure regime moves the work of correction onto the least informed party in the transaction, and the required correction is larger than most clients will make. Removing the conflict is the only remedy whose success does not depend on the client's sophistication, which is the whole case for commission bans and for fee-based advice, and it is a stronger case than the fairness argument usually offered for them.

The region matters, because the result is about adviser behaviour rather than about disclosure itself.

   does disclosure   what is recommended   the client   is disclosure
   license bias?     under disclosure      ends up with an improvement?
   no                the commission             97.0000   yes
   YES               the high commission        92.8000   no

Without the licensing effect, disclosure lifts the client to 97.0000, a real improvement over 95.0000. So the policy question is empirical: does being allowed to warn make people bolder? The experimental evidence says it does, and the sign of the whole result depends on that finding rather than on anything in the arithmetic.

4.2 Separating front and back office, and what collusion actually costs it

The summary's effective operations item is a single phrase, separation of front office and back office, and it is worth more than a phrase. Without separation one dishonest person can complete a fraud. With separation two must agree.

   the arrangement          people needed   probability of   improvement factor
                            to complete     a completed fraud
   no separation                          1           0.020000              1.0000x
   front and back split                   2           0.000400             50.0000x
   three-way split                        3           0.000008           2500.0000x

Splitting front and back office cuts the fraud probability from 0.020000 to 0.000400, a factor of 50.0000, which is exactly one over the individual dishonesty rate. Each further pair of eyes applies the same factor again, so the returns are geometric rather than diminishing, which is unusual among control measures and is why the four-eyes principle is so widely adopted.

All of that assumes independence, which is the assumption a fraudster attacks. Introduce correlation between the two officers' dishonesty and recompute.

   correlation   probability both   the benefit of   share of the benefit
   rho           are dishonest      separation       still retained
   0.0000                  0.000400         0.019600             100.0000%
   0.2500                  0.005300         0.014700              75.0000%
   0.5000                  0.010200         0.009800              50.0000%
   0.7500                  0.015100         0.004900              25.0000%
   1.0000                  0.020000         0.000000               0.0000%

The benefit is exactly halved at a correlation of 0.5000 and vanishes only at perfect collusion, and the halving point is parameter free. At individual rates of 0.0050, 0.0200 and 0.1000 the retained share is 0.500000 in every case, because the correlation term scales with the same p(1-p) that the benefit does and the two cancel. So the retained share equals 1 - rho exactly, whatever the underlying dishonesty rate.

But there are two natural ways to measure the benefit and they disagree sharply.

   correlation   benefit as a       share of the   improvement   share of the
   rho           probability drop   drop retained  factor        FACTOR retained
   0.0000                  0.019600       100.0000%       50.0000x       100.0000%
   0.2500                  0.014700        75.0000%        3.7736x         7.5472%
   0.5000                  0.009800        50.0000%        1.9608x         3.9216%
   0.7500                  0.004900        25.0000%        1.3245x         2.6490%

The absolute reduction in fraud probability is linear in the correlation and still worth half at a correlation of one half. The improvement factor is not: it collapses from 50.0000 times to 1.9608 times at the same point, retaining only 3.9216 per cent. So the usual complaint that colluding staff defeat segregation is half right, and the half matters. Segregation remains worth having in absolute terms and stops being the near-elimination the independent case advertises. At a correlation of 0.7500 the factor is 1.3245 times, which is barely worth the organisational cost, and that is the honest answer to a firm asking whether the control is working: it depends entirely on whether the two offices are socially separate, not merely organisationally separate.

4.3 Activism has an exact minimum stake, and it is not a governance test

The summary's activism item says to define the situations in which a manager can intervene. The arithmetic defines them, because intervention is a cost borne by one holder for a benefit shared by all of them.

   cost of        value created   minimum stake   as a % of the   who else
   intervening    by intervening  to break even   company         benefits free
   4.0000                 80.0000        0.050000          5.0000%         76.0000
   12.0000                80.0000        0.150000         15.0000%         68.0000
   24.0000                80.0000        0.300000         30.0000%         56.0000
   40.0000                80.0000        0.500000         50.0000%         40.0000

The minimum stake is the cost divided by the value created, exactly, and at a cost of 12.0000 against an 80.0000 uplift it is 15.0000 per cent. It does not depend on the size of the company, only on the ratio of the cost to the uplift. Every other holder receives 68.0000 for nothing, which is the free-rider problem in one line.

Now apply it to real holders.

   the holder                stake in one   gain from the   cost   does the holder
                             company       intervention           intervene?
   a broad index fund               0.5000%          0.4000 12.0000   NO
   a large active fund              3.0000%          2.4000 12.0000   NO
   a concentrated fund              9.0000%          7.2000 12.0000   NO
   an activist fund                25.0000%         20.0000 12.0000   yes
   a controlling holder            51.0000%         40.8000 12.0000   yes

A broad index fund gains 0.4000 from an intervention costing 12.0000, a ratio of 30.0000 to one against acting. So the holders who own the most of the market in aggregate have the least reason to intervene in any one company, which is an awkward fact for every stewardship code and is arithmetic rather than a failure of will. Notice that even a concentrated fund at 9 per cent stays out.

The way round is collective action, which is why the summary's item asks for situations to be defined in advance rather than judged case by case.

   holders acting   cost each   minimum stake   an index fund at 0.50%
   together         bears       each needs      now participates?
   1                    12.0000        0.150000   no
   2                     6.0000        0.075000   no
   5                     2.4000        0.030000   no
   10                    1.2000        0.015000   no
   30                    0.4000        0.005000   yes

Acting together lowers the minimum stake in exact proportion, so thirty holders need a thirtieth of the stake, 0.005000, which is exactly a broad index fund's own holding. The number of co-operating holders needed to bring such a fund in is therefore exactly 30.0000, and ten are not enough at 0.015000. Collective action helps the medium-sized holder long before it helps the smallest one, and thirty institutions agreeing on one company is a coordination problem of its own.

So the useful definition of when a manager may intervene is a stake threshold rather than a list of governance failings. Below the threshold, intervention destroys value for the intervening fund's own clients however bad the governance is, and a manager who intervenes anyway is spending its clients' money on a public good. That is worth saying plainly because it contradicts how stewardship policies are usually drafted, which is as a list of triggers about board composition and remuneration with no reference to holding size at all.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The single most useful habit from this lecture is to ask, of any control, whose competence it depends on. Section 4.1's disclosure depends on the client's, so it fails wherever clients are unsophisticated, which is exactly where conduct regulation is aimed. Section 4.2's segregation depends on nobody's, which is why it is the strongest of the three mechanisms and why its failure mode is social rather than procedural: two officers who share a car to work have a correlation the organogram cannot see.

On section 4.2, the practitioner's version of the two-measures problem is that firms report control effectiveness as a factor and experience it as a probability. A board told the control cuts fraud fifty-fold will treat the residual as negligible, and at a plausible correlation the true factor is under two. The correct question to ask of a segregation control is not whether the duties are split but how independent the splitters are: rotation, geographic separation and independent reporting lines all move the correlation, and moving the correlation is the only lever that matters once the split exists.

On section 4.3, the awkward truth is that the free-rider arithmetic is why the largest asset managers vote on everything and engage on almost nothing. Voting is nearly free, so it clears the threshold for any holder. Engagement costs real money, so it clears the threshold for almost none. A stewardship report that counts votes cast is therefore measuring the cheap activity and calling it the expensive one, and the honest metric would be engagement spending per unit of holding, which nobody publishes.

The examinable point that ties all three together: the summary's lists are lists of harms with implied remedies, and the remedies differ enormously in whether they work. A candidate who reproduces the seven principles has answered nothing. A candidate who names the harm, names the remedy and says whose competence the remedy depends on has answered a question worth marks, and section 4.4's first table is the form the answer should take.

One limit worth stating. Section 4.1's licensing effect is an experimental finding about individual advisers, and it is not obvious that it survives at institutional scale, where the recommendation is made by a committee following a documented process rather than by a person in a room. The direction of the result is robust to the magnitude, since any licensing at all narrows the region in which disclosure helps, but the specific 58.3333 per cent is a function of the invented product gaps and should not be quoted as a general figure.

Connections

What this assumes. The two-term framework this lecture applies to the firm is set up in the principles of financial regulation, where the total cost of a rule was direct machinery plus expected failures. The trustee on the other side of every one of these principles is in trust law and fiduciary duty, whose 42.5634 per cent figure section 4.4 reuses to explain why trustee decisions go unmeasured. Six of the summary's eleven practices were priced earlier: expert advice, effective decision making and performance measurement in governance, trustees and delegation, and explicit mandates, benchmarks and asset allocation in mandates and the statement of investment principles. The clear objectives item and its contradiction with relative performance risk is in objectives, liabilities and constraints, and the fee chain that section 4.4's first item points at is in the business model.

Where this goes next. Lecture 10 covers corporate governance, which is the other side of section 4.3: this lecture asked when a shareholder will intervene, and lecture 10 asks what the board does when nobody does. The free-rider threshold of 15.0000 per cent is therefore the quantitative reason the governance mechanisms of lecture 10 have to be internal to the company rather than imposed by its owners. Lecture 11 covers responsible investment, where the same threshold governs whether an exclusion or an engagement is the rational instrument. Lectures 12 and 13 cover listing and competition authorities and accounting standards, lecture 14 covers market participants and infrastructure, and the six indices lectures close the module.

Where the bank sits opposite. Every one of the summary's seven customer principles applies to a bank in a harder form, because a bank's customer is also its funder. Section 4.1's conflict is the reason bank product governance is regulated on suitability rather than on disclosure, and the deposit and loan pricing that creates the conflict is in bank financial management: the margin on a product is exactly the adviser's incentive to sell it. Section 4.2's segregation is a bank's central operational control and appears there as the three-lines-of-defence model and the maker-checker requirement, quantified alongside the loss data in operational risk, where the correlation problem this lecture identifies is the reason internal fraud losses cluster rather than arriving independently. And the maintain-enough-financial-resources principle is, for a bank, the entire capital framework in capital management, sized to absorb losses rather than to fund an orderly wind-down, which is a materially higher standard than the one applied to an asset manager and for the reason lecture 7 gave: the size of the failure.
