The first jurisdiction to adopt a common accounting standard delivers exactly zero comparability

One jurisdiction on IFRS is just another jurisdiction with its own standard, so the share of comparable firm pairs is 0.100000 before and after, identical to fifteen decimal places. The marginal benefit of the kth adopter is exactly 2(k-1) over the square of the number of jurisdictions, so it rises linearly and the last adopter delivers nine times what the second does. Along the way: halving a bid-ask spread is worth 400 basis points to a quarterly holder and 5 to a twenty-year one, and a reviewed number beats an audited one.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The summary's IFRS material is three lines: reliable accounting data means less information asymmetry and a lower cost of capital, a single set of standards makes comparison easier, and transparency. Every one of the three is correct, and every one carries an exact magnitude the summary does not give. This lecture supplies the three magnitudes, and also settles the deferred tax asset that lectures 6 and 11 both flagged and left open.

Start with the cost of capital. Asymmetry shows up as a bid-ask spread, a spread is a round-trip cost, and a round-trip cost is amortised over however long the investor holds. So halving a 2 per cent spread is worth 400 basis points a year to a quarterly holder and 5 to a twenty-year one, a ratio of 80 to one. The summary's claim is true and its magnitude depends entirely on the marginal investor's holding period rather than on the quality of the disclosure, which inverts the usual argument: the long-term investor who most loudly demands disclosure benefits from it least.

Then comparability. Two firms are comparable if they report on the same basis, so the share of comparable pairs is the sum of the squared shares of each standard. Take ten equal jurisdictions and migrate them one at a time: the share is 0.100000 with nobody on IFRS and 0.100000 with one jurisdiction on it, identical to fifteen decimal places, because one standard with one user is indistinguishable from a local standard with one user. The marginal benefit of the kth adopter is exactly 2(k-1)/J², so it rises linearly from nothing and the tenth adopter delivers nine times what the second does. That explains the observed history of convergence better than any argument about political will.

Third, transparency, which the summary does not expand. Inside it is a trade-off between reliability and timeliness, and it has an interior optimum: at a decay rate of 1.4 a year, a reviewed number one month late scores 0.756435 against an unaudited estimate now at 0.700000 and an audited number a quarter late at 0.704688. A fully reliable number can be worth less than a rough one, and the break-even decay rate for waiting a quarter for an audit is 1.426700 a year.

Finally the deferred tax asset. A carried-forward loss of 400 at a 28 per cent rate has a face value of 112.0000 and is worth that only if profits are certain and immediate. At a 30 per cent probability seven years out it is worth 17.2421, an overstatement of 84.6053 per cent of the carrying amount. And the probability is lowest exactly where the balance is largest, because a firm needs profits to use a loss and only has the loss because it made one.

Step 2

Why this matters

This is the shortest section of the summary in the whole module and it sits underneath everything else in the track. Lecture 12 showed asymmetry sets the discount on a new issue. Lecture 11's factor table showed that a factor with a transmission channel and no disclosure is not investable. The valuation module's accounting ratios all take reported numbers as inputs. Accounting standards decide whether those numbers mean anything, and the summary devotes three lines to the question.

The holding-period result has an immediate practical use. It says the case for disclosure is strongest where turnover is highest, which is the opposite of how the argument is usually made and has a testable consequence: a company that wants to reduce its cost of capital through better reporting should first ask who holds its shares. If the register is institutional and long-term, the reporting improvement is worth a handful of basis points and the money is better spent elsewhere. If the stock is actively traded, it is worth hundreds.

The comparability result matters for how to read any claim about accounting harmonisation. Because the first adopter gains nothing, adoption is a coordination problem rather than a cost-benefit problem, and the standard-setter's job is to solve the coordination rather than to prove the benefit. That reframing is worth having in an exam, because a question asking why convergence took so long has an arithmetic answer rather than a political one.

The Feynman version. Ten countries each keep their books their own way, so if you pick two companies at random there is a one-in-ten chance they are comparable. Now one country switches to a shiny new international standard. How many more comparable pairs are there? None. Zero. The country that switched used to be the only one using its own rules and now it is the only one using the new rules, and being alone is being alone.

The second country to switch creates the first comparable pair the new standard has ever produced. The third creates two more. By the time nine have switched, the tenth is creating nine at once. So nobody wants to go first, everybody wants to go last but one, and the whole thing sits still for years and then happens all at once. That is not politics. It is a square.

Step 3

The mental model

Accounting exists because an investor cannot see inside a company, so every question about accounting is a question about how much of the gap it closes and what closing it is worth. Hold three separate quantities and do not merge them.

The first is how much is not known, which shows up as a spread and a discount and is what the summary's first aim addresses. The second is whether what is known can be compared, which is a property of the population of reporters rather than of any one report, and is what the second aim addresses. The third is when it becomes known, which is what the third aim addresses and is the one the summary leaves as a single word.

The important structural point is that the three have different shapes. The first is linear in the spread and inverse in the holding period. The second is quadratic in the adoption share, which is what produces the zero first step. The third has an interior optimum, because reliability and speed trade against each other. Any argument that treats better accounting as a single monotone good will get at least one of the three wrong, and section 4.4 tabulates which.

Step 4

The detail

4.1 The cost of capital benefit is inverse to the holding period

The summary's first aim says reliable accounting data means less information asymmetry and a lower cost of capital. Asymmetry shows up as a bid-ask spread, and a spread is a round-trip cost amortised over however long the investor holds.

   holding period   a 2% spread costs   a 1% spread costs   the saving from   the saving in
   in years         each year           each year           halving it       basis points
   0.2500                     8.0000%             4.0000%           4.0000%       400.0000
   1.0000                     2.0000%             1.0000%           1.0000%       100.0000
   5.0000                     0.4000%             0.2000%           0.2000%        20.0000
   20.0000                    0.1000%             0.0500%           0.0500%         5.0000

Halving the spread is worth 400.0000 basis points a year to a quarterly holder and 5.0000 to a twenty-year one, a ratio of 80.0000 to one. So the summary's claim is true and its magnitude depends entirely on the marginal investor's holding period rather than on the quality of the disclosure.

Which inverts the argument usually made for disclosure. The firms that gain most from better accounting are those held by short-horizon investors, and a company whose register is full of twenty-year holders gains 5.0000 basis points from halving its spread. The long-term investor who most loudly demands disclosure benefits from it least, and that is not a paradox but a consequence of amortisation: a cost paid once and held for twenty years is a twentieth of a cost paid once and held for one.

Now close the loop with lecture 12, because the same asymmetry appeared there as an issue discount. A company raising equity pays the asymmetry once, at issue, rather than every year.

   the asymmetry        the issue     the cost paid   spread cost over   which channel
   discount at issue    proceeds      once            20 years at 2%     dominates?
   0.0000                   1000.0000          0.0000            20.0000   the spread
   0.0429                    957.1000         42.9000            20.0000   the issue

A 4.2900 per cent issue discount costs 42.9000 once against 20.0000 for twenty years of spread. So for a company that rarely issues the spread channel dominates over a long life, and for one issuing frequently the discount channel does. The summary's aim is about both channels and names neither, which matters because they call for different remedies: the spread responds to continuous disclosure and the discount responds to the allocation rule of lecture 12.

4.2 The first adopter of a common standard delivers exactly nothing

The summary's second aim says a single set of standards makes comparison easier. Two firms are comparable if they report on the same basis, so the share of comparable pairs is the sum of the squared shares of each standard. Take ten equal jurisdictions and migrate them one at a time.

The first adopter delivers exactly zero. The share of comparable pairs is 0.100000 before and 0.100000 after, identical to fifteen decimal places, because one jurisdiction on IFRS is just another jurisdiction with its own standard. The marginal benefit of the kth adopter is exactly 2(k-1)/J², verified at every k from 1 to 10, so it rises linearly and the last adopter delivers 9.0000 times what the second does.

Which explains the observed history of accounting convergence better than any argument about political will. An adoption process whose first mover gains nothing and whose marginal gain rises linearly will stall at the start and accelerate at the end, so decades of slow progress followed by rapid adoption is the arithmetic rather than a change of heart.

The region matters, because the model assumed equal jurisdictions and a natural objection is that a dominant one would move first and carry the others.

   the large jurisdiction only        0.277778         0.000000            0.000000

The first adopter delivers exactly zero whichever it is, large or small, because one standard with one user is indistinguishable from a local standard with one user however big that user happens to be. So the result is not an artefact of equal sizes and the coordination problem is genuine: nobody gains by going first and everybody gains once somebody has. That is the standard-setter's actual problem, and it is why the early history of IFRS was about persuading a bloc to move together rather than about persuading anybody that the standard was good.

4.3 A deferred tax asset is an option carried as though it were cash

Lecture 6 found a carried-forward tax loss to be an option rather than an asset, because it pays only if future gains arise, and left its valuation here. Price it: a loss of 400 at a 28 per cent corporate rate has a face value of 112.0000, discounted at 10 per cent.

   probability of     years until   the face   the true    the overstatement
   future profits     it is used    value      value       in the accounts
   1.0000                    0.0000   112.0000    112.0000             0.0000
   0.9000                    2.0000   112.0000     83.3058            28.6942
   0.6000                    4.0000   112.0000     45.8985            66.1015
   0.3000                    7.0000   112.0000     17.2421            94.7579

A certain and immediate deferred tax asset is worth its face value of 112.0000 exactly. One at a 30 per cent probability seven years out is worth 17.2421, so the accounts overstate it by 94.7579, which is 84.6053 per cent of the carrying amount.

And notice where the probability comes from. A firm needs future profits to use a loss, so the asset is worth most to a profitable firm and it only exists because the firm made a loss. The probability is therefore negatively correlated with the asset's existence.

   the firm's recent record   probability it   the true value   is the asset
                             earns enough     of the 112 face  reliable?
   consistently profitable              0.9500          79.9399   yes
   one bad year in a cycle              0.7500          63.1104   yes
   three consecutive losses             0.3500          29.4515   NO
   a failing business                   0.1000           8.4147   NO

A failing business's 112.0000 of face value is worth 8.4147 and a profitable firm's is worth 79.9399. So the firms carrying the largest deferred tax assets are the least likely to realise them, which makes the item systematically overstated exactly where it matters most.

Which is why Basel deducts deferred tax assets arising from losses in full from common equity tier one rather than haircutting them. A haircut would require the supervisor to estimate the probability, and the probability is lowest precisely where the balance is largest, so a full deduction is the only rule that cannot be gamed. The accounting standard and the capital rule disagree on purpose, and a candidate who notices that has understood both.

4.4 Reliability and timeliness trade off, with an interior optimum

The summary's third aim is transparency, the one word it does not expand. The trade-off inside it is between reliability and timeliness. An audited number is fully reliable and arrives late; an unaudited estimate is partly reliable and arrives now. Information decays, so compute which is worth more at a decay rate of 1.4 a year.

So the case for audit is an empirical claim about how fast information goes stale in a particular market, which is why quarterly reporting is reviewed rather than audited and annual reporting is audited. Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The habit worth forming from this lecture is to ask, of any accounting improvement, who pays for it and who collects. Section 4.1 says the collector is whoever trades most often, and the payer is the company, which means better reporting is a transfer from long-term shareholders to short-term ones unless the spread reduction is large. That is an uncomfortable conclusion and it is arithmetic rather than opinion, and it explains why the loudest institutional demands for disclosure tend to come from investors whose actual holding periods are shorter than their rhetoric.

On the deferred tax asset, the practitioner's version of section 4.3 is that the item is the single most reliable indicator of an overstated balance sheet available in any set of accounts, precisely because its reliability moves inversely with its size. A large deferred tax asset on a profitable company's books is fine and small. A large one on a loss-making company's books is both large and worthless, and it will be the item that disappears in a rights issue prospectus. Anybody screening on book value should deduct it before ranking, and the Basel treatment is the regulator having reached that conclusion first.

On convergence, the professional judgement is that section 4.2's arithmetic is why local GAAP survives in the jurisdictions where it survives. A country whose companies are held mainly by domestic investors gains almost nothing from comparability with foreign firms, because its investors are not making those comparisons. Convergence therefore arrives with foreign ownership rather than causing it, and a country arguing about IFRS adoption is really arguing about how open it intends its capital market to be.

One limit on section 4.4. The exponential decay model is a convenience and the decay rate is not observable, so the break-even figure of 1.426700 should be read as a way of asking the question rather than as an answer. What survives generalisation is the shape: reliability and timeliness both add value, they trade against each other, and therefore the optimum is interior. Anybody arguing for either more audit or faster reporting without naming the decay rate they assume is arguing for a corner solution to a problem that does not have one.

And one limit on section 4.1. The amortisation argument assumes the spread is paid once on entry and once on exit, which is right for a buy-and-hold investor and wrong for one who rebalances. A twenty-year holder who rebalances annually pays the spread twenty times, and their effective holding period for this purpose is one year rather than twenty. So the relevant quantity is turnover rather than intended horizon, and those two differ by more than most investors admit.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The asymmetry mechanism this lecture measures is derived in the principles of financial regulation, where the collapse threshold was exactly 2, and appears as an issue discount in listing authorities and competition regulation, whose 4.2857 per cent figure section 4.1 sets against the spread channel. The deferred tax asset was identified as an option in taxation of investment returns and listed as an unpriced item in ESG and responsible investment, and both left its valuation here. The accounting ratios that take these numbers as inputs are in valuation, the audit committee that produces them is in corporate governance, and the bid-ask spread as a transaction cost is derived in transition management and overlays.

Where this goes next. Lecture 14 covers market participants and infrastructure, which is where the spread of section 4.1 is actually set, by market makers whose inventory risk is the source of it. That completes the environment module, and the six indices lectures then follow, where section 4.2's squared-share arithmetic reappears in a different guise: a concentration measure and a comparability measure are the same functional form, so an index's effective number of constituents and a standard's comparable-pair share are computed identically.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank is where the deferred tax asset of section 4.3 has the largest consequences, because the Basel deduction removes it from common equity tier one entirely, which means a loss-making bank loses capital twice: once for the loss and again for the tax asset the loss created. The framework is in capital management. Banks are also the clearest case of the reliability-timeliness trade-off of section 4.4, since a bank's most important number is its expected credit loss provision, which is forward-looking by construction and therefore unauditable in the sense section 4.4 uses: the treatment is in IFRS 9 impairments, where the standard deliberately chose relevance over reliability and accepted the consequence. And the comparability argument of section 4.2 is why the Basel framework prescribes its own risk-weight calculations rather than relying on accounting numbers: a supervisor comparing banks across jurisdictions faces exactly the squared-share problem this lecture computes, and solved it by writing a separate standard rather than waiting for accounting convergence, as set out in the Basel IV finalisation.
