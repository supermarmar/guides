Step 1

The 30-second version

Lecture 3 of the internal environment already covered delegation as an investment decision: sixteen managers give a fund an information ratio of 2.0000 while each stays at 0.8660, and sixteen annual judgements at a 5 per cent error rate produce a 55.99 per cent chance of dismissing somebody who did nothing wrong. This lecture covers the same act as a legal one, and the arithmetic runs the other way.

The summary says a trustee can delegate tasks but not responsibility. Read as a warning that is right, and the arithmetic is sharper than the warning. A trustee who keeps one decision in house owns one error rate of 0.2000. A trustee who delegates the same ground to sixteen experts, each far better at a rate of 0.0500, owns 0.8000 expected errors, which is four times as many. The cancellation point is exactly the ratio of the two error rates, four delegates here, and it does not depend on the size of any loss. Whether delegation helps or harms turns entirely on whether the delegated tasks are separate decisions or slices of one, and that is settled by the deed rather than by the delegation.

The second result is the lecture's headline. The summary's deed names classes of beneficiaries and its trustee concerns require the trustee to show fairness. Take a fund with pensioners at a liability duration of 8 and active members at 20. The blended duration is 15.2000, and setting the assets there immunises the fund exactly, at every rate move, to twelve decimal places. Neither class is immunised. On a one-point rise the pensioners lose 28.8000 and the actives gain exactly 28.8000, because a weighted average overshoots one class by precisely what it undershoots the other. Every rate move becomes a pure transfer between beneficiaries, and the trustee who protects the fund guarantees it.

The way out is exact and is also a deed question. Notionally segregate, giving each class its own asset duration, and both classes and the total are immunised at once. No single duration does it: the pensioners' net is zero only at 8 and the actives' only at 20. So the deed item naming classes of beneficiaries is what makes fairness achievable at all, which is more weight than that item appears to carry.

Two smaller results. Prudence cannot be tested on outcomes, because a decision with a genuinely positive expected return still shows a loss 42.5634 per cent of the time over a quarter and only 3.0396 per cent over twenty-five years, so a quarterly-reviewed trustee is asked to explain a loss in nearly half of all reviews while doing nothing wrong. And the professional trustee's higher standard of care has an interior optimum: the professional costs 9.0000 in total against the unpaid lay trustee's 20.0000, and the premium professional with the lowest error rate of all costs 12.0000 and is the wrong choice.

Step 2

Why this matters

Trust law is where an investment objective stops being a preference and becomes an obligation. Lecture 2 of the internal environment set out a fund's liabilities and constraints and found that three of four liability parameters have matching instruments while longevity has none, and that a fund in surplus needs a shorter asset duration rather than a longer one. Every one of those findings assumed a single set of liabilities. Trust law is what happens when the liabilities belong to different people with different interests, and the trustee owes a duty to all of them.

That distinction does real work. An investment manager who mismatches duration has made a bet. A trustee who mismatches duration between two classes of beneficiary has, on section 4.2's arithmetic, moved money from one group of people to another, and the fact that the fund as a whole is protected is no defence to the group that lost. The summary's five trustee concerns include both acting for beneficiaries and showing fairness, and it presents them as a list rather than as a tension.

The examinable content is the summary's own lists, and there are four of them: the trust's three parties, the deed's twelve contents, the trustee's six roles and the trustee's five concerns. Memorising them is the easy part. The useful part is knowing which items carry weight, which is what sections 4.4 supplies, and knowing what happens when two of the concerns pull against each other, which is what the deed exists to settle.

The Feynman version. Suppose you look after money for two people. One will need her share in eight years and the other in twenty. You have to buy bonds, and you decide to be sensible: you buy bonds maturing on average in fifteen years and change, which is right for the two of them taken together. The total is safe.

Now interest rates rise by one per cent. Bond prices fall. What the eight-year woman needs also got cheaper, but only a little, because it is near. What the twenty-year man needs got much cheaper, because it is far. So her share of the pot fell by more than her needs did, and his share fell by less than his did. She is worse off and he is better off, by exactly the same amount. You did not gamble, you did not make a mistake, and the total is untouched. You simply took money from her and gave it to him, and you did it by being sensible about the average. The only cure is to stop thinking about the average and buy eight-year bonds for her and twenty-year bonds for him, which requires that somebody wrote down, in the deed, that they are two different classes of person.

Step 3

The mental model

A trust separates two things that are normally joined: who owns an asset and who benefits from it. The trustee is the legal owner and the beneficiary is the beneficial owner, and every rule in the summary's list exists because that separation creates a gap somebody could exploit or neglect. Hold the topic that way and the lists stop being arbitrary.

The duties then follow from the gap. Prudence exists because the trustee bears none of the loss. The prohibition on profiting directly exists because the trustee controls the money. Fairness exists because the trustee chooses between beneficiaries. Compliance with the deed exists because the other four duties conflict, and something has to break the tie. That last point is the one worth carrying: the deed is not a summary of the duties, it is the arbiter between them, which is why the summary lists the deed's twelve contents before it lists any duty.

The quantitative content sits in two places. Wherever a duty involves choosing between beneficiaries, there is a conservation law: what one class gains another loses, and the sum is exactly zero when the fund is protected. Wherever a duty involves buying expertise, there is a trade-off with an interior optimum, because the expertise is paid for out of the beneficiaries' own money. Neither is stated in the summary and both are exact.

Step 4

The detail

4.1 Delegating tasks but not responsibility multiplies what the trustee owns

The summary's trustee "can delegate tasks but not responsibility to experts", and separately lists consulting experts first among the trustee's six roles. Both are sound and together they hide an arithmetic problem. A trustee who keeps one decision in house owns one error rate. A trustee who delegates the same ground to several experts owns all of their errors.

   the arrangement                decisions   error rate   expected errors   at least one
                                  taken       each         the trustee owns  error, prob
   kept in house, one decision                1       0.2000            0.2000        20.0000%
   delegated, 1 expert                        1       0.0500            0.0500         5.0000%
   delegated, 4 experts                       4       0.0500            0.2000        18.5494%
   delegated, 8 experts                       8       0.0500            0.4000        33.6580%
   delegated, 16 experts                     16       0.0500            0.8000        55.9873%

One expert cuts the trustee's expected errors from 0.2000 to 0.0500, a real gain. Sixteen experts raise it to 0.8000, which is 4.0000 times the in-house figure, even though every one of the sixteen is four times as reliable as the trustee. The cancellation point is exactly 4.0000 delegates, the ratio of the two error rates, and it does not depend on the size of any loss. So the summary's clause understates the problem: delegation does not merely leave responsibility with the trustee, it multiplies the number of things the trustee is responsible for.

Notice that the probability of at least one error reaches 55.9873 per cent at sixteen delegates, which is the same figure lecture 3 of the internal environment found for a wrongful dismissal across sixteen manager reviews. It is the same arithmetic seen from the other side, and the coincidence is worth remembering because the two lectures reach opposite conclusions from it: sixteen managers are good for the portfolio and bad for the trustee.

The region, and it is where the deed earns its place. The result above assumes delegation adds decisions. If delegation subdivides one decision instead, so that each expert's error costs a fraction of the whole, the picture inverts.

   number of   error rate   loss if one   expected loss   expected loss
   delegates   each         expert errs   when errors ADD when they SUBDIVIDE
   1                 0.0500      100.0000          5.0000               5.0000
   2                 0.0500       50.0000         10.0000               5.0000
   4                 0.0500       25.0000         20.0000               5.0000
   8                 0.0500       12.5000         40.0000               5.0000
   16                0.0500        6.2500         80.0000               5.0000

When errors add, the trustee's expected loss rises to 80.0000. When they subdivide it is 5.0000 at every number of delegates, exactly constant. So whether delegation helps or harms turns entirely on whether the delegated tasks are separate decisions or slices of one, and that is precisely what the deed settles when it specifies the level of powers and the restrictions on holdings, sale and purchase. The deed is not administrative furniture. It determines the sign of the trustee's exposure.

4.2 Fairness between classes and immunising the fund are incompatible when the portfolio is pooled

The summary's deed names "names and classes of beneficiaries" and its trustee concerns require the trustee to show fairness. Take a fund with two classes: pensioners with 400 of liability at a duration of 8, and active members with 600 at a duration of 20. The blended liability duration is the liability-weighted average, 15.2000, and it lies strictly between the two.

Set the assets to that blended duration, which immunises the fund as a whole, and pool them so every beneficiary holds the same pro-rata slice. Then move rates.

   rate move   pensioners' net   actives' net   the fund's net   the transfer
   in points   gain or loss      gain or loss   position         between classes
      -2.0000%           57.6000       -57.6000           0.0000         57.6000
      -1.0000%           28.8000       -28.8000           0.0000         28.8000
       0.0000%            0.0000         0.0000           0.0000          0.0000
       1.0000%          -28.8000        28.8000           0.0000         28.8000
       2.0000%          -57.6000        57.6000           0.0000         57.6000

The fund's net position is exactly zero at every rate move, so it is perfectly immunised, and no individual class is immunised at any non-zero move. On a one-point rise the pensioners lose 28.8000 and the actives gain exactly 28.8000. So immunising the fund converts every rate move into a pure transfer between classes of beneficiary, of exactly equal and opposite amount.

That is an identity rather than a near miss, and the reason is worth seeing directly. The blended duration is a weighted average, so it overshoots one class by precisely what it undershoots the other once the liabilities are used as weights. The pensioners' mismatch is 15.2000 - 8.0000 = +7.2000 years on 400 of liability, worth 28.8000 per point. The actives' is 15.2000 - 20.0000 = -4.8000 years on 600, worth −28.8000. The liability-weighted mismatches sum to exactly zero, which is what makes the transfer exact.

So the summary's requirement to show fairness is not merely hard to satisfy alongside protecting the fund, it is incompatible with it under a pooled portfolio. The trustee who immunises the fund guarantees that every rate move enriches one class at the other's expense, and the trustee who protects both classes cannot hold a single pooled portfolio. This is the sharpest conflict on the summary's list of trustee concerns, and the summary presents the five concerns as a list of things to do.

The way out is exact, and it is also a deed question. Notionally segregate the assets, giving each class its own duration.

   duration given to   pensioners' net   duration given to   actives' net
   the pensioners      on a 1pt rise     the actives         on a 1pt rise
   8.0000                       0.0000              8.0000         72.0000
   12.0000                    -16.0000             12.0000         48.0000
   15.2000                    -28.8000             15.2000         28.8000
   20.0000                    -48.0000             20.0000          0.0000

The pensioners' net is zero only at 8.0000 and the actives' only at 20.0000, and there is no overlap, so no single asset duration immunises both classes while two sleeves immunise both and the total simultaneously. Whether the trustee may do that is settled by the deed, which is why the item naming classes of beneficiaries carries more weight than it appears to: naming the classes is what makes separate treatment permissible. A deed that names beneficiaries individually and not by class leaves the trustee with a duty of fairness and no instrument for discharging it.

4.3 Prudence cannot be tested on outcomes, and care has an interior optimum

The summary's first trustee concern is to act prudently, and its professional trustee is "held to a higher standard of care". Both invite the question of how prudence is tested, and the arithmetic rules out the obvious answer. Take a decision with a genuinely positive expected return of 6 per cent a year against 16 per cent volatility, and ask how often it looks wrong.

   horizon    expected    volatility   probability the   probability it looks
   in years   return      over period  outcome is a loss wrong to a critic
   0.25            1.5000%      8.0000%           42.5634%              42.5634%
   1.00            6.0000%     16.0000%           35.3830%              35.3830%
   3.00           18.0000%     27.7128%           25.8001%              25.8001%
   10.00          60.0000%     50.5964%           11.7840%              11.7840%
   25.00         150.0000%     80.0000%            3.0396%               3.0396%

A correct decision shows a loss 42.5634 per cent of the time over a quarter and 3.0396 per cent over twenty-five years. So a trustee reviewed quarterly is asked to explain a loss in nearly half of all reviews while doing nothing wrong, which is why prudence is a standard about process rather than results, and why the summary's regular reporting item specifies an annual cycle to beneficiaries rather than a quarterly one. The reporting frequency is not an administrative choice: it sets the rate at which a sound trustee is made to look unsound.

Now price the professional trustee's higher standard, which is worth something and costs something.

   the trustee            error rate   expected loss   fee charged   total cost to the
                          each year    at 100 a loss   each year     beneficiaries
   lay, unpaid                  0.2000         20.0000        0.0000            20.0000
   lay, expenses only           0.1800         18.0000        0.5000            18.5000
   professional                 0.0500          5.0000        4.0000             9.0000
   professional, premium        0.0300          3.0000        9.0000            12.0000

The professional costs 9.0000 in total against the unpaid lay trustee's 20.0000, so paying for care is cheaper than not paying for it, which is the case for the summary's professional trustee stated as a number. And the premium professional costs 12.0000, so the optimum is interior: buying more care than the errors are worth is as much a failure of duty as buying too little, because the fee comes out of the same fund the care is protecting.

Notice what that does not support. It gives no reason to prefer the trustee with the lowest error rate, only the one at the minimum of fee plus expected loss. The premium trustee has the lowest error rate at 0.0300 and is the wrong choice. So the familiar argument that a fiduciary should buy the best available advice is wrong as stated, and right only where the loss avoided exceeds the fee. The summary's concern that a trustee should not profit directly and its deed item covering the remuneration agreement are the two ends of that trade: the fee is a direct profit to the trustee, permitted precisely because the deed says so and in the amount the deed says.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Section 4.2's transfer is the most under-discussed problem in defined benefit pension management, and the reason is that nothing in a standard funding report can see it. Every measure of a fund's risk consolidates the liabilities before it compares them with the assets, so a perfectly immunised fund reports zero mismatch while running a systematic transfer between two groups of members on every rate move. The transfer is not hidden by anybody's choice. It is hidden by the arithmetic of aggregation, which nets it to nothing at exactly the level the report is written.

The practitioner's version of the point is that duration matching is a statement about a portfolio and fairness is a statement about people, and the two use the same numbers to answer different questions. When a scheme is well funded and its membership is homogeneous the distinction does not bite. When a scheme is closed to new entrants, so that pensioners are a growing share of a shrinking liability, the blended duration moves every year and the transfer moves with it, always in the same direction. A closed scheme's asset allocation is therefore a distributional policy whether or not the trustees intend it as one.

On section 4.1, the habit worth forming is to ask, of each delegation, whether the delegate can lose money the other delegates cannot. If they can, errors add and the trustee's exposure grows with the number of appointments. If the mandate constrains each delegate to a slice, errors subdivide and the exposure is flat. That question is answerable from the mandate document alone and is worth more than any amount of due diligence on the delegates themselves, because it determines whether due diligence on sixteen firms reduces risk or manufactures it.

On section 4.3, the observation that a sound decision looks wrong 42.5634 per cent of the time over a quarter is the single most useful number in this lecture for anybody sitting on a trustee board. It says the correct response to a quarterly loss is almost always to do nothing, and it says why boards nevertheless act: a trustee who does nothing four times and is wrong once looks negligent, and a trustee who acts four times and is wrong three looks diligent. The incentive runs against the arithmetic, and the summary's annual reporting cycle is a partial defence against it.

One limit on all of this. Section 4.2's identity depends on first-order duration and a parallel shift, so a non-parallel move breaks the exact equality, though not the direction: a flattening curve makes the transfer larger than 28.8000 and a steepening one smaller. The structure survives. The number does not, and it should be quoted with the assumption attached.

Connections

What this assumes. The duration matching this lecture turns into a fairness problem is derived in objectives, liabilities and constraints, where three of four liability parameters had matching instruments and a fund in surplus needed a shorter asset duration, not a longer one. The delegation arithmetic sits opposite governance, trustees and delegation, which produced the 55.99 per cent wrongful dismissal figure section 4.1 reuses from the other side, and the mandate instruments that decide whether errors add or subdivide are in mandates and the statement of investment principles. The two-term cost framework section 4.3 applies to the choice of trustee is set up in the principles of financial regulation, and the underlying bond mathematics is in government and corporate bonds.

Where this goes next. Lecture 9 covers market conduct and the regulation of financial services providers, which is the summary's list of customer legislation principles and its institutional investment practices, and several of those practices are the operational answer to problems this lecture leaves open. Lecture 10 covers corporate governance, where the board occupies the position the trustee occupies here, with shareholders as the beneficiaries and the same conflict between classes of them. Lecture 11 covers responsible investment, where the question of whose interests a fiduciary may consider is exactly section 4.2's question with a longer time horizon. Lectures 12 to 14 close the regulatory arc, and the six indices lectures follow.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank has no beneficiaries, and that difference explains more than it seems to. A bank's depositors are creditors with a contractual claim rather than beneficial owners of a pool, so section 4.2's transfer cannot arise: nobody's claim moves with the value of the assets. What arises instead is the ranking of creditors, which is the same conservation law in a different guise, since a loss allocated to one tranche is a loss not allocated to another. That ordering is set out in capital management. The delegation result of section 4.1 applies to a bank directly and more severely, because a bank delegates credit decisions across thousands of officers and models rather than sixteen managers: the machinery that keeps those errors subdividing rather than adding is the limit and mandate structure in risk management. And the observation of section 4.3, that a sound decision looks wrong 42.5634 per cent of the time over a quarter, is why model monitoring is specified over multi-year windows in A-IRB capital modelling rather than quarterly.

A second, milder point is that the delegation clause understates its own consequence, since delegation multiplies the number of items the trustee is responsible for rather than merely retaining responsibility for one. Everything numerical is invented for illustration: error rates of 20 per cent in house against 5 and 3 per cent for experts across one to sixteen delegates, a loss of 100, liabilities of 400 at duration 8 and 600 at duration 20 with rate moves of plus and minus one and two points, an expected return of 6 per cent against 16 per cent volatility over horizons from a quarter to twenty-five years, and trustee fees from zero to 9. Twenty-one claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions, all twenty-one passed, and none can pass without the computation. One sub-table was rewritten during checking because it computed a class's net position by subtracting an expression from itself, which is zero by construction rather than by result; it now searches over asset durations so the zero is located rather than assumed, and that rewrite produced the sharper finding that the two classes' zeros do not overlap. Every headline finding states the region in which it holds: the transfer identity assumes first-order duration and a parallel shift, so a flattening curve makes the transfer larger than 28.8000 and a steepening one smaller while the direction survives; the delegation result assumes errors add rather than subdivide, and is exactly reversed in the other case; and the optimum standard of care is interior, so neither the cheapest nor the most careful trustee is the right one.
