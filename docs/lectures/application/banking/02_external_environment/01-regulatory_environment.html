The regulatory environment

Banks are regulated more heavily than almost any other industry on earth. The regulation comes in three layers, has four aims, and produces an alphabet soup of agencies. Once you see the structure, the soup makes sense.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Banking regulation sits in three layers. At the top, a small group of supranational bodies (the Basel Committee, the Financial Stability Board, the IASB) writes standards. Standards are not law. Each country then chooses to adopt them through its own national regulators, who write the binding rules. Finally, the national regulators supervise individual banks, asking for capital, liquidity, returns, recovery plans, and the rest.

The regulation has four aims: maintain financial stability, protect consumers, prevent financial crime, and ensure fair competition. Almost every regulatory acronym you will meet (PRA, FCA, FSCA, PA, SARB, FCA, ECB, EBA, OCC) maps to one or two of these aims. The dominant modern design is the twin peaks model, in which one regulator does prudential supervision (is the bank safe?) and a separate regulator does conduct supervision (is the bank treating customers fairly?).

Feynman test Imagine you ran a country and you had to stop banks from blowing up your economy, robbing your citizens, laundering criminal money, and forming a cartel. You would not give all four jobs to one office, because the skills and incentives are different. So you set up two offices, one watching how much risk the bank takes (prudential) and one watching how it sells to people (conduct). Both offices apply rules that mostly came from international committees you helped write. That is the whole regulatory environment, minus the names.
Step 2

Why this matters

Every model an actuary builds inside a bank exists to answer a regulator's question. The A-IRB capital model exists because the PRA, SARB or ECB requires it. The IFRS 9 ECL model exists because the IASB published the standard. The ICAAP exists because Pillar 2 requires it. The recovery plan exists because of the FSB. If you do not know which regulator wrote the rule you are implementing, you cannot interpret it correctly when the wording is ambiguous, and you cannot anticipate the next revision when the regulator publishes a consultation paper.

The architecture also matters at moments of stress. When Silicon Valley Bank failed in March 2023, the FDIC was the receiver, the Federal Reserve was the lender of last resort, the OCC sat with the failed bank's national-bank licence, and the SEC supervised the holding company. Four agencies, one bank, two days. A clean view of who does what is the difference between a clear analysis and confusion.

For the F107 syllabus specifically, the South African regulators (SARB, PA, FSCA, NCR, Information Regulator, FIC) get more weight than they would in a UK or EU course. Memorise the acronyms by function, not by alphabet.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture a three-layer stack. International standard-setters at the top, national regulators in the middle, individual banks at the bottom. Standards flow down (sometimes via regional bodies like the EBA), reports and disclosures flow up.

A useful second picture: the twin peaks. One regulator looks at the balance sheet (prudential), the other looks at the customer-facing conduct. The twin peaks model exists in the UK, South Africa, Netherlands, Australia, New Zealand and Belgium. The US and EU run different models that look like twin peaks if you squint but differ in detail.

Step 4

The detail

The supranational layer

Standards-setters that have no legal authority over anyone, but whose standards become law in most large economies. The fact that the same Basel rules apply (with national tweaks) in Johannesburg, London and Frankfurt is the single most important piece of co-ordination in modern finance.

BCBS (Basel Committee on Banking Supervision)
Hosted by the BIS in Basel, Switzerland. Membership of about 28 jurisdictions' central banks and bank supervisors. Writes the Basel framework, which is the global standard for capital, liquidity, large exposures, leverage, disclosure and supervision. Basel I (1988), Basel II (2004), Basel III (2010 onward), Basel IV / "Basel III finalisation" (BCBS d424, effective from 2023 onward with phased transitions). Not legally binding; binding only after adoption by national regulators.
FSB (Financial Stability Board)
Established 2009 as successor to the Financial Stability Forum, hosted by the BIS. Co-ordinates the work of national financial-stability authorities and standard-setters. Authored the post-2008 G-SIB framework, the TLAC standard for loss-absorbing capacity, and the Key Attributes of Effective Resolution Regimes. Roughly the "constitutional" body sitting above the BCBS, IAIS and IOSCO.
IASB (International Accounting Standards Board)
The standard-setting body of the IFRS Foundation. Writes IFRS standards, including IFRS 9 (financial instruments), IFRS 17 (insurance), and the rest. Used in over 140 jurisdictions. Not used in the US, which retains US GAAP under FASB. Covered in detail in Lecture 2 (accounting environment).
FATF (Financial Action Task Force)
The global standard-setter for anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing. Publishes the FATF Recommendations and runs mutual evaluations of national AML / CFT regimes. South Africa was grey-listed by FATF in February 2023 over deficiencies in its AML framework; removal expected during 2025. Hosted at the OECD in Paris.
IOSCO and IAIS
IOSCO sets standards for securities markets; IAIS for insurance supervision. Both feed into the FSB. Less central to bread-and-butter banking regulation but matter for banks running broker-dealer or bancassurance arms.

The national layer: South Africa

The South African regulatory architecture moved to a twin peaks model in 2017 with the Financial Sector Regulation Act (FSR Act, Act 9 of 2017), splitting prudential supervision from market conduct.

SARB (South African Reserve Bank)
The central bank, with a constitutional mandate to "protect the value of the currency in the interest of balanced and sustainable economic growth". Owns financial stability, monetary policy, payment-system oversight and the issuance of notes and coins. Houses the Prudential Authority as a subsidiary. Acts as lender of last resort. Covered as a central bank in Lecture 5.
PA (Prudential Authority)
Operational since 2018 as a separate juristic person within SARB, modelled on the UK PRA. Supervises banks, insurers, market infrastructures and financial conglomerates against the Banks Act, the Insurance Act and Basel-derived prudential standards. Authorises and licenses banks. Sets capital and liquidity requirements; runs the supervisory review process.
FSCA (Financial Sector Conduct Authority)
Operational since 2018, successor to the Financial Services Board (FSB-ZA). Conduct supervisor for the entire financial sector: market integrity, treating customers fairly (TCF), product disclosure, and enforcement against misconduct. Enforces FAIS (Financial Advisory and Intermediary Services Act), the FMA (Financial Markets Act), and the Conduct of Financial Institutions (COFI) Bill once enacted.
NCR (National Credit Regulator)
Established under the National Credit Act (NCA, 2005). Regulates the consumer-credit industry: registration of credit providers and debt counsellors, affordability rules, prohibition of reckless lending, the National Loans Register. Note the NCR sits outside the twin peaks proper, reporting to the dti rather than SARB or National Treasury.
FIC (Financial Intelligence Centre)
South Africa's financial-intelligence unit, established under the Financial Intelligence Centre Act (FICA, 2001). Receives suspicious and unusual transaction reports from accountable institutions (banks, attorneys, estate agents, etc.) and shares intelligence with law enforcement. Pushed to grow rapidly after the 2023 FATF greylisting.
CoDI (Corporation for Deposit Insurance)
SARB subsidiary that runs the new South African Deposit Insurance Scheme, fully operational from April 2024. Covers retail deposits up to R100,000 per depositor per bank. Funded by levies on participating banks.
Information Regulator
Independent body enforcing the Protection of Personal Information Act (POPIA, in force July 2021) and PAIA. Matters increasingly for banks given the scale of customer-data processing.

The national layer: United Kingdom

The UK moved to twin peaks in 2013 under the Financial Services Act 2012, after the tripartite model (Treasury / FSA / Bank of England) failed to spot the 2008 crisis. The new design put the prudential regulator inside the central bank.

Bank of England (BoE)
The central bank. Houses the PRA, the FPC (macroprudential), the MPC (monetary policy), and the Resolution Directorate (executes resolutions of failed banks under the Banking Act 2009). The BoE's Governor sits at the apex of UK financial supervision.
PRA (Prudential Regulation Authority)
A part of the BoE. Authorises and prudentially supervises banks, building societies, credit unions, insurers and major investment firms. Implements the UK version of Basel: the PRA Rulebook plus the CRR (UK CRR + the near-final amendments now being phased in), with supervisory statements such as SS3/24 (definition of default) and SS4/24 (the IRB approach).
FCA (Financial Conduct Authority)
A separate entity, not part of the BoE. Conduct regulator for around 50,000 firms across banking, asset management, consumer credit and markets. Enforces the Consumer Duty (in force July 2023), product-governance and market-integrity rules, and conducts criminal investigations under FSMA powers.
FPC (Financial Policy Committee)
Macroprudential committee of the BoE, established by the Financial Services Act 2012. Sets the UK countercyclical capital buffer (CCyB), conducts the annual UK bank stress test, and identifies system-wide risks. Distinct from the FCA's microprudential and conduct work.
FSCS (Financial Services Compensation Scheme)
The UK deposit-insurance and investor-compensation scheme. Pays out up to GBP 85,000 per eligible depositor per failed bank. Funded by levies on the industry. Operates alongside the BoE's resolution function: if a bank is resolved as a going concern, the FSCS may not need to pay out.

The national layer: European Union

EU banking supervision sits between the supranational and national layers. Significant institutions (about 110 banks covering 80% of euro-area banking assets) are directly supervised by the ECB; the rest sit with national competent authorities (BaFin in Germany, ACPR in France, etc.) under common EU rules.

ECB (European Central Bank)
Operates the Single Supervisory Mechanism (SSM) since November 2014. Directly supervises significant banks in the euro area. Also runs monetary policy for the eurozone. Covered as a central bank in Lecture 5.
EBA (European Banking Authority)
Located in Paris (post-Brexit move from London in 2019). Writes binding technical standards (RTS, ITS), runs the biennial EU-wide stress test, and publishes guidelines on definition of default, ECL, internal models. Maintains the EU "Single Rulebook" with all 27 member states.
SRB (Single Resolution Board)
Brussels-based; the resolution authority for significant euro-area banks. Approves recovery and resolution plans, sets MREL targets, and would execute resolution actions if a major bank failed. Manages the Single Resolution Fund.
ESMA, EIOPA
EU-level securities and insurance authorities. ESMA approves prospectuses, supervises CCPs, and oversees benchmarks; EIOPA writes Solvency II technical standards and runs insurance stress tests.

The national layer: United States (for context)

The US is famously fragmented. A bank may be chartered nationally (regulated by the OCC) or by a state (regulated by the state banking department, with the Federal Reserve or FDIC as the federal supervisor). Holding companies sit with the Federal Reserve; the FDIC runs deposit insurance and acts as receiver; the SEC and CFTC handle securities and derivatives; the CFPB handles consumer protection; FinCEN handles AML / CFT. The model is the opposite of consolidated twin peaks, but the political cost of reform is high enough that the architecture has been broadly stable since the Dodd-Frank Act of 2010.

Twin peaks regulation

The twin peaks model places prudential supervision and conduct supervision in two separate authorities, on the principle that the two have different cultures and incentives. A prudential supervisor cares whether the bank survives. A conduct supervisor cares whether the bank's customers thrive. These goals are usually aligned but not always: a fee or a sales practice that is good for the bank's profitability can be bad for the customer. Combining the roles in one agency makes the dual mandate harder to police.

Twin peaks jurisdictions:

  • Australia (1998): APRA (prudential) and ASIC (conduct). The original.
  • Netherlands (2002): DNB and AFM.
  • United Kingdom (2013): PRA inside the BoE, plus FCA.
  • South Africa (2018): PA inside SARB, plus FSCA.
  • Belgium (2011): NBB and FSMA.
  • New Zealand (2011 onward): RBNZ prudential, FMA conduct.

The alternative integrated model (one regulator does both, as the UK's FSA did from 2001 to 2013) is what the post-2008 reforms moved away from. The institutional / functional model in the US assigns regulators by institution type or function, rather than by aim.

Financial-stability rules in more detail

The bulk of an actuary's work in central functions sits here. Prudential supervisors require banks to hold capital and liquidity proportionate to their risk, evidenced by an internal capital adequacy assessment (ICAAP) and liquidity assessment (ILAAP). The supervisor reviews these annually under the supervisory review and evaluation process (SREP in the EU; equivalent processes elsewhere).

The key tools:

  • Pillar 1 minimum capital: at least 8% of risk-weighted assets, of which 4.5% must be CET1. Calculated under standardised or internal-model approaches.
  • Pillar 2 capital add-ons: bank-specific top-ups set by the supervisor after reviewing the ICAAP. Pillar 2A covers risks not adequately captured in Pillar 1; Pillar 2B (the "PRA buffer" in the UK) covers stress losses.
  • Capital buffers: Capital Conservation Buffer (2.5%), Countercyclical Capital Buffer (0% to 2.5%+, set by the FPC or equivalent), G-SIB and D-SIB surcharges (1% to 3.5%), Systemic Risk Buffer.
  • LCR and NSFR: 30-day stressed liquidity coverage (LCR >= 100%) and one-year stable funding (NSFR >= 100%).
  • Recovery and resolution planning: every bank must maintain a recovery plan (what it will do in stress) and a resolution plan / "living will" (how the authorities will resolve it). MREL or TLAC targets ensure enough loss-absorbing debt sits behind the bank's depositors.

Consumer protection rules in more detail

Three blocks: market-conduct rules, deposit protection, and data protection.

Market-conduct rules govern how products are designed, sold, and serviced. The South African enforcement spine is FAIS (the Financial Advisory and Intermediary Services Act, governing advice and intermediation), the FMA (Financial Markets Act, governing licensed markets and infrastructure), and the COFI Bill once enacted (Conduct of Financial Institutions). The South African Treating Customers Fairly (TCF) framework sets six outcomes that firms must demonstrate, from "customers are confident that fair treatment is central to firm culture" to "products perform as customers expect". The UK equivalent is the Consumer Duty under PRIN 12 (in force July 2023 for new products, July 2024 for closed books), which requires firms to act to deliver "good outcomes" across four areas: products and services, price and value, consumer understanding, and consumer support.

Deposit insurance is the consumer-protection backstop against bank failure. It exists in every major banking jurisdiction:

Deposit insurance has a well-known side-effect: it can encourage risk-taking by managers and laziness by depositors. The standard response is to combine the insurance with effective supervision, with risk-based premiums, and with co-insurance or step-down haircuts (none of which are used in CoDI, FSCS or FDIC's standard regime).

Data protection. POPIA (South Africa, in force July 2021), GDPR (EU and EEA since 2018), and the UK GDPR (post-Brexit) all set out similar principles for the lawful processing of personal data:

  • Lawfulness, fairness, transparency.
  • Purpose limitation.
  • Data minimisation.
  • Accuracy.
  • Storage limitation.
  • Integrity and confidentiality.
  • Accountability.

Banks usually sit as "responsible parties" or "data controllers" under these regimes, and as "operators" or "processors" when handling another firm's data (for instance, a card-network's transaction data). Fines under POPIA can reach ZAR 10 million; under GDPR they reach the higher of EUR 20 million or 4% of global turnover. Operational risk teams take these seriously.

Financial-crime prevention rules in more detail

The AML / KYC stack rests on FATF's 40 Recommendations, implemented locally through FICA (SA), the Money Laundering Regulations (UK), the Bank Secrecy Act and USA PATRIOT Act (US). Banks must:

  • Identify and verify customers at on-boarding (KYC / CDD), with enhanced due diligence (EDD) for higher-risk customers, including PEPs, customers in higher-risk jurisdictions, and complex ownership structures.
  • Monitor transactions continuously and file reports on suspicious or unusual activity (CTRs, SARs).
  • Screen against sanctions lists (OFAC, UN, EU, UK OFSI, SA).
  • Maintain a Money Laundering Reporting Officer (MLRO) function with sufficient authority and independence.

South Africa's FATF greylisting in February 2023 reflected weaknesses in (i) availability and use of beneficial-ownership information, (ii) timely investigation of money laundering and terrorist financing, and (iii) effective sanctions enforcement. Removal is conditional on meeting an action plan; banks have invested heavily in additional CDD and monitoring capability since 2023, and SARS, FIC and Hawks have ramped up cases.

Fair competition rules in more detail

Easy to overlook because competition law is rarely the headline regulator for a bank, but it does intrude. The South African Competition Commission has investigated currency-trading collusion (the "rand-rigging" cases, ongoing since 2017), bank-to-bank merger control, and access to payment-systems infrastructure. The UK Competition and Markets Authority drove the rollout of Open Banking in 2018, requiring the nine largest UK banks to expose customer-account data to authorised third parties via APIs under PSD2.

Fair-competition rules also include the broader Companies Act framework (Companies Act 71 of 2008 in SA, Companies Act 2006 in the UK), which sets the governance baseline for shareholders, directors and external auditors.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Twin peaks did not prevent the 2008 crisis; it was a response to it

The Australian twin-peaks model was operational well before 2008 and its banks held up well. But the UK FSA model failed: it could not see the wood for the trees, and Northern Rock failed under its watch. Twin peaks gained traction afterwards as a structural correction. Whether it would prevent the next crisis is unproven; what it does is force two different cultures to look at the same bank, which is a defensible institutional design.

Standards are soft until they bite

BCBS standards have no legal authority. But when a regulator considers a bank's internal model under SS4/24 (UK) or the EBA's IRB guidelines (EU), the assessment is benchmarked against the BCBS-published rule. Refusing to follow BCBS standards is technically lawful, but practically a route to model rejection and a higher Pillar 2 add-on. The line between "soft law" and "binding law" is finer than the textbook suggests.

Regulatory arbitrage is the dual of regulation

Every rule creates an incentive to do the same economic activity in the cheapest regulated form. Capital weights for corporate exposures drove banks to securitise corporate loans into the trading book pre-2008; the regulator responded with the new market-risk framework and FRTB. Leverage-ratio constraints drove some banks to push prime-brokerage activity into separate vehicles. Liquidity rules push deposits into money-market funds during stress (the 2023 March deposit migration in the US). The regulator and the regulated are in a constant cat-and-mouse equilibrium; new rules close yesterday's loopholes and create tomorrow's.

South Africa specifics for F107

Three structural features matter and are often examined.

First, the FSR Act re-architecture in 2017 is the single largest change in SA financial regulation since the Banks Act of 1990. Memorise the date and the names (FSR Act, PA, FSCA, FSCC). The Council of Financial Regulators (CFR) co-ordinates between them.

Second, CoDI's deposit-insurance scheme is recent (April 2024) and small (ZAR 100,000 limit). Compare with FDIC's USD 250,000 and FSCS's GBP 85,000. The relatively low limit means CoDI is a consumer-protection scheme rather than a bank-stability scheme; large depositors would still run.

Third, the FATF greylisting matters. Expect a question on its causes, on the action plan, or on its implications for SA bank cross-border operations (correspondent-banking de-risking pressure has risen, and banks now spend materially more on AML / sanctions).

Common F107 traps

Watch out Six traps. (1) Confusing "BIS" (the bank, hosting the BCBS) with "BCBS" (the committee writing standards). (2) Treating the PRA as separate from the Bank of England; it is part of the BoE. (3) Treating the FSCA's predecessor "FSB" (Financial Services Board) as the same thing as the FSB (Financial Stability Board); they are unrelated. (4) Forgetting that the NCR is not part of the twin peaks; it sits under the dti. (5) Assuming all EU banks are supervised by the ECB; only the 110 or so "significant institutions" are. (6) Treating deposit insurance as covering every type of deposit; corporate deposits above the limit are not protected, which is why SVB's collapse spread fast in 2023.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. State the four aims of banking regulation and give one example of a rule for each aim.
  2. Explain the twin peaks model and identify the prudential and conduct regulator in South Africa, the UK and Australia.
  3. What is the difference between the BCBS and the BIS? Between the FSB (Stability) and the FSCA (Conduct)?
  4. Why is FATF relevant to South African banks specifically as of 2023?
  5. Describe the role of CoDI. How does its coverage limit compare with the FSCS and the FDIC?
  6. Why was the UK FSA replaced in 2013, and what model replaced it?
  7. List the seven POPIA principles for processing personal data. How would a breach of one affect a bank?
  8. What is the difference between Pillar 1 capital, Pillar 2A and Pillar 2B? Who sets each?
  9. How does the ECB's Single Supervisory Mechanism allocate supervision between EU-level and national-level supervisors?
  10. Why does deposit insurance create moral hazard, and how have regulators tried to mitigate it?
