Central banks

A central bank is several institutions at once: the government's bank, the banks' bank, the currency's bank, the regulator's home, and the system's last line of defence. Each role connects to the others in ways that look messy but mostly work.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A modern central bank does six things. It (1) sets monetary policy (interest rates and the money supply), (2) issues notes and coins, (3) manages the country's foreign reserves, (4) maintains the payment system, (5) acts as lender of last resort, and (6) in most jurisdictions either supervises banks directly or houses the prudential regulator that does. The mix differs by country: the Fed and SARB do most of these themselves, the Bank of England houses the PRA but the FCA is separate, the ECB shares supervision with national authorities through the SSM.

The two roles that matter most for banks day-to-day are monetary policy (it sets the rate every loan is priced off) and lender of last resort (it backstops liquidity if the wholesale market freezes). The two roles that matter most for the system are financial stability (the central bank decides the CCyB, runs stress tests, and operates the resolution framework) and payment-system oversight (the central bank operates the high-value real-time gross settlement system on which interbank transfers depend).

Feynman test Imagine a country's economy as a swimming pool full of money. The central bank is the pool attendant. It sets the temperature (the policy rate), tops up the water (QE) or drains it (QT), watches who is doing what at the deep end (financial stability), and dives in to rescue anyone who looks like drowning (lender of last resort). It does not run any individual swimming lesson; it runs the pool itself. Once you see that, the policy debates and the alphabet soup of operations make sense.
Step 2

Why this matters

Every floating-rate loan in the country reprices off a central-bank-set policy rate. Every bank's liquidity buffer is calibrated to a stress in which the only counterparty still lending is the central bank. The interest-rate risk in the banking book (IRRBB) lecture's NII / NIM sensitivities are quoted in basis points of policy-rate moves. The 2022 to 2024 cycle (BoE base rate from 0.1% to 5.25%, SARB repo from 3.5% to 8.25%, Fed funds from 0 to 5.5%) was the largest test of bank IRRBB management in 40 years.

Equally important, an actuary in central functions interacts with the central bank constantly: ICAAP and ILAAP submissions go to the PA or PRA; supervisory dialogue, stress tests, and recovery and resolution planning all flow through central-bank-supervised processes. Reading a Monetary Policy Committee minute, a Financial Stability Report, or a Quarterly Bulletin is part of the job.

For F107, expect questions on the central bank's role in monetary policy, money-market operations, lender-of-last-resort, and the countercyclical capital buffer. The South African specifics (SARB, the PA inside SARB, the MPC's inflation-targeting mandate) are likely to be examined.

Step 3

The mental model

One institution, six hats. Each hat has its own committee or division, with its own committee chair, statutory mandate and operating tools. The hats interact: monetary-policy decisions affect financial stability; supervisory decisions feed into recovery planning; payment-system operations support both.

Step 4

The detail

Who central banks bank

A central bank is not a high-street bank. It does not take retail deposits, run current accounts for households, or lend to corporates. Its customers are governments and other banks:

  • The government. The Treasury or Ministry of Finance holds its cash balances at the central bank, issues debt to the market via the central bank as agent, and receives the central bank's seigniorage profits.
  • The banking system. Each commercial bank holds a reserve account at the central bank. These reserves count as the highest quality liquid asset on the bank's balance sheet, can be used to settle interbank payments, and form the basis on which the central bank conducts monetary policy.
  • Other central banks. Foreign central banks hold reserves and gold at the BIS in Basel and at peer central banks (the New York Fed holds the largest single repository of foreign gold reserves in the world).

1. Monetary policy

Almost every modern central bank operates an inflation-targeting framework. The mandate is defined by statute (Bank of England Act 1998, SARB Act 1989 with later amendments, Federal Reserve Act 1913). The target is typically expressed as a single number (UK: 2% CPI, SA: 3% to 6% CPI target range with 4.5% midpoint, US: 2% PCE), with secondary objectives (employment, financial stability) at varying levels of formality.

The decision is taken by a committee:

  • UK: Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), 9 members, monthly meetings (now 8 a year), simple majority vote.
  • South Africa: Monetary Policy Committee, 6 members chaired by the Governor, 6 meetings a year, consensus-based.
  • US: Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC), 12 voting members (7 Board governors + 5 regional Fed presidents), 8 meetings a year.
  • EU: Governing Council of the ECB, 25 members, meetings every six weeks.

The toolkit:

  • Policy rate. The interest rate at which the central bank lends reserves overnight (the Bank Rate in the UK, the Repo Rate in SA, the Fed Funds Target Rate in the US, the Deposit Facility Rate in the eurozone). This is the rate from which the rest of the yield curve hangs.
  • Open-market operations. Daily buying and selling of short-dated government securities to keep market rates near the policy rate.
  • Quantitative easing (QE) and tightening (QT). Large-scale buying or selling of government bonds and other securities to influence longer-dated yields and the money supply. Operative since 2009 (in major economies), reversed from 2022 onward.
  • Forward guidance. Statements about the expected path of the policy rate, designed to anchor market expectations.
  • Reserve requirements. In some jurisdictions, banks are required to hold a minimum fraction of liabilities as central-bank reserves. The Fed reduced its requirement to zero in March 2020; the SARB and ECB still use modest requirements.

Quantitative easing in one paragraph

When the policy rate is already at zero (or close to it) and inflation is still below target, central banks have an extra lever: buying long-dated government bonds (and sometimes corporate bonds, MBS, and ETFs) from banks and investors. The mechanical effect is to expand the central-bank balance sheet, push down long-dated yields, and increase commercial-bank reserves at the central bank. The behavioural effect is to encourage investors to substitute into riskier assets (the "portfolio balance channel") and to signal commitment to easy policy. QE was first deployed by the Bank of Japan in 2001; expanded massively post-2008 by the Fed, BoE and ECB; and reversed (QT) from 2022 onward as inflation surged. The South African Reserve Bank did not do QE in the 2008 to 2021 period, partly because rates were not at the lower bound; SARB did a modest bond-purchase programme in March 2020 to address gilt-market dysfunction.

2. Currency and foreign reserves

The central bank holds a monopoly on issuing notes and coins, which gives it seigniorage income (the interest earned on the assets backing the currency, minus the cost of producing it). The central bank also manages the country's foreign reserves, intervening in FX markets when necessary to smooth disorderly conditions or, in fixed-exchange-rate regimes, to defend the peg.

South Africa runs a floating-rate exchange-rate regime and intervenes rarely, but SARB holds about USD 60 billion in gross reserves (mostly USD-denominated). The Bank of England holds reserves on behalf of HM Treasury, with policy intervention coordinated jointly. The Fed holds the world's largest reserve currency (USD), so the question of "managing reserves" has a different shape.

3. Payment systems

Central banks operate (or oversee) the high-value real-time gross settlement (RTGS) system through which large interbank payments settle. Each transfer settles individually in central-bank money, eliminating settlement risk between the banks involved. Names worth knowing:

  • CHAPS in the UK, operated by the Bank of England since 2017.
  • SAMOS (South African Multiple Option Settlement) operated by SARB.
  • Fedwire in the US, operated by the Federal Reserve.
  • TARGET2 (succeeded by TARGET / T2 in March 2023) operated by the Eurosystem.

Below the RTGS sit retail payment systems (Faster Payments and BACS in the UK; PayShap, EFT and SAGE in SA; ACH and Zelle in the US; SEPA in the eurozone). Some are run by industry consortia rather than the central bank, but the central bank typically has oversight authority. The migration to ISO 20022 messaging (richer payment data) is the dominant modernisation project in 2022 to 2027.

4. Lender of last resort

The classical formulation of the lender-of-last-resort role is Walter Bagehot's 1873 rule: "lend freely, at a high rate, against good collateral, to solvent but illiquid institutions". Each clause matters.

  • Lend freely to stop the panic spreading. The central bank should not stint, because a half-rescue can confirm doubts and accelerate the run.
  • At a high rate to deter abuse in normal times. The central-bank facility must be more expensive than market funding when the market is functioning.
  • Against good collateral to protect the central-bank balance sheet. Loans are repaid out of the collateral if the bank fails.
  • To solvent institutions only. A central bank should not lend to a bankrupt institution; that is a fiscal decision for the government, not a monetary one for the central bank.

The modern toolkit:

  • Discount window (Fed) or Operational Standing Facilities (BoE) or Standing Facility (SARB): an always-available overnight loan facility against eligible collateral.
  • Emergency Liquidity Assistance (ELA): extraordinary, conditional lending to a named institution. Used in the 2007 Northern Rock rescue, the 2012 Cyprus crisis, the 2022 Credit Suisse stress.
  • Bilateral swap lines between central banks (Fed-ECB-BoE-SNB-BoC-BoJ): provide foreign-currency liquidity to domestic banks when international wholesale markets freeze. Activated in 2008, 2020 and 2023.

Recourse to central-bank funding signals difficulty. Banks aim to avoid it in normal conditions. The 2020 Covid stress saw the BoE actively encourage banks to use its facilities, breaking the stigma; the 2023 SVB stress saw the Fed introduce a new Bank Term Funding Programme to lend against par-valued (not mark-to-market) collateral, an extraordinary deviation from the Bagehot template.

5. Supervision

In most jurisdictions, the bank supervisor is housed at or within the central bank. The architecture varies:

  • South Africa: the Prudential Authority (PA) sits within SARB as a juristic person established by the FSR Act 2017.
  • United Kingdom: the Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA) sits within the Bank of England, established by the Financial Services Act 2012.
  • Eurozone: the ECB directly supervises significant institutions through the Single Supervisory Mechanism (SSM) since 2014; smaller banks are supervised by national authorities.
  • United States: the Federal Reserve supervises bank holding companies and state-chartered Fed member banks. The OCC supervises national banks. The FDIC supervises state-chartered non-member banks. Three federal supervisors, with the Fed central but not sole.

The principal supervisory cycle each year is the supervisory review and evaluation process (SREP in the EU; the SRP in the UK; equivalent processes in SA). This consists of ICAAP and ILAAP reviews, on-site inspections, regulatory return analysis, and a written feedback letter setting Pillar 2 capital and liquidity requirements. The cycle was covered in Lecture 1 of this section.

6. Financial stability

Where supervision focuses on individual banks, financial stability focuses on the system. The instruments are macroprudential, set by a committee within the central bank.

  • Countercyclical capital buffer (CCyB). The headline macroprudential tool. Set in the range 0% to 2.5% (or higher in some jurisdictions), countercyclically. The UK FPC, the ECB Governing Council with national designated authorities, and the SARB Financial Stability Committee each set the CCyB for their jurisdiction.
  • Systemic risk buffer (SRB). An additional capital buffer for G-SIBs and D-SIBs, set on a permanent rather than cyclical basis.
  • Sectoral buffers. Additional capital weights or LTV caps on specific lending sectors (UK PRA on the buy-to-let mortgage market; ECB on residential real estate in several countries).
  • Stress tests. Annual scenario analyses of the major banks, covering capital, leverage and (since 2020) climate risk. The UK uses the FPC-led Bank of England Annual Stress Test. The EU uses biennial EBA / ECB stress tests. The US uses the Fed's CCAR / DFAST programme. SA conducts a stress test annually but the framework is less formal.
  • Recovery and resolution planning. Each bank submits a Recovery Plan (what it will do in stress) and the authorities maintain a Resolution Plan (how they will resolve it if recovery fails).

Inflation targeting and the post-2022 cycle

The 2020 to 2024 inflation experience is the central event in modern monetary policy. Inflation surged in the second half of 2021 (US PCE peaked at 7%, UK CPI peaked at 11.1% in October 2022, SA CPI peaked at 7.8% in July 2022) and the major central banks responded with the steepest hiking cycle in 40 years.

Central-bank independence

Most modern central banks have legal independence from the executive branch on monetary-policy decisions, while remaining accountable to parliament (the Bank of England Act 1998 set the BoE's MPC as operationally independent; the SARB has constitutional protection for its primary mandate). The empirical case for independence is well established: independent central banks deliver lower and more stable inflation, all else equal.

The 2020s pressure-tested this norm. Politicians in the US, UK and other countries publicly criticised tight monetary policy during the 2022 to 2024 cycle; central banks held position. The South African MPC has periodically faced political criticism over the inflation target itself (calls for a lower target or for nominal-GDP targeting); the formal mandate has not changed.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Lender-of-last-resort versus solvency support

Bagehot's rule said lend only to solvent but illiquid institutions. The 2008 and 2023 episodes showed that the distinction between liquidity and solvency is hard to make in real time. SVB was technically solvent (its bond portfolio would have returned par if held to maturity) but illiquid (depositors demanded their money before the bonds matured). The Fed's response (lending against par-valued collateral through the Bank Term Funding Programme) collapsed the distinction. Whether this is a wise extension or a precedent for moral hazard is unresolved. Expect the question on the F107.

Monetary and macroprudential policy can pull in different directions

In a typical cycle, both lean against the wind: tight money + a high CCyB in the boom, loose money + a released CCyB in the bust. In the 2022 to 2024 cycle, the picture was more complex. The 2022 UK mini-budget bond crisis required the BoE to intervene with bond purchases (a quasi-QE operation) to stabilise the gilt market at the same time it was tightening rates. Macroprudential and monetary policy were temporarily on opposite sides of the trade. The lesson: the two functions must coexist within the same institution but can pull in opposite directions.

Central-bank digital currency (CBDC) is coming, slowly

Every major central bank is exploring a retail and / or wholesale CBDC. The Bank of England's "Digital Pound" consultation in 2023 to 2024, the ECB's "Digital Euro" preparation in 2023 to 2026, the SARB's Project Khokha (wholesale CBDC, 2018 onward), and the Fed's research programme all aim at the same problem: how should central-bank money look in a digitalising payments world? Implications for banks include reserve dynamics, deposit-displacement risk, and competitive positioning. Expect this to migrate from "future topic" to "live regulatory question" within the F107 examination window.

The 2008 to 2024 expansion of the central-bank toolkit

The functions listed in section 4 are the textbook six. The reality is that the central-bank balance sheet has become a primary policy instrument in itself. The Fed balance sheet grew from USD 900 billion in 2008 to USD 9 trillion in 2022; the BoE's from GBP 100 billion to GBP 1 trillion; the ECB's from EUR 1 trillion to EUR 8 trillion. Each round of QE, each liquidity facility, each emergency programme leaves a residue on the balance sheet that has to be unwound (or held permanently). Whether central-bank balance sheets can fully normalise in the 2020s is one of the biggest open questions in macroeconomics.

South African specifics

Five points worth memorising for F107.

First, SARB's mandate is in section 224 of the Constitution: "to protect the value of the currency in the interest of balanced and sustainable economic growth". This phrasing is broader than the strict inflation-targeting language used by the BoE or Fed, and the political debate about widening the mandate to include employment or nominal GDP comes back periodically.

Second, the inflation target is 3% to 6% (set jointly by SARB and National Treasury), with a 4.5% midpoint. The midpoint was added in 2017 as an anchor for expectations.

Third, SARB's organisational structure has the PA as a juristic subsidiary, the MPC and the Financial Stability Committee as committees, and several departments (Financial Markets, Currency Management, Banking Services, Statistics, Economic Research and the IRD).

Fourth, the SARB has a private-shareholder structure (around 800 shareholders), unusual among central banks but constitutionally protected. Shareholders receive a capped dividend and have no influence on monetary or supervisory policy.

Fifth, the SARB acts as the registrar of banks under the Banks Act, the licensing and de-licensing authority operating through the PA.

Common F107 traps

Watch out Six traps. (1) Confusing the SARB (central bank) with the PA (prudential authority within SARB). The PA was created in 2018; the SARB has existed since 1921. (2) Calling Bagehot's principle "lend freely against any collateral at any rate"; the actual rule is high-rate, good-collateral, solvent institutions. (3) Treating QE as inflationary by direct money-supply expansion; the empirical link from 2009 to 2021 QE to consumer-price inflation is much weaker than the 2010-era arguments predicted. (4) Forgetting that the central bank is the lender of last resort, not the lender; commercial banks lend to the real economy in normal times. (5) Confusing the central bank's reserve account with deposit insurance; the reserve account is the bank's working balance, not protection for depositors. (6) Assuming the Fed sets a single policy rate; the FOMC sets a target range, with the IORB and the ON RRP rates the actual operating tools.
