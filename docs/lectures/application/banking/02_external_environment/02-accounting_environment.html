The accounting environment

A bank's accounts are not a description of the bank, they are an argument about it. Accounting standards exist so that the argument is intelligible to people who do not work there, and audit and enforcement exist so that the argument is honest.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Two accounting frameworks dominate the world: IFRS (written by the IASB in London, used in 140+ jurisdictions including the UK, EU, South Africa, Australia, Japan, Canada, Brazil) and US GAAP (written by FASB in Norwalk, used in the US). Both demand that companies disclose enough material information that an outside reader can make an informed decision. Neither body has enforcement power; that is done by national securities regulators, stock exchanges, and audit oversight bodies.

For banks, three IFRS standards do most of the heavy lifting: IFRS 9 (financial instruments: classification, measurement, impairment, hedging), IFRS 7 (disclosures about financial instruments), and IFRS 13 (fair-value measurement). On top of these, regulators add Basel-derived disclosure regimes (Pillar 3), so a bank's full reporting stack is the AFS plus the Pillar 3 plus the supervisory returns. The AFS is for investors; Pillar 3 is for the market; the returns are for the supervisor.

Feynman test Suppose you are about to lend money to a stranger and they hand you a folder of their own financial records. You would want the records to follow some rules so you know what you are reading, you would want a credible third party to check them, and you would want a referee you can complain to if the stranger lies. The accounting environment is exactly that: shared rules (IFRS), independent auditors (the Big Four), and national enforcement bodies (FRC, JSE, SEC). For banks, the same machinery runs at industrial scale.
Step 2

Why this matters

An actuary inside a bank lives at the seam between regulatory capital and accounting numbers. IFRS 9's expected credit loss model uses the same PD, LGD and EAD inputs as the A-IRB capital model, but with different conditioning (lifetime versus 12-month, point-in-time versus through-the-cycle, forward-looking versus regulatory-stress). The reconciliation between the two is one of the most asked-about parts of an annual report, and one of the most error-prone parts of the model estate.

Beyond IFRS 9, every product launched by the bank gets accounted for somehow. Derivatives in IFRS 9; insurance contracts in IFRS 17; revenue (fees) in IFRS 15; leases in IFRS 16; financial-instrument disclosures in IFRS 7; fair-value approach in IFRS 13. The accounting choices are not merely cosmetic. They determine when income hits the P&L, how volatility shows up in equity (through OCI), and how earnings respond to interest-rate and credit cycles. Two banks running identical economics but different IFRS 9 staging policies will report very different ECL.

For the F107 syllabus, expect questions on the IFRS / FASB split, the role of the IASB, IFRS 9's three-stage ECL model, and how the AFS supports the four aims of regulation from Lecture 1. Pillar 3 disclosure is a known F107 gap and is touched here.

Step 3

The mental model

A bank's external reporting sits on three legs: the accounting standards (what to measure and how), the auditors (independent assurance), and the enforcement bodies (consequences for breach). Reading from the top down, the IASB / FASB write the rules; the Big Four (or local equivalents) audit the application; the FRC / JSE / FSCA / SEC enforce when something looks wrong.

Step 4

The detail

The IASB and the IFRS Foundation

The International Accounting Standards Board (IASB) is the standard-setting body of the IFRS Foundation, an independent private-sector not-for-profit registered in Delaware and operating from London. The Foundation is overseen by Trustees (geographically diverse, drawn from regulators, accounting firms and academia). The Board itself has 14 members, supported by a much larger technical staff.

The IASB's product is IFRS Accounting Standards. As of 2025 there are 17 active IFRSs (IFRS 1 to IFRS 18, with various older IASs and IFRSs co-existing), plus a Conceptual Framework and an extensive interpretations programme through the IFRS Interpretations Committee (IFRIC). The standards apply to most listed companies in the jurisdictions that have adopted IFRS, with some adoption nuance for unlisted entities (the IFRS for SMEs is a simpler parallel standard).

The IASB has no enforcement power. It cannot fine a non-compliant company, withdraw a listing, or sanction an auditor. Adoption of IFRS in any jurisdiction is a national-government decision; enforcement is a national-regulator and stock-exchange function. The IASB's leverage is technical credibility, plus the fact that capital markets that adopt IFRS attract more cross-border investment because investors can compare the books.

A short list of the IFRSs that matter for a bank

IFRS 9 — Financial instruments
Classification and measurement of financial assets (amortised cost, FVOCI, FVTPL) based on the business model and the SPPI test. Expected-credit-loss impairment (three-stage; 12-month ECL in Stage 1, lifetime ECL in Stages 2 and 3). Hedge accounting. Effective from 2018, replacing IAS 39.
IFRS 7 — Financial instruments: disclosures
Disclosure regime for everything IFRS 9 classifies. Covers credit risk, liquidity risk, market risk, fair-value hierarchy, and hedge accounting. For a bank, this is the bulk of the AFS notes.
IFRS 13 — Fair value measurement
Defines fair value as "the price that would be received to sell an asset or paid to transfer a liability in an orderly transaction between market participants at the measurement date" (an exit price). Sets the Level 1 / Level 2 / Level 3 hierarchy on observable versus unobservable inputs. For trading books, this is the operational backbone of mark-to-market.
IFRS 15 — Revenue from contracts with customers
The five-step revenue recognition model: identify contract, identify performance obligations, determine transaction price, allocate to obligations, recognise revenue when (or as) each obligation is satisfied. For a bank, this is mostly fee income; interest income is in IFRS 9.
IFRS 16 — Leases
Effective 2019. Brings most lessee leases onto the balance sheet as a right-of-use asset and a lease liability. Removed the on-balance / off-balance distinction that IAS 17 allowed. Matters for banks with large branch and office footprints.
IFRS 17 — Insurance contracts
Effective 2023, replacing IFRS 4. Standardises measurement of insurance contracts (GMM / VFA / PAA models). Matters for bancassurance and for the insurance subsidiaries of a bank group; not part of the bank's banking-book accounting.
IAS 1 / IAS 7 / IAS 8 / IAS 12
The general-purpose standards. Presentation of financial statements (IAS 1, being replaced by IFRS 18 from 2027); cash flow statement (IAS 7); accounting policies, changes and errors (IAS 8); income taxes (IAS 12). The structural skeleton of every AFS.
IFRS S1 and IFRS S2 — Sustainability and climate disclosures
Published in June 2023 by the ISSB (the sustainability sister-body of the IASB). Adoption by the JSE, the FRC and the UK Sustainability Disclosure Standards is being phased in. Within five years these are likely to be as load-bearing as IFRS 9 for bank disclosures.

The FASB and US GAAP

The Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB) is the US equivalent of the IASB. Established 1973, based in Norwalk, Connecticut. Like the IASB it is a private-sector body, but its standards are recognised by the SEC as authoritative for all SEC-registered companies. Funding comes from accounting-firm support fees and from a publicly-funded levy under the Sarbanes-Oxley Act 2002.

The most notable IFRS-versus-US-GAAP divergence for banks is impairment. IFRS 9's expected credit loss model has a 12-month-versus-lifetime two-stage trigger (Stages 1 and 2). The US FASB equivalent, ASC 326 ("Current Expected Credit Loss" or CECL), requires lifetime ECL from origination for everything in scope. CECL is more capital-conservative in upward-sloping cycles and arguably more comparable across portfolios. The IASB chose two stages to limit the day-one provisioning hit on long-dated loans; the FASB chose one stage to remove the staging-judgement battles. Cross-listed banks (HSBC, Barclays, JPMorgan) deal with both; pure-domestic banks deal with one.

Audit and the Big Four

External audit is the independent assurance step that makes IFRS work. Without audit, the company's IFRS-compliance assertion is uncheckable. The global market is dominated by four firms — PwC, Deloitte, EY, KPMG — which audit nearly every large bank in the UK, EU, South Africa and the US. Local firms (in SA: SizweNtsalubaGobodo, BDO, Mazars, Nexia SAB&T) take a meaningful share of mid-tier mandates.

Auditors apply International Standards on Auditing (ISAs), set by the IAASB. The auditor's opinion on the AFS is the small but load-bearing block that lets a reader rely on the numbers. There are four types of opinion: unqualified ("clean"), qualified (specific exception), adverse (the statements are materially misstated), and a disclaimer of opinion (the auditor cannot form a view). Adverse opinions and disclaimers are rare; qualified opinions are infrequent; almost all bank AFSs carry unqualified opinions, which is part of why the exceptional cases are interesting.

Audit rotation rules (in the UK, the EU and South Africa) require audit firms to rotate at least every 10 years and audit partners more frequently. Non-audit-services restrictions limit how much consulting the audit firm can sell to its audit client. Both rules tightened after the Carillion (UK, 2018), Steinhoff (SA, 2017) and Wirecard (DE, 2020) failures.

Enforcement bodies

South Africa

Enforcement of IFRS in South Africa is shared:

FSCA (Financial Sector Conduct Authority)
Market-conduct regulator. Oversees market abuse and the implementation of the Financial Markets Act. Can investigate insider trading, market manipulation, and fraudulent reporting in listed-company contexts.
JSE (Johannesburg Stock Exchange) — Issuer Regulation Division
Front-line listing regulator. Applies the JSE Listings Requirements, which mandate IFRS for listed companies. Investigates non-compliance, can censure or fine issuers, and can suspend listing in extreme cases.
IRBA (Independent Regulatory Board for Auditors)
Oversees and registers external auditors in South Africa, including the partners who sign bank audits. Investigates audit failures (the IRBA was central to the post-Steinhoff investigations) and can deregister auditors and partners.
SAICA (South African Institute of Chartered Accountants)
The professional body for CAs(SA). Sets professional conduct rules, runs examinations, and disciplines members who breach the IFAC Code of Ethics. Not a statutory regulator but materially shapes the auditor population that IRBA oversees.

United Kingdom

FRC (Financial Reporting Council)
UK regulator for corporate governance, financial reporting, audit and actuarial work. Conducts thematic reviews of bank AFSs (annual climate-disclosure reviews; periodic IFRS 9 reviews). Enforces against directors and auditors. Successor body, ARGA, has been promised since 2018 but not yet legislated.
FCA Disclosure Guidance and Transparency Rules (DTRs)
Used to ensure listed UK companies meet their reporting obligations. The FCA acts where information is misleading or late, drawing on the Listing Rules and FSMA.

European Union

ESMA (European Securities and Markets Authority)
Co-ordinates enforcement of IFRS across the EU. Publishes annual enforcement priorities to which auditors and preparers attend. Local enforcement is done by national securities regulators (AFM in the Netherlands, AMF in France, BaFin in Germany).

United States

SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission)
Statutory regulator of US-listed companies. The SEC's Division of Corporation Finance reviews filings and may require restatements. SEC enforcement actions carry civil penalties and possible criminal referrals to the DoJ.
PCAOB (Public Company Accounting Oversight Board)
Created by Sarbanes-Oxley 2002. Inspects audit firms that audit US public companies, including the Big Four. Issues firm-level inspection reports that are public and influence audit-firm reputation.

Disclosure, materiality and the AFS

The full disclosure principle: companies must disclose all material information that could influence the economic decisions of users of the financial statements. The IFRS conceptual framework defines materiality from the user's perspective: an item is material if its omission or misstatement could influence decisions. This is a low bar in practice; banks disclose extensive notes covering credit risk, liquidity risk, market risk, the capital position, and significant judgement areas.

The structure of a bank's annual financial statements is broadly:

  1. Chairman's and CEO's reports. Narrative; not part of the audited statements.
  2. Audit report. Independent opinion plus key audit matters (KAMs).
  3. Primary statements. Statement of profit or loss; statement of other comprehensive income; statement of financial position; statement of changes in equity; statement of cash flows.
  4. Notes. The substantive content. For a bank, around 70 to 120 individual notes, often making up the majority of the report's page count. Includes IFRS 9 staging tables, ECL movements, credit-risk concentration tables, fair-value hierarchy, hedge effectiveness, regulatory-capital reconciliation, and segmental information.
  5. Pillar 3 disclosures. Often a separate document, published alongside or shortly after the AFS, covering Basel-mandated capital, leverage, liquidity and risk-weighted-asset disclosures.

Disclosures as a regulatory tool

Disclosures support all four aims of regulation:

AimHow disclosure helps
Financial stabilityMarket discipline: investors and counterparties price risk into the bank's bond yields and CDS spreads, encouraging prudent management. This is the explicit rationale for Pillar 3.
Consumer protectionCustomers (and intermediaries) can compare products and providers when key terms and risks are disclosed.
Financial-crime preventionDisclosure of beneficial ownership and of related-party transactions exposes structures used for laundering and corruption.
Fair competitionComparable financial information lets investors allocate capital between competitors and lets new entrants benchmark themselves.

Pillar 3 disclosure: the bridge between accounting and prudential

Pillar 3 of the Basel framework requires banks to publish standardised regulatory disclosures so that the market can assess capital adequacy and risk profile independently of the regulator. The disclosures cover capital structure, risk-weighted assets (broken down by exposure type and approach), credit-risk exposures by PD band, securitisation positions, market-risk and operational-risk capital, leverage ratio, LCR and NSFR.

Pillar 3 is templated. Banks fill in standardised tables (the EU's EBA templates, the PRA's PS17/23 templates, the SA Prudential Authority's BA returns reformulated as Pillar 3). Standardisation makes the disclosures comparable across banks in a way that IFRS notes are not. The two regimes complement: IFRS measures economic value and recognised income; Pillar 3 measures regulatory capital and exposure.

Pillar 3 is a flagged F107 gap; a dedicated lecture is planned in Phase 2.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The two-stage IFRS 9 model has cyclical bite

When a recession hits, banks must increase ECL because loans migrate from Stage 1 (12-month ECL) to Stage 2 (lifetime ECL). The provisioning is back-loaded relative to the economic cycle, by design. The 2020 Covid stress was the first real test: provisions spiked in Q1 and Q2 2020 and unwound through 2021 as the recovery exceeded forecasts. The result was a wave of "model-overlay" debates as banks tried to capture economic conditions that the historical-data-trained models could not see. Expect IFRS 9 staging and overlays to remain a contentious area for years.

FAS 326 / CECL is one stage, lifetime ECL — and the procyclicality is the opposite

Day-one provisioning for a 30-year mortgage in CECL is much heavier than in IFRS 9. US banks lobbied hard against CECL during the 2017 to 2019 implementation; some still argue the rule discourages long-dated lending. Empirically, the US 2020 Q1 provision build was even larger than in IFRS jurisdictions, partly because of CECL but mostly because of the heavier macroeconomic shock.

Auditors are not regulators

An auditor's role is to opine on whether the financial statements give a true and fair view. The auditor does not police IFRS interpretation, does not enforce capital rules, and is not normally a first line of defence against fraud. Auditors do have a duty to evaluate whether the entity is a going concern; the post-Carillion debate on whether they take this seriously enough is unresolved. The FRC's "audit firm governance" reforms continue.

South African specifics for F107

Three points. First, the JSE Listings Requirements have always required IFRS; SA was an early adopter of IFRS, with full transition in 2005. Second, IRBA's investigations into the audit failures around Steinhoff, KPMG-VBS and African Bank shaped the post-2017 regulatory environment; expect questions on what the failures revealed about audit independence and on the IRBA's response. Third, the FSCA's enforcement role is recent (post-2018); the JSE was the dominant enforcer prior to that.

Common F107 traps

Watch out Five traps. (1) Calling IFRS "IAS"; IAS standards were the predecessors, but IFRSs supersede them from 2003 onward. (2) Saying the IASB enforces IFRS; it does not. Enforcement is national. (3) Forgetting that US GAAP is different from IFRS; CECL versus the two-stage ECL model is the canonical example. (4) Treating Pillar 3 as part of the AFS; it is a separate Basel-mandated disclosure that often references the AFS but stands alone. (5) Confusing IRBA (SA audit regulator) with IRB (the Basel internal-ratings-based approach for credit-risk capital). The names are unfortunate but they refer to entirely different things.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. What is the IASB, and how does it differ from the BIS?
  2. State the four aims of regulation and explain how IFRS disclosure supports each of them.
  3. Name the three IFRSs most central to bank accounting and summarise what each one does.
  4. Compare IFRS 9's two-stage ECL model with the US FASB's CECL model. Which is more conservative at loan origination, and why?
  5. Identify the audit firm, the listing regulator, and the audit oversight body for a JSE-listed South African bank.
  6. What is materiality and why is it foundational to disclosure?
  7. Distinguish Pillar 3 disclosures from IFRS-mandated AFS notes. Why do regulators want both?
  8. What is the difference between Level 1, Level 2 and Level 3 fair values under IFRS 13?
  9. Why did the FRC, the IRBA and the PCAOB get more powers after the audit failures of the late 2010s? Give one example failure for each.
