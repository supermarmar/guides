The economic environment

Bank credit and the real economy run in feedback. In good times the loop amplifies growth; in bad times it amplifies recessions. Understanding why is the difference between treating the cycle as background noise and treating it as the dominant variable.

Lecture 3 of Phase 1, external environment Reading time, 25 minutes Source: docs/wiki/application/banking/02_external_environment/03-economic_envrionment.md
Step 1

The 30-second version

Bank lending and economic growth feed each other. In expansion, employment is high, incomes are rising, and asset values are climbing, so borrowers repay easily and banks lend more freely. Cheap credit fuels more investment and consumption, which fuels more growth. In contraction, the loop runs in reverse: unemployment rises, repayment falters, credit losses climb, and banks tighten lending, which deepens the slowdown.

Bank capital and liquidity buffers exist precisely to dampen the second half of that loop. The countercyclical capital buffer (CCyB), the through-the-cycle PD, and the conservatism in IFRS 9 forward-looking information are all attempts to make banks accumulate firepower in the boom so they can keep lending in the bust. The lesson of 2008 was that this had not happened sufficiently; the lesson of 2020 to 2021 was that the post-2008 reforms had moved things substantially in the right direction.

Feynman test Imagine you owned a small lending business. When your customers' jobs are secure and house prices are rising, you would say yes to more loans, charge less interest, and grow the book. When jobs are disappearing and house prices falling, you would tighten up: smaller loans, higher rates, fewer borrowers. Now multiply your decisions across every bank in a country at the same time, and you can see why credit and growth become coupled. The whole F107 financial-stability framework is about stopping that coupling from spinning out of control.
Step 2

Why this matters

Every credit-risk model an actuary builds is conditional on the economic environment somehow. PIT PDs respond directly to the cycle; TTC PDs are meant not to; IFRS 9 forward-looking information (FLI) explicitly conditions ECL on macroeconomic scenarios; stress-testing simulates downturn paths to estimate capital adequacy. The choice of macro model, the weighting of scenarios, and the lookback period for empirical calibration all hinge on understanding how lending and the economy interact.

For the F107 syllabus, the economic environment is the macro context against which every risk type is measured. Expect questions on the feedback loop, the historical pattern of asset bubbles and busts, and the role of capital buffers (especially the CCyB) in mitigating procyclicality. The 2007 to 2008 crisis remains the canonical reference and the 2020 to 2021 Covid period the second reference. South Africa's 2008 to 2010 cycle, 2015 to 2017 commodity slowdown, and 2020 Covid stress are local touchstones.

The mental model

Two loops, one positive and one negative, running through the same banking system.

Step 4

The detail

How banks enable the real economy

Strip away the regulatory architecture and four core economic functions remain:

  • Savings mobilisation: a safe place to store cash, with interest. This converts idle balances into investable capital.
  • Consumption smoothing: credit lets households buy houses, cars and education they cannot afford in cash, repaying over time.
  • Investment finance: working capital, equipment finance, project finance, trade finance for companies. Banks intermediate between the savers and the entrepreneurs.
  • Payments and settlement: the everyday plumbing through which transactions happen. Even where banks no longer originate the largest payments (cards networks, mobile money), they sit underneath as settlement agents.

These functions are unglamorous when they work and devastating when they break. The 2010 to 2012 EU sovereign-debt crisis interrupted small-business lending across southern Europe and shaved years of growth off Greece and Portugal. The 2023 SVB failure froze California venture-backed payroll for a fortnight until the FDIC stepped in. Bank credit is the connective tissue.

The expansion loop in detail

Pick any starting point and the loop continues. Start with a recovery. Employment rises, wages rise, consumer confidence rises. Households can comfortably service existing debt and look at new borrowing (mortgages, car loans, credit cards). Defaults fall. Bank credit losses fall. The bank's reported ROE rises, its share price rises, and competitive pressure pushes pricing down and origination volumes up.

At the same time, asset prices rise. House prices, equity prices, commercial-property values all benefit from cheap credit. Rising collateral values let banks lend more against the same property; the LTV constraint is relative not absolute. New borrowers enter (first-time buyers); old borrowers re-leverage (cash-out refinancing). Households' debt-to-income ratios drift upward, often without alarm because asset values are rising too.

Net interest margin (NIM) tends to compress during late expansion as competition pushes deposit rates up and loan rates down. Banks chase volume to compensate. Origination standards loosen at the margin: higher LTVs, longer maturities, more interest-only structures, weaker covenants on corporate debt (the "cov-lite" trend in US leveraged finance since 2014). The IFRS 9 ECL response is muted because the stage migration is dominated by Stage 1 12-month losses on a benign macro backdrop. Through-the-cycle PDs barely move; point-in-time PDs fall.

The contraction loop in detail

The loop reverses, usually in response to an external shock or a sharp asset-price correction. Unemployment rises, incomes fall, asset prices begin to drop. Households and corporates that took on borrowing late in the cycle, at high LTVs or low fixed rates that have now reset, struggle to service it. Defaults rise unevenly, beginning with the marginal borrowers.

Banks respond to higher credit losses by tightening: smaller loans, higher spreads, more conservative LTV requirements, more documentation. The ECL response is sharp because IFRS 9 staging migrates large parts of the book from Stage 1 (12-month ECL) to Stage 2 (lifetime ECL) on the SICR trigger. Bank capital is consumed by both reported losses and risk-weight inflation (PIT migration of the IRB curve). Banks pull back on lending to defend the capital ratio.

The macro effect: less credit, less investment, slower demand, more unemployment, more defaults. The loop self-reinforces unless an external actor (the central bank cutting rates, the fiscal authority running a deficit, the regulator reducing the CCyB) intervenes. The 2020 Covid response was a textbook intervention: rates cut to the lower bound, large fiscal transfers, payment-holiday rules, IFRS 9 staging guidance ("do not migrate purely because of Covid-related forbearance"), and CCyB cut from 1% to 0% in the UK, EU and SA.

The pattern is fairly stable: (1) a positive economic story justifies higher asset valuations; (2) credit availability rises and standards loosen, accelerating the rise; (3) a marginal shock (often a rate rise, a corporate scandal, a geopolitical event) triggers selling; (4) selling cascades because leverage means small price moves wipe out collateral; (5) banks pull back on credit to protect capital; (6) the real economy follows the asset prices down. Hyman Minsky's "financial instability hypothesis" formalises this; the historian Charles Kindleberger's Manias, Panics and Crashes is the canonical narrative.

The 2007 to 2008 crisis in one paragraph

US house prices nearly doubled in the ten years to 2006, supported by aggressive originate-to-distribute mortgage lending. Mortgages were securitised into asset-backed securities and re-securitised into collateralised debt obligations, often rated AAA by the rating agencies on the strength of internal correlation assumptions that turned out to be wrong. The CDOs were sold to banks worldwide and held in trading books, off-balance-sheet conduits or money-market funds. When US house prices began to fall in mid-2006, the AAA tranches lost value disproportionately because the underlying mortgages were correlated rather than diversified. Bear Stearns failed in March 2008; Lehman Brothers in September 2008; AIG was rescued; banks across the Atlantic suffered solvency and liquidity events simultaneously. The policy response was unprecedented: TARP in the US, mass bank recapitalisations in the UK, ECB liquidity operations, and the entire Basel III reform agenda.

The Covid crisis in one paragraph

Q1 2020 brought a synchronous global shutdown. Banks went into the crisis with substantially more capital and liquidity than in 2008, plus a regulatory toolkit (CCyB releases, payment holidays, IFRS 9 staging guidance) explicitly designed to keep credit flowing. Fiscal policy did most of the work (CARES Act, EU SURE programme, SA TERS scheme, Bank of England Covid Corporate Financing Facility). Banks lent rather than tightened, in part because they were directed to. ECL provisions spiked in Q1 and Q2 2020 but unwound through 2021 as defaults stayed below forecasts. The CCyB returned to non-zero values in the UK from December 2022. The episode is the strongest empirical evidence so far that post-2008 reforms have moved bank behaviour in the right direction.

South Africa specifics

The South African cycle has its own features.

  • 2003 to 2007 boom: house-price doubling, retail-credit expansion, NCA enacted in 2005 partly in response to over-indebtedness concerns.
  • 2008 to 2010 mini-recession: limited bank failures (one African Bank precursor stress), but consumer-credit losses spiked. African Bank Investments Limited (ABIL) collapsed in 2014, leading to the curatorship and the creation of the "good bank" / "bad bank" structure.
  • 2015 to 2017 commodity-driven slowdown: Chinese demand fall, mining sector losses, sovereign-rating downgrades (Moody's downgraded SA to non-investment grade in 2017).
  • 2020 Covid stress: SARB cut the repo rate by 300 bps in three months; payment-relief schemes; banks released around ZAR 30 billion of capital under regulatory relief. The SA banks performed credibly through Covid.
  • 2022 to 2024 rate cycle: SARB hiked the repo from 3.5% to 8.25% to control inflation, with consumer credit visibly stressed but no systemic bank distress. Cuts began in late 2024.

The cycle and the model

Three modelling implications.

First, the choice between point-in-time (PIT) and through-the-cycle (TTC) PD changes everything. PIT PDs move with the cycle and produce more accurate point-in-time ECL but more procyclical capital. TTC PDs are stable across the cycle and produce more stable capital but worse pricing signals. IRB capital uses a hybrid: TTC for stability, with cycle dimensions in the LGD-downturn add-on and the asset-correlation curve. IFRS 9 uses PIT with FLI overlays.

Second, downturn LGD is required by the IRB rules because empirical LGDs are lower in benign periods than in stress. The LGD-downturn adjustment is the place where the cycle is explicitly priced in.

Third, forward-looking information in IFRS 9 conditions ECL on macroeconomic scenarios. Most banks use three to five weighted scenarios (a base case, an upside, one or two downsides). The scenario weights themselves become a judgement variable; the 2020 and 2022 ECL prints both saw extensive scenario-weight overlays.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Minsky moments are still useful, even if the framework is incomplete

Hyman Minsky's distinction between hedge, speculative and Ponzi finance, and the gradual migration up the risk ladder during expansions, is one of the most predictive frameworks in macro-finance. It does not tell you when the moment comes, but it tells you what to look for: rising debt-to-income ratios, falling debt-service-to-income ratios as rates fall, increasing reliance on asset-price growth to service debt, deteriorating origination standards. Most post-2008 macroprudential dashboards (the ECB's risk dashboard, the BoE's FPC indicators) are descendants of Minsky's signals.

The countercyclical capital buffer is the policy instrument the cycle deserves

The CCyB is set by the macroprudential authority (FPC in the UK, ECB and national designated authorities in the EU, SARB in SA) within bounds specified by Basel III. It is supposed to rise in expansions, building bank capital while credit losses are low, and fall in contractions, freeing capital to absorb losses and keep lending. The UK has used it actively (1% in 2020, cut to 0%, rising to 2% by 2023). The EU has been more cautious. South Africa held the CCyB at 0% through 2023 and only began signalling a positive setting in 2024. Whether the CCyB is large enough to make a difference is debated; whether it should be larger, or augmented with sectoral buffers (already in CRR3 / Basel III final), is the active policy frontier.

Procyclicality of accounting rules

IFRS 9 staging is cyclical by design: Stage 2 lifetime ECL is heavier than Stage 1 12-month ECL, so a downturn that triggers SICR migration mechanically increases provisions. This is what the IASB intended; provisions should rise when expected losses rise. But it sharpens the procyclicality of bank profits and capital. The IASB and the BCBS have published joint guidance to soften this (the 2017 transition arrangements, the 2020 Covid guidance) without changing the underlying standard. The tension between accounting accuracy and capital-stability is unresolved.

Bank lending and inflation are not the same thing

The 2022 to 2024 inflation surge in the US, UK and EU was a real-economy episode driven by Covid supply chains, fiscal stimulus and energy shocks, not a credit boom. Bank lending grew modestly. Yet central banks responded with the steepest hiking cycle in 40 years. The lesson is that the cycle is multi-dimensional: a credit boom and a goods-inflation episode look different, and macroprudential policy is not a substitute for monetary policy. Both must be live at once.

Common F107 traps

Watch out Five traps. (1) Equating "the economic environment" with "GDP growth". The relevant variables are unemployment, asset prices, real wages, interest rates, exchange rates and credit spreads. (2) Treating the Basel CCyB as a Pillar 1 buffer; it is a macroprudential add-on, not part of the Pillar 1 minimum. (3) Saying IFRS 9 is "procyclical" without explaining why: it is procyclical because of staging, not because of the principle of forward-looking ECL. (4) Conflating the 2008 GFC with the 2010 to 2012 euro crisis; the causes were different (US household leverage versus European sovereign-bank doom loop). (5) Forgetting the South African specifics: ABIL 2014, SARB rate cycle, NCA, and the post-Covid response with payment-relief schemes.
