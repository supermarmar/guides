Ratings agencies

A small number of private firms hand out letter grades that determine the cost of capital for half the world. The grades are opinions, not facts; the agencies are paid by the entities they rate; and yet the system has remained central to credit markets for over a century. Understanding the role, the conflicts and the calibration is foundational.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A credit rating is a forward-looking opinion about an entity's ability and willingness to repay debt. The three dominant global agencies (S&P, Moody's, Fitch) issue ratings on letter scales (AAA down to D or C, with notches). The ratings are used by investors to price debt, by regulators in the Basel standardised approach for capital, by central banks in collateral frameworks, and by issuers themselves in pricing and in covenants.

The system has three structural features that you must understand. First, the issuer-pays model: the rated entity pays the agency, creating a conflict of interest. Second, the through-the-cycle convention: agencies smooth ratings through the economic cycle, so a rating change signals a structural rather than cyclical view. Third, the regulatory embedding: many regulations explicitly require minimum ratings for certain investments, which gives the agencies quasi-regulatory power. Each of these features was implicated in the 2008 crisis and is now regulated, but none has been fundamentally changed.

Feynman test Imagine a teacher hand-selected by every student to grade their own homework, with grades that determine whether the student can borrow a textbook from the library. That is the rating-agency model, applied to bond markets. The fact that it works at all is a tribute to reputation: agencies that grade too easily lose subscriber trust, see their ratings ignored, and lose their NRSRO status. The fact that it does not always work is the story of 2008.
Step 2

Why this matters

Three reasons ratings sit at the centre of bank work.

First, the Basel standardised approach for credit risk uses external ratings to determine risk weights on bank exposures to sovereigns, banks, corporates and securitisations. A AAA corporate gets a 20% risk weight; a BB corporate gets 100%; an unrated SME gets 100% under the standardised approach (or 75% under the retail bucket). The mapping is set by the Basel framework and adopted in CRR / SS3 / Banks Act regulations.

Second, banks themselves are rated, and the rating drives the cost of wholesale funding. A one-notch downgrade for a G-SIB can lift senior-unsecured funding costs by 10 to 30 basis points and force collateral posting under derivative documentation (ISDA CSAs). A multi-notch downgrade can cut off interbank market access entirely. Most bank Treasuries run a dedicated "ratings strategy" function precisely because the agency view materially shapes the cost of doing business.

Third, the agencies' methodologies are themselves credit-risk-modelling artefacts. The internal CRM team at a bank typically benchmarks its PD models against agency rating implied PDs; the IFRS 9 staging triggers often include a "external rating downgrade" criterion; and the IRB master scale is calibrated to map onto agency-scale equivalents for transparency.

For F107, expect questions on the role of ratings in Basel, on the conflicts of interest, on the 2008 critique, and on the difference between issuer ratings and instrument ratings.

Step 3

The mental model

An agency takes information about an issuer (financial statements, management interviews, sector data) and produces a letter grade. Investors and regulators take the grade as an input to their own decisions. The agency earns a fee from the issuer, faces reputational risk from investors, and operates under a regulatory perimeter that authorises its ratings to be used in capital and collateral calculations.

Step 4

The detail

The Big Three

S&P Global Ratings
Founded 1860 by Henry Varnum Poor; merged with Standard Statistics in 1941 to form Standard & Poor's. Now a division of S&P Global Inc. (NYSE: SPGI). Roughly 40% global market share by rating count. Letter scale: AAA, AA, A, BBB, BB, B, CCC, CC, C, D, with +/- modifiers.
Moody's Investors Service
Founded 1909 by John Moody. Now a division of Moody's Corporation (NYSE: MCO). Roughly 35% global market share. Letter scale: Aaa, Aa, A, Baa, Ba, B, Caa, Ca, C, with numeric 1/2/3 modifiers (Aa1 to Aa3, etc.).
Fitch Ratings
Founded 1914 by John Knowles Fitch. Now a subsidiary of Hearst Corporation. Roughly 15% global market share. Letter scale: AAA, AA, A, BBB, BB, B, CCC, CC, C, RD, D, with +/- modifiers.

The three are dominant in long-term issuer and instrument ratings; the rest of the global market is fragmented between regional agencies (Japan's R&I and JCR; Canada's DBRS Morningstar; Germany's Scope Ratings; China's CCXI and Dagong) and emerging-market sovereign rating specialists.

In South Africa, the rated sovereign is followed by all three of the Big Three. Bank ratings within SA are dominated by S&P and Moody's; Fitch has a smaller presence. The local affiliate GCR Ratings (Global Credit Ratings, Johannesburg) and its parent Moody's (since 2022 acquisition) cover sub-investment-grade African corporates and sub-sovereign issuers.

The "investment grade / sub-investment grade" boundary sits between BBB- and BB+ on the S&P / Fitch scale, and between Baa3 and Ba1 on Moody's. The boundary is regulatorily significant: many pension funds, insurance companies and money-market funds have mandates restricting them to investment grade. A downgrade across this boundary ("fallen angel") can trigger forced selling.

Indicative one-year PDs above are illustrative, not authoritative. The agencies publish their own historical default and transition matrices; the realised one-year PDs for AAA have been close to zero in the past 40 years, while CCC-rated issuers have realised one-year default rates above 25% in some vintages.

Issuer ratings versus instrument ratings

The distinction matters operationally.

  • An issuer rating (or counterparty / IDR) measures the credit risk of the entity itself. A single rating applies to all senior-unsecured obligations.
  • An instrument rating applies to a specific debt instrument and reflects the issuer rating adjusted for the seniority and security of the instrument. Senior secured debt sits above the issuer rating; subordinated debt sits below; equity is unrated.

Structured-finance ratings (RMBS, CMBS, CLOs, ABS) are instrument-level by construction; the issuer is a special-purpose vehicle with no economic meaning of its own. The 2008 crisis was largely a failure of structured-finance ratings, not of corporate or sovereign ratings.

Rating actions

Three types of rating action that matter.

  • Upgrade or downgrade: a change in the rating itself. By convention, agencies move in one-notch steps unless the news is severe.
  • Outlook change (Positive / Stable / Negative / Developing): a 12 to 24 month signal about the likely direction of the rating.
  • Credit watch or rating watch (Positive / Negative / Developing / Evolving): a stronger signal of likely action over 90 days, typically driven by a specific event (an acquisition, a sovereign downgrade, a regulatory ruling).

Investors generally read the outlook and watch signals as much as the headline rating. A "stable BBB" and a "BBB on negative watch" trade at very different spreads.

Through-the-cycle versus point-in-time

The Big Three publicly target through-the-cycle (TTC) ratings, meaning the rating should reflect the long-term creditworthiness across an economic cycle, not the current point-in-time conditions. The methodology paragraphs are explicit: stresses are run, conservative assumptions are taken, and ratings are intended to be stable. In practice ratings do migrate during cycles, but more slowly than point-in-time PDs would suggest.

The TTC convention has consequences for bank modelling. A bank's IRB PD model is usually a hybrid PIT / TTC: more PIT than the agencies, because the bank wants to manage origination decisions in real time, but more TTC than naive empirical PDs, to limit cyclicality in regulatory capital. Mapping an internal PD to an external rating requires reconciling these conventions: a BBB issuer on a TTC basis might map to a higher internal PIT PD during a downturn.

The 2008 critique

The Big Three rated trillions of dollars of US RMBS and CDOs at AAA. Many of those instruments collapsed in 2007 to 2009; some to single B or worse, some to default. Investigations by the SEC, the FCIC and various private litigants identified several failures.

  • Issuer-pays conflict. The originators paid for the ratings and shopped between agencies for the highest grade ("ratings shopping"). Agencies that gave more conservative ratings lost business.
  • Methodological errors. Correlation assumptions for the underlying mortgage pools were materially understated. The models assumed a national US house-price decline was extremely unlikely, partly because the modelling period (mid-1990s onward) did not include any such episode.
  • Inadequate surveillance. Once a rating was assigned, ongoing monitoring was thin; downgrades came late.
  • Regulatory hard-wiring. Capital rules required AAA paper; investors needed AAA paper to meet mandates; that created insatiable demand for AAA-rated structured product, which the agencies supplied.

The post-2008 reforms attacked some of these features without dismantling the model.

  • Dodd-Frank (US, 2010): established the SEC's Office of Credit Ratings, removed many regulatory references to ratings ("hard-wiring removal"), and required NRSROs to disclose methodologies and conflicts of interest.
  • CRA Regulation (EU, 2010 then revised): ESMA became the single supervisor of CRAs operating in the EU. Mandatory rotation of rating analysts; disclosure of unsolicited ratings.
  • Basel III and IV: reduced reliance on external ratings in the standardised approach where banks can use internal ratings; introduced an external credit risk assessment ("ECRA") with floor.
  • FSB principles: agencies signed onto the FSB Principles for Reducing Reliance on Credit Rating Agencies (2010).

The structural conflict of interest (issuer-pays) has not been removed. Various alternatives (investor-pays, government-funded, auction-based assignment) were proposed and rejected as introducing different problems. The 2023 Credit Suisse failure is generally considered to have been not caused by rating failure; the 2023 SVB failure is partly a rating failure (Moody's downgraded only days before collapse).

The NRSRO designation and EU registration

In the US, the SEC designates "Nationally Recognized Statistical Rating Organizations" (NRSROs). Only NRSRO ratings can be used in certain regulatory contexts. There are currently nine NRSROs. The designation gives credibility but also imposes disclosure and conflicts-of-interest obligations.

In the EU, CRAs must be registered with ESMA to issue ratings for regulatory use. The European Securities and Markets Authority took over CRA supervision in 2011 and now publishes a public register of all authorised agencies, supervisory findings and rating-data dissemination via CEREP / RADAR.

In South Africa, the FSCA is the designated authority for CRA supervision under the Credit Rating Services Act (2012). The FSCA registers and supervises CRAs operating in SA; ratings issued by foreign agencies (S&P, Moody's, Fitch) are recognised but the issuers' SA affiliates fall within the FSCA perimeter.

Ratings in Basel and bank prudential regulation

Three places where ratings are embedded.

Standardised approach for credit risk: risk weights depend on the external rating of the counterparty. For corporates, the rating maps to weights of 20% (AAA to AA-), 50% (A+ to A-), 75% (BBB+ to BBB-, in CRR3 / Basel III final), 100% (BB+ to B-) and 150% (below B-). Sovereigns have a different scale, including a 0% risk weight for own-currency sovereign exposures in many jurisdictions. The Basel III final framework introduced a "due-diligence" requirement: banks may not rely solely on the rating but must perform their own credit due diligence and, if their internal view is worse than the rating, must use the worse view.

Securitisation framework: the External Ratings-Based Approach (ERBA) under SEC-ERBA assigns risk weights to securitisation exposures based on the external rating and the seniority of the tranche. Senior AAA tranches get 15%; mezzanine tranches step up sharply. The framework imposes a 1,250% risk weight ("dollar-for-dollar capital") on the lowest-rated tranches.

Central-bank collateral frameworks: the ECB, Bank of England, and SARB all accept rated securities as collateral for monetary operations, with haircuts that depend on the rating and tenor. A downgrade can disqualify a security from collateral eligibility or sharply raise its haircut.

Sovereign ratings

Sovereign ratings warrant their own paragraph. They drive the cost of government borrowing, the corporate "country ceiling" (most corporates cannot be rated above the sovereign), and the capital treatment of banks holding sovereign debt. South Africa's sovereign rating history is instructive: BBB+ in 2010, gradual decline through 2014, downgrade to BBB- in 2017 (Fitch) and Ba1 in 2017 (Moody's), further downgrades to non-investment grade across the Big Three by 2020. Each step shifted the rand, lifted SA Government bond yields, and rippled into the bank ratings (which are typically anchored to the sovereign).

"Country ceiling" matters in cross-border bank rating: a UK bank with a meaningful SA subsidiary is unlikely to see the subsidiary rated above the SA sovereign, regardless of standalone strength. Similarly, no SA corporate bond was rated investment grade by all three agencies after the 2020 SA downgrade.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Ratings are opinions, not measurements

The exam-relevant phrasing in the source note is correct: ratings are opinions and not substitutes for analysis. A bank with a direct borrower relationship often has better information than the agency; the agency relies on what the issuer chooses to share and what is publicly available. Banks routinely assign internal ratings that differ from the agency rating, sometimes higher (when the bank has a longer relationship and proprietary cash-flow information) and sometimes lower (when the bank suspects something the agency has not yet noticed).

The issuer-pays model is the original sin, but no alternative has stuck

The investor-pays model (used by some smaller agencies like Egan-Jones) has the cleaner incentives but a coverage problem: paying-investors do not collectively subsidise ratings for issuers they do not own, so coverage is thin. Government-funded ratings raise concerns about political pressure. Auction-based assignment of rating mandates (proposed by the SEC in 2011 but not adopted) introduces a different selection problem. The issuer-pays model has survived because alternatives have worse failure modes, not because it is good.

Through-the-cycle is a discipline, not a guarantee

The agencies say they rate through the cycle, but the empirical evidence is mixed. In sustained downturns, sovereign and corporate ratings do migrate. The 2017 SA downgrades followed a multi-year deterioration in fiscal indicators; they were not "moments". The 2020 to 2022 inflation cycle saw far less rating migration than the GFC. The smoothness of agency ratings relative to PIT PDs is real but smaller than the marketing material suggests.

Reduced regulatory reliance is incomplete

Dodd-Frank instructed US regulators to remove ratings references from prudential rules, and the SEC, Federal Reserve and OCC have largely done so. The Basel framework has reduced (not eliminated) reliance through internal-ratings approaches and due-diligence overlays. But sovereign rating thresholds still drive ECB collateral haircuts, money-market-fund eligibility, and many private institutional mandates. The "hardwiring" critique persists.

The South African angle for F107

Three SA-specific points worth holding in mind. First, the country was downgraded below investment grade by all three Big Three agencies between 2017 and 2020. Second, SA bank ratings sit a notch or two below the sovereign, capped by the country ceiling. Third, GCR Ratings (Johannesburg-based, Moody's-owned since 2022) covers African corporates that the Big Three do not. Expect questions on the timeline and the implications for bank funding costs.

Common F107 traps

Watch out Six traps. (1) Treating ratings as cardinal numbers; they are ordinal letter grades and the implied PD differences are non-linear. (2) Confusing the issuer rating with the rating of a specific instrument; an "AAA-rated bank" can issue AT1 capital rated five notches lower. (3) Forgetting the issuer-pays conflict; the question "who pays for the rating?" answers half the 2008 critique. (4) Assuming the agencies are unregulated. They are supervised by the SEC (NRSROs), ESMA (EU), FSCA (SA) and equivalents elsewhere. (5) Confusing "investment grade" boundaries: BBB- / Baa3 on S&P / Fitch versus Moody's. (6) Treating the TTC convention as identical to TTC PD in the IRB sense; they are related but the calibration and granularity differ.
