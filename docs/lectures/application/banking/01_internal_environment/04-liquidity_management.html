Liquidity management

The other thing that kills banks. Why cash flow over time matters more than net worth, and how a treasury team keeps the lights on.

Lecture 4 of Phase 1 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: docs/wiki/application/banking/01_internal_environment/04-liquidity_management.md
Step 1

The 30-second version

Liquidity is the ability to pay obligations as they fall due. Solvent banks fail when they cannot. The bank's liabilities (deposits, funding lines) can leave on short notice; its assets (mortgages, corporate loans) cannot be sold quickly without taking a loss. The job of liquidity management is to bridge that gap, day by day, currency by currency, in normal times and under stress.

The three concepts to keep separate are funding liquidity (do I have cash today?), trading liquidity (can I sell this asset?), and redemption liquidity (when does this asset become cash on its own?). And the three sources of funds are customer deposits (sticky), wholesale funding (skittish), and the central bank (emergency only).

Feynman test Imagine you are wealthy on paper: you own a 5 million GBP house. But your salary stops next month. You still have to pay rent on your flat (the mortgage), groceries, and the car. None of those can be paid with "house". You either sell the house quickly (at a steep discount) or you default. That is exactly what happens to a bank in a liquidity squeeze. Capital is your net worth. Liquidity is your cash flow.
Step 2

Why this matters

Banks rarely fail because they ran out of capital first. They fail because they ran out of cash, and the running-out-of-cash happened so fast that the capital position became irrelevant. Three cases sit at the heart of every liquidity lecture:

  • Northern Rock, 2007: the first UK bank run in 150 years. The bank was solvent. Its wholesale funding markets seized up in days, retail depositors queued round the block, and it could not bridge the gap.
  • Lehman Brothers, 2008: collapsed in a weekend when overnight repo funding evaporated. By the time the rescue talks started, there was no time left.
  • Silicon Valley Bank, 2023: died in 48 hours. High deposit concentration (tech startups, mostly uninsured), large unrealised losses on Treasury bonds, and social media made the run instant.

Each case is the same story in different decades. The bank was holding long, illiquid assets funded by short, fast-moving liabilities. Confidence broke. The buffers were too thin or too slow to mobilise. The bank could not pay obligations as they came due. End of bank.

Step 3

The mental model

The cleanest way to hold the whole discipline in your head is to think of a bank as a household running two separate bank accounts. A savings account covers unexpected losses — medical bills, redundancy, the boiler exploding. A checking account covers day-to-day bills — rent, utilities, groceries. Both need to be kept above their respective minima, and both are drained by stress, but by different kinds of stress.

A bank is the same. Capital, which you met in lecture 3, is the savings account: it absorbs losses from defaults, market moves and operational events. Liquidity is the checking account: it pays obligations as they fall due — deposit withdrawals, salaries, interest payments, drawdowns on committed lines. Both have a regulatory minimum, both have buffers above that minimum, and both fail catastrophically if the cushion runs out.

The crucial difference is in the failure mode. A bank that runs out of capital is insolvent: its assets are worth less than its liabilities at maturity. A bank that runs out of liquidity is illiquid: it cannot pay today's obligations even if its assets would eventually be worth more than its liabilities. SVB in March 2023 was a textbook liquidity failure — its long-dated bond holdings were worth less than reported because of interest-rate losses, but the killer was a 24-hour deposit run that outpaced the bank's ability to liquidate even high-quality holdings. Liquidity can kill a solvent bank in days, where capital problems usually take quarters or years to unfold.

The other difference is the time dimension. Capital is essentially a stock measure: you have a CET1 ratio at a point in time, you have it or you don't. Liquidity is a flow measure: on every future day, are inflows at least as big as outflows, including under stress? The LCR (30 days) and NSFR (12 months) regulate this at standardised horizons; the bank's own internal model goes day by day across the full balance sheet, in every currency, asking the same question on every rung: can we pay tomorrow's bills even if the world looks worse than it did this morning?

Step 4

The detail

Why this is structural, not optional

Liquidity risk is the inevitable consequence of maturity transformation, which we met in Lecture 1. Banks originate long-dated assets (residential mortgages with maturities up to 30 years, project finance loans up to 50 years, committed but undrawn credit facilities). They fund those assets with short-dated liabilities (current accounts, instant-access savings, money-market funding). The model only works if the bank can keep rolling over its short funding or replace it with new funding. Liquidity management is the discipline of making sure that assumption stays valid.

The challenge has two faces:

Asset funding
Securing funding for assets over their full life, including committed but undrawn assets such as credit card lines and contingency facilities. These can be drawn at any time.
Liability withdrawal
Meeting immediate withdrawal requests from depositors, given that a large proportion of customer liabilities are on-demand (current accounts, instant-access savings).

The bridge between the two is behavioural modelling. Contractually, a current account is payable on demand. Behaviourally, most balances sit for years. A bank that funded long-dated mortgages with current account balances would be reckless on contractual maturity, but perfectly prudent on behavioural maturity, provided the behavioural model is right. The whole apparatus of liquidity management is the answer to the question, "how much can we trust the behavioural model under stress?"

Three types of liquidity, not one

Confusingly, "liquidity" gets used to mean three different things. Treasury teams distinguish them carefully — and the relationship between them is one-directional: trading and redemption are two routes by which assets become cash, and that cash is what gives the bank funding liquidity.

Funding liquidity
The ability of the bank to pay obligations as they fall due. The salary-vs-bills analogy. This is what regulators and ALCOs care about most.
Trading liquidity
The ease with which an asset can be sold in size, with a narrow bid-offer spread and no material price impact. The Naspers-vs-private-stake analogy. This is what determines whether HQLA actually delivers cash when you need it.
Redemption liquidity
The fact that an asset becomes cash on its own as it nears maturity, regardless of market conditions. A bond two weeks from maturity is essentially cash-in-waiting, even if the same bond was illiquid four years ago.
Why this matters Funding liquidity is the goal. Trading and redemption liquidity are two of the ways you achieve it. HQLA work because they have trading liquidity (you can sell them now) and short-dated investments work because they have redemption liquidity (they turn into cash automatically). The art is in mixing the two so that even when trading liquidity dries up under stress, redemption liquidity carries you through.

The three sources of funding

All bank liabilities ultimately come from one of three places.

SourceSub-typesBehaviourUse
Customer deposits Retail current, retail savings, retail term, corporate operational, corporate term Most stable. Retail deposits in particular show "sticky" behaviour, with balances persisting for years. The preferred funding base. The whole strategy of a retail bank is to grow it.
Wholesale funding Asset managers, pension funds, other banks, CD / CP, interbank deposits, capital-market bonds Rate-sensitive. Short-dated wholesale is volatile under stress; multi-banked professional depositors move fast. Long-dated capital-market bonds are stable regardless of source. Supplements customer deposits. In South Africa especially, structural reliance is higher because of lower discretionary retail savings.
Central bank Daily open market operations, emergency lending facilities Last resort. Reliance outside daily operations signals that the bank is no longer a viable standalone entity. BAU = none. Stress = the line that keeps the system from collapsing.

The liability stability hierarchy

Under stress, not all funding behaves the same way. The behavioural hierarchy from "most likely to stay" to "first to leave" is the most important table in this lecture. Memorise the order.

A few features of this hierarchy are worth pulling out. Retail current accounts are the best example of behavioural vs contractual maturity: contractually overnight, behaviourally multi-year. Wholesale and interbank deposits are the opposite: contractually short, and behaviourally even shorter under stress. Non-bank financial institutions (asset managers, hedge funds, money-market funds) behave like wholesale even when their account types look retail; they are market-aware and run fast.

The metrics treasury uses

Banks calculate and monitor six internal baseline metrics plus two regulatory metrics. The internal six are the day-to-day operating dashboard for the treasury team and ALCO; the two regulatory ones are the binding constraints from Basel III.

Six internal metrics

Metric What it measures
Short term
Loan-to-deposit ratio (LDR) The simplest stability check. Above 100% means deposits do not cover the loan book; the bank relies on wholesale funding.
1m liquidity ratio (LR) Liquid assets vs liabilities maturing in one month. A point-in-time view.
Cumulative liquidity model (CLM) The bank's view of the liquidity ladder we drew above: net cash position day by day, out to a horizon.
Liquid asset buffer (LAB) The stock of unencumbered HQLA available to monetise under stress.
Long term
Liquidity risk factor (LRF) Structural funding measure capturing the mismatch over longer horizons.
Concentration and funding source report Who funds the bank? How concentrated is funding by counterparty, instrument, currency?

One seventh metric, the inter-entity lending report, applies to groups: it tracks whether subsidiaries are funding themselves vs being funded from the parent. Important for ring-fenced UK banks and for understanding which entity actually carries the liquidity risk.

Liquidity is also managed by currency, not just in aggregate. Currencies are not freely convertible under stress (think the rand or the lira in a crisis), so a bank with adequate sterling liquidity and a dollar shortfall is still in trouble.

Two regulatory metrics

Basel III added two minimum ratios, covered in detail in their own lectures later under risk measurement. They mirror the internal metrics above — one short-term test, one long-term test.

Short term
LCR
Liquidity coverage ratio. HQLA divided by net cash outflows under a 30-day stress scenario. Minimum 100%. The "can you survive a month?" test.

$$ \text{LCR} = \frac{\text{HQLA}}{\text{Net cash outflows over 30 days, stressed}} \geq 100\% $$

Long term
NSFR
Net stable funding ratio. Available stable funding divided by required stable funding over a one-year horizon. Minimum 100%. The "is your structural funding mix sustainable?" test.

$$ \text{NSFR} = \frac{\text{Available stable funding}}{\text{Required stable funding}} \geq 100\% $$

LCR addresses short-term survival; NSFR addresses structural mismatch. Both are binding. Both can be game-able through behavioural assumptions, which is why supervisors look hard at the inputs.

The central bank as the line of last resort

Every developed banking system has a central bank line of last resort. Bagehot's rule from 1873 still governs the idea: lend freely, at a penal rate, against good collateral. The penalty rate matters because it ensures banks do not use the line as cheap funding; the collateral requirement protects the central bank from absorbing losses.

A bank that draws on central bank support outside of normal daily operations is signalling distress. In the 2008 crisis and the 2023 mini-crisis, central banks created emergency facilities precisely to make use of the line less stigmatising (the Fed's discount window vs the BTFP in 2023, for instance), with mixed success. Treasury teams plan for the line being available but never assume it is a substitute for ordinary liquidity buffers.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Liquidity kills faster than capital

Solvency and liquidity failures both kill banks, but liquidity kills faster. A capital problem can persist for quarters while management searches for new equity or sells businesses. A liquidity problem can play out in 48 hours. SVB had a respectable CET1 ratio on the morning of its failure. So did Northern Rock. The death came from the funding side, not the asset side.

The behavioural model is where the action is

Every liquidity ratio depends on behavioural assumptions: what fraction of retail deposits stays in a 30-day stress, what proportion of an undrawn credit line is drawn, what haircut applies to a corporate bond's sale value. These assumptions are calibrated from historical data, which has a fundamental problem: the worst stresses are by definition not in the historical sample. So treasury teams overlay supervisory haircuts (the LCR run-off rates, the NSFR factors) and bank-specific stress scenarios on top. The art is knowing when your own behavioural model is being too kind to the bank.

Idiosyncratic vs market-wide stress

There are two flavours of liquidity stress and a good treasury models both. Idiosyncratic stress is a run on your bank specifically: a reputation event, an earnings miss, social media. Wholesale funders move away; retail depositors slowly follow. Market-wide stress is when the whole system seizes up: 2008 was market-wide; SVB was idiosyncratic. They have different implications. Under idiosyncratic stress you can still sell HQLA (the market still works); under market-wide stress HQLA prices fall too. The LCR uses haircuts that pretend to handle market-wide, but in 2008 even Level 1 sovereigns moved.

The deposit franchise is a real asset

A retail bank with millions of small, sticky current account holders is fundamentally more valuable than a bank with the same balance sheet funded by wholesale markets, even if the regulatory ratios look identical. The deposit franchise is intangible: it is built over decades by branches, brand, customer service, and small unit economics. It is hard to replicate and hard to disrupt (which is why fintechs find it hard). It is the most underrated asset on a bank's balance sheet.

Common F107 traps

Watch out Three traps. (1) Confusing solvency with liquidity. The question "could SVB have been saved by more capital?" almost certainly has the answer "no, it needed more liquidity." (2) Forgetting that liquidity is managed by currency, not just in aggregate. A bank can be liquid in GBP and illiquid in USD at the same time. (3) Treating HQLA as a fixed number. HQLA is what you can monetise; under market-wide stress the haircuts widen and the buffer shrinks just when you need it most.
