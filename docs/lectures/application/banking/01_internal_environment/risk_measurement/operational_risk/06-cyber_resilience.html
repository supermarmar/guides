Cyber risk and operational resilience

Cyber risk is a sub-category of operational risk under the 2004 Basel taxonomy (categories 2 and 6), but its frequency, severity, and rate of evolution have elevated it to a board-level prudential priority. Operational resilience — the ability of a bank to deliver its critical business services through any disruption — became a stand-alone supervisory concept after a decade of major cyber incidents, IT failures, and pandemic disruption. The BCBS published Principles for Operational Resilience in March 2021 (BCBS d516), and the PRA, FCA, EBA, SARB Prudential Authority, and FSCA have all issued specific guidance. The supervisory framework rests on four practical concepts: important business services, impact tolerance, mapping of dependencies, and scenario testing.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Cyber risk is the risk of loss from unauthorised access to, disruption of, or damage to the bank's information systems, data, or services. It is technically a sub-category of operational risk under the Basel taxonomy, falling primarily under external fraud (category 2) when the attacker is external and business disruption and system failures (category 6) when the impact is service availability. Operational resilience is the broader supervisory concept that emerged in the late 2010s: the bank's ability to deliver its critical business services through any disruption, whether from cyber attack, IT failure, third-party failure, natural disaster, or pandemic. The BCBS's Principles for Operational Resilience (March 2021, BCBS d516) codified seven principles that supervisors expect every bank to implement: governance, operational risk management, business continuity planning, mapping of interconnections, third-party dependency management, incident management, and ICT/cyber security. The practical implementation centres on four operational concepts: identifying important business services, setting impact tolerances for each, mapping the people, processes, systems, and third parties on which each depends, and scenario testing against severe-but-plausible disruption.

Feynman test Cyber risk differs from other operational risk in two fundamental ways. First, it is adversarial: someone is actively trying to break your defences, and the threat evolves as the defenders adapt. Traditional operational risk losses come from accidents, errors, and external events that do not fight back. Cyber attackers do. Second, cyber failures are contagious: a single compromise of one bank can spread to others through shared infrastructure — correspondent banking networks, cloud providers, software supply chains, common identity systems. The 2017 NotPetya attack was the canonical example: it spread globally in hours and caused billions of dollars of losses at companies that had no direct connection to the original Ukrainian target. Cyber risk therefore needs a different management framework from traditional operational risk, and operational resilience is the supervisory term for that framework. The pivot from "business continuity" to "operational resilience" in the 2018-2021 supervisory consultations reflects the same shift: continuity assumes a single firm restoring its own service after a single event; resilience assumes complex interdependencies, repeated attacks, and the need to keep critical services running even when individual systems fail.
What is beyond the source The op risk wiki notes cover cyber implicitly under "systems" and "external fraud" but do not treat it as a stand-alone topic. This lecture treats cyber risk and operational resilience as a self-contained subject, with: a structured threat landscape (six attack categories from DDoS to supply-chain compromise); a detailed walk-through of the BCBS d516 principles and their UK and South African implementations; the four-step operational resilience methodology (identify, set, map, test); recent supervisory incidents (Target, Bangladesh Bank, NotPetya, Equifax, SolarWinds, ICBC, the 2020 pandemic stress); the third-party amplification problem and the cloud concentration risk; the cyber insurance market and why it is hardening; and the SARB / FSCA Joint Standard on Cybersecurity and Cyber Resilience that applies to all South African financial institutions from 2024.
Step 2

Why this matters

The case for treating cyber and operational resilience as board-level concerns is made by the loss histories themselves. A short list of significant incidents in the last decade illustrates the scale.

Target, 2013. Attackers gained access to Target's payment systems via a compromised HVAC contractor and exfiltrated payment card data for 40 million customers. The eventual cost to Target exceeded $200 million in direct expenses and is estimated at over $1 billion in indirect costs. The incident established that retailers were viable targets for nation-state-grade attacks and that third-party vendors were the soft underbelly.

Bangladesh Bank, 2016. Attackers compromised the central bank's SWIFT credentials and submitted 35 fraudulent transfer instructions totalling almost $1 billion. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York executed 5 of the instructions before flagging the rest as suspicious; $81 million was successfully laundered through Philippines casinos and never recovered. The incident showed that the global payment messaging infrastructure could be attacked at endpoints even when SWIFT itself was uncompromised.

NotPetya, 2017. A nation-state attack on Ukrainian tax software (M.E.Doc) spread globally through software updates and caused approximately $10 billion of damage worldwide. Maersk lost $300 million. Merck lost $670 million. FedEx's TNT subsidiary lost $400 million. The targeted entities had no direct connection to Ukraine; the attack vector was supply-chain compromise of a routine accounting tool. The episode established supply-chain attacks as a top-tier threat category.

Equifax, 2017. Attackers exploited an unpatched vulnerability in Apache Struts to exfiltrate personal data on 147 million people. The total cost to Equifax exceeded $1.4 billion in remediation, settlements, and regulatory fines. The incident established that data breach at scale produced regulatory consequences (under the FTC, multiple state regulators, and eventually GDPR) as severe as direct financial theft.

Capital One, 2019. A misconfigured AWS firewall allowed a former AWS employee to access customer records for 106 million Capital One customers in the US and Canada. Total costs exceeded $290 million. The incident drew attention to cloud configuration as a primary risk surface for financial institutions adopting public-cloud infrastructure.

SolarWinds, 2020. Russian-attributed attackers compromised the build pipeline for SolarWinds Orion network management software, distributing malware to 18,000 customers including US federal agencies, Microsoft, FireEye, and major financial institutions. The incident escalated the supply-chain threat to a level that required co-ordinated national response. It also exposed the limits of perimeter-based cyber defence: even a fully patched, fully monitored system can be compromised if the software being run is itself trojanised at source.

ICBC US broker-dealer, November 2023. A ransomware attack on ICBC Financial Services in New York disrupted the firm's ability to clear US Treasury trades. ICBC was forced to settle trades manually using USB drives between counterparties. The attack briefly disrupted the world's most liquid market and demonstrated that cyber attacks on bank subsidiaries could affect the broader financial system, not just the bank itself.

Pandemic 2020. COVID-19 produced the largest simultaneous operational resilience test in the history of banking. Every major bank had to relocate trading floors, call centres, and operational staff to remote working within days. Most succeeded, but the experience revealed weaknesses (lack of remote-working bandwidth, dependence on on-premise systems, inability to support paper-based processes from home) that have shaped resilience planning since. The pandemic is also the reason the BCBS d516 principles were finalised in 2021: the consultation had been drafted before the pandemic, but the operational experience of 2020 reinforced every principle in the final document.

For an ASSA candidate, two specific implications matter. First, the SARB Prudential Authority and the FSCA issued a Joint Standard on Cybersecurity and Cyber Resilience in 2024 that applies to all South African financial institutions, requiring board-level ownership, written cyber strategy, controls covering identity, access, and data, testing, incident response, and cyber resilience. The Joint Standard is the current South African specification of what operational resilience means in practice. Second, cyber risk losses feed the SMA Loss Component (Lecture 4) like any other operational loss, so a bank with poor cyber resilience pays not only the direct cost of incidents but also higher SMA capital through the ILM in subsequent years.

Step 3

The mental model

The supervisory framework for cyber and operational resilience has two halves. The first half is the threat landscape: what kinds of cyber attack actually occur, what they target, and how they unfold. The second half is the resilience methodology: how to organise the bank so that critical services keep running when an attack succeeds (because some will). The two halves are complementary, not interchangeable: a bank that understands threats but has no resilience methodology will be permanently reactive; a bank that has a resilience methodology but does not understand the current threats will be defending the wrong things.

The four-step methodology applies to every disruption type, not just cyber. The same identification, tolerance-setting, mapping, and testing framework covers IT failure, third-party failure, natural disaster, pandemic, and physical attack. The framework is general; the controls within each step vary with the threat. For cyber specifically, the controls are organised around identity management, access control, data protection, vulnerability management, incident detection, and recovery testing.

Step 4

The detail

The cyber threat landscape

Six attack families dominate the modern cyber threat landscape for banks. Each has a different signature, a different control set, and a different relationship to the bank's resilience framework.

Distributed denial of service (DDoS)
Flooding a bank's public-facing systems (websites, mobile apps, payment APIs) with traffic until they become unavailable to legitimate customers. Modern DDoS attacks reach hundreds of gigabits per second and are typically mounted from botnets of compromised consumer devices. DDoS is a service-availability attack rather than a data-theft attack; the bank's losses are reputational and revenue-based rather than direct theft, but regulators treat extended DDoS-driven outages as resilience failures.
Ransomware
Malware that encrypts data and demands payment for the decryption key. Modern ransomware operations also exfiltrate data before encrypting it (double extortion), threatening to publish if the ransom is not paid. The ICBC US broker-dealer ransomware attack of November 2023 is the most recent significant example affecting a regulated financial institution. The supervisory expectation is that banks have offline backups, tested recovery procedures, and a clear policy on whether and when to pay ransoms (US OFAC sanctions complicate payment to sanctioned threat actors).
Data exfiltration
Theft of sensitive data — customer records, employee data, internal business documents, trading algorithms — for sale, espionage, or extortion. The Equifax 2017 breach (147m customer records) is the canonical large-scale example. Data exfiltration produces both direct costs (forensic investigation, customer notification, credit monitoring) and indirect costs (regulatory fines under GDPR, POPIA, and similar regimes, plus class-action litigation).
Business email compromise (BEC) and phishing
Attacks that exploit the email channel to impersonate executives or trusted counterparties and trigger fraudulent payment instructions. BEC is the highest-volume cyber loss category for banks today, often executed without any malware: the attacker simply emails the finance team with a plausible payment request from a compromised or spoofed account. Phishing is the broader category, encompassing credential theft and malware delivery via fraudulent emails. Controls are primarily training (employee awareness), authentication (call-back verification of payment instructions), and email security technology (domain authentication, attachment sandboxing).
Insider threat
Misuse of legitimate access by current or former employees, contractors, or trusted third parties. Insiders bypass perimeter defences by construction. The Capital One 2019 breach was conducted by a former AWS employee with knowledge of the bank's cloud configuration. The Nick Leeson and John Rusnak cases (Lecture 1) are older examples in non-cyber form. Controls are segregation of duties, monitoring of privileged user activity, joiner-mover-leaver processes that revoke access promptly, and behavioural anomaly detection.
Supply-chain attack
Compromise of a vendor, software provider, or service provider that enables onward attack of the vendor's customers. The 2020 SolarWinds compromise affected 18,000 organisations through a single trojanised software update. The 2017 NotPetya attack reached its victims through a Ukrainian accounting tool. Supply-chain attacks are the hardest to defend against because the compromise occurs outside the bank's own perimeter. Controls are vendor due diligence, software composition analysis, network segmentation that limits the blast radius of a compromised vendor, and contractual rights to audit and to demand security attestations.

The BCBS Principles for Operational Resilience (BCBS d516)

BCBS d516, published in March 2021, sets out seven principles that supervisors expect every internationally active bank to implement. The principles are not prescriptive about specific controls; they describe the outcomes and the governance framework within which the bank's own choices must operate.

Principle Focus Headline expectation
1. Governance Board and senior management ownership Operational resilience is a board-level responsibility; the board approves the resilience framework and oversees its implementation.
2. Operational risk management Integration with op risk framework Operational resilience is built on the bank's existing operational risk management framework (Lectures 1–4), not separate from it.
3. Business continuity planning and testing BCP/DR for critical operations The bank has documented continuity plans for critical operations, tested regularly under realistic scenarios.
4. Mapping interconnections and interdependencies End-to-end dependency view For each critical operation, the bank knows the people, processes, technology, facilities, and third parties on which delivery depends.
5. Third-party dependency management Outsourcing and vendor risk The bank manages risks arising from external dependencies including outsourcing, cloud, and software supply chains.
6. Incident management Detection, response, recovery The bank has documented incident response procedures with clear roles, escalation paths, and communication plans for customers, supervisors, and the market.
7. ICT including cyber security Technology and cyber controls The bank maintains effective controls over its information and communication technology, including specific cyber security measures aligned with the threat environment.

The companion document BCBS d517 (also March 2021) revises the 2011 Principles for the Sound Management of Operational Risk to align with d516. The two documents together replace the 2003 and 2011 op risk principles documents as the supervisory baseline.

Important business services and impact tolerances

The UK PRA's SS1/21 (March 2021) and the FCA's PS21/3 give the most prescriptive supervisory specification of how operational resilience translates into bank practice. The core operational concept is the important business service (IBS): a service the bank provides to external customers, the failure of which could cause intolerable harm to consumers or threaten the integrity of the financial system. Examples include retail current accounts (payments, withdrawals, deposits), mortgage administration, custody services, securities clearing and settlement, and online banking. Internal services (payroll, internal reporting) are not IBS even if they are operationally important.

Important business service (IBS)
A service provided by the bank to external customers, the disruption of which could harm consumers, threaten the integrity of the financial system, or affect the bank's safety and soundness. Each IBS is identified at the level of granularity that maps to a customer-facing outcome. A typical UK bank identifies 20–40 IBS; a global universal bank may identify 100+.
Impact tolerance
The maximum tolerable level of disruption to each IBS, expressed in measurable terms. Typical metrics include time (e.g. "card payments restored within 4 hours"), volume (e.g. "no more than 1% of customers unable to access mobile banking"), and customer impact (e.g. "no customer left unable to pay a mortgage instalment by the due date"). Impact tolerances are set by the board and must be defensible to the supervisor; they are not optimistic targets but supervisory commitments.

The supervisory bite of the impact tolerance is that the bank must be able to demonstrate, through testing, that it can stay within the impact tolerance under severe-but-plausible disruption scenarios. If testing reveals that the bank cannot stay within tolerance, the bank must remediate: invest in additional controls, restructure dependencies, or change the impact tolerance with supervisory approval. The PRA's expectation is that banks reach full compliance with their impact tolerances by 31 March 2025; transitional progress reports were required from 2022 onwards.

Mapping and dependency analysis

For each IBS, the bank produces an end-to-end map of the people, processes, technology, facilities, and third parties on which delivery depends. The map identifies single points of failure and concentrated dependencies. A typical IBS map might show: the customer interface (mobile app, web, branch), the core banking system, the payment switch, identity verification, anti-fraud monitoring, the staff who operate each component, the data centres where each system runs, and the third-party providers (cloud, payment networks, identity providers, telecommunications) that support each step.

Mapping reveals problems that no individual component owner would identify. A single cloud region hosting both the primary and secondary versions of a critical system is a concentrated dependency; a single staff team supporting the operational handover between two systems is a single point of failure; a single identity provider used by 80 per cent of the bank's IBS is a systemic dependency. These are precisely the issues the mapping exercise is designed to surface, and the remediation programmes that flow from them are typically multi-year investments in diversification.

Scenario testing

The fourth step is testing the IBS framework under severe-but-plausible disruption scenarios. The supervisory expectation is that scenarios are diverse (covering cyber, IT, third-party, physical, and pandemic), severe (representing realistic worst cases, not best cases), and plausible (grounded in real-world threat intelligence and incident history). Banks are expected to conduct annual scenario tests covering each IBS, with the results documented and reported to the board and the supervisor.

Test types include: tabletop exercises (paper-based walk-through with key personnel), simulation exercises (live testing of recovery procedures), and joint exercises with third parties and other banks. The UK has run several industry-wide cyber exercises ("Resilient Shield" series) coordinated by the Bank of England's CBEST framework. The European Central Bank's TIBER-EU framework is the equivalent for euro-area banks. South Africa's SARB Prudential Authority has indicated similar industry-wide testing as part of the Joint Standard implementation.

Third-party and supply-chain dependency

Modern banks are deeply dependent on third parties: cloud providers (AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud), payment networks (Visa, Mastercard, SWIFT), identity providers (national ID systems, OAuth providers), KYC vendors, market data vendors, transaction monitoring software vendors, model validation consultants, and many more. Each dependency is a potential source of disruption. The BCBS principles, the EBA Guidelines on outsourcing arrangements (2019), the PRA's SS2/21, and the SARB Joint Standard all require banks to maintain a register of material outsourcing arrangements, to conduct due diligence on outsourced providers, to retain the ability to bring services back in-house, and to test failover procedures.

Cloud concentration is the dependency of greatest current supervisory concern. A small number of cloud providers serve a large fraction of the financial system; an outage or compromise at a single cloud provider could disrupt many banks simultaneously. The BCBS and the Financial Stability Board have both flagged cloud concentration risk as a systemic concern, and the EU's Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA, effective January 2025) introduces oversight of designated critical third-party providers (including major cloud platforms) by European supervisors directly.

South African framework: the SARB / FSCA Joint Standard

The South African Prudential Authority and the FSCA jointly issued the Joint Standard on Cybersecurity and Cyber Resilience in 2024, with full effective date in 2025. The Joint Standard applies to all South African financial institutions (banks, insurers, market infrastructure, asset managers) and requires:

  • Board-level ownership of cyber strategy, with a designated executive (typically the CISO) reporting to the board on cyber risk.
  • A written cyber strategy aligned with the institution's risk appetite, business strategy, and threat environment.
  • Cyber risk management integrated with the broader operational risk framework, including risk identification, assessment, mitigation, monitoring, and reporting.
  • Awareness and culture: training for all staff and targeted training for technical personnel.
  • Identity, access, and data controls: privileged access management, multi-factor authentication, data classification and protection, encryption in transit and at rest.
  • Vulnerability and patch management: scheduled assessment, prioritised remediation, threat intelligence integration.
  • Testing: penetration testing, red-team exercises, scenario-based simulations.
  • Incident management: detection, response, recovery, reporting to the supervisor.
  • Third-party cyber risk: due diligence, contractual provisions, ongoing monitoring.

The Joint Standard is the South African specification of what BCBS d516 means in practice. For an ASSA F107 candidate, it is the directly examinable domestic framework.

Relationship to Basel capital

Cyber risk losses feed the same operational loss database that drives the SMA Loss Component (Lecture 4). A ransomware payment, a regulatory fine following a data breach, the costs of remediation after a major incident: all are recorded as operational losses, assigned to a Basel event category (typically external fraud or business disruption), and counted in the bank's ten-year loss history for the ILM. The mechanism is not new; what is new is the volume and frequency of cyber-related losses in the most recent loss vintages.

Pillar 1 SMA does not, however, generate a specific capital charge for forward-looking cyber risk or for resilience deficiencies. That sits in Pillar 2A. Major supervisors (PRA, ECB, Federal Reserve, SARB) impose Pillar 2A add-ons where the bank's resilience self-assessment, scenario testing, or supervisory examination reveals gaps. The size of the Pillar 2A add-on is supervisor discretion; in the UK, multi-percentage-point CET1 add-ons for operational resilience are not unknown for banks with significant identified weaknesses.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The shift from "business continuity" to "operational resilience" is conceptual, not cosmetic

Before 2018, the supervisory term was business continuity, and the framework assumed a single bank restoring its own service after a single event (a fire, a power outage, a flood). The post-2018 framework is operational resilience, and it assumes complex interdependencies, repeated and overlapping attacks, and the need to keep critical services running even when individual systems remain offline. The change in vocabulary is the visible part of a deeper change in supervisory expectation: continuity assumes the bank's defences will mostly hold, with continuity plans engaging only when they fail; resilience assumes that defences will be breached and asks how the bank stays operational under sustained pressure.

The third-party amplification problem is not solvable at the bank level

A bank can do everything right and still be brought down by a third-party failure. Cloud outages, payment network disruptions, identity provider compromises, and major software supply-chain attacks are outside the bank's direct control. The supervisory response — third-party register, due diligence, contractual rights, failover testing — reduces the bank's exposure but does not eliminate it. The EU's DORA introduces direct supervision of critical third-party providers, which is a more fundamental response: instead of regulating banks' use of cloud providers, the EU now regulates the cloud providers themselves. Whether this approach scales globally and whether US, UK, and emerging-market supervisors follow are open questions. For the actuary, the practical implication is that the bank's resilience cannot be fully captured by internal modelling; some exposure is irreducibly external.

Ransomware moved the supervisory conversation

Before 2015, the cyber risk supervisory conversation was about data breach: how the bank protects customer information from theft. After 2015, ransomware introduced the possibility that the bank could be operationally disabled rather than merely embarrassed. A bank that cannot operate cannot meet its obligations; a bank that cannot meet its obligations may be subject to resolution. The systemic implications are larger than data breach, and the supervisory response has been correspondingly more intense. The 2023 ICBC incident, which briefly disrupted US Treasury trading, is the proof of concept that cyber attacks on regulated financial entities can affect the broader market, not just the entity itself.

Cyber insurance is hardening rapidly

Cyber insurance was a growth market through the late 2010s, with banks and corporates purchasing coverage against ransomware, data breach, and business interruption. Underwriters experienced sharp loss-ratio deterioration in 2020 and 2021 as ransomware claims escalated. The market has since hardened: premiums have risen substantially, coverage has tightened, and many insurers have introduced sub-limits or exclusions for ransomware-driven business interruption. For banks, the practical implication is that the cyber insurance premium is no longer a cheap substitute for cyber controls; underwriters increasingly require evidence of strong controls before binding cover, and the underwriting questionnaires are themselves a useful internal control challenge.

Model risk is the cyber-adjacent topic that actuaries already work on

Model risk — the risk of loss from incorrect or misused model outputs — has been recognised as a sub-category of operational risk since the Federal Reserve's SR 11-7 (2011). The PRA's SS1/23 Model Risk Management Principles (effective May 2024) is the current supervisory standard for UK banks. For an actuary working on PD, LGD, or IFRS 9 models, SS1/23 is the daily framework: it requires model identification and inventory, materiality assessment, governance, validation, monitoring, and a documented model risk appetite. The cyber connection is that compromise of model code or model inputs is simultaneously a cyber incident and a model risk event; modern operational risk programmes treat the two together, with shared incident management and shared remediation.

Watch out Do not equate cyber risk with IT risk. IT risk includes accidental failures, capacity issues, and software defects, and is a long-established sub-category of systems risk. Cyber risk specifically refers to adversarial action — someone is trying to harm the bank — and requires controls aimed at adversaries (threat intelligence, red teaming, security operations centres, intrusion detection). The two are related but distinct. A complete operational resilience framework covers both. An exam answer that treats cyber and IT as synonyms will lose marks; an answer that treats operational resilience as just cyber will also lose marks.
