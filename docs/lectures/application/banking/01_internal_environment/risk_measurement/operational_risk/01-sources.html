Operational risk: sources, taxonomy, and conduct risk

Operational risk is the residual category that captures every loss a bank can suffer that is not a credit or market loss. The 2004 Basel II accord made it a Pillar 1 capital category for the first time, codifying a four-source definition (people, systems, processes, external events) and a seven-category loss event taxonomy that still drives every operational risk model in use today. Conduct risk sits at the intersection of operational, legal, and reputational risk and is now a stand-alone supervisory concern in its own right.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Operational risk is defined in Basel II as the risk of loss from inadequate or failed internal processes, people, and systems, or from external events. The definition includes legal risk but excludes strategic and reputational risk. Three features distinguish operational risk from credit and market risk. First, it is always present: a bank that conducts no lending and holds no trading book still faces the risk of fraud, system failure, and operational error. Second, it cannot generally be diversified away in the same way market and credit risk can: two settlement errors are not negatively correlated with each other. Third, its loss distribution is fat-tailed and lumpy. Most operational risk losses are small and routine, but occasional catastrophic events (rogue trading, major system failures, regulatory fines) dwarf the routine losses and dominate the capital calculation. This lecture establishes the conceptual foundation: the four sources, the seven Basel loss event categories, and where conduct risk fits in the supervisory landscape.

Feynman test Think of a bank as a factory that processes other people's money. In any factory you have equipment that breaks (systems), workers who make mistakes or commit fraud (people), production procedures that go wrong (processes), and floods or fires outside your control (external events). Operational risk is the risk that the factory fails in any of these four ways. Unlike the risk that a customer will not pay back their loan (credit risk) or the risk that interest rates move against you (market risk), operational risk is always switched on: every payment, every booking, every customer interaction carries it. The Basel Committee made it a Pillar 1 risk in 2004 because a decade of high-profile disasters (Barings, Daiwa, Allied Irish Banks) had made it impossible to ignore. The lesson the disasters taught was not that operational risk was new, but that the existing capital framework had no language to describe it.
What is beyond the source The wiki note covers the four sources, the seven Basel event categories, and the conduct risk framework. This lecture adds the historical incidents that forced operational risk onto the regulatory agenda (Barings 1995, Allied Irish Banks 2002, Knight Capital 2012, the UK PPI scandal); the precise mapping between the four sources and the seven Basel categories (with the boundary cases made explicit); a closer reading of the FCA's three categories of conduct risk drivers and the behaviours it works to eradicate; the distinction between the Basel II definition (which excludes reputational risk) and modern supervisory practice (where Pillar 2A add-ons for conduct effectively capture reputational consequences); and the South African dimension: FSCA, the CoFI Bill, and Treating Customers Fairly.
Step 2

Why this matters

The history of operational risk regulation is a history of disasters that regulators did not anticipate. Each major incident exposed a different failure mode and pushed supervisors closer to treating operational risk as a category that deserved its own capital charge, its own management infrastructure, and its own internal control framework.

Barings Bank collapsed in February 1995 when Nick Leeson, head of futures trading in Singapore, had accumulated unauthorised losses of £827 million through speculative positions and concealed them in a fictitious error account (the famous account 88888). Leeson was simultaneously responsible for executing trades and for settling and reconciling them, an internal control failure that allowed him to hide losses for almost three years. The Barings collapse is the classic failure of segregation of duties: one person, too much access, no independent reconciliation. The bank, founded in 1762 and one of the oldest merchant banks in the world, was sold to ING for £1 the same month.

Allied Irish Banks announced losses of $691 million in February 2002 after its foreign exchange trader John Rusnak, working in the Allfirst subsidiary in Baltimore, had concealed five years of directional bets on the Japanese yen using fictitious options positions to mask the mark-to-market losses. The Ludwig Report commissioned by AIB into the incident concluded that Allfirst's risk management infrastructure was demonstrably inadequate for the trading activities it permitted. The pattern matched Barings closely: a single trader, weak supervision, broken reconciliation.

Knight Capital Group lost $440 million in 45 minutes on the morning of 1 August 2012 when a software deployment error reactivated an old trading algorithm that began inadvertently market-making at enormous size in 154 stocks. The firm had no kill switch adequate to stop the bleeding before it consumed nearly the firm's entire capital base. This was a systems risk event in the purest sense: software deployment failure, inadequate testing in the production environment, no emergency shutdown capability.

The UK's payment protection insurance (PPI) scandal resulted in over £50 billion of redress paid by UK banks between 2011 and 2021, the largest retail conduct event in banking history. PPI was sold alongside loans and credit cards, often without customers' explicit knowledge or for products they could not use (self-employed customers sold policies that excluded self-employment, for instance). The failures were partly in sales incentive structures (people), partly in product design (processes), and largely in inadequate customer disclosures: meeting the Basel definition of "clients, products and business practices" almost exactly. The episode demonstrated that conduct-driven operational losses could exceed the largest credit or market losses any UK bank had ever experienced.

These four examples share a pattern: a failure that was legally operational in character but that had catastrophic consequences for the franchise. They also demonstrate that operational risk is not a secondary concern for bank management. It can destroy the bank entirely (Barings), wipe out years of profit (PPI for the UK majors), or end a firm's independent existence (Knight, sold within months of the loss).

For an actuary, the relevance of operational risk is threefold. First, actuarial models feed bank capital models: an actuary who produces a loss-given-default estimate or an IFRS 9 provision without appreciating the operational risk environment around the data collection and the model deployment has missed a material source of uncertainty. Second, conduct risk and model risk are increasingly treated as operational risk sub-categories by supervisors: model validation, the PRA's model risk management principles (SS1/23), and IFRS 9 governance all connect to operational risk management. Third, the ASSA F107 syllabus tests operational risk capital approaches directly: knowing the BIA, TSA, AMA, and SMA formulas (Lectures 3 and 4) is a direct exam requirement, and the conceptual material in this lecture underpins all of them.

Step 3

The mental model

The mental model for operational risk is simpler than for credit or market risk: two parallel classification systems that complement each other. The first describes where operational risk comes from: the four sources. The second describes what losses look like once they occur: the seven Basel II loss event categories. The first is a management tool, telling a bank where to focus controls and investment. The second is a regulatory taxonomy, telling the bank how to label each loss event in the database that feeds the capital model.

The two systems are complementary, not interchangeable. A loss event is described in terms of its source for risk management and control design, and in terms of its event category and business line for capital reporting. The classification choice matters: the event category determines which cell of the 7 × 8 grid the loss feeds into, which affects the frequency and severity distributions estimated for that cell, which affects the capital charge under the Advanced Measurement Approach. Categorisation is not a documentation exercise. It is a capital exercise.

Step 4

The detail

The four sources

The Basel II definition organises operational risk by its origin. The four sources are mutually exclusive at the level of cause, although a single loss event can have proximate causes in more than one source (for example, a process failure that goes undetected because of a system failure). The conventional ordering puts people first because people-driven losses are the largest by event count and have historically produced the largest individual losses.

People
Operational losses arising from the actions or inactions of individuals inside the bank. This includes deliberate acts (internal fraud, unauthorised trading, embezzlement), negligent acts (errors of execution, failures of judgement, inadequate supervision), and legal disputes arising from the employment relationship (discrimination claims, wrongful dismissal, harassment). People risk is also the channel through which conduct risk events typically originate: a sales force that mis-sells, a trader who manipulates a benchmark, a relationship manager who fails to identify a suspicious transaction.
Systems
Operational losses arising from technology failures: hardware (servers, network equipment, ATM infrastructure), software (bugs, deployment errors, untested upgrades), data integrity (corruption, loss, inconsistency across systems), and cyber-attacks that exploit technology vulnerabilities. Systems risk has grown materially as banks have migrated to digital channels and become dependent on third-party cloud, payment, and identity providers. A core banking system outage that prevents millions of customers from accessing their accounts is the canonical systems event.
Internal processes
Operational losses arising from the failure of the bank's own business processes: settlement errors, failed reconciliations, booking errors, inadequate documentation, errors in customer onboarding, and process failures in trade execution or post-trade management. Process risk is the most diffuse of the four sources. Thousands of small transactions each carry a small probability of process failure, and the aggregate exposure is not always visible until a loss event clusters or a regulatory review identifies a systemic flaw.
External events
Operational losses caused by events outside the bank's control: natural disasters (floods, earthquakes, pandemics), third-party fraud (external theft, forgery, cybercrime executed by outsiders), acts of vandalism or terrorism, and losses from suppliers or other external parties. External events tend to be low-frequency and high-severity: the 2020 pandemic produced simultaneous operational disruption across the entire banking system and is the most recent systemic external-event stress.

The Basel II loss event taxonomy

The 2004 Basel II accord codified the classification of operational risk events into seven Level 1 categories, each subdivided into Level 2 categories and Level 3 activity examples. Every bank using the Advanced Measurement Approach must assign each material loss event to exactly one Level 1 category and one of eight business lines (covered in Lecture 2). The taxonomy has survived essentially unchanged into the Basel III framework and the Standardised Measurement Approach, and it remains the canonical way operational losses are described in the industry.

Level 1 category Definition Level 2 examples Primary source
1. Internal fraud Losses from acts intended to defraud, misappropriate property, or circumvent regulations or company policy, involving at least one internal party. Unauthorised activity (e.g. rogue trading); theft and fraud by employees. People
2. External fraud Losses from acts intended to defraud or misappropriate property by a third party. Theft and external fraud; systems security breaches by outsiders. External events
3. Employment practices & workplace safety Losses from acts inconsistent with employment, health or safety laws; personal injury claims; diversity or discrimination events. Employee relations; safe environment; diversity and discrimination. People
4. Clients, products & business practices Losses from unintentional or negligent failure to meet professional obligations to clients (including fiduciary and suitability requirements), or from the nature or design of a product. Suitability, disclosure, and fiduciary; improper business or market practices; product flaws; advisory failures. People / processes
5. Damage to physical assets Losses from loss or damage to physical assets from natural disasters or other events. Disasters; vandalism; terrorism. External events
6. Business disruption & system failures Losses from disruption of business or system failures. Hardware, software, telecommunications failures; utility outages. Systems
7. Execution, delivery & process management Losses from failed transaction processing or process management; failed relations with trade counterparties and vendors. Transaction capture, execution, and maintenance; monitoring and reporting; customer intake and documentation; trade counterparties; vendors and suppliers. Internal processes

Category 4 (clients, products and business practices) is the broadest and the most operationally significant for retail and wholesale franchises: it is the home of mis-selling, market manipulation, suitability failures, and most conduct risk events. The UK PPI losses, the LIBOR rigging fines, the interest rate hedging product redress, and most large enforcement actions of the last fifteen years all sit in this single category.

Conduct risk

Conduct risk is not a stand-alone Pillar 1 category in Basel II: most conduct events fall within category 4 above. But supervisors in the UK (FCA), the EU (national conduct supervisors), and South Africa (FSCA) have elevated conduct to a stand-alone supervisory priority with its own governance requirements, culture assessments, and Pillar 2A capital add-ons. The FCA, in particular, placed conduct risk at the centre of its supervisory framework when it was created in 2013.

Conduct risk
The risk that a bank's, or an individual's, behaviour results in unfair customer outcomes, market disruption, or damage to the integrity of the financial system. Conduct events typically originate in misaligned incentives, inadequate controls over sales or advice processes, or a culture that prioritises short-term revenue over long-term customer outcomes. The FCA distinguishes conduct risk from broader operational risk by its focus on outcomes for customers and markets rather than on the bank's own losses.

The FCA categorises conduct risk drivers in three groups:

Driver category What it covers
Inherent drivers The complexity of products and services, the information asymmetry between the bank and the customer, and the difficulty customers face in assessing quality before and after purchase.
Structures and behaviours Incentive structures (sales targets, bonus schemes), cultural norms, governance weaknesses, and insufficient challenge from senior management or the second line.
Environmental factors Competitive pressure, technological change, macroeconomic conditions, and changes in customer behaviour (digital adoption, demographic shifts).

The FCA also publishes a list of behaviours it considers indicative of poor conduct and works to eradicate. The headline three are: prioritising profits ahead of fair customer outcomes; tick-box compliance that satisfies the letter but not the spirit of rules; and limited responsibility for customer outcomes after the sale is made. These three together capture the cultural failures that produced PPI, interest rate hedging product mis-selling, and packaged bank account mis-selling: products sold under aggressive targets, sales documentation that ticked every required box but disclosed nothing meaningful, and a presumption that the customer's responsibility began at the point of purchase.

Modern operational risk sub-categories

The 2004 Basel II taxonomy was authoritative at publication and remains the regulatory reference for loss-event classification. But the operational risk universe has evolved over the last two decades, and supervisory practice has identified four sub-categories that warrant their own management attention even though none of them is a new Pillar 1 category. Each fits within the existing seven Basel categories; each has acquired its own supervisory framework, its own incident history, and its own dedicated infrastructure inside major banks. Cyber risk and operational resilience are covered in detail in Lecture 6; the four are previewed here so that the broader operational risk picture is complete.

Cyber risk
The risk of loss from unauthorised access to, disruption of, or damage to the bank's information systems, data, or services. Falls primarily under Basel category 2 (external fraud) when the attacker is outside the bank and category 6 (business disruption and system failures) when the impact is on service availability. Distinguished from general IT risk by its adversarial character: someone is actively trying to harm the bank, and the threat evolves as defences improve. Frequency, severity, and rate of evolution have elevated cyber to a board-level concern in every major jurisdiction.
Operational resilience
The bank's ability to deliver its critical business services through any disruption, whether from cyber attack, IT failure, third-party failure, natural disaster, or pandemic. Broader than business continuity (which assumes a single bank restoring service after a single event) and broader than cyber (which addresses only adversarial threats). Codified in BCBS d516 (March 2021) and given prescriptive form in UK PRA SS1/21 and the FCA's PS21/3. The operational concept rests on identifying important business services, setting impact tolerances, mapping dependencies, and scenario testing.
Model risk
The risk of loss from incorrect or misused model outputs. Recognised as an operational risk sub-category since the Federal Reserve's SR 11-7 (2011); the current UK standard is PRA SS1/23 (effective May 2024). Directly relevant to actuaries: any material PD, LGD, EAD, IFRS 9, capital, or pricing model falls within scope. Requirements include model inventory and identification, materiality assessment, governance, independent validation, ongoing monitoring, and a documented model risk appetite. Conceptually related to but distinct from operational risk: model risk events that produce realised losses also feed the operational risk loss database.
Third-party and outsourcing risk
The risk that disruption, failure, or compromise at a third-party provider results in operational loss to the bank. Includes outsourcing of business processes (KYC vendors, transaction monitoring, model validation consultants), technology infrastructure (cloud providers, payment networks, identity systems), and software supply chains (open-source dependencies, vendor-supplied software). Modern banks are deeply dependent on third parties, and the supervisory framework (EBA Guidelines on outsourcing 2019, PRA SS2/21, EU DORA 2025) has tightened substantially. Cloud concentration risk is the dependency of greatest current supervisory concern.

The relationship between these sub-categories and the broader operational risk framework is one of overlapping perspectives rather than disjoint categories. A ransomware attack delivered through a compromised third-party vendor that disrupts a critical service and includes a fraudulent data exfiltration is simultaneously cyber risk, third-party risk, an operational resilience failure, and potentially a model risk event (if the affected systems include credit or pricing models). Modern operational risk programmes treat all four together, with shared governance, shared incident management, and shared remediation. The Basel category remains the regulatory tagging for loss reporting; the sub-categories are the management framework.

Regulatory frameworks

Jurisdiction Body Framework
Global OECD Multilaterally agreed guidelines for corporate governance and consumer financial protection; voluntary but influential as a baseline for national regulators.
United Kingdom FCA (launched 2013) Conduct risk placed at the centre of its supervisory agenda; Senior Managers and Certification Regime (2016) imposing personal accountability; ongoing thematic reviews and enforcement.
South Africa National Treasury / FSCA Conduct of Financial Institutions (CoFI) Bill incorporating the Treating Customers Fairly (TCF) principles; the FSCA Conduct Standard for Banks; market conduct supervision separated from prudential supervision (which sits with the Prudential Authority at SARB).

South Africa's split between the Prudential Authority (within SARB) for prudential supervision and the FSCA for conduct supervision implements the "twin peaks" model. The FSCA's mandate covers all financial institutions, not just banks, and it has its own Conduct Standard for Banks that operates alongside the prudential requirements of the Banks Act. For an ASSA F107 candidate, the key fact is that the conduct framework in South Africa is not embedded within the Basel capital rules but sits alongside them, with the FSCA setting standards on outcomes and treatment and the PA setting standards on capital and risk management.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The boundary problem is unavoidable

In practice, many significant operational risk events straddle two or more of the seven categories. A cyber attack that breaches customer data and enables subsequent fraud is simultaneously external fraud (Level 2, systems security) and business disruption and system failures. A rogue trader who deliberately mis-marks positions, hides losses in suspense accounts, and submits false reports to the regulator simultaneously triggers internal fraud, clients/products, and execution/delivery. The Basel framework requires each loss event to be assigned to a single primary category, which forces a judgement call. That call is not trivial: the chosen category determines which AMA cell the loss feeds into, which affects the frequency and severity distributions for that cell, which affects the tail estimate and ultimately the capital charge. Miscategorisation is a capital error, not a documentation error.

Conduct risk and Pillar 2 are where the real capital exposure sits

For large UK and EU banks, the PPI settlement demonstrated that the largest single operational risk loss events of the last two decades were conduct events: mis-selling of payment protection insurance (over £50 billion in industry redress), interest rate hedging products (over £2 billion), and packaged bank accounts (around £1 billion). None of this was anticipated in the Pillar 1 AMA capital of UK banks prior to 2010, partly because the AMA data sets were too short to capture an event of this scale, and partly because the regulatory recognition of conduct as a capital-relevant concern lagged the actual exposure. PRA Pillar 2A add-ons for conduct are now a routine line item in the SREP letter for any UK bank with a material retail franchise. The pattern is the same in the EU.

Strategic risk and reputational risk are excluded by design

Basel II explicitly excludes strategic risk and reputational risk from the operational risk definition. Reputational risk is excluded because it is seen as a consequence rather than a primary risk category: a fraud (operational) or a mis-selling scandal (also operational) causes reputational damage, but the reputational damage is a secondary effect. Strategic risk (the risk that the business model itself fails) is excluded because it is not amenable to the same kind of capital quantification. For exam purposes, a question that describes a bank losing market share because customers no longer trust its brand is describing a reputational consequence, not an operational risk event, even if the underlying cause was operational. The Pillar 1 capital formula does not cover the secondary damage. Pillar 2 processes are where supervisors are expected to address it.

The South African layer for ASSA candidates

South Africa's twin peaks model, fully implemented from 1 April 2018, splits prudential supervision (Prudential Authority within SARB) from market conduct supervision (FSCA). For banks, this means that the Basel capital framework continues to apply through the PA, while conduct standards are set and enforced separately by the FSCA. The CoFI Bill, when enacted, will consolidate conduct standards across the entire financial sector under a single act. For an F107 candidate, two facts are directly examinable: that conduct supervision in South Africa is institutionally separate from prudential supervision, and that the CoFI framework imports the Treating Customers Fairly principles previously embedded in supervisory guidance.

Watch out The Basel II definition of operational risk has not changed since 2004, but the practice has evolved substantially. Conduct risk is now a recognised supervisory category in its own right in most jurisdictions, even though it is technically subsumed within category 4 of the Basel taxonomy. Cyber risk is now a supervisory priority in its own right (BCBS published "Principles for operational resilience" in 2021), even though it falls within Basel categories 2 and 6. Climate risk is being treated as a Pillar 2A concern across multiple jurisdictions. The taxonomy is stable; the supervisory emphasis on particular sub-categories is not. An exam answer that recites the Basel definition without acknowledging the modern supervisory emphasis will score lower than one that does both.

Test yourself

  1. State the Basel II definition of operational risk in full. Which two risk categories does the definition explicitly exclude, and why?
  2. Name the four sources of operational risk and give two examples of loss events for each.
  3. List the seven Basel II Level 1 loss event categories. Which one captures rogue trading? Which one captures cyber attacks by an external party? Which one captures mis-selling of insurance products to retail customers?
  4. Distinguish the four sources from the seven event categories. Why does each framework exist, and which is the one used for regulatory loss data collection?
  5. The 1995 Barings collapse, the 2002 Allied Irish Banks loss, the 2012 Knight Capital loss, and the UK PPI scandal each illustrate one of the four sources of operational risk. Match each to its primary source.
  6. State the FCA's definition of conduct risk. What are the three driver categories the FCA identifies, and what types of behaviour does it work to eradicate?
  7. Why does Basel II exclude reputational risk from the operational risk definition? Where is reputational risk addressed in the modern supervisory framework?
  8. Describe the South African twin peaks model. Which body supervises prudential risk at banks, and which supervises conduct risk? How does the CoFI Bill fit in?
  9. Why does the choice of Level 1 event category matter for AMA capital? Give an example of a loss event that could plausibly be classified into more than one category.
  10. How does conduct risk connect to operational risk capital? Give one example of a conduct event whose redress costs exceeded the bank's Pillar 1 operational risk capital at the time the event occurred.
  11. Name the four modern operational risk sub-categories identified in this lecture. For each, identify the Basel category or categories it sits within and the principal supervisory framework that governs it.
  12. Distinguish cyber risk from general IT risk. Why is the distinction supervisorily significant, and what kinds of control does cyber risk specifically require that general IT risk does not?
