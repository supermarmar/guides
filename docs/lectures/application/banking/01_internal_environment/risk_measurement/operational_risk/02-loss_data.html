Operational risk: loss data, measurement, and modelling

Once operational risk is defined and events classified, the next question is how to quantify it. Quantification requires loss data: records of every operational risk event above a threshold, tagged with event category, business line, dates, gross loss, and recoveries. This lecture covers the mechanics of an operational risk loss database, the statistical distributions that describe frequency and severity, the conceptual framework of the Loss Distribution Approach, and the eight Basel II business lines that, together with the seven event categories from Lecture 1, form the 7 × 8 grid at the heart of the AMA capital model.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Operational risk capital under the Advanced Measurement Approach (Lecture 3) and the Internal Loss Multiplier in the Standardised Measurement Approach (Lecture 4) both depend on the same primary input: the bank's internal operational risk loss database. A loss database records every operational risk event above a defined collection threshold, with the gross loss amount, the date or dates of the event, the event category and business line, any recoveries, and a description sufficient to allow categorisation review. The standard quantitative framework is the Loss Distribution Approach (LDA): model frequency separately from severity within each cell of the 7 × 8 event-category-by-business-line grid, combine the two via Monte Carlo simulation to produce an aggregate loss distribution per cell, and read the 99.9 per cent value-at-risk off the aggregate distribution to give the capital charge. Frequency is conventionally Poisson; severity is conventionally lognormal or Pareto, with extreme-value theory used for the tail.

Feynman test Imagine you run an insurance company that insures banks against operational risk. To set the premium, you need to answer two questions for each type of event: how often does it happen, and when it happens, how big is it? Frequency and severity, modelled separately, then convolved to get the total annual loss distribution. Capital is the 99.9 percentile of that distribution, minus the expected loss. The mathematics is exactly the same as in non-life insurance: you have a Poisson number of claims per year, each of an uncertain severity, and you want the right tail of the aggregate. The only difference is that the bank is its own insurer, and the regulator is checking the maths.
What is beyond the source The wiki note describes the loss database mechanics, the distribution assumptions, and the seven event categories and eight business lines. This lecture adds: a worked LDA example showing how Poisson frequency and lognormal severity combine to produce a heavy-tailed aggregate; the distinction between accounting, discovery, and occurrence dates and why it matters for backtesting; the practical problem of survivorship bias in external loss data consortia (banks that have failed are not represented); the rationale for credibility weighting between internal and external data; and a note on why extreme value theory (peaks-over-threshold) is the standard tail-estimation technique for operational risk despite its conceptual difficulty.
Step 2

Why this matters

Data quality determines capital quality. The Advanced Measurement Approach only works if the loss database is complete, consistently categorised, long enough to estimate tail probabilities, and supplemented by external data and scenario analysis where the internal data is too thin. Banks that moved to AMA status in the late 2000s discovered three structural problems with their loss data, all of which contributed to the eventual abolition of the AMA in Basel III.

The data is too short to reach the tail. A bank with ten years of loss data has perhaps a few hundred losses above the collection threshold and a handful of losses in the truly catastrophic range. Estimating the 99.9 per cent quantile of an aggregate loss distribution from a sample that contains, at best, a couple of observations in its top decile is statistically heroic. The empirical distribution is essentially uninformative about the tail, which is exactly the region that drives capital. This is why external data and scenario analysis are mandatory under the AMA: the regulator forces banks to look outside their own experience because their own experience is not enough.

External data has survivorship bias. Consortium databases (ORX is the canonical one for major banks) pool loss data across member institutions, allowing each bank to benchmark its own losses against the wider industry. But banks that have failed because of operational risk events (Barings, Lehman, the various pre-2008 thrifts in the US) are typically not represented in the consortia: the consortia were set up by surviving banks. The most extreme operational losses are therefore systematically underrepresented in the data set that is supposed to inform the tail.

Categorisation choices drive capital. An event classified as internal fraud feeds the internal fraud cell of the LDA grid; an event classified as execution and delivery feeds a different cell with different frequency and severity distributions. The two cells produce different tail estimates, and the difference can be material. Banks with discretion over categorisation have an incentive to push events towards cells with lower implied capital. The AMA put this discretion in the bank's hands, with predictable results: capital variability across banks with apparently similar risk profiles was high, comparability was low, and supervisors found themselves unable to challenge bank-specific model choices in any robust way.

These three problems collectively are why the Basel III Standardised Measurement Approach (Lecture 4) reduced reliance on internal modelling for the tail and pinned capital primarily to a financial statement proxy (the Business Indicator). The internal loss data still matters under the SMA, but only as a multiplier on the BIC rather than as the entire input to a tail estimate. Internal loss data has not disappeared as a topic. It has been re-purposed.

Step 3

The mental model

The Loss Distribution Approach is the standard quantitative framework for operational risk and is conceptually identical to the collective risk model used in non-life insurance. For each cell of the 7 × 8 grid (event category by business line), the bank specifies a frequency distribution (number of losses per year) and a severity distribution (size of each loss). The aggregate annual loss for that cell is the sum of the random number of independent severity draws. Repeating across all cells, summing (with appropriate correlation), and reading the 99.9 per cent quantile gives total operational risk capital.

Three operational consequences of this framework are worth noting. First, the granularity of the cells matters: 56 cells with a few hundred losses between them leaves most cells empty or sparsely populated, forcing reliance on external data or scenario analysis to populate them. Second, the choice of severity distribution dominates the tail: lognormal and Pareto give different 99.9 percentiles even with identical fit to the body of the distribution. Third, the correlation assumption across cells matters: assuming perfect correlation (sum the cell VaRs) is conservative; assuming independence (sum of variances rather than means) is aggressive; supervisors typically prefer something between the two.

Step 4

The detail

Loss database concepts

An operational risk loss database is a structured record of every operational risk event whose gross loss exceeds the bank's defined collection threshold. The minimum data elements are the event description, the event category (one of seven), the business line (one of eight), the dates of the event, the gross loss amount, any recoveries (direct from the cause, from insurance, or from other sources), and the net loss. Banks operating internationally typically collect substantially more: the responsible business unit, the legal entity, the cause of the event in narrative form, the control failures identified, and the remediation actions taken.

Gross loss
The total economic loss caused by an operational risk event, before any recoveries. Includes direct costs (write-offs, settlements, restitution payments) and indirect costs (legal fees, regulatory fines, external advisory costs). Excludes opportunity costs, foregone revenue, and reputational damage (these are not directly quantifiable). Gross loss is the primary input to the severity distribution in the LDA.
Net loss
Gross loss less recoveries. Recoveries include amounts recovered from the original cause (e.g. funds clawed back from a fraudster), amounts recovered under insurance policies, and amounts recovered from other parties (e.g. counterparties who reimbursed under contractual indemnities). Net loss is the figure that affects the bank's P&L. Net loss is normally what is reported under the SMA's Loss Component, although banks must also retain the gross figure for governance and supervisory review.
Loss collection threshold
The minimum gross loss amount above which an event must be recorded in the loss database. Set by each bank but subject to supervisory review. In South Africa, the regulatory reporting threshold has historically been R10,000. Lower thresholds give more complete data but increase the recording burden; higher thresholds may exclude the body of the distribution and bias the LDA estimation. Most large banks use thresholds in the range of €10,000 to €50,000 for primary data collection and a lower threshold (or zero) for monitoring purposes.

Three dates need to be distinguished. The occurrence date is when the underlying event happened (the rogue trade was executed, the cyber breach occurred, the system failed). The discovery date is when the bank became aware of the event. The accounting date is when the loss was recognised in the P&L. For events with rapid discovery (a settlement error noticed the next morning), all three are close. For latent events (a fraud running for years before detection, a mis-selling pattern only identified after a regulatory thematic review), they can be separated by years. The standard practice for AMA modelling is to use the occurrence date for frequency analysis (to align the event with the macroeconomic and business environment that produced it) and the accounting date for severity (to align with the eventual cost).

Grouping of related events is a recurring practical question. A single rogue trader executing many unauthorised trades over time, a single misselling pattern affecting many customers, or a single system outage causing many transaction failures, can be recorded as one event (with a single gross loss) or as many. The Basel guidance is to group events that share a single root cause, but the boundary is judgemental. Banks typically have a written policy that defines, for each event category, the criteria for grouping. The PPI episode, for example, was recorded by some UK banks as a single, multi-year event and by others as many smaller events broken down by customer or product.

Distribution assumptions

The LDA separates loss generation into a frequency component (how often events occur) and a severity component (how large each event is). Conditional on the number of events, the individual losses are typically assumed independent of each other and of the count. The aggregate annual loss for a cell is then:

$$S = \sum_{i=1}^{N} X_i, \quad N \sim \text{Frequency distribution}, \quad X_i \sim \text{Severity distribution}$$

The standard frequency distribution is the Poisson. The standard severity distribution is the lognormal for the body of the distribution and a Pareto or generalised Pareto (via peaks-over-threshold extreme value theory) for the tail.

Frequency: Poisson
N ~ Poisson(λ), where λ is the expected number of events per year. The Poisson has a single parameter and is the standard model for counts of independent events occurring at a constant rate. The mean equals the variance: a property that empirical data often violates (operational risk losses exhibit clustering, with the variance exceeding the mean). The negative binomial distribution is used as an alternative when over-dispersion is material.
Severity: lognormal
X ~ Lognormal(μ, σ): ln(X) is normally distributed. The lognormal has a finite mean but a heavier right tail than the normal, making it a natural fit for loss sizes. It is the conventional default for the body of the severity distribution. It fits routine losses well but may underestimate the truly extreme losses.
Severity tail: Pareto / EVT
Above a threshold u, the conditional distribution of (X − u | X > u) is approximated by a generalised Pareto distribution. This is the peaks-over-threshold formulation of extreme value theory: a result that holds asymptotically for a wide class of underlying distributions, justifying the use of GPD for the tail even when the body is fitted by something else (typically lognormal). The shape parameter ξ controls the heaviness of the tail; ξ > 0 indicates a fat tail (no upper bound on the loss).

Goodness-of-fit tests routinely applied to the severity distribution include the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (based on the maximum vertical distance between empirical and fitted CDFs), the Anderson-Darling test (more sensitive to the tail), the chi-square test (binned data), and the Shapiro-Wilk test (for normality after a log transformation). For frequency, the chi-square test is the standard, with overdispersion diagnostics to flag departures from Poisson.

The eight Basel II business lines

The seven event categories from Lecture 1 form one axis of the LDA grid. The eight Basel II business lines form the other. Each Level 1 business line is subdivided into Level 2 sub-lines and Level 3 activity examples. The eight Level 1 business lines also carry the beta factors used in the Standardised Approach (Lecture 3) and were one of the targets of the Basel III SMA review (the SMA collapses the eight business lines into a single Business Indicator).

Business line Sub-categories Activity examples
Corporate finance Corporate finance; municipal/government finance; merchant banking; advisory services M&A, underwriting, privatisations, securitisation, debt and equity syndications, IPOs
Trading and sales Sales; market-making; proprietary positions; treasury Fixed income, equity, FX, commodities, credit, funding, lending and repos, brokerage
Retail banking Retail banking; private banking; card services Retail and private lending and deposits, banking services, trust and estates, card products
Commercial banking Commercial banking Project finance, real estate, export finance, trade finance, factoring, leasing, guarantees
Payment and settlement External clients Payments and collections, funds transfer, clearing, and settlement
Agency services Custody; corporate agency; corporate trust Escrow, depository receipts, securities lending, issuer and paying agents
Asset management Discretionary fund management; non-discretionary fund management Pooled, segregated, retail, institutional, closed, open, private equity
Retail brokerage Retail brokerage Execution and full-service brokerage

Allocation of gross income across business lines is non-trivial for banks whose income statement does not naturally produce a business-line split. Basel II provided allocation principles based on standard accounting categorisations, but allowed banks discretion in operationalising them. This was another source of variability across banks under TSA, AMA, and (initially) the SMA.

Data integration: external data, scenario analysis, BEICFs

Internal loss data alone is insufficient for capital estimation: the tail is too sparse. Basel II therefore requires the AMA to combine four data inputs.

Internal loss data
The bank's own loss database, with at least 5 years of history (3 when first moving to AMA). The primary input for the body of the severity distribution and for the frequency parameters. Insufficient on its own for the tail.
External loss data
Industry data, either from a consortium (ORX is the canonical one for major banks) or from public sources (regulatory disclosures, news reports, vendor compilations). Provides observations of severe losses that the individual bank has not experienced. Requires scaling adjustments to reflect differences in business size, business mix, and reporting threshold across the data sources.
Scenario analysis
Expert assessment of the frequency and severity of low-probability, high-impact events that have not yet been observed in internal or external data. Typically conducted as a structured workshop with risk owners, control owners, and central operational risk function. Extends the loss distributions beyond actual experience, particularly into the tail. The conceptual analogue is the actuarial use of catastrophe scenarios in insurance.
Business Environment and Internal Control Factors (BEICFs)
Forward-looking indicators of operational risk derived from KRIs, KPIs, and KCIs (covered in Lecture 3). Used to adjust the loss distributions to reflect the bank's current control environment rather than its historical losses. BEICFs are the most controversial of the four inputs because their quantitative link to capital is the most tenuous; they were one of the practices supervisors found most variable across banks.

Integration of these four inputs is conventionally done via Bayesian inference or credibility models. Bayesian inference treats external data and scenario analysis as a prior, internal loss data as the likelihood, and produces a posterior that combines both. Credibility models (familiar from non-life insurance) place a weight on internal data that increases with the number of observations, with the complement of the weight applied to an external prior. The two approaches are mathematically equivalent in particular cases; the choice between them is more about reporting and governance than about substance.

Insurance can be recognised as a mitigant against AMA capital up to a maximum of 20 per cent of the AMA capital charge. The insurance must meet specific conditions (high-quality insurer, residual term, payment certainty), and the recognition is subject to supervisory approval. The 20 per cent cap exists because regulators were concerned about banks over-relying on insurance contracts whose payment behaviour under stress is uncertain.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Internal loss data is a leading indicator and a lagging indicator simultaneously

An internal loss database is paradoxical. Each individual loss event is a lagging indicator: by the time the loss is recorded, the control failure has already happened and the money has already gone. But the pattern of losses across categories, business lines, and time is a leading indicator of where the next loss will come from: a cluster of small failed-reconciliation events in a specific business line is often the precursor of a large process-failure event in the same business line. The good operational risk function reads the loss database not as a record of past failures but as a diagnostic of the current control environment. The poor operational risk function reads it as a compliance exercise.

External data scaling is unsolved

Scaling a €500 million fraud loss observed at a global bank to its implication for a domestic mid-market bank is one of the hardest problems in operational risk modelling. The conventional approach scales by gross income or by business volume, but neither scaling is theoretically grounded: there is no reason to believe that a rogue trader at a bank with twice the income produces twice the loss. The Basel guidance is general (banks must establish a documented scaling methodology), and supervisors have not converged on a single method. This is one of the reasons that external data is typically used qualitatively (to identify event types and ranges) rather than quantitatively (to plug numbers into the distributions).

Scenario analysis is where modellers and risk owners meet

Done well, scenario analysis is the most powerful of the four AMA inputs: it brings together the people who actually run the controls, the people who model the risk, and the senior management who own the appetite. Done badly, it is a structured way of writing down whatever number the participants think the regulator wants to see. The difference between well and badly done is mostly about facilitation: who runs the workshop, how challenging questions are received, and whether dissenting views are documented or suppressed. From an actuarial perspective, scenario analysis sits in the same conceptual space as expert judgement in IFRS 9 forward-looking information: the same governance issues, the same risks of unconscious bias, the same need for documented challenge.

The granularity question never has a clean answer

The 7 × 8 grid produces 56 cells. A bank with 10,000 loss events spread across 56 cells averages 180 losses per cell, but the actual distribution is highly uneven: large cells (retail banking × execution and delivery) might have thousands of small losses, while small cells (corporate finance × employment practices) might have a handful. Modellers face an unavoidable trade-off: finer granularity gives more risk-sensitive capital but produces noisier estimates per cell; coarser granularity gives more stable estimates but obscures risk concentrations. The standard practice is to model at the 56-cell level where data permits, and to aggregate to coarser groupings (often 4 × 4 or 3 × 3) where data does not.

Watch out The collective risk model (Poisson frequency, independent severity) assumes that within each cell, the individual loss events are independent and identically distributed. This is a strong assumption that empirical data routinely violates: losses cluster in time (a single root cause can produce many events), and severity often depends on the business environment (losses are larger during periods of operational stress). Sophisticated AMA implementations relaxed these assumptions, typically via a copula on the cell-level aggregates rather than at the event level. The SMA sidesteps the problem by using the 15× annual average rather than a full LDA simulation, which is one of the reasons the SMA produces more comparable capital across banks: it has fewer modelling choices to argue about.
