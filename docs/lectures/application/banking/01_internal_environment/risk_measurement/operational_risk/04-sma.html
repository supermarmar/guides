Operational risk capital: Basel III Standardised Measurement Approach

BCBS d424, the Basel III finalisation document of December 2017, abolished the BIA, TSA, and AMA and replaced them with a single Standardised Measurement Approach (SMA) effective from 1 January 2023. The SMA has two components: a Business Indicator Component (BIC) computed from financial statement data, and an Internal Loss Multiplier (ILM) that adjusts the BIC up or down based on the bank's actual loss experience over the preceding ten years. The construction is risk-sensitive in a way the BIA never was, comparable across banks in a way the AMA never was, and simple enough to compute that the gaming problem is largely solved.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The Standardised Measurement Approach (SMA) computes operational risk capital in two stages. First, the Business Indicator (BI) is calculated from three-year averages of three financial statement components: an interest, leases and dividends component (ILDC), a services component (SC), and a financial component (FC). The BI is the SMA's replacement for gross income; it captures the scale of operations more robustly across different business models. Second, marginal coefficients (12%, 15%, 18%) are applied to BI tranches to produce the Business Indicator Component (BIC): a higher rate applies to the larger banks. The capital charge is then BIC multiplied by the Internal Loss Multiplier (ILM), which uses the bank's ten-year average of internal operational losses to scale capital up (for banks with high losses relative to BIC) or down (for banks with low losses). Small banks in Bucket 1 (BI ≤ €1bn) use ILM = 1 by default, sidestepping the loss-data requirement for institutions that lack the data infrastructure to support it.

Feynman test Operational risk capital under the SMA is two numbers multiplied together. The first number says "how big is this bank, accounting for its business mix?" — that is the BIC, a tariff on the bank's income and balance sheet that rises with size in steps. The second number says "how has this bank actually performed on operational risk over the last decade?" — that is the ILM, a multiplier that pushes capital above or below the BIC based on real losses. Multiply the two and you have the SMA capital charge. Compared to the AMA, which tried to model the 1-in-1,000 tail directly, the SMA admits that the tail cannot be modelled reliably and instead uses the actual loss experience as a fairly blunt scaling. Less precise in principle, more comparable in practice.
What is beyond the source The wiki note gives the SMA formulas and the marginal coefficient table. This lecture adds: the policy rationale for replacing gross income with the three-part BI (gross income failed to differentiate retail-heavy banks from trading-heavy banks of similar size); the rationale for the specific bucket thresholds at €1bn and €30bn (calibrated during the SMA QIS to broadly preserve aggregate industry capital); the derivation of the ILM formula and what its shape means (logarithmic scaling that flattens out for very large or very small loss histories); the data requirements for using the ILM and the supervisory discretion regarding banks that lack ten years of data; and the South African PA's implementation timeline (effective 2023 alongside the European Union and other major jurisdictions).
Step 2

Why this matters

The SMA is the live capital standard for operational risk in every major jurisdiction from 2023 onwards. It replaces three approaches (BIA, TSA, AMA) with one, and it changes the rules of operational risk capital management substantially. Banks that previously relied on AMA to reduce their capital below the BIA or TSA level no longer have that option. Banks that previously relied on the BIA's simplicity at the cost of an unrisk-sensitive capital charge now have a slightly more complex but more accurate formula. The aggregate effect, by design, is to roughly preserve industry capital while improving cross-bank comparability.

The motivation for the change was set out in the BCBS's 2014 and 2016 consultation papers. Four shortcomings of the existing approaches were identified.

Gross income is a poor proxy for operational risk. The empirical relationship between gross income and operational losses is far from linear. A trading-book-heavy bank with €1bn of gross income can have very different operational risk than a retail bank with the same gross income. Gross income also moves with the interest rate environment in ways that have nothing to do with operational risk.

The TSA's business-line betas were largely arbitrary. The 12–18 per cent range was calibrated during QIS exercises in the early 2000s and was not updated subsequently. There is no robust empirical basis for the specific values or the ranking across business lines; the gap between retail banking (12%) and trading and sales (18%) was a policy judgement, not a measured fact.

The AMA produced unjustifiably variable capital. Banks with similar risk profiles produced AMA capital figures differing by factors of three or more. The variability was driven by legitimate but consequential model choices (severity distribution, tail threshold, integration of external data, correlation structure). Comparability across banks, a foundational requirement for a banking standard, was demonstrably absent.

The AMA was expensive to implement and to supervise. Major banks invested years and substantial resources to obtain AMA approval, and supervisors spent comparable resources reviewing the models. The capital benefit, where it existed, was often modest relative to the implementation cost. The SMA is intended to be cheaper for both banks and supervisors.

For an ASSA F107 candidate, the SMA is the current regime. The Basel II approaches in Lecture 3 are foundational and remain examinable, but the SMA is the formula every bank now applies. Knowing the BI components, the marginal coefficient table, and the ILM formula is the direct exam requirement.

Step 3

The mental model

The SMA is a four-step calculation. Each step uses inputs that are either directly available from the bank's financial statements (BI) or from its operational risk loss database (LC). There is no distribution fitting, no Monte Carlo simulation, no tail estimation. The output is deterministic given the inputs.

Two design features of the SMA deserve attention up front. First, the marginal coefficient structure in step 2 is progressive: a larger bank pays a higher average rate on its BI than a smaller bank, on the theory that operational risk does not scale linearly with size. Second, the ILM in step 3 uses a logarithmic functional form, which flattens the response of capital to loss experience at the extremes. A bank with very low losses gets capital below BIC but with a floor; a bank with very high losses gets capital above BIC but the multiplier rises slowly rather than exploding. The shape was designed to avoid the AMA's tail-volatility problem while still providing some risk sensitivity.

Step 4

The detail

Step 1: the Business Indicator

The Business Indicator (BI) replaces gross income as the SMA's measure of bank scale. It has three components, each a three-year average of specific financial statement line items. The construction is designed to capture operations across very different business models: retail banks with stable net interest income, trading-heavy banks with volatile P&L, and fee-driven asset managers all receive a BI that better reflects their operational risk than gross income would.

Business Indicator (BI)
A financial-statement-based proxy for operational risk equal to the sum of the ILDC, SC, and FC components. Each component is computed as a three-year average. The BI replaces gross income as the input to the marginal coefficient table.
Interest, Leases and Dividend Component (ILDC)
Captures the bank's interest, lease, and dividend earnings, bounded above by an asset-based cap to prevent perverse incentives in low-rate environments. The cap is 3.5% of interest-earning assets, designed to ensure that a bank with a thin net interest margin does not get an artificially low BI.
$$\text{ILDC} = \min\!\left(\overline{|II - IE|},\; 0.035 \cdot \overline{IEA}\right) + \overline{DI}$$

where II is interest income, IE is interest expense, IEA is interest earning assets, DI is dividend income, and the overbars denote three-year averages.

Services Component (SC)
Captures fee, commission, and other operating income and expense. Uses the larger of income and expense in each pairing to ensure that fee-driven business models (where fee expense to other parties is large) are captured at the appropriate scale.
$$\text{SC} = \max\!\left(\overline{OOI},\; \overline{OOE}\right) + \max\!\left(\overline{FI},\; \overline{FE}\right)$$

where OOI / OOE are other operating income and expense and FI / FE are fee and commission income and expense.

Financial Component (FC)
Captures the absolute value of net P&L from the trading book and the banking book. The absolute value treatment ensures that a loss-making trading book does not reduce the bank's BI: it contributes the same operational risk indicator as a profit-making book of the same size.
$$\text{FC} = \overline{|NPLT|} + \overline{|NPLB|}$$

where NPLT and NPLB are net P&L for the trading book and the banking book respectively.

$$\text{BI} = \text{ILDC} + \text{SC} + \text{FC}$$

Step 2: the Business Indicator Component

Business Indicator Component (BIC)
The BI multiplied by marginal coefficients applied tranche by tranche. Three buckets, each with a fixed coefficient: Bucket 1 (BI ≤ €1bn) at 12%, Bucket 2 (€1bn < BI ≤ €30bn) at 15%, Bucket 3 (BI > €30bn) at 18%. The marginal structure means a bank in Bucket 3 still pays 12% on its first €1bn of BI, not 18%.
Bucket BI range Marginal coefficient
1≤ €1bn12%
2€1bn < BI ≤ €30bn15%
3> €30bn18%

Worked example: BIC for a Bucket 3 bank

A bank has BI = €35bn. The marginal coefficients are applied tranche by tranche:

BIC = (€1bn × 12%) + (€29bn × 15%) + (€5bn × 18%)
BIC = €0.12bn + €4.35bn + €0.90bn
BIC = €5.37bn

The average rate of 5.37 / 35 ≈ 15.3% sits between the marginal rates of 15% and 18%, exactly as the marginal structure intends. A pure-tariff comparison: a bank with BI of exactly €30bn would have BIC = 0.12 + 4.35 = €4.47bn, an average rate of 14.9%. The marginal step from €30bn to €35bn adds €5bn × 18% = €0.90bn of BIC, the largest marginal increment in the schedule.

Step 3: the Internal Loss Multiplier

Loss Component (LC)
15 times the ten-year average of the bank's net annual operational losses. The factor of 15 calibrates the LC against the BIC: a bank with average losses equal to 1/15 of BIC has LC = BIC and therefore ILM = 1. The ten-year averaging window smooths short-term fluctuations and gives the bank credit for sustained good loss performance.
$$\text{LC} = 15 \cdot \overline{\text{Annual Internal Losses (10yr)}}$$
Internal Loss Multiplier (ILM)
A scaling factor based on the ratio LC / BIC, with a logarithmic functional form that keeps the multiplier bounded for extreme ratios. ILM = 1 when LC = BIC. The 0.8 exponent and the e − 1 offset are calibration choices that shape the response curve.
$$\text{ILM} = \ln\!\left(\mathrm{e} - 1 + \left(\frac{\text{LC}}{\text{BIC}}\right)^{0.8}\right)$$

To see why ILM = 1 when LC = BIC, set LC / BIC = 1: then ILM = ln(e − 1 + 1) = ln(e) = 1. Below that ratio, the multiplier falls below 1; above it, rises above 1. The shape of the curve is gentle: doubling LC relative to BIC does not double ILM, it raises it from 1 to roughly 1.27. Halving LC relative to BIC reduces ILM from 1 to roughly 0.79. The logarithmic damping is deliberate: it prevents the SMA from producing wildly different capital for banks that happen to have a single outlier loss event in the ten-year window.

Condition ILM Interpretation
LC > BICILM > 1Actual loss experience worse than BI implies. Capital exceeds BIC.
LC = BICILM = 1Loss experience consistent with BI. Capital equals BIC.
LC < BICILM < 1Actual loss experience better than BI implies. Capital below BIC.

Step 4: the capital requirement

$$K_{\text{SMA}} = \text{BIC} \cdot \text{ILM}, \qquad \text{RWA} = 12.5 \cdot K_{\text{SMA}}$$

The RWA conversion multiplies capital by 12.5 (the reciprocal of the 8 per cent minimum total capital ratio) to produce a notional risk-weighted asset amount that is then added to credit and market risk RWA for the bank's headline capital ratios.

Data requirements for the ILM

Loss history available Treatment
10 or more years Full ten-year average used as the basis of LC.
5 to 9 years Available years used; supervisors may require additional scenario data to make up the shortfall.
Less than 5 years ILM set to 1 by default, unless the supervisor allows the bank to use available data where it would result in ILM > 1 and the data is representative.
Bucket 1 (BI ≤ €1bn) ILM is set to 1 by default; banks may opt in to using internal loss data with supervisory consent, but most do not.

The asymmetric treatment of banks with limited data (ILM = 1 unless the data would push capital up) reflects a regulatory choice: supervisors do not want banks to benefit from incomplete loss data, but they will accept incomplete data when it supports a higher capital charge. This is the prudent floor that the SMA imposes on small or newly-AMA-graduated institutions.

Worked example: an SMA capital calculation

Worked example: SMA capital for a mid-size bank

A bank has:

BI = €5bn (averaged across the past three years)
Net annual operational losses, ten-year average = €120m

Step 1: BIC. The bank is in Bucket 2 (€1bn < BI ≤ €30bn).
BIC = (€1bn × 12%) + (€4bn × 15%) = €0.12bn + €0.60bn = €0.72bn

Step 2: LC.
LC = 15 × €0.12bn = €1.80bn

Step 3: ILM.
LC / BIC = 1.80 / 0.72 = 2.5
ILM = ln(e − 1 + 2.5^0.8) = ln(1.718 + 2.083) = ln(3.801) ≈ 1.335

Step 4: K and RWA.
K_SMA = €0.72bn × 1.335 ≈ €0.961bn
RWA = €0.961bn × 12.5 ≈ €12.0bn

The bank's loss experience (LC = €1.80bn) is well above its BIC (€0.72bn), reflecting a heavier-than-average operational risk burden. The ILM of 1.335 raises capital above BIC by about a third. A bank with the same BI but average losses of only €30m would have LC = €0.45bn, LC/BIC = 0.625, ILM = ln(1.718 + 0.625^0.8) = ln(1.718 + 0.690) = ln(2.408) ≈ 0.879, and capital of approximately €0.633bn: about €330m less than the high-loss bank.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The SMA is backward-looking by design, and that is its weakness

The ILM uses ten years of loss data. A bank that experienced a major operational risk event nine years ago will continue to be penalised in its capital for that event for another year, even if the underlying control weakness was fixed within months. Conversely, a bank that has not yet had a major event but is operating with weakened controls will pay capital based on its (currently good) loss history, even though the next major event is plausibly imminent. The AMA tried to solve this through scenario analysis and BEICFs; the SMA does not. The cost of this simplicity is that the SMA's risk sensitivity is concentrated in the past rather than projecting forward.

The BI is better than gross income but is still an income statement proxy

Operational risk is not, conceptually, a function of income. It is a function of the bank's processes, systems, and people: the inputs to operations, not the outputs of operations. The BI is an improvement on gross income because it is more robust across different business models, but it is still an income statement number. Two banks with identical BIs but very different control environments will, in year zero, pay the same BIC; the ILM will only differentiate them after years of loss experience accumulates. This is one of the reasons that Pillar 2 capital add-ons for operational risk and conduct risk remain material for major banks: the Pillar 1 SMA does not capture forward-looking exposure.

Bucket 1 banks are effectively on a flat 12 per cent tariff

The decision to set ILM = 1 by default for Bucket 1 banks is a proportionality choice. Banks with BI ≤ €1bn typically lack the loss data infrastructure to compute a meaningful ten-year LC, and the supervisory effort to validate that infrastructure would be disproportionate. The cost of this choice is that small banks have no capital incentive to invest in operational risk management beyond the basic supervisory expectations: their capital is fixed at 12 per cent of BI regardless of their actual losses. For most small banks this is acceptable because their operational risk exposures are simple and their losses are routine. For atypical small banks (a small trading-heavy bank, for instance), the flat tariff may understate their capital need.

The South African PA implementation aligns with international timing

The South African Prudential Authority implemented the SMA effective from 1 January 2023, aligned with the European Union and most other major jurisdictions. The PA's Banks Act implementation does not modify the SMA formulas; it uses the BCBS d424 specification directly. South African banks must therefore compute SMA capital exactly as set out above, including the ten-year loss data requirement for Bucket 2 and 3 banks. Implementation details (loss data collection thresholds, classification of business indicators) follow the PA's prudential standards, which mirror the BCBS guidance closely.

Watch out The SMA uses a 10-year loss window, but in the European Union's implementation (CRR2 and CRR3), national supervisors retain discretion to allow shorter windows during transition. Some jurisdictions also retain BIA or TSA on a transitional basis for smaller banks (typically with a "look-through" requirement that SMA capital be no less than what BIA or TSA would produce). For exam purposes, the canonical formulas are as above; for working practice in a specific jurisdiction, always check the local supervisor's implementation. The SARB Prudential Authority and the FSCA have published guidance that aligns with the BCBS approach.

Test yourself

  1. State the four shortcomings of the Basel II operational risk approaches that motivated the SMA.
  2. Define the three components of the Business Indicator (ILDC, SC, FC). For each, identify the financial statement line items used and any caps or floors.
  3. State the BI formula. Why does the SMA use the absolute value of net P&L for the trading and banking books in the FC?
  4. State the BIC marginal coefficient table. Which buckets exist, what are the BI thresholds, and what are the marginal coefficients?
  5. A bank has BI = €20bn. Compute its BIC, treating the marginal coefficients correctly.
  6. State the formula for the Loss Component (LC). What does the factor of 15 do, and why ten years of loss history rather than five?
  7. State the ILM formula. Show algebraically that ILM = 1 when LC = BIC.
  8. Why is the ILM functional form logarithmic? What problem does this functional choice address?
  9. State the SMA capital formula and the RWA conversion. Why is 12.5 used as the RWA multiplier?
  10. How is the ILM treated for banks in Bucket 1, and how is it treated for banks in Buckets 2 and 3 with less than five years of loss history? Explain the asymmetric treatment.
  11. A bank has BI = €5bn and a ten-year average annual loss of €120m. Compute the BIC, the LC, the ILM, and the SMA capital. Compare with a bank of identical BI but with average losses of only €30m.
