Operational risk capital: Basel II approaches (BIA, TSA, AMA)

Basel II (2004) made operational risk a Pillar 1 capital category for the first time. It offered three progressively more risk-sensitive approaches: the Basic Indicator Approach (15 per cent of gross income), the Standardised Approach (eight business lines with beta factors), and the Advanced Measurement Approach (internal models, specifically the Loss Distribution Approach). All three were intended to coexist, with banks progressing up the ladder as their capabilities matured. In practice, the AMA produced too much capital variability and was abolished in 2017; this lecture covers all three as the foundation for understanding what came next.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Basel II offered three approaches to operational risk capital. The Basic Indicator Approach (BIA) charges 15 per cent of the three-year average of positive gross income: a single number with no risk sensitivity, available to any bank without qualifying criteria. The Standardised Approach (TSA) allocates gross income to eight business lines, each with its own beta factor between 12 and 18 per cent, and takes the three-year average of the sum: more granular than the BIA but still tied to gross income as a proxy. The Advanced Measurement Approach (AMA) uses the bank's own Loss Distribution Approach model to compute a 99.9 per cent value-at-risk directly from internal loss data, external data, scenario analysis, and business environment factors: risk-sensitive in principle, but in practice subject to wide variability across banks. All three approaches survived until BCBS d424 abolished them in 2017 in favour of the single Standardised Measurement Approach (Lecture 4).

Feynman test Three ways to set the operational risk capital of a bank. The first treats every bank the same: 15 per cent of income. The second treats every bank by its business mix: a percentage of income that varies between 12 and 18 per cent across eight business activities. The third lets the bank tell the regulator what its own model says: the bank computes a 99.9 per cent VaR from its loss history and pays capital equal to that VaR. Each step up adds risk sensitivity but adds modelling burden and supervisor oversight. The Basel Committee imagined banks climbing the ladder over time. What actually happened was that the top of the ladder turned out to be unstable: bank-specific model choices produced capital figures so different across banks that the top rung was eventually sawn off.
What is beyond the source The wiki note states each approach in formula form. This lecture adds: the rationale for the BIA's 15 per cent (a number chosen during the Basel II calibration QIS exercises, not derived from first principles); the Alternative Standardised Approach (ASA) that some jurisdictions used for retail and commercial banking; a side-by-side worked example showing BIA, TSA, and AMA capital for the same hypothetical bank; the rationale for the 99.9 per cent confidence level and the one-year horizon under AMA; the 20 per cent insurance offset cap and why it existed; the role of KRIs, KPIs, and KCIs in the AMA's qualitative requirements; and the goodness-of-fit tests that supervisors typically expect.
Step 2

Why this matters

The choice of approach was, in principle, a choice the bank made based on its capabilities. In practice, it was a choice with material capital consequences. A bank with low operational risk losses relative to its gross income would find the BIA punishing: 15 per cent of gross income translates to a capital charge that bears no relation to actual operational risk. The same bank under AMA (with well-calibrated loss data and a clean tail estimate) could pay materially less capital. This is exactly why Basel II offered the progression: the framework was meant to reward investment in risk measurement infrastructure. Banks responded as expected. Every major internationally active bank invested in AMA capability through the late 2000s and into the 2010s, and many achieved AMA approval for at least part of their business.

The controversy that followed is the central episode in the post-2004 operational risk regulatory story. As AMA models came online, supervisors began to observe wide variability in the capital outputs of banks with apparently similar risk profiles. Two banks with comparable size, business mix, and loss experience could produce AMA capital estimates differing by a factor of three or more, depending entirely on modelling choices: the severity distribution selected, the threshold for the tail fit, the integration method for external data, the scenario analysis assumptions, and the correlation structure across cells. The supervisory community concluded that the AMA, as implemented, produced regulatory capital that was not comparable across banks. Comparability is one of the foundational requirements of a banking standard. The AMA failed it.

BCBS d424, published in December 2017 as the Basel III finalisation document, abolished the AMA along with the BIA and TSA. From 1 January 2023, a single Standardised Measurement Approach replaced all three (with national supervisors allowing transitional retention of BIA and TSA in some jurisdictions). This lecture covers the three Basel II approaches for two reasons: first, they are the foundation on which the SMA was built and is best understood; and second, they remain testable in their own right under syllabuses that have not yet been fully updated.

For an ASSA candidate, the practical impact is that you must be able to write down the formulas for BIA, TSA, and AMA, explain the qualifying criteria for each, and discuss the policy rationale for the progression and the eventual abolition. The capital formulas themselves are simple. The institutional history around them is what makes them examinable.

Step 3

The mental model

The three Basel II approaches differ along three dimensions: the input data they require, the risk sensitivity they offer, and the qualifying criteria a bank must meet to use them. The progression is one of increasing sophistication: more data, more risk-sensitivity, more supervisory burden.

Dimension BIA TSA AMA
Primary input Aggregate gross income Gross income by business line Internal loss data + external + scenario + BEICF
Capital formula α × 3-yr avg positive GI 3-yr avg of Σ(β × GI) per line 99.9% VaR of LDA aggregate
Key parameter α = 15% β = 12%–18% by business line Distribution fit + correlation
Risk sensitivity None: same rate for all banks Coarse: business mix only High: own loss history drives capital
Qualifying criteria None Sound op risk management, documented loss-data process Full AMA approval: governance, data, model, validation
Insurance offset Not recognised Not recognised Up to 20% of AMA capital
Typical user Small and mid-size banks Mid-size to large banks Large, internationally active banks
Status from 2023 Abolished (retained transitionally in some jurisdictions) Abolished (retained transitionally in some jurisdictions) Abolished by BCBS d424

The progression is monotonic in risk sensitivity but not monotonic in capital. A bank with a clean loss history and disciplined modelling might pay less under AMA than under TSA; a bank with a poor loss history might pay more. This is, of course, the point: the framework was meant to reward genuinely lower risk and penalise genuinely higher risk. What it actually did, in many cases, was reward sophisticated modelling and penalise honest data collection.

Step 4

The detail

Basic Indicator Approach (BIA)

Basic Indicator Approach
Operational risk capital equals 15 per cent (the alpha factor) of the three-year average of positive annual gross income, with negative or zero years excluded from both numerator and denominator. Gross income is defined as net interest income plus net non-interest income, gross of provisions and operating expenses. No qualifying criteria; available to any bank. Intended as the entry-level approach for banks without the data infrastructure to support TSA or AMA.
$$K_{\text{BIA}} = \alpha \cdot \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3} \max(GI_i,\; 0)}{n}$$

where α = 0.15, GI_i is gross income in year i, and n is the number of years in the three-year window where gross income was positive. The exclusion of negative or zero years is asymmetric and produces a perverse incentive: a bank that has a loss-making year does not get capital relief, because the year is dropped from both the numerator and the denominator, leaving the average roughly unchanged. The alpha factor of 15 per cent was calibrated during the Basel II Quantitative Impact Study (QIS) exercises in the early 2000s and has no theoretical foundation; it was chosen to produce an aggregate capital level broadly consistent with industry expectations at the time.

The Standardised Approach (TSA)

The Standardised Approach
Gross income is allocated to the eight Basel II business lines (introduced in Lecture 2), each with its own beta factor between 12 per cent and 18 per cent. Capital is the three-year average of the sum across business lines of beta times gross income, with negative single-year totals allowed to offset positives (the key difference from the BIA). Requires sound operational risk management and a documented loss-data collection process to qualify.
$$K_{\text{TSA}} = \frac{1}{3} \sum_{t=1}^{3} \max\!\left( \sum_{j=1}^{8} \beta_j \cdot GI_{j,t},\; 0 \right)$$

where β_j is the fixed beta factor for business line j, GI_{j,t} is gross income for line j in year t, and the outer max(·, 0) floors each annual sum at zero. The TSA differs from the BIA in two important ways. First, it allows different rates for different business activities, reflecting (in principle) different operational risk intensities. Second, it allows negative gross income in one business line to offset positive gross income in another within the same year, which the BIA does not.

The pattern of beta factors is itself a Basel II policy statement. The highest-beta business lines (18 per cent for corporate finance, trading and sales, payment and settlement) are the ones with the largest historical operational losses per unit of gross income; the lowest-beta lines (12 per cent for retail banking, asset management, retail brokerage) are the ones the Committee judged to be less operationally risky. The betas were not estimated from a structural model: they were calibrated during the QIS exercises to produce an aggregate capital level for the industry that the Committee considered appropriate.

Alternative Standardised Approach (ASA)

The ASA is a variant of TSA permitted under national supervisory discretion for retail banking and commercial banking only. Instead of using gross income as the indicator, it uses outstanding loans as the indicator, multiplied by a fixed scaling factor. The motivation is that gross income for retail and commercial banking can be highly cyclical and can produce capital that fluctuates with the interest rate environment rather than with the underlying operational risk. Substituting loan volumes gives a more stable indicator.

$$K_{\text{RB}} = \beta_{\text{RB}} \cdot m \cdot LA_{\text{RB}}, \qquad K_{\text{CB}} = \beta_{\text{CB}} \cdot m \cdot LA_{\text{CB}}$$

where m = 0.035, LA_RB and LA_CB are outstanding retail and commercial loans, and β_RB = 12 per cent and β_CB = 15 per cent as in the standard TSA. Other business lines continue to use gross income as the indicator. The ASA was used widely by Asian and continental European supervisors where retail and commercial banking dominate the franchise; it was used less in the UK and the US.

Advanced Measurement Approach (AMA)

Advanced Measurement Approach
Banks use their own operational risk models to compute the 99.9 per cent value-at-risk over a one-year horizon, with capital equal to that VaR. The standard model is the Loss Distribution Approach (Lecture 2): for each cell of the 7 × 8 grid, fit frequency and severity distributions to internal loss data, supplemented by external data and scenario analysis, and convolve via Monte Carlo simulation. Eligible insurance can offset up to 20 per cent of AMA capital. Requires extensive qualitative governance and quantitative validation infrastructure; approval was granted only to a minority of large banks.
$$K_{\text{AMA}} = \text{VaR}_{99.9\%}\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{7}\sum_{j=1}^{8} \text{AggLoss}_{i,j}\right)$$

where AggLoss_{i,j} is the aggregate annual loss for cell (i, j) and the outer VaR is computed at the 99.9 per cent quantile of the (correlation-adjusted) sum across cells. The 99.9 per cent confidence level was chosen by the Basel Committee to give a one-in-thousand-year stress: the same confidence level used in the credit risk IRB formula. The one-year horizon matches credit risk and is the natural unit for capital planning. Both choices align operational risk with the broader Basel II Pillar 1 framework.

The AMA imposed four mandatory data inputs:

Data type Description
Internal loss data Database with at least 5 years of history (minimum 3 years when first moving to AMA). The primary input for the body of the severity distribution and the frequency parameters.
External loss data Competitor experience purchased from consortia (e.g. ORX) or built from public information. Most important for measuring the severity of infrequent, high-severity events that the bank has not itself experienced.
Scenario analysis Expert opinion on the most severe loss events (e.g. 1-in-1,000-year events). Extends loss distributions beyond actual experience or adjusts correlations for multiple simultaneous events.
Business environment and internal control factors (BEICFs) Forward-looking indicators expressed as KRIs, KPIs, and KCIs: counts, values, percentages, and ratios (e.g. unmatched trades, failed trades, disputed collateral calls). Intended to make the risk assessment forward-looking rather than backward-looking.

The BEICFs sit on top of three families of operational indicators that any well-run bank tracks regardless of AMA status.

Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)
Operational metrics that monitor business performance against targets: system uptime, customer-call response times, staff turnover, productivity ratios. KPIs are not specifically risk indicators but provide context: a deterioration in KPIs often precedes an increase in operational risk events.
Key Control Indicators (KCIs)
Metrics that monitor the effectiveness of specific operational controls: outstanding reconciliations, audit exceptions, breaches of authorisation limits, segregation-of-duties violations. KCIs are leading indicators of control failure and therefore of operational risk.
Key Risk Indicators (KRIs)
A curated selection of KPIs and KCIs explicitly mapped to the bank's key operational risks. A KRI dashboard is the standard tool for operational risk management committees. Each KRI has thresholds (green / amber / red) that trigger escalation when breached.

Goodness-of-fit testing was a routine supervisory expectation for AMA models. The standard tests applied to severity distributions are the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (maximum vertical distance between empirical and fitted CDFs), the Anderson-Darling test (more sensitive to the tail), the chi-square test (binned data), and the Shapiro-Wilk test (used for normality after log-transformation when assessing lognormal fit). For frequency distributions, the chi-square test is the standard, with overdispersion diagnostics to flag departures from Poisson. Supervisors typically expected at least two independent test statistics to support each distributional choice.

Worked example: BIA vs TSA vs AMA on the same hypothetical bank

Worked example: a mid-size universal bank

Consider a bank with €3,250m in aggregate annual gross income, distributed across business lines as in the TSA table above. Assume gross income is stable across the three-year averaging window (each year identical to the totals above). The bank has a well-developed AMA model with internal loss data showing an annual average loss of €110m and a 99.9 per cent annual VaR of €380m (computed via Monte Carlo simulation on the cell-level frequency and severity fits).

BIA capital. K_BIA = 0.15 × 3,250 = €487.5m. Same in each year, so the three-year average equals the single-year figure.

TSA capital. K_TSA = €472.5m (from the worked table above). Lower than BIA because the bank's business mix is dominated by retail banking (β = 12%) and the higher-beta lines (corporate finance, trading and sales, payment and settlement) make up a smaller share.

AMA capital. K_AMA = €380m (the LDA VaR). Lower again, because the bank's actual loss history is benign relative to what the gross-income proxy suggests. The bank could further reduce this to a minimum of €380m × 0.80 = €304m through eligible insurance recognition (up to the 20 per cent cap).

The story. Climbing the ladder reduces capital in this example by 22 per cent from BIA to AMA (and by 38 per cent if insurance is fully recognised). For a bank of this size, that is roughly €100–€180m of CET1 freed up, which is enough to fund several years of investment in the AMA infrastructure. This was the economic logic behind the AMA build-out in major banks through the late 2000s and 2010s. It is also why the SMA's abolition of internal modelling was so contested: many banks lost the AMA capital benefit they had spent years earning.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The BIA penalises risk management

The BIA's 15 per cent of gross income is unconditional. A bank that reduces its operational losses by half does not see its BIA capital fall: the input is gross income, not losses. The same bank operating with poor controls and high losses also pays 15 per cent of gross income. The BIA is operationally a tax on banking activity, not a risk-sensitive capital charge. This was always recognised as a flaw, but it was tolerated as the entry-level approach: the Committee's intent was that banks would graduate to TSA and AMA as they matured. The flaw became salient when the SMA's BIC structure (Lecture 4) replaced gross income with the Business Indicator, which better reflects the actual scale of operations.

The AMA was gamed: not directly but inevitably

The AMA was not gamed in the sense of banks falsifying data. It was gamed in the sense that legitimate modelling choices systematically reduced capital. A bank could choose the lognormal over the Pareto for severity (lower tail), or a higher threshold for the EVT fit (less weight on the worst observations), or a longer reference period for downturn LGD (more observations diluting the recent stress), or a copula that allowed diversification benefit across cells (lower aggregate VaR). Each choice was defensible; the aggregate effect across choices was substantial reduction in capital. Supervisors did not have the analytical bandwidth to challenge every choice at every bank, and the bank-specific model architectures made cross-bank comparison difficult. The SMA's abolition of internal modelling was a confession that this gaming could not be policed effectively.

Insurance recognition was an under-appreciated dynamic

The 20 per cent insurance offset cap looked modest on paper but was material for banks with active risk transfer programmes. Major banks negotiated portfolio policies covering rogue trading, fraud, business interruption, and IT failure, often with insurers willing to write coverage at premiums that were attractive relative to the capital saving. Insurers progressively raised premiums as the AMA matured, and several insurers withdrew from the market entirely after the 2008 crisis when claims experience deteriorated. The lesson is general: regulatory capital relief schemes that depend on private market participation are stable only when the private market participants find them economically attractive. The SMA does not allow insurance to reduce capital, simplifying this issue out of existence.

KRIs are valuable; KRI dashboards often are not

A well-designed KRI is a leading indicator of operational risk: a rising count of failed reconciliations in a settlement function genuinely predicts settlement losses, and a falling count of overdue audit issues genuinely predicts a reduction in control failures. The problem is that a KRI dashboard with 200 indicators across an entire bank is unreadable, and the natural response (selecting a small number of indicators for board reporting) makes the dashboard a political artefact rather than a risk tool. The best operational risk functions maintain a comprehensive set of KRIs at the working level and a curated set at the board level, with documented justification for which indicators are escalated. The worst confuse the dashboard with the risk: if it is amber on the dashboard, it is treated as significant; if it is green, it is ignored, even when actual losses are clustering.

Watch out Do not confuse the AMA with the LDA. The AMA is the regulatory permission to use an internal model for operational risk capital under Basel II. The LDA is the modelling framework (Lecture 2) that most AMA models used in practice but that is not formally required. The Basel II framework allowed alternative AMA models (the Scenario-Based Approach, the Internal Measurement Approach, the Scorecard Approach) that did not use a full LDA, but most banks converged on the LDA because it was the most defensible to supervisors. From 2023, all of this is moot: the AMA is gone, and the SMA replaces it with a simpler construction that does not require any of the four AMA model families. But for exam purposes, the AMA-vs-LDA distinction may still come up.
