Anti-money laundering and know your customer

AML and KYC sit at the intersection of operational risk, regulatory compliance, and financial crime prevention. Money laundering converts the proceeds of criminal activity into apparently legitimate assets through three phases: placement, layering, and integration. Banks are the principal targets of money launderers because of their role in payments, lending, and custody. Failure to prevent money laundering exposes the bank to criminal sanctions, regulatory fines, and reputational damage. For South African candidates, the FICA framework, the FSCA's conduct role, and the FATF grey-listing of South Africa in 2023 are all directly examinable.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Money laundering is the process by which the proceeds of criminal activity (the predicate crime) are converted into assets that appear to have a legitimate origin. The conventional model describes three phases: placement (introducing dirty cash into the financial system), layering (concealing the origin through complex transactions), and integration (using the now-legitimate-appearing funds for ordinary commerce). Banks are the front line: every account opening, every wire transfer, every property purchase is a potential placement, layering, or integration step. The international AML framework rests on the FATF 40 Recommendations (Financial Action Task Force), implemented through national legislation. Know Your Customer (KYC) is the banking-industry application of FATF principles: customer acceptance policies, customer due diligence (with enhanced due diligence for higher-risk relationships), ongoing monitoring, information management, and suspicious transaction reporting. For South African banks, FICA (the Financial Intelligence Centre Act 2001) is the primary domestic AML legislation; the Prudential Authority oversees prudential risk and the FSCA oversees conduct.

Feynman test Imagine someone arrives at a bank with a suitcase of cash whose source they cannot explain. The bank's job, in three steps: refuse the deposit if the customer cannot account for the source (placement control); flag and investigate any subsequent activity that looks designed to disguise the trail (layering control); and apply enhanced scrutiny to any large or unusual transfer out, especially to a high-risk jurisdiction (integration control). KYC is the structured form of these instincts: a regulated process for deciding which customers to onboard, what documentation to require, and what activity to watch for. AML is the wider regulatory framework within which KYC operates. Both exist because banks are uniquely positioned to detect money laundering at the moment it enters the financial system, and uniquely culpable when they fail to.
What is beyond the source The wiki note states the three phases, the FATF framework, the risk-based approach, the three lines of defence, and the five KYC pillars. This lecture adds: real examples of regulatory enforcement (HSBC 2012, Standard Chartered 2012, Danske Bank 2018) and what they reveal about systemic AML failure modes; the relationship between AML and conduct risk under modern supervisory practice; the de-risking phenomenon and its unintended consequences for financial inclusion; the ultimate beneficial ownership (UBO) problem and the EU's response (the 4th, 5th, and 6th AML Directives); the FATF grey-listing of South Africa announced in February 2023 and what it means for South African banks; and the modern role of transaction monitoring technology (rule-based and machine-learning approaches) in suspicious-transaction reporting.
Step 2

Why this matters

The cost of AML failure is substantial and directly visible in the operational risk loss histories of major banks. A short list of headline enforcement actions illustrates the scale.

HSBC, 2012. A $1.9 billion settlement with US authorities (Department of Justice, Federal Reserve, OCC, FinCEN) for systemic AML control failures. The US Senate investigation found that HSBC's Mexican subsidiary had been used to launder billions of dollars in Sinaloa cartel drug proceeds through the US dollar clearing system, that the bank had circumvented US sanctions in dealings with Iran and Sudan, and that compliance reports had been altered or ignored. The settlement was the largest AML fine in US history at the time. The bank was placed under a five-year deferred prosecution agreement and a court-appointed monitor.

Standard Chartered, 2012 and 2019. Two enforcement actions, totalling roughly $1.7 billion, for systemic violations of US sanctions against Iran. The bank had used a practice of "wire-stripping" to remove identifying information from US dollar payments processed on behalf of Iranian counterparties, allowing the payments to flow through New York correspondent banks. The 2019 settlement extended the original 2012 deferred prosecution agreement.

Danske Bank, 2018. The Estonian branch of Denmark's largest bank was found to have processed approximately €200 billion of suspicious payments between 2007 and 2015, much of it from Russian and other former-Soviet jurisdictions. The episode triggered the CEO's resignation, a Danish criminal investigation, a US criminal investigation, and a fundamental supervisory rebuilding of the Nordic banking system's AML controls. Danske ultimately reached a $2 billion settlement with US authorities in 2022.

South Africa, 2023. In February 2023, the Financial Action Task Force placed South Africa on its grey list (officially the "increased monitoring" list), citing strategic deficiencies in the country's AML and counter-terrorist-financing (CFT) framework. Grey-listing has direct consequences for South African banks: enhanced due diligence is required on transactions from grey-listed jurisdictions by counterparties in other countries, correspondent banking relationships become harder to maintain, and the cost of cross-border payment processing rises. South Africa was removed from the grey list in late 2025 after substantial reforms; but the episode is directly examinable for ASSA candidates and illustrates that the supervisory framework for AML operates at country level as well as at bank level.

The lessons across these cases are consistent. Systemic AML failures are typically driven by the same factors: weak culture (compliance treated as a cost rather than a control), inadequate technology (transaction monitoring systems unable to detect patterns at scale), and structural conflicts of interest (revenue-generating units with effective control over the compliance function in their region). The mitigation framework, in turn, is consistent: a risk-based approach, three lines of defence, escalation mechanisms that cannot be overridden by business heads, and supervisory engagement that holds individuals accountable.

Step 3

The mental model

Money laundering follows a lifecycle. Each phase has a typical mechanism, a typical bank exposure point, and a typical KYC/AML control. Understanding the lifecycle is the foundation of an effective control framework: the bank needs to be looking for different things at different stages.

The placement-layering-integration model is a simplification. Real money laundering operations frequently combine the phases (a placement that is also a layering step, an integration that itself triggers further layering), and trade-based money laundering blurs the model entirely. But for control design and supervisory understanding, the three-phase framework remains the canonical reference and is directly examinable.

Step 4

The detail

The three phases of money laundering

Predicate crime
The underlying criminal act whose proceeds are being laundered. Examples include drug trafficking, human trafficking, tax evasion, bribery and corruption, embezzlement, and proceeds of organised crime. The FATF Recommendations require national legislation to criminalise the laundering of proceeds from at least 21 designated categories of predicate crime.
Placement
Phase 1: the introduction of illegally obtained funds into the financial system. Typical mechanisms include cash deposits structured to avoid reporting thresholds (smurfing), bulk purchases of monetary instruments (cashier's cheques, prepaid cards), and exchange of cash for chips at casinos.
Layering
Phase 2: concealing the criminal origin through a series of transactions that disguise the trail. Typical mechanisms include rapid wire transfers across multiple jurisdictions, foreign exchange transactions, the use of shell companies, and trade-based laundering through over- or under-invoicing of goods.
Integration
Phase 3: the use of the now-apparently-legitimate funds for ordinary economic activity. Typical mechanisms include real estate purchases, investment in legitimate businesses, acquisition of luxury goods, and ordinary banking and investment activity that produces apparently legitimate income.

The international AML framework

FATF (Financial Action Task Force)
The intergovernmental body responsible for setting international AML and CFT (counter-terrorist financing) standards. Founded by the G7 in 1989; currently has 39 member jurisdictions plus 2 regional organisations. FATF assesses member compliance through periodic mutual evaluations and publishes lists of non-compliant jurisdictions.
FATF 40 Recommendations
The international standards on combating money laundering, terrorist financing, and proliferation financing. First issued in 1990, last comprehensively revised in 2012, with periodic updates. The Recommendations cover customer due diligence, record-keeping, suspicious transaction reporting, the regulation of designated non-financial businesses, international cooperation, and the responsibilities of national authorities.
Grey-listing
Inclusion on the FATF list of "Jurisdictions under Increased Monitoring": countries with strategic deficiencies in their AML/CFT regimes that have committed to address them within an agreed timeframe. Grey-listing does not impose mandatory countermeasures but does trigger enhanced due diligence requirements globally. South Africa was grey-listed in February 2023 and removed in 2025.
Body Focus Standards
BCBS Prudential regulation and risk management of banks; AML treated as an operational risk and conduct risk topic. Sound management of risks related to money laundering and financing of terrorism (June 2017); Customer due diligence for banks (October 2001, since superseded).
FATF AML and CFT across all sectors (banks, securities, insurance, designated non-financial businesses, virtual asset service providers). The FATF 40 Recommendations and Interpretive Notes; methodology for assessing technical compliance and effectiveness.

Two FATF Recommendations are particularly load-bearing for KYC. Recommendation 10 defines "customer" broadly: any person entering into a business relationship with the institution or conducting an occasional transaction above a designated threshold (USD/EUR 15,000 for wire transfers, lower for other transactions in some jurisdictions). Recommendation 11 requires retention of all customer due diligence records and transaction records for at least five years after the business relationship ends or the occasional transaction is completed.

The risk-based approach

Risk-based approach
The principle, now embedded in the FATF Recommendations, that AML measures should be proportionate to the assessed level of risk. Higher-risk customers, products, geographies, and transactions receive enhanced controls; lower-risk situations receive simplified controls. The risk-based approach replaces an earlier rule-based approach in which all customers received uniform treatment.

The risk-based approach involves five iterative steps:

Step What it involves
1. IdentifyIdentify the money laundering and terrorist financing risks across customers, products, services, geographies, and delivery channels.
2. AssessAssess the likelihood and impact of each identified risk. Rank risks to focus attention on the highest-priority exposures.
3. MitigateDesign and implement controls proportionate to the assessed risks. High-risk customers receive enhanced due diligence; lower-risk customers may receive simplified procedures.
4. MonitorMonitor the effectiveness of controls and the changing risk environment. Adjust controls as the risk profile shifts.
5. DocumentDocument the risk assessment, the controls implemented, and the rationale for the risk ratings assigned to specific customers and products. Documentation supports supervisory examination and internal audit.

Higher-risk categories that the FATF specifically identifies include politically exposed persons (PEPs), customers based in or transacting through high-risk jurisdictions, customers in cash-intensive industries (gambling, money services businesses), customers operating through complex ownership structures, and customers with significant unexplained wealth.

Politically exposed persons (PEPs)
Individuals who hold prominent public functions (heads of state, senior politicians, senior officials in judiciary or military, senior executives of state-owned enterprises) and their family members and close associates. PEPs are not by definition corrupt, but their proximity to political power raises the risk that their assets may include the proceeds of bribery or embezzlement. Enhanced due diligence is required for all PEP relationships.

The three lines of defence

Line Parties Role
First Business units (e.g. retail banking, corporate banking, private banking) Identify, assess, and control AML/CFT risks at the customer interface. Perform CDD at onboarding, monitor relationships, escalate suspicions. The first line owns the risk.
Second Chief AML/CFT officer, compliance function, supporting functions in HR and technology Independently assess the effectiveness of first-line controls. Define policies, standards, and procedures. File regulatory reports (suspicious transaction reports, terrorist financing reports). Report to senior management and the board on the state of AML/CFT controls.
Third Internal audit; external auditors and supervisory examiners Provide independent assurance on the design and operating effectiveness of the AML/CFT framework. Test compliance with policies and procedures. Report findings to the audit committee and to senior management.

The three-lines model is industry standard for operational and compliance risk generally, not just for AML. The key feature for AML specifically is the position of the second line: the chief AML/CFT officer is the bank's nominated point of contact with national financial intelligence units and is the person on whose decision the filing of suspicious transaction reports ultimately rests. The role is regulated in most jurisdictions and carries personal accountability under regimes such as the UK's Senior Managers and Certification Regime.

KYC: the five pillars

Know Your Customer is the banking-industry operationalisation of the FATF customer due diligence requirements. It rests on five pillars, each of which must be in place and documented for an effective KYC programme.

1. Customer acceptance policy
A formal policy setting out the categories of customer the bank will and will not accept, the documentation required for each category, and the approval authority for higher-risk categories. The policy excludes certain customer types entirely (e.g. shell banks, anonymous accounts) and requires escalated approval for others (e.g. PEPs, high-net-worth individuals from sanctioned jurisdictions).
2. Customer and beneficial owner identification
Verification of the customer's identity through documentary evidence (passport, national identity card, company registration documents) and, for corporate customers, identification of the ultimate beneficial owner (UBO) — the natural person(s) who ultimately own or control the customer. Standard CDD applies to lower-risk customers; enhanced due diligence (EDD) applies to PEPs and other higher-risk categories.
Customer due diligence (CDD)
The standard set of identification and verification procedures applied to all customers. Includes identity verification, beneficial ownership identification for entity customers, understanding the nature and purpose of the business relationship, and ongoing monitoring.
Enhanced due diligence (EDD)
A heightened level of due diligence applied to higher-risk customers and relationships: PEPs, customers in high-risk jurisdictions, complex ownership structures, and unusual transaction patterns. EDD typically requires source-of-funds and source-of-wealth verification, more frequent reviews, and senior management approval.
3. Ongoing monitoring
Continuous monitoring of customer activity to detect transactions inconsistent with the customer's known profile, the stated purpose of the relationship, or expected patterns of behaviour. Modern monitoring is technology-driven, using rule-based engines (e.g. flagging transactions above thresholds or to specific high-risk jurisdictions) and, increasingly, machine-learning approaches to detect anomalous patterns.
4. Management of information
Retention and management of CDD records and transaction records for the regulatory minimum period: FATF Recommendation 11 requires at least five years after the business relationship ends or the transaction is completed. Records must be retrievable on request from competent authorities. Many banks retain records for longer than five years to align with broader legal and tax requirements.
5. Reporting of suspicious transactions
Filing of suspicious transaction reports (STRs) with the national financial intelligence unit when the bank has reasonable grounds to suspect that a transaction is related to money laundering, terrorist financing, or another predicate crime. The decision to file an STR is the bank's, not the customer's; STRs are confidential ("tipping off" the customer is itself an offence).
Suspicious transaction report (STR)
A confidential report filed by a bank or other regulated institution with the national financial intelligence unit. In South Africa, STRs are filed with the Financial Intelligence Centre (FIC) under FICA. The bank does not need to prove that money laundering has occurred; reasonable grounds for suspicion are sufficient. STR data is one of the FIC's primary inputs for identifying patterns of financial crime.

South Africa: FICA, the FIC, the PA, and the FSCA

South Africa's AML framework is principally set by the Financial Intelligence Centre Act, 2001 (FICA) and its supporting regulations. FICA establishes the Financial Intelligence Centre (FIC) as the national financial intelligence unit, designates the categories of "accountable institutions" subject to AML obligations (banks, securities firms, attorneys, estate agents, and many others), and sets out the customer due diligence, record-keeping, and reporting requirements.

Three regulatory bodies operate within the broader framework. The Financial Intelligence Centre (FIC) receives STRs and other reports, analyses them, disseminates intelligence to law enforcement, and supervises accountable institutions on AML compliance. The Prudential Authority (PA), within SARB, supervises banks on prudential risk management, which includes operational risk management of which AML is a sub-component. The FSCA supervises conduct of business, which intersects with AML at the customer-treatment level (TCF principles, the CoFI Bill).

The FATF grey-listing of South Africa in February 2023 cited deficiencies in the FIC's resourcing, in beneficial ownership transparency, in the effectiveness of money laundering and terrorist financing investigations, and in international cooperation. South African banks responded with substantial investment in transaction monitoring, beneficial ownership verification, and STR quality. South Africa was removed from the grey list in late 2025 after the deficiencies were assessed as substantially addressed.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

De-risking is the unintended consequence of heavy AML pressure

The cost of AML compliance is not uniform across customers. A high-volume retail customer is cheap to onboard and monitor; a small money services business in a high-risk jurisdiction is expensive. Banks under regulatory pressure have rationally responded by exiting customer segments and relationships where the compliance cost outweighs the revenue. The phenomenon is known as de-risking, and its consequences fall most heavily on the customers banks exit: correspondent banking relationships in small countries, money services businesses serving migrant remittance corridors, and non-profit organisations operating in conflict zones. The FATF, the World Bank, and the BCBS have all expressed concern that aggressive AML enforcement, while reducing money laundering at the margins, has also produced financial exclusion at scale.

Beneficial ownership is the hardest KYC problem

A corporate customer can be identified by its registration documents. Its beneficial owner — the natural person who ultimately owns or controls it — often cannot. Complex ownership structures involving multiple jurisdictions, nominee shareholders, and trusts are routinely used to obscure UBOs. The EU's Fifth AML Directive (2018) required member states to establish public registers of beneficial ownership; the Sixth AML Directive (2018) extended criminal liability for money laundering and harmonised predicate offences. The CJEU's 2022 ruling in WM and Sovim partially struck down public access to UBO registers on privacy grounds. The state of the EU UBO framework remains in flux, and the equivalent project in the US (the Corporate Transparency Act, 2021) is the subject of ongoing litigation. For banks, the practical implication is that UBO identification cannot be outsourced to a national register: the bank must do its own verification, and its own escalation when UBO documentation is incomplete.

The conduct-AML overlap is widening

Historically, AML and conduct risk were treated as separate compliance disciplines. Modern supervision increasingly treats them as overlapping: a bank that fails to identify a vulnerable customer being defrauded is failing both AML controls (the receiving account is a money mule, often unwittingly) and conduct controls (the vulnerable customer is being mistreated). The PRA, FCA, and SARB Prudential Authority all now expect integrated reporting on financial crime and conduct, recognising that the underlying customer-relationship management is the same. For an ASSA candidate, the relevant point is that AML is no longer a stand-alone compliance topic: it sits inside a wider conduct and financial crime framework that includes consumer protection, market conduct, and prudential risk.

Transaction monitoring is moving from rules to models

Traditional AML transaction monitoring uses rule-based engines: flag transactions above thresholds, flag transactions to high-risk jurisdictions, flag patterns that match published typologies. Rule-based monitoring has two problems: it produces high false-positive rates (an investigator can spend a working week clearing alerts that are not, in fact, suspicious), and it misses novel patterns that the rule designer did not anticipate. Machine-learning approaches (anomaly detection, network analysis, graph analytics) supplement the rule engines by identifying patterns that rules cannot specify. The transition is well underway at major banks and is a recurring theme in supervisory engagement. For an actuary, the technical parallels with credit risk modelling are direct: feature engineering, model validation, backtesting against known frauds, and the same governance challenges around model risk management.

Watch out The five-year record retention requirement under FATF Recommendation 11 is a minimum, not a target. Many jurisdictions impose longer retention requirements through other legislation (tax records, contractual records, sanctions investigations). South Africa's FICA aligns with the FATF five-year minimum, but other domestic legislation (the Tax Administration Act, for example) may require longer retention. In practice, large banks retain customer due diligence and transaction records for ten years or longer because the marginal cost of additional retention is low and the cost of missing a record during a supervisory examination is high.
