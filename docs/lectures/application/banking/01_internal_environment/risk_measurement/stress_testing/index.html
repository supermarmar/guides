Risk measurement, phase 3

Stress testing

The forward-looking half of capital adequacy, and the one place where every risk type in this collection has to be projected on one consistent path. Three lectures: the Pillar 2B mechanics that turn a scenario into a capital buffer, the named supervisory programmes that set the scenarios, and reverse stress testing, which inverts the question and is the most useful of the three for a board. The root CLAUDE.md lists stress testing as the largest F107 gap in this collection, and the wiki holds a single 37-line note, so these lectures reach well beyond it.

How this track is organised

Mechanics before programmes, because the programmes only make sense once you know what a stress test produces. Lecture 1 takes the capital planning buffer apart: how a scenario becomes a projected capital path, why the trough matters rather than the average, and the four drivers that move it. Lecture 2 turns to who sets the scenarios and how the named programmes differ, which is where jurisdictional detail lives. Lecture 3 covers reverse stress testing, which asks what would break the bank rather than what a given scenario does to it, and which is the only one of the three that can surface a risk nobody thought to model.

Status badges show what is built (built) or planned. Because the wiki coverage here is thin, most content is drawn from published supervisory material and is flagged as such.

Lectures

Mechanics

  • built01. Pillar 2B and the capital planning buffer — how a macroeconomic scenario becomes a capital number, the capital planning buffer as a peak shortfall on a projected path, the four drivers and why impairments and risk-weight inflation compound in a ratio, IFRS 9's front-loading effect, management actions, and the South African Individual Capital Requirement. Includes a worked five-year capital path.

Who sets the scenarios

  • built02. The named supervisory programmes — three axes of difference (who runs the models, whether there is a pass mark, what the result does), the Bank of England, EBA and Federal Reserve programmes placed on each, the stress capital buffer formula, the comparability-against-realism trade-off, and why supervisor-run models concentrate model risk. Built entirely from published programme material.

Inverting the question

  • built03. Reverse stress testing — viability rather than solvency as the failure point, the five method steps, and a worked reverse test run two ways: single-factor, which concludes failure is impossible, and combination, which reaches 93 per cent of the same destruction from unremarkable components. Why single-factor testing is the real failure mode, and the chain into recovery and resolution planning.

Why this is the largest F107 gap

The root CLAUDE.md puts stress testing first in its summary of priority gaps, and the wiki bears that out: one note covering Pillar 2B, with the named jurisdictional programmes and reverse stress testing absent entirely. That single note is nonetheless a good one, containing the capital planning buffer formula, the capital evolution equation, the four stress drivers and the South African Individual Capital Requirement, so lecture 1 has a real base to build on. Lectures 2 and 3 do not.

For an actuary the appeal of this material is that it is the only place in banking regulation where the whole balance sheet is projected forward on one coherent path, which is what an actuarial model does by default. The scenario narrative requirement discussed in the writing track's module 8 belongs here, and so does everything about assumption setting under conditions the data does not contain.

Source material

Published sources used across the track include the Basel Committee's stress testing principles, the PRA's approach to setting Pillar 2 capital and its published stress test scenarios, EBA and ECB stress test methodological notes, and the Federal Reserve's supervisory stress test disclosures.
