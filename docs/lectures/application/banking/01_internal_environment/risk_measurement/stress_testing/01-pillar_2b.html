Pillar 2B and the capital planning buffer

The buffer is the peak shortfall on a projected path, not the loss in the worst year, and the two are rarely the same year. Impairments cut the numerator while risk-weight inflation raises the denominator, so a capital ratio falls from both ends at once.

Lecture 1 of 3 Reading time, 36 minutes Source: wiki stress_testing/01-pillar_2b.md; PRA and BCBS material
Step 1

The 30-second version

Pillar 2B is the forward-looking component of Pillar 2 capital, set by stress testing rather than by point-in-time assessment. Its purpose is to size a buffer sufficient to keep the bank above its Pillar 1 and Pillar 2A requirements throughout an adverse scenario, and the resulting number is the capital planning buffer.

The buffer is defined as a peak shortfall: the largest gap between the requirement and the projected capital ratio at any point on the stress path. That makes it a property of the path rather than of the scenario's worst year, and step 4's worked projection shows the trough arriving a year after the heaviest impairment charge.

Four drivers move the path. Impairments from probability of default migration and loss given default increases, revenue compression through margin and fee income, risk-weighted asset inflation as borrowers migrate to worse grades, and dividend policy, which most banks would suspend. The first and third compound, because one reduces the numerator of a capital ratio while the other raises its denominator.

And the buffer is a buffer, which is the conceptual point most easily missed. It is designed to be used in stress, unlike Pillar 1 and Pillar 2A, which are minima. Consuming it is the intended behaviour; breaching a minimum is a different kind of event entirely.

Feynman test Project your capital ratio forward through a bad recession, find the worst point on the path, and hold enough extra capital today to have stayed above your minimum at that worst point. That extra amount is the buffer, and you are supposed to spend it when the recession actually arrives.
Beyond the wiki The wiki's single stress testing note supplies the capital planning buffer formula, the capital evolution equation, the four drivers, the three scenario types and the South African Individual Capital Requirement, and it is a good note. This lecture adds the worked capital path, the numerator-and-denominator compounding argument, IFRS 9's front-loading effect on the impairment profile, the buffer-against-minimum distinction and its consequences, the treatment of management actions, and the no-double-counting point about how a stress-implied buffer interacts with the regulatory capital buffers already in place.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this is where every other risk type in the collection has to be projected on one consistent path. Credit impairments, net interest margin, risk weights and operational losses all appear in the same capital projection, and they have to be mutually coherent, which is a discipline none of them faces individually. A stress test is the only exercise that forces a bank to state how its risks interact.

The second reason is that the buffer is frequently the binding capital constraint. Pillar 1 and Pillar 2A are visible and comparatively stable; the stress-implied buffer moves with the scenario and with the bank's own modelling, and for many banks it is the largest single component of the capital they actually hold above the minimum.

The third reason is the buffer-against-minimum distinction, which has real operational consequences and is routinely muddled. A bank that has consumed a buffer in a stress has done what the framework intended and will face supervisory dialogue and probably distribution restrictions. A bank that has breached a minimum requirement is in a different regulatory position altogether. Confusing the two produces both unnecessary alarm and misplaced complacency.

And a fourth reason for actuaries specifically. This is a cash flow projection under an adverse scenario with a coherence requirement across assumptions, which is what actuarial modelling is. The unfamiliar part is not the technique but the fact that the projection has a capital consequence set by a supervisor who has seen everyone else's.

Step 3

The mental model

Where Pillar 2B sits in the capital stack, the equation that generates the path, and the compounding that makes the ratio fall faster than the losses.

Capital planning buffer
The peak shortfall between the Pillar 1 plus Pillar 2A requirement and the projected capital ratio, measured over the whole stress horizon. A property of the path rather than of any single year.
Severe but plausible
The standard a stress scenario must meet. Severe enough to be informative and plausible enough that management and supervisors will act on the result, which is a tension rather than a specification.
Individual Capital Requirement
The South African Pillar 2B requirement, combining the stress-derived capital need with any ad-hoc risks the Prudential Authority identifies that Pillar 2A does not capture. Bank-specific and not publicly disclosed.

The trough is Y3, at 8.4%. They are different years, because in Y3 the net movement is still negative (-0.3) even though impairments have more than halved. Revenue is still depressed and RWA relief has only just turned positive, so capital continues to erode after the credit losses have peaked. A bank sizing its buffer off the worst loss year would have reported 1.8 points and needed 2.1. WHY REVENUE MATTERS MORE THAN IT LOOKS Revenue is the only thing rebuilding capital through the stress. Note Y1 revenue at +1.2 against Y5 at +1.6: the recovery in earnings is what turns the path around, and a scenario that suppresses revenue for longer produces a deeper trough even with identical impairments. That is why net interest margin compression matters to a stress test out of proportion to its size in any single year. It removes the mechanism of recovery. WHAT THE BANK STILL HAS NOT ACHIEVED At Y5 the ratio is 10.0% against a 10.5% requirement. Five years in, the bank is still below its minimum, which is why the buffer has to be sized on the whole path and why supervisors care about the recovery trajectory rather than only the trough.

IFRS 9 and the shape of the impairment path

The impairment profile in that projection front-loads, and under the current accounting standard that is not an artefact of the illustration. IFRS 9 recognises lifetime expected credit loss on exposures that have suffered a significant increase in credit risk, so a stress that triggers stage migration brings the whole remaining life of expected loss into the current period rather than recognising losses as they are incurred.

The consequence for stress testing is a sharper and earlier capital hit than the previous incurred-loss regime produced, for the same underlying credit deterioration. A scenario that pushes a large tranche of a portfolio from stage 1 to stage 2 in year one produces an impairment charge covering several years of expected losses in that year, and the charge in later years is correspondingly smaller because the loss has already been taken.

Which means an impairment path under IFRS 9 is not a projection of losses, it is a projection of provisions, and the two have different shapes. This is worth being explicit about because it changes how the trough behaves: front-loaded provisioning deepens the early path and speeds the later recovery, so it moves the trough earlier and can make it sharper. The procyclicality concern that attended IFRS 9's introduction is exactly this effect operating across a banking system at once.

Management actions, and which ones a supervisor will accept

Assumed actionCredible in a supervisory stress test?Why
A bank projects what it would do, and the supervisor decides what to believe
Suspending or cutting dividends Yes, and generally expected Within the bank's control, requires no counterparty, and is what boards actually do. The wiki lists it as a driver for this reason.
Suspending share buybacks and reducing variable remuneration Yes Same reasoning. Both are discretionary distributions the bank can stop unilaterally.
Cost reduction programmes Partly, and heavily discounted Achievable but slowly, and the savings arrive after restructuring costs. A plan assuming immediate cost relief in year one is not credible.
Reducing lending to shrink risk-weighted assets Treated sceptically, and for a systemic reason Individually rational and collectively harmful, since every bank deleveraging at once deepens the recession the scenario describes. Supervisors constrain it precisely because the aggregate effect defeats the purpose.
Raising new equity Generally not Requires a market that is open, and the scenario is one in which it may not be. Assuming access to capital in a stress assumes away the stress.
Selling assets at book value No Asset sales in a stress happen at stressed prices, and a plan assuming otherwise has assumed a liquid buyer in an illiquid market.

The fourth row is the one with the deepest reasoning behind it and it is worth understanding rather than memorising. An individual bank cutting lending to protect its ratio is behaving sensibly. A banking system doing so simultaneously withdraws credit from an economy already contracting, which worsens the scenario for every bank including the one that acted. That is why supervisory stress tests typically constrain balance sheet shrinkage, and it is a clear instance of a macroprudential objective overriding a microprudential one.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Avoiding double counting against the buffers already there

A bank does not hold the stress-implied requirement on top of every other buffer it already carries, and understanding why requires seeing what the regulatory buffers are for.

A bank already holds a capital conservation buffer, potentially a countercyclical buffer, and for large institutions a systemic buffer. Those exist to be drawn down in stress, which is the same function the stress-implied buffer performs. Adding a full stress requirement on top would make the bank hold two buffers for one purpose.

The resolution adopted in practice is to set the stress-derived requirement only to the extent that it exceeds the buffers already in place, so it fills a gap rather than stacking. In the United Kingdom the PRA buffer works this way, being set where the stress test indicates a need beyond the combined regulatory buffers, and where the existing buffers suffice no additional requirement follows.

That construction has a consequence worth noticing. As the countercyclical buffer is raised in benign conditions, the incremental stress-derived requirement falls, because the bank is already holding usable capital. So the two instruments substitute for one another, and a supervisor tightening one is partly relieving the other. Anyone comparing capital requirements across jurisdictions or across time needs to know which components are stacked and which are netted, because the headline numbers are not comparable otherwise.

What a stress test can and cannot tell you

The exercise is genuinely valuable and it is worth being precise about the limits, because stress testing attracts more confidence than its construction supports.

It can tell you whether a bank's capital survives a specified path, and that is a real and useful answer. It forces coherence across risk types that are otherwise modelled separately, which is arguably its greatest benefit and one that has nothing to do with the resulting number. And it produces a comparable figure across banks when the scenario is prescribed, which is what makes supervisory programmes informative.

It cannot tell you whether the scenario is the right one, and this is the binding limitation. A stress test is conditional on a path someone chose, and the events that actually damage banking systems have a habit of not resembling the previous crisis, which is what the prescribed scenarios are calibrated from. A bank can pass every scenario put to it and fail on something outside all of them.

Nor can it capture second-round effects well. The scenario specifies what happens to the economy and the bank responds, but a real stress involves the banking system's response feeding back into the economy, which the fourth row of step 4's table gestures at and which single-bank stress testing structurally cannot represent. That gap is what reverse stress testing partly addresses, and it is why lecture 3 argues it is the most useful of the three exercises for a board.

Reading this as an actuary

The methodology will feel familiar and three differences in emphasis are worth naming.

The projection horizon is short. A stress test runs three to five years, where an actuarial projection may run decades, and the consequence is that the terminal position matters less and the path matters more. Step 4's example makes the point: what mattered was the timing of the trough, not the year-five position, which is the reverse of the emphasis in a typical liability projection.

The assumptions are prescribed rather than best-estimate. An actuary setting a reserving basis argues for the most appropriate assumption; a bank running a supervisory stress test is handed the macroeconomic path and argues only about how its book responds to it. That narrows the modelling discretion to the transmission mechanism, which is where all the disagreement then concentrates.

And the coherence requirement is the transferable insight in the other direction. A stress test fails if unemployment rises while credit losses do not, or if rates rise while margins do not respond, and the discipline of making every assumption belong to one story is exactly what module 8 of the writing track argued about scenario narratives. Actuaries writing economic scenario generators face the same requirement and state it less often.

Common exam error Computing the buffer as the capital lost in the worst year of the scenario. It is the largest gap between the requirement and the projected ratio at any point on the path, and step 4 shows those falling in different years. A related error is treating the buffer as an additional minimum: it is a buffer, designed to be consumed in the stress it was sized for.
Exam tip The four drivers are worth learning with their direction of effect on the ratio, not just their names, because the marks are in noticing that impairments and risk-weight inflation act on opposite sides of the same fraction. If you can add that dividend suspension is the one driver within management's control and therefore the one management action supervisors reliably accept, you have connected the mechanics to the governance.
