The named supervisory programmes

Three jurisdictions, three answers to the same question, and the differences are structural rather than cosmetic. One has the supervisor run the models, one forbids a pass mark, and one converts the result into a capital requirement by formula.

Lecture 2 of 3 Reading time, 34 minutes Source: published BoE, EBA and Federal Reserve material
Step 1

The 30-second version

The major programmes differ on three axes, and once you have the axes the detail follows. Who runs the models: banks under constraint, or the supervisor independently. Whether there is a pass mark: a hurdle rate, an explicit refusal to set one, or a formula. What the result does: informs disclosure, feeds supervisory judgement, or sets a requirement directly.

The Bank of England exercise is concurrent across major UK banks, assessed against published hurdle rates, and its scenario is deliberately countercyclical: severity increases as the Bank judges risks to be building. That last feature is a genuine innovation, because it addresses the charge that stress tests are most lenient just before they are most needed.

The EBA exercise is a constrained bottom-up test: banks apply a prescribed scenario and a detailed methodology to their own books under caps and floors, traditionally on a static balance sheet. The EBA sets no hurdle rate and declares no pass or fail, and the results feed supervisory judgement through Pillar 2 Guidance rather than a formula.

The Federal Reserve does the opposite of the EBA on both counts. It runs its own models on bank-submitted data, and the result feeds a formula: the stress capital buffer is the peak-to-trough decline in the capital ratio under the severely adverse scenario plus four quarters of planned dividends, floored at 2.5 per cent. That is the tightest link between a stress test and a capital requirement anywhere.

Feynman test Everyone runs the same recession past the banks. The British publish a pass mark and make the recession nastier when they are more worried. The Europeans refuse to say who passed and let supervisors decide what it means. The Americans run the banks' books through the regulator's own models and turn the answer straight into a capital requirement.

Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the design choices are not administrative. Whether the supervisor or the bank runs the models determines where model risk sits, whether there is a hurdle determines how the market reads the result, and whether a formula converts the output into capital determines how hard a bank will work to influence the input. Each choice has a consequence you can predict from the choice itself.

The second reason is the trade-off that generates all the differences. A prescribed scenario applied on a static balance sheet under a constrained methodology produces results that are comparable across banks and unrealistic for any of them. A bank-designed scenario with dynamic management actions produces a realistic answer that cannot be compared with anyone else's. Every programme sits somewhere on that line, and no position on it is correct.

The third reason is that these exercises are where a great deal of a bank's modelling capacity actually goes. A large bank runs the supervisory scenario, its own internal scenarios for the capital assessment, and often a reverse stress test, on infrastructure built primarily to satisfy the first. Understanding what the supervisory exercise is for explains why the infrastructure looks as it does.

And a fourth reason for an ASSA candidate. The South African arrangement, which lecture 1 covered through the Individual Capital Requirement, is closer to the supervisory-judgement model than to the formulaic one, so knowing which family it belongs to is more useful than memorising foreign programme names.

Step 3

The mental model

Three axes, three programmes placed on them, and the trade-off that explains why they diverge.

Constrained bottom-up
A stress test in which banks apply a prescribed scenario to their own portfolios using their own models, subject to a detailed common methodology with caps, floors and restrictions. Comparability is bought through the constraints rather than through common models.
Stress capital buffer
The US requirement derived directly from the supervisory stress test: the peak-to-trough decline in the common equity ratio under the severely adverse scenario, plus four quarters of planned common dividends, subject to a floor of 2.5 per cent.
Concurrent stress test
An exercise in which all participating banks are tested against the same scenario at the same time, which is what permits system-wide rather than firm-by-firm inference.

Why the static balance sheet is both wrong and necessary

The EBA's traditional static balance sheet assumption holds the composition and size of the book constant through the scenario, so maturing assets are replaced with identical assets and no management action alters the shape. It is the most criticised feature of the exercise and the criticism is correct on its own terms: no bank would behave that way.

The defence is that it is the only assumption that produces a comparable answer. Once banks may assume management actions, the results diverge on the credibility of those assumptions rather than on the resilience of the books, and a supervisor comparing fifty banks would be comparing fifty forecasts of managerial behaviour. Lecture 1's table on which management actions a supervisor will accept shows how much judgement that involves, and a comparative exercise cannot absorb that much judgement.

So the static assumption is a deliberate exchange of realism for comparability, and the honest way to read a static-balance-sheet result is as a measure of the book's resilience rather than a forecast of the bank's capital. A bank that fails such a test has a vulnerable portfolio; it does not necessarily have a vulnerable future, because it would in fact act. That distinction is the whole content of the criticism and of the defence, and both are right.

The stress capital buffer, and what a formula does to behaviour

THE FORMULA stress capital buffer = peak-to-trough decline in the CET1 ratio under the severely adverse scenario + four quarters of planned common dividends subject to a floor of 2.5 per cent WHAT EACH TERM IS DOING The peak-to-trough decline is lecture 1's capital planning buffer logic: size the buffer to the worst point on the path. Adding four quarters of planned dividends prevents a bank from reducing its requirement by planning to distribute less. If distributions are planned they must be pre-funded. The 2.5 per cent floor means the buffer can never fall below the capital conservation buffer level, so a bank whose stress result is benign still holds the standard buffer. WHAT A FORMULA CHANGES Under a judgement-based regime a bank argues about what the result means. Under a formula it argues about the inputs, because the inputs are the only thing that moves the answer. Since the supervisor runs the models, the bank cannot argue about the models either. What is left is the portfolio data submitted and the composition of the book itself. So the formula plus supervisor-run models pushes the bank's response out of the modelling function and into the balance sheet: if you cannot change the model or the interpretation, you change the exposures. IS THAT GOOD OR BAD? Both, and it is worth holding both. It is exactly what a capital rule is supposed to do, which is to make risky exposures expensive. And it concentrates the entire modelling judgement in one institution, which step 5 argues is the deepest structural difference between the programmes.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Supervisor-run models concentrate model risk in one place

The deepest difference between the programmes is who owns the models, and its consequence is a familiar one in a new setting.

When each bank runs its own models under constraint, fifty banks produce fifty views. Those views vary in quality and some are self-serving, which is why the constraints exist. They are also diverse, so a modelling error is unlikely to be common to all of them, and the supervisor sees a distribution of answers rather than a single one.

When the supervisor runs one set of models across every bank, the answers become comparable in the strongest possible sense, because the same functions are applied to every portfolio. And every bank's requirement now depends on one modelling apparatus. If that apparatus misjudges a portfolio type, every bank holding that portfolio is mis-capitalised in the same direction at the same time, and no diversity of view exists anywhere in the system to reveal it.

That is the same trade-off market risk faced between internal models and the standardised approach, and it resolved differently there. FRTB's standardised approach imposes common parameters and accepts crudeness in exchange for comparability and immunity from optimism, while internal models preserve bank-specific judgement at the cost of comparability and with elaborate machinery to police the optimism. A supervisor-run stress test is the standardised philosophy carried further: not merely common parameters but a common model, run by the party setting the requirement.

Neither resolution is obviously right and the choice should be made knowingly. The relevant question is whether you are more worried about banks being wrong in self-serving ways, which argues for supervisor-run models, or about everyone being wrong in the same way at once, which argues against.

Disclosure makes a soft test hard

The EBA's position that its exercise has no pass or fail is formally accurate and practically incomplete, and the gap between those is instructive.

Results are published bank by bank in granular detail. An analyst can therefore compute whatever hurdle the EBA declined to set, rank the banks, and publish the ranking, and the market will price the outcome regardless of the supervisor's refusal to grade it. A bank whose capital ratio falls furthest under the scenario faces funding cost consequences that no formal designation was needed to produce.

So disclosure converts a judgement-based exercise into a quasi-binding one through market discipline, which is a mechanism the writing track's material on obscurity would recognise: the information is published, and publishing it has effects the publisher did not have to intend. The EBA gets comparability and market pressure without having to defend a threshold, which is a defensible position and not a neutral one.

It also creates the incentive that any published measure creates. A bank that knows its result will be published and ranked has a reason to manage towards the exercise, which brings us to the sharpest criticism of the whole enterprise.

Teaching to the test, and correlated positioning

Concurrent stress testing with a prescribed scenario has an effect that is easy to state and hard to design around. Every participating bank is optimising against the same scenario at the same time.

Individually that is the intended behaviour: a bank that reduces its vulnerability to a severe recession has done what the exercise wanted. Collectively it means the system becomes well hedged against the prescribed path and no better hedged against anything else, and possibly worse, if hedging the prescribed scenario requires taking positions that are exposed to a different one. Diversity of positioning is a source of systemic resilience, and a common test erodes it.

That is the strongest argument for the two things this track covers next to the prescribed exercise. A bank's own scenarios, required in its internal capital assessment, exist partly so that its risk management is not entirely shaped by the supervisor's imagination. And reverse stress testing, which lecture 3 treats, inverts the question specifically so that the scenario is derived from the bank's vulnerabilities rather than chosen in advance, which is the only one of the three exercises capable of surfacing a risk nobody had listed.

The honest summary is that supervisory stress testing is a good instrument with a known failure mode, and the failure mode is not fixable within the instrument. It is addressed by running other exercises alongside it, which is why the framework requires them rather than treating the supervisory test as sufficient.

Common exam error Describing the EBA exercise as having a pass mark, or the US exercise as merely informing supervisory judgement. The EBA sets no hurdle and says so; the US result feeds a formula that produces a capital requirement. Getting these the wrong way round loses the point of the comparison, which is that jurisdictions made genuinely different choices about how binding to make the output.
Exam tip Learn the three axes rather than the programme details, because the axes are stable and the calendars are not. Who models, whether there is a pass mark, and what the result does. If you can then say that the Bank of England's scenario is calibrated countercyclically and explain why that matters, you have the one design feature in this area worth remembering by name.
