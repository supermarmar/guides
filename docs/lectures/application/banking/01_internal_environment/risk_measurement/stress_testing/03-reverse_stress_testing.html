Reverse stress testing

Run on a single risk factor it always concludes that failure is implausible. Run on combinations it concludes that failure is entirely plausible, and the second answer is the correct one. That difference is the whole exercise.

Lecture 3 of 3 Reading time, 34 minutes Source: wiki one-line definition; PRA and published material
Step 1

The 30-second version

A forward stress test asks what a given scenario does to capital. A reverse stress test asks what would make the business model unviable, works backwards to the magnitude of loss that implies, identifies scenarios capable of delivering it, and then assesses whether any of them is plausible.

The target is viability, not solvency, and that is what makes it different in kind rather than merely in direction. A bank can be adequately capitalised and non-viable, if the market withdraws funding or the franchise is destroyed, and it can breach a buffer and remain perfectly viable. Defining the failure point is therefore the first and hardest step.

It is also the only one of the three exercises that can surface a risk nobody had listed, because it starts from the outcome rather than from a scenario. Forward tests can only test risks someone already named, which is lecture 2's correlated-positioning problem, and reverse testing is the structural answer to it.

The catch is that the plausibility judgement carries all the weight and is unfalsifiable, and the bank has an obvious incentive to find the identified scenario remote. Step 4 shows why that incentive is usually satisfied by a methodological choice rather than by dishonesty: single-factor reverse testing produces absurd requirements, and combination testing does not.

Feynman test Do not ask what a recession does to you. Ask what would kill you, work out how big a hole that is, then work out what could dig it. If you only consider one thing going wrong at a time you will conclude nothing could. Consider several ordinary things going wrong together and you will conclude otherwise, and you will be right.
Beyond the wiki The wiki gives reverse stress testing one sentence, as the third of three scenario types. Everything here is built from published supervisory expectations and from the mechanics themselves. The single-factor against combination argument in step 4, with its worked arithmetic, is this lecture's own, as is the connection in step 5 to the writing track's treatment of unfalsifiable claims.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that it answers the question a board actually has. A forward stress test answers whether the bank passes, which is a compliance question. A reverse stress test answers how far the bank is from failure and by what route, which is what a director needs to know and what no other exercise in this track provides.

The second reason is that it is the structural answer to the limitation the previous two lectures identified. Lecture 1 conceded that a stress test cannot tell you whether the scenario is right; lecture 2 argued that concurrent testing against one prescribed path erodes diversity of positioning. Both problems come from starting with a scenario. Reverse testing does not start with one.

The third reason is that it is where the failure point gets defined, and that definition has consequences well beyond this exercise. Recovery planning calibrates its triggers relative to the failure point, and resolution planning assumes it has been reached, so a bank that has defined non-viability loosely has miscalibrated two other frameworks at once.

And a fourth reason about intellectual honesty. This is the one exercise in the collection whose entire output is a judgement that cannot be checked, and watching how a well-run bank handles that is instructive for anyone who has to make unfalsifiable claims defensibly. Which is most professionals, most of the time.

Step 3

The mental model

The direction of inference reversed, the five steps, and where reverse testing sits in the chain that ends in resolution.

Reverse stress test
An exercise that begins from a defined point of business model non-viability, works back to the loss magnitude implied, identifies scenarios capable of producing it, and assesses their plausibility.
Point of non-viability
The point at which the business model can no longer be carried on, whether through capital exhaustion, loss of funding access, franchise destruction or operational failure. Normally reached before a capital minimum is breached.
Recovery trigger
A pre-defined indicator level at which a bank's recovery plan actions are considered or invoked, calibrated relative to the failure point that reverse stress testing identifies.
Step 4

The detail

Worked backwards, two ways, with opposite conclusions

A retail bank with R500bn of risk-weighted assets and a 14 per cent capital ratio, so R70bn of capital. Its Pillar 1 and Pillar 2A requirement is 10.5 per cent. It judges that funding access would be lost, and the business model would become unviable, at around 7 per cent, well below the requirement but above zero. All figures invented; the arithmetic is reproducible.

SIZE THE HOLE capital today = 500 x 14.0% = R70bn capital at failure point = 500 x 7.0% = R35bn CAPITAL DESTRUCTION REQUIRED = R35bn ROUTE A: ONE RISK FACTOR AT A TIME Ask what the mortgage book alone would have to do. Mortgage book = R300bn. required loss rate on the book = 35 / 300 = 11.67% Convert to a default rate at various loss severities: at LGD 40% -> default rate 29.2% at LGD 30% -> default rate 38.9% at LGD 25% -> default rate 46.7% CONCLUSION: between roughly 29% and 47% of the mortgage book must default. No mortgage market has done that. The scenario is dismissed as implausible. And notice: the BETTER the collateral, the lower the LGD, the HIGHER the default rate required, so the more absurd the answer looks. A well-secured book makes single-factor reverse testing conclude implausibility more emphatically. ROUTE B: SEVERAL THINGS AT ONCE, NONE OF THEM EXTREME mortgage losses 10% PD x 30% LGD x R300bn R 9.00bn SME and corporate 8% PD x 55% LGD x R120bn R 5.28bn an operational loss event R 4.00bn net interest income compression over horizon R 6.00bn market and other losses R 3.00bn subtotal R27.28bn Plus the effect lecture 1 identified: RWA inflates in the same scenario. A 15% inflation takes RWA from R500bn to R575bn, so holding 7% now requires R40.25bn rather than R35bn, an extra R 5.25bn EFFECTIVE TOTAL R32.53bn required R35.00bn coverage 93% CONCLUSION: five unremarkable components plus ordinary procyclical risk-weight inflation get 93% of the way to non-viability. A modest worsening of any one of them closes the gap. Not one component is individually implausible. A 10% mortgage default rate is a bad recession. An 8% SME default rate is a bad recession. A large operational loss happens to someone every year. THE POINT Route A concludes failure is impossible. Route B concludes failure is a bad recession plus one piece of bad luck. Same bank, same failure point, same arithmetic. The difference is entirely methodological, and Route B is the correct method. WHY THIS MATTERS FOR THE PLAUSIBILITY JUDGEMENT A bank wishing to conclude implausibility does not need to be dishonest. It only needs to run the exercise one risk factor at a time, which is the natural way to run it, because that is how the risk inventory is organised. Single-factor reverse stress testing is the mechanism by which an honest bank reaches a false conclusion.

Defining the failure point

Candidate definitionIs it the failure point?Why
The definition determines the answer, so it is where the exercise is won or lost
Capital falls below the Pillar 1 minimum No, too late A bank in that position has usually already lost funding access and been recapitalised or resolved. Working back from here understates how close failure is.
Capital falls below the combined buffer requirement No, too early Buffers exist to be used, so breaching one is the framework working. Distribution restrictions follow, and viability does not end.
The point at which wholesale funding access is withdrawn Frequently yes, for a bank reliant on it Funding withdrawal ends viability regardless of the capital position, and it happens at a level counterparties choose rather than one the framework specifies.
The point at which the liquidity coverage ratio can no longer be met Yes, and it may bind first Liquidity failure is faster than capital failure. A bank can be solvent on Friday and gone on Monday, which is the point IRRBB lecture 4 closed on.
Loss of a critical franchise or a core deposit base Yes, and hardest to quantify A bank whose deposit franchise has left is not viable in its current form even if ratios hold, because the business model depended on cheap funding that no longer exists.
An operational or conduct event that removes the licence to operate a business line Yes, and usually omitted Non-financial and therefore invisible to a capital-based reverse test, yet it has ended banks. A reverse test defined purely in capital terms cannot find this route at all.

The last row is the one that most reveals the exercise's dependence on its own framing. A reverse stress test defined as "what capital loss makes us non-viable" cannot discover a route to failure that does not run through capital, and several historical bank failures did not. Defining failure in terms of the business model rather than the balance sheet is what makes the search genuinely open, and it is harder work.

The link into recovery and resolution

The exercise's output feeds two other frameworks and the connection is mechanical rather than thematic. A recovery plan specifies indicators, trigger levels, and management options available as conditions deteriorate. Those triggers have to be set somewhere, and the only defensible reference point is the failure point: triggers are placed far enough above it that the options still have time to work.

Which means a bank that has located its failure point too low, by defining it as a capital minimum breach rather than as loss of funding access, will set its recovery triggers too low as well. The plan will then invoke options at a point where the market has already withdrawn, so the options that depended on market access, raising capital, issuing debt, selling assets at reasonable prices, are precisely the ones no longer available.

Resolution planning inherits the same assumption from the other side. A resolution authority planning for a bank's failure needs to know what the bank looks like at the point of non-viability, and if the bank's own analysis puts that point in the wrong place then the resolution strategy has been designed for a different institution. That is why supervisors take the failure point definition seriously rather than treating it as a modelling preliminary.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

An unfalsifiable conclusion needs a governance answer, not a technical one

Step 4 of the method asks whether an identified route to failure is plausible, and there is no test for the answer. The bank asserts a judgement, the supervisor accepts or challenges it, and no data settles the matter, because the whole point is that the event has not happened.

That is a familiar problem in a new setting, and the writing track's closing module names it precisely. "The scenario is considered remote" is exactly the level-3 formulation that lecture 7 of module 9 described: technically true, unfalsifiable, and discharging an obligation without informing anyone. It is what an honest person writes when the alternative is a difficult conversation.

So the answer has to be procedural rather than analytical, and three requirements do most of the work. The judgement must be made by the board rather than by the modelling function, because it is a judgement about the institution's tolerance rather than about a model. It must be argued rather than asserted, with the reasoning for implausibility written down and specific. And it must name what would change the answer, which is the Toulmin rebuttal condition from the same module: a plausibility judgement with no stated conditions under which it would flip is not a judgement, it is a position.

The practical test worth applying is the one that module offered for any unfalsifiable claim. Would the author accept this formulation if they were the reader who had to act on it? A board member reading "management considers the scenario remote" has been told nothing, and a board member reading "this route requires a mortgage default rate of 29 per cent, which exceeds anything observed in this market, and our conclusion would change if the combination analysis in appendix B were extended to include a funding cost shock" has been told something they can act on.

Why single-factor testing is the real failure mode

The step 4 arithmetic is the most useful thing in this lecture and its implication deserves stating directly. A bank does not need to be dishonest to reach a false conclusion from a reverse stress test. It only needs to run the analysis the way its risk inventory is organised.

Risk registers are structured by risk type. Credit risk sits in one place, operational in another, market risk in a third, and the natural way to ask what would cause failure is to ask it of each in turn. Every answer comes back absurd, because no single risk type acting alone can destroy a well-capitalised bank, and the aggregate conclusion is that failure is implausible.

That conclusion is false and every individual step was reasonable, which is the most dangerous shape an analytical error can take. The combination is where the plausibility lives, and combinations are not in the risk register because the register is a taxonomy rather than a model of how things go wrong together.

Which suggests the single most valuable discipline available in this area. Require the reverse stress test to work in combinations from the outset, and require the combinations to be built from components that are each individually unremarkable. If a bank can only reach its failure point through an extreme value of one variable, it has not finished the exercise.

What this exercise is really for, and its honest limits

Reverse stress testing produces no number that goes into a capital requirement, no comparable metric, and no supervisory ranking. Judged as a measurement exercise it fails on every count, which is why it receives less attention than the forward tests despite being more informative.

Its value is different in kind. It is the only exercise that can identify a route to failure nobody had listed, and the only one that answers the question a board is accountable for. It also forces the failure point to be defined, which propagates usefully into recovery and resolution planning, and it forces someone to think about combinations, which nothing else in the framework does.

Three limits should be held alongside that. The plausibility judgement is unfalsifiable and the governance answer above mitigates rather than solves it. The search is only as open as the failure definition, so a capital-framed test cannot find a conduct-driven failure. And there is no comparability, so a supervisor cannot benchmark one bank's reverse stress test against another's, which means the quality of the exercise depends almost entirely on the seriousness with which the individual firm conducts it.

That last point is the honest closing observation for this track. Of the three exercises, the two that produce comparable numbers are the two that cannot find an unlisted risk, and the one that can find an unlisted risk produces nothing a supervisor can compare. That is not a defect anyone has solved; it is why the framework requires all three.

Common exam error Defining the failure point as a breach of the capital requirement. Non-viability is normally reached earlier, because funding access is withdrawn at a level counterparties choose rather than one the framework specifies, and a reverse test anchored to the regulatory minimum will understate how close failure is and miscalibrate the recovery triggers that depend on it.
Exam tip If asked to critique a reverse stress test, the two highest-value observations are that the failure point may be defined too narrowly, in capital terms only, and that the analysis may have been run one risk factor at a time. Both produce a false conclusion of implausibility, and both are far commoner than deliberate optimism.
