The modelling approach

Hazard, exposure and vulnerability, which is the catastrophe modelling triad borrowed intact from general insurance. A bank cannot change the hazard, and the worked example shows that changing vulnerability alone can take loss given default from nineteen per cent to zero.

Lecture 2 of 2 Reading time, 36 minutes Source: wiki climate_risk/02-modelling_approach.md
Step 1

The 30-second version

The framework has three components. A hazard for physical risk, or a driver for transition risk, being the thing that causes damage. Exposure, the extent to which a counterparty is exposed to it. And vulnerability, any adaptation the counterparty has already made to reduce the impact.

That is the catastrophe modelling triad from general insurance, borrowed intact, and its structure matters: the three terms combine multiplicatively, so a bank that cannot influence the hazard can still act on the other two. Exposure is a lending decision and vulnerability is a lending condition.

The data requirement splits the two channels apart entirely. Physical risk needs geographic granularity, from weather station, satellite or interpolated sources, each with distinct weaknesses. Transition risk needs sectoral granularity and firm-level emissions, which are hard to source, mostly undisclosed among wholesale exposures, and uncertain even when firms estimate them themselves.

And the honest closing position, which the wiki states directly: there is no best way to model this and no single benchmark model in use, despite several central banks having run climate stress tests. What exists is a framework, a data problem and a set of published scenarios.

Feynman test Three questions: how often does the bad thing happen, how much of your book is standing in front of it, and has anyone built a wall. You cannot change the first. The example below shows that building the wall can take your loss from nineteen per cent of the loan to nothing.

Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the triad tells a bank where it has agency. Almost all climate discussion is about the hazard, which no individual bank affects. The framework's decomposition shows that a lender's levers are exposure and vulnerability, and the step 4 arithmetic shows the vulnerability lever is powerful enough to eliminate a loss given default entirely on a normally geared mortgage.

The second reason is that the data asymmetry has an expensive operational consequence. Physical risk needs data organised by location and transition risk needs it organised by sector and counterparty emissions, and a bank's systems are organised by neither, being built around product and legal entity. Two new data architectures are required, which is why climate programmes cost what they cost.

The third reason is that stranded assets are the mechanism by which transition risk becomes credit loss, and they have a technical property worth knowing: they impair collateral value and borrower cash flow simultaneously, so probability of default and loss given default move together for a structural rather than a cyclical reason. Credit models normally handle that correlation as a downturn adjustment, and this is not a downturn.

And a fourth reason about intellectual honesty. This is an area where the correct professional answer to many questions is that it cannot currently be estimated, and lecture 1 established why. Knowing which parts are genuinely tractable, physical risk on geocoded collateral, and which are not, counterparty-level transition risk, is more useful than a method that pretends to cover both.

Step 3

The mental model

The triad, where each data category feeds it, and which terms a bank can actually move.

Hazard and driver
The factor causing damage. Hazard for physical risk, being a climate event or condition; driver for transition risk, being the source such as a carbon policy or technology displacement.
Vulnerability
Adaptation the counterparty has already implemented to reduce a hazard's impact. The term a lender can influence through conditions, and the one the worked example shows is capable of eliminating a loss given default.
Stranded asset
An asset that has suffered an unanticipated or premature write-down, whether through technological obsolescence, unsustainable property values, or dependence on a high-emission industry. The mechanism converting transition risk into credit loss.
Step 4

The detail

From hazard to credit loss, with the vulnerability lever quantified

A mortgage book in a flood zone, taken through the triad to a loss given default. All figures invented; the arithmetic is reproducible.

A SINGLE PROPERTY property value R1,500,000 loan R1,200,000 loan to value 80% VULNERABILITY, WITHOUT ADAPTATION: 35% damage value after the flood R 975,000 loan to value now 123% LOSS GIVEN DEFAULT 18.75% VULNERABILITY, WITH FLOOD DEFENCES: 12% damage value after the flood R1,320,000 loan to value now 91% LOSS GIVEN DEFAULT 0.00% >>> THE LEVER IS DECISIVE, AND HERE IS WHY At 80% loan to value the loan has a R300,000 equity cushion. A 12% loss of value, R180,000, is absorbed entirely by that cushion. A 35% loss, R525,000, is not. So reducing vulnerability does not merely reduce the loss proportionately. It can move the outcome across the threshold at which the bank has any exposure at all, and on this gearing it does. Which gives a lending policy implication: in a flood zone, requiring adaptation as a condition of lending is worth more than pricing for the risk, because it changes whether there is a risk to price. NOW THE PORTFOLIO, WITHOUT ADAPTATION properties in the zone 8,000 hazard: annual probability of the flood 2% expected properties flooded per year 160 baseline, no flood: PD 1.5%, LGD 2% conditional on flood: PD 8.0%, LGD 18.75% expected loss on those 160 properties baseline 160 x 1.5% x 2.00% x R1.2m = R 57,600 post-flood 160 x 8.0% x 18.75% x R1.2m = R2,880,000 MULTIPLE 50x DECOMPOSING THE FIFTY PD effect alone 8.0 / 1.5 = 5.3x LGD effect alone 18.75 / 2.00 = 9.4x product 5.3 x 9.4 = 50x >>> THIS IS THE POINT THAT MATTERS TECHNICALLY The fifty-fold increase is not a large PD shock. It is a moderate PD shock multiplied by a large LGD shock, and the two arise from the same event. Credit models routinely treat PD and LGD as separately estimated and combine them, handling their correlation through a downturn adjustment to LGD. A flood is not a downturn. It is a single event that impairs the borrower's cash flow and the collateral's value simultaneously, so the correlation is structural. A model that shocks PD for climate and leaves LGD at its long-run average will understate the loss by roughly the LGD multiple, which here is a factor of nine.

Why sector is a weak proxy, stated statistically

The wiki gives the objection concretely: two utilities firms, one generating from coal and one from renewables, carry very different carbon profiles and the same sector classification. It is worth putting that in the general form, because it tells you when the proxy is acceptable and when it is not.

A proxy variable is useful in proportion to how much of the variation in the thing you care about it explains, which means the between-group variance must be large relative to the within-group variance. Sector works well as a proxy for, say, the cyclicality of revenue, because firms in one sector genuinely share a cycle. It works badly for carbon intensity because the within-sector spread is enormous: utilities span coal and wind, transport spans shipping and rail, and manufacturing spans cement and software assembly.

That reframing is useful because it is testable. A bank using sector as a transition proxy can measure the within-sector dispersion of emissions intensity on the subset of counterparties that do disclose, and thereby estimate how much error the proxy introduces across the rest. That is a considerably better position than either using the proxy uncritically or refusing to use it, and it is an ordinary experience-analysis exercise.

What is currently estimable, and what is not

QuestionCurrently estimable?Why
The honest division, given the wiki's admission that no benchmark model exists
What proportion of my mortgage collateral sits in a flood zone? Yes Requires geocoded exposure data and published hazard maps. Both exist, the method is the catastrophe modelling one, and the answer is checkable.
What does a flood do to loss given default on those exposures? Yes, with assumptions Step 4's calculation. The damage ratio is the uncertain input and insurance loss experience informs it.
How will physical hazard frequency change by 2050 under a given scenario? Partly Published pathways give regional projections with genuine uncertainty ranges. Usable for direction and magnitude, not for a point estimate.
What is this corporate borrower's emissions intensity? Rarely A minority of wholesale counterparties disclose, proxies carry the dispersion problem above, and self-reported figures are uncertain.
What carbon price will apply in 2040? No A policy forecast, not a modelling output. Scenarios assume a path rather than predicting one, which is what makes them scenarios.
What is the probability of default adjustment for climate risk? No benchmark exists The wiki states this directly. The parameters that must move are identified and the magnitude is not, which is the honest position.

The pattern in that column is the same asymmetry lecture 1 identified. Physical risk questions are tractable in proportion to how much they resemble a catastrophe modelling problem, and transition risk questions are intractable in proportion to how much they depend on a policy forecast. A methodology that treats both channels identically has misjudged at least one.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Climate scenarios cannot use stress test machinery

A bank that has built the infrastructure the stress testing track described has a natural instinct: run the climate scenario through it. That instinct is wrong for a structural reason worth being explicit about.

A supervisory stress scenario runs three to five years, holds the balance sheet approximately static, and asks whether current capital survives. A climate scenario runs thirty to eighty years, over which no bank's balance sheet survives unchanged: the mortgage book turns over several times, businesses that do not yet exist become borrowers, and the bank's strategy changes more than once. Holding the balance sheet static over that horizon does not produce a conservative answer, it produces a meaningless one.

So climate scenario analysis has to model balance sheet evolution, which the stress testing framework deliberately suppresses in order to achieve comparability. That is not a technical inconvenience; the two exercises answer different questions. A stress test asks whether today's book survives a recession. A climate scenario asks whether the business model survives a transition, which is much closer to the reverse stress test question and much further from the capital adequacy one.

Which explains why the central bank exercises in this area have been styled as exploratory rather than capital-setting. They are, in the taxonomy from lecture 2 of the stress testing track, disclosure-and-learning exercises with no pass mark and no formula, and that is the appropriate design given what they are measuring. Anyone expecting a climate capital charge to emerge from them has misread the exercise.

The scenario families, and why there are three rather than a severity range

The published scenario sets are organised as a small number of coherent narratives rather than as a range of severities on one axis, and lecture 1 explained why: the physical and transition channels trade off, so severity is not one-dimensional.

The three shapes are an orderly transition, in which policy tightens early and predictably so transition costs are moderate and physical damage is contained; a disorderly transition, in which action is delayed and then abrupt, so transition costs are severe while physical damage is still partly contained; and a failure to transition, in which policy does not tighten materially, transition costs stay low and physical damage becomes severe later.

A bank reading those three should notice that its own vulnerability differs across them in a way that reveals its positioning. A carbon-intensive lender is hurt most by the disorderly path and least by the failure path. A coastal-property and agricultural lender is the reverse. If a bank finds all three roughly equally damaging, it is genuinely diversified across the channels; if it finds one clearly worst, it has learned what it is short of, and that is more useful information than a single severity number.

Note also that the naming and composition of published scenario families has changed across vintages, so an answer should describe the three shapes and their logic rather than reciting labels, which is both safer and more informative.

Where an actuary should be confident and where not

Lecture 1 argued that general insurance is ahead on physical risk. This lecture makes that concrete, because the framework in step 3 is the catastrophe modelling framework, unchanged. Hazard, exposure and vulnerability is exactly how a property catastrophe model is constructed, and the step 4 calculation is a cat model output feeding a credit parameter.

So an actuary approaching bank physical risk should be confident, and should bring three specific habits. Geocoded exposure at the individual property level, because averaging across regions destroys the answer, which the wiki says directly. Vulnerability functions that vary with construction and adaptation rather than a single damage ratio. And an explicit uncertainty range on the hazard, because catastrophe modelling has never pretended a return period is known precisely.

Where the confidence should stop is transition risk, and the reason is worth stating without hedging. There is no actuarial technique for forecasting legislation. The scenarios substitute an assumed policy path for a forecast, which is legitimate and is not estimation, so a transition risk number carries the uncertainty of the policy assumption entirely and no amount of modelling sophistication reduces it. An actuary who presents a transition risk estimate with the same confidence as a physical risk estimate has misrepresented the second.

The honest closing position for this track is the wiki's own. No best way exists, no benchmark model is in use, and several central banks have run exercises without converging. That is not a gap awaiting a method; for the transition channel it may be a permanent feature, because the quantity being estimated depends on decisions not yet made.

Common exam error Shocking probability of default for climate risk and leaving loss given default unchanged. Step 4 shows the loss given default effect can be the larger of the two and that both arise from the same event, so treating the correlation as a cyclical downturn adjustment understates the loss. If asked how climate risk affects credit parameters, name both and say why they move together.
Exam tip The triad is the highest-value thing to memorise because it structures every answer: hazard or driver, exposure, vulnerability. Add that a bank can influence only the second and third, and that physical risk needs geographic granularity while transition risk needs sectoral, and you have covered the framework and the data problem in two sentences.
