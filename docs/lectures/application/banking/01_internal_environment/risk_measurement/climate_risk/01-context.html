Lectures / Risk measurement / Climate risk

Context, disclosure and transmission

A bank barely faces climate risk directly. It faces it through its customers, which makes measurement a data problem about counterparties before it is a modelling problem, and the risk's horizon is longer than every measurement window the bank owns.

Lecture 1 of 2 Reading time, 36 minutes Source: wiki climate_risk/01-context.md
Step 1

The 30-second version

The Paris Agreement, adopted by 196 countries in 2015, aims to hold warming below 2°C and preferably to 1.5°C against an 1850 to 1900 baseline, using mandatory reporting and a ratchet that raises ambition over time. Without policy change, trajectories point well above 3°C by the end of the century.

Climate is the long-term pattern of weather, and climate risk arises because human society was built around an assumption that those patterns hold. Two responses are distinguished throughout: adaptation reduces the impact, and mitigation reduces the extent.

The organising fact for a bank is that it does not primarily face this risk directly. The main exposure runs through clients and counterparties, so the practical approach is to channel climate risk through the risk types the bank already measures: credit, market, operational and liquidity. That is also why a bank's own emissions are largely Scope 3 downstream, the funded emissions of activities its lending made possible.

And the honest position the wiki states plainly: there is no benchmark or best practice for adjusting probabilities of default or loss given default for climate risk. The parameters that would have to move are identified, and how far to move them is not.

Feynman test A bank does not own many things that flood. Its borrowers do. So the bank's climate problem is really a question about who its customers are, where their assets sit, and what a carbon policy would do to their cash flows, and banks have not historically collected any of that.
Beyond the wiki The wiki's 01-context.md is one of the better notes in the collection and supplies almost all of steps 3 and 4: the Paris framework, the institutions, the greenhouse gases, the definitions, the trends including the South African regional detail, the TCFD four pillars, the Scope 1 to 3 framework and funded emissions, and the transmission into each risk type. This lecture defines physical against transition risk, which the wiki uses without defining, quantifies the rate-of-change comparison, records what has replaced the TCFD, adds the horizon mismatch argument in step 5, and offers one careful correction on the warming figure.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the transmission insight determines everything a bank can actually do. If the exposure arrives through counterparties, then no amount of sophistication in the bank's own models substitutes for knowing where a borrower's collateral sits and how carbon-exposed its revenue is. Climate risk measurement is therefore a data acquisition programme with a modelling exercise attached, which is the reverse of how most risk types develop.

The second reason is that channelling the risk through existing risk types is the only approach that lets a bank use the skills it has. A separate climate risk model would need its own validation, its own governance and its own capital treatment. Expressing climate as an adjustment to probability of default, loss given default, market factors, operational loss frequency and funding cost keeps it inside machinery that already exists and is already challenged.

The third reason is the horizon problem, which is structural rather than a matter of effort. Pillar 1 capital runs one year, a supervisory stress test three to five, and even IFRS 9 lifetime expected loss on a mortgage reaches perhaps twenty-five. Climate risk materialises over decades, so the risk outlives every window in which the bank measures anything, and that is why disclosure and scenario analysis carry so much of the regulatory weight here rather than capital.

And a fourth reason for an ASSA candidate. The South African regional divergence the wiki records, coastal against inland and eastern against western, is directly examinable and it makes the general point concrete: a national average tells a bank almost nothing, because its exposures are geographically specific.

Step 3

The mental model

Two risk channels, the transmission through counterparties, and the four existing risk types the exposure lands in.

Physical risk
Loss arising from the climate itself, split into acute events such as flood and wildfire, and chronic shifts such as changing rainfall or sea level. Materialises whether or not policy responds.
Transition risk
Loss arising from the response to climate change: policy and legal change, technology displacement, shifting market preference and reputational effects. Materialises because policy responds, and on a faster timescale than physical risk.
Funded emissions
Emissions arising from activities made possible by a bank's funding. Sit in Scope 3 downstream and constitute the large majority of a bank's emissions footprint, which is why a bank's own operational footprint is close to irrelevant.
Step 4

The detail

The rate of change, quantified

The wiki makes a comparison worth putting numbers on, because the numbers are more persuasive than the words. It notes that the difference between the last ice age and the current climate is about 6°C, that this occurred over roughly 100,000 years, and that the human-induced change has occurred over roughly 100 years.

GLACIAL TRANSITION 6.0 C over 100,000 years = 0.000060 C per year MODERN WARMING, on the wiki's figures 1.5 C over 100 years = 0.015000 C per year RATIO 0.015000 / 0.000060 = 250 times faster ROBUSTNESS, because the exact figures are contested 1.1 C over 150 years -> 122x faster 1.2 C over 120 years -> 167x faster 1.5 C over 100 years -> 250x faster 1.5 C over 150 years -> 167x faster The conclusion survives every reasonable choice of figures: the rate of change is two orders of magnitude faster than the glacial transition, somewhere in the range of 120 to 250 times. WHY THE RATE MATTERS MORE THAN THE LEVEL FOR A BANK Adaptation has a cost and a lead time. Flood defences, relocation, crop switching and building standards all take years to implement and are financed. A given temperature change arriving over millennia imposes no adaptation cost, because the adjustment is absorbed by ordinary replacement cycles. The same change arriving within the life of a 25-year mortgage does not fit inside a replacement cycle, so it must be paid for out of current cash flows, which is exactly the borrower cash flow impairment the transmission diagram describes. A CAREFUL NOTE ON THE 1.5 C FIGURE The wiki states that global average temperature has increased by approximately 1.5 C against the pre-industrial average. That is best read as describing recent individual years rather than the multi-decade average, which assessment reports have put somewhat lower, closer to 1.1 C for the 2011 to 2020 decade against 1850 to 1900. Both statements can be true at once because single years now reach or exceed the long-run figure. The distinction matters when quoting a number, because 1.5 C is also the Paris aspiration, and conflating the observed level with the target invites the reply that the target has already been missed.

Disclosure: the four pillars, and what has replaced the body that wrote them

The Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures was formed in 2015 and published its framework in 2017, recommending disclosure across four areas. The wiki lists them and they are worth learning in the order that shows the logic: governance, then strategy, then risk management, then metrics and targets, running from who oversees it down to what is measured.

PillarWhat it asksWhy it is where it is
Four pillars, running from oversight to measurement
Governance Board and management oversight of climate risks and opportunities First, because a disclosure about metrics from a firm whose board does not consider the subject is not credible. Governance is what makes the rest believable.
Strategy Actual and potential impacts on the business strategy Second, because climate risk is a strategic question before it is a measurement one: which businesses does the firm intend to be in.
Risk management How climate risks are identified, assessed and managed Third, the process by which strategy becomes control. This is the pillar the rest of this track occupies.
Metrics and targets Quantitative exposure measures and stated reduction targets Last, because a metric without the preceding three is a number with no owner, no purpose and no process behind it.

The wiki correctly notes that the framework was recommendatory rather than mandatory, while some central banks and regulators required firms to follow it. What it does not record, because the notes predate it, is that the Task Force completed its work and was disbanded, with its recommendations absorbed into the sustainability disclosure standards issued by the International Sustainability Standards Board, whose climate standard incorporates the same four-pillar architecture.

So the substance survives and the label has changed. For an exam answer the safe formulation is to describe the four pillars, attribute them to the TCFD, and note that they have been carried into the ISSB standards and into jurisdictional requirements built on them. A candidate who describes the TCFD as the current live body is describing the position as at the wiki's writing rather than as at today.

Scope 3, and why a bank's own footprint barely matters

THE THREE SCOPES SCOPE 1 emissions from processes and assets the firm owns or controls a bank: its buildings, its vehicle fleet SCOPE 2 emissions from generating the electricity the firm purchases a bank: powering branches and data centres SCOPE 3 indirect emissions, split two ways UPSTREAM firms higher in the supply chain DOWNSTREAM firms lower in the chain, which for a bank means ITS CLIENTS THE CONSEQUENCE FOR A BANK A bank's Scope 1 and 2 emissions are those of a large office-based employer: real, measurable, and small. Its Scope 3 downstream emissions are the emissions of every activity its lending financed. These are the FUNDED EMISSIONS, and they dominate the total by orders of magnitude. So a bank that has decarbonised its own buildings has addressed a rounding error. The material question is what it lends to. WHY THIS MIRRORS THE TRANSMISSION POINT Notice the same structure as step 3's diagram. The bank's direct exposure to climate risk is small and its exposure through clients is large. The bank's direct emissions are small and its emissions through clients are large. Both facts have the same cause, which is that a bank is an intermediary. Its balance sheet is other people's activity, so both its risk and its contribution are other people's. THE MEASUREMENT PROBLEM THIS CREATES Funded emissions require, for each borrower, its emissions and the bank's proportionate share of its financing. That is counterparty-level data most banks do not hold, for borrowers who often do not measure it themselves. An accounting standard for financed emissions exists to make the calculation consistent, and consistency is not the binding constraint. Data availability is.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The horizon mismatch is why the regulatory response looks the way it does

Set the horizons side by side and an apparent puzzle resolves. Pillar 1 capital is calibrated to a one-year horizon. A supervisory stress test runs three to five years. The longest forward-looking measure in a bank is IFRS 9 lifetime expected credit loss, which on a mortgage might reach twenty-five years. Climate risk materialises over decades and its worst manifestations sit beyond the century's midpoint.

So the risk is real, potentially large, and structurally invisible to every instrument the bank uses to price risk. A one-year probability of default barely moves for a physical risk that crystallises in 2050, and a discounted lifetime loss on a twenty-five-year mortgage weights that year at almost nothing. This is the tragedy of the horizon in its precise form: the costs fall outside the window of the people whose incentives are set by the window.

Which explains a regulatory response that otherwise looks weak. Supervisors have not imposed a climate capital charge, and the reason is not merely that the modelling is immature. It is that a capital charge calibrated to a one-year horizon cannot express a decades-long risk without becoming arbitrary, so the weight has gone instead onto disclosure, scenario analysis and governance expectations, all of which operate on the strategy rather than on the balance sheet.

That is a defensible design and it has the weakness lecture 2 of the stress testing track identified. Disclosure works through market discipline, which requires a market willing to price the disclosed information over a horizon longer than its own evaluation period. Whether equity markets do that is an empirical question with a discouraging literature.

The physical and transition channels trade off, which most treatments miss

The two channels are not additive and the relationship between them is the most important structural fact for anyone designing a scenario. Strong early mitigation produces a fast, disruptive transition and limits the physical damage. Weak mitigation produces little transition disruption and severe physical damage later. A bank cannot face the worst of both.

That has a specific consequence for portfolio management which is easy to state and uncomfortable to act on. A bank heavily exposed to carbon-intensive lending is exposed to transition risk and partly hedged against physical risk, because the scenario that destroys its borrowers is the one in which warming is contained. A bank heavily exposed to coastal property and agriculture is the reverse. Diversifying across both does not diversify the risk, it guarantees participation in whichever scenario occurs.

Which is why the published scenario sets are constructed as a small number of coherent narratives, an orderly transition, a disorderly transition and a failure to transition, rather than as a range of severities on one axis. The choice a bank faces is not how bad but which shape, and the writing track's insistence that a scenario must be one coherent story rather than a collection of adverse assumptions is doing real work here.

What this looks like to an actuary, and where the discipline is ahead

General insurance has been pricing physical climate risk for longer and with better data than banking, because a property insurer reprices annually and observes claims, so its feedback loop is short. That gives the actuarial profession a genuine advantage in this material and one worth being explicit about.

The catastrophe modelling apparatus, hazard modelling, exposure mapping and vulnerability functions, is precisely what a bank needs to assess physical risk on a mortgage book, and it already exists. A bank asking what proportion of its collateral sits in a flood zone is asking a question a catastrophe modeller answers routinely, and the transferable insight is that the answer requires geocoded exposure data at property level, which is the data acquisition programme step 2 described.

Where banking is harder is transition risk, and the asymmetry is instructive. Physical risk is a natural hazard problem with a scientific basis and improving data. Transition risk is a policy forecasting problem, and no actuarial technique tells you what a government will legislate in 2035. So the two channels of the same risk sit at opposite ends of the modellability spectrum, and a bank that treats them with one methodology has misjudged at least one of them.

The honest summary for a practitioner is that the physical channel is a data problem with known methods, and the transition channel is a scenario problem with no method at all, which is why the wiki's admission that no benchmark exists for the parameter adjustment is the right admission to have made.

Common exam error Treating a bank's climate exposure as its own operational footprint. A bank's Scope 1 and 2 emissions are those of a large office employer and are immaterial; its exposure and its contribution both run through what it finances. An answer about branch energy efficiency has answered a rounding error, and the same mistake in risk terms is to assess flood risk to premises rather than to collateral.
Exam tip The highest-value structural points are that the exposure is transmitted through counterparties, that physical and transition risk trade off against each other rather than adding, and that the risk horizon exceeds every measurement window the bank has. Any of the three earns marks, and together they explain why the regulatory response is weighted to disclosure rather than capital.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. State the Paris Agreement's targets, baseline period and mechanism, and what trajectories imply without policy change.
  2. Distinguish weather, climate and climate change. Where does the risk actually come from?
  3. Define adaptation and mitigation, and give an example of each.
  4. Define physical and transition risk, including the sub-categories of each.
  5. Why do the two channels trade off rather than add? What does that imply for a carbon-intensive lender?
  6. Explain why a bank does not primarily face climate risk directly, and what follows for measurement.
  7. Do the arithmetic. Compute the ratio of the modern warming rate to the glacial transition rate on the wiki's figures, then test it on two alternative sets. Why does the rate matter more than the level for a lender?
  8. What careful distinction should be drawn about the 1.5°C figure, and why does conflating two readings of it invite a bad reply?
  9. Give the four TCFD pillars in order and justify the ordering. What has since happened to the body that wrote them?
  10. Give the three emissions scopes and say which dominates for a bank and why.
  11. What structural fact about banks explains both the transmission of risk and the concentration of emissions in Scope 3?
  12. What is the binding constraint on measuring funded emissions, and what is not?
  13. Set out the horizon mismatch and explain why it accounts for a regulatory response weighted to disclosure rather than capital.
  14. Name the four risk types the wiki channels climate risk through, with one transmission mechanism each. What consistency requirement applies where two overlap?
  15. Why is the actuarial profession ahead on physical risk, and why does that advantage not extend to transition risk?
