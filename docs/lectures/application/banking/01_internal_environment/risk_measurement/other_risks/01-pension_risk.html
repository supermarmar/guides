Pension obligation risk

A deficit consumes common equity and a surplus supplies none, which is one-sided by design. And the worked stress shows the capital charge is nearly five times the existing deficit, because it is a charge for the scheme's mismatch rather than for its shortfall.

Lecture 1 of 2 Reading time, 34 minutes Source: wiki other_risks/01-pension_risk.md
Step 1

The 30-second version

A bank's pension scheme creates risk for the bank only where it is defined benefit, or where benefits are otherwise not aligned to the performance of the scheme's assets, such as a minimum guarantee. In South Africa most schemes are defined contribution apart from legacy books, so the direct exposure is limited.

Where the exposure exists, the liability is sensitive to three things: the discount rate, typically a corporate bond yield, inflation where benefits are indexed, and longevity. The Pillar 2A charge is the stressed deficit in excess of what capital has already absorbed, with standard stresses being a fall in risk-free rates, a fall in equity markets and an adverse inflation movement.

The structural feature worth carrying is an asymmetry. A deficit must be covered from CET1 capital, and a surplus is not admitted to the bank's capital supply. Good years in the scheme do not help the bank's capital position and bad years hurt it, which is deliberate rather than an oversight.

And the Bank of England's definition reaches beyond contract. It captures liabilities to a scheme and also payments a firm makes because of a moral obligation, or because it considers it needs to, which is an economically real obligation with no legal basis, and the third instance of that pattern in this collection.

Feynman test If the bank promised people a pension rather than promising to pay into a pot, the bank owns the shortfall. Regulators make it hold capital against the shortfall getting worse, they do not let it count the surplus when things go well, and the worked example shows the charge depends far more on how the scheme is invested than on how big the hole currently is.

Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the charge is often much larger than the deficit that prompts it, and the worked example shows why. A bank looking at a modest accounting deficit and assuming a modest capital charge has misread the mechanism: the charge is set by what the deficit becomes under stress, and a scheme with a duration mismatch and equity exposure produces a stressed deficit several times the base.

The second reason is that the charge is largely a matter of choice, which is unusual among Pillar 2A risks. A bank cannot much reduce its model risk or its business model risk charge in the short term. It can reduce its pension charge substantially by changing the scheme's asset allocation, and step 4 quantifies how much.

The third reason is the asymmetry, because it explains a corporate behaviour that otherwise looks irrational. If surpluses do not count as capital and deficits do consume it, a bank has an incentive to run the scheme with a buffer above full funding and no incentive to run it at a surplus for its own sake, and it has a strong incentive to remove the volatility entirely, which is one reason so many defined benefit schemes have been closed and de-risked.

And a fourth reason for actuaries. This is the topic in the banking track where existing training transfers with no adjustment at all. The liability valuation, the sensitivities and the matching argument are the same as in any scheme funding exercise, and the only unfamiliar element is the regulatory treatment of the result.

Step 3

The mental model

When the risk exists, what moves the liability, the asymmetric capital treatment, and the two tests a mitigation must pass.

Pension obligation risk
In the Bank of England's formulation, the risk to a firm from its contractual or other liabilities to a pension scheme, or that it will make payments or contributions because of a moral obligation or because it considers it needs to for some other reason.
Stressed deficit
The scheme's shortfall of assets against liabilities recomputed under an adverse scenario. The Pillar 2A charge is the excess of this over what capital has already absorbed, so the charge measures the deterioration rather than the level.
Admissible mitigation
An offset or management action whose effectiveness depends neither on the firm's future financial performance nor on third parties' future agreement. In practice this restricts recognition to actions already taken.
Step 4

The detail

The formula, and how to read it without double counting

THE WIKI'S FORMULA EC(pension) = max( 0, stressed deficit - base deficit - CET1 deduction ) THE ECONOMIC PRINCIPLE BEHIND IT Charge capital only for the deterioration that capital has not already absorbed. The base deficit is already reflected somewhere, so charging for it again would be double counting. A READING NOTE WORTH BEING CAREFUL ABOUT If "base deficit" and "CET1 deduction" referred to the same quantity, subtracting both would relieve the charge twice. The formula is coherent because they need not be the same. The base deficit is an actuarial or accounting measure of the shortfall; the CET1 deduction is a regulatory adjustment computed under capital rules, and the two measurement bases differ. So read the formula as: take the stressed deficit, remove the shortfall already recognised, and remove separately whatever regulatory capital has already been deducted, being careful that the two removals do not overlap. Where they do coincide, only one subtraction belongs. This is worth checking in any implementation, because it is the kind of ambiguity that survives a long time in a formula everyone recognises.

A worked stress, and what actually drives the answer

A scheme with liabilities of 2,100 and assets of 2,000, so a base deficit of 100. Liability duration is 18 years. The stress is a 100 basis point fall in rates and a 30 per cent fall in equities. Three asset strategies, to isolate what the charge is really measuring. All figures invented; the arithmetic is reproducible.

BASE POSITION liabilities 2,100 assets 2,000 DEFICIT 100 THE LIABILITY UNDER STRESS, common to all three rates fall 100bp, liability duration 18 years liabilities -> 2,100 x (1 + 18 x 0.01) = 2,478 the liability rises by 378 on its own STRATEGY A: 40% equities, bonds of duration 12 years (a typical mismatch) equities 800 -> 560 (fall 30%) bonds 1,200 -> 1,344 (duration 12, rates -100bp) assets -> 1,904 stressed deficit = 2,478 - 1,904 = 574 INCREMENT over base = 474 STRATEGY B: 40% equities, bonds duration-matched at 18y equities 800 -> 560 bonds 1,200 -> 1,416 assets -> 1,976 stressed deficit = 502 INCREMENT over base = 402 STRATEGY C: no equities, fully duration-matched bonds bonds 2,000 -> 2,360 assets -> 2,360 stressed deficit = 118 INCREMENT over base = 18 THE THREE ANSWERS A 474 B 402 C 18 Matching and de-risking together reduce the charge by 96 per cent against A. >>> THE FIRST OBSERVATION: SCALE The base deficit is 100 and the Strategy A charge is 474, which is 4.7 times it. A bank reasoning from the size of its accounting deficit to the size of its capital charge will be wrong by a large multiple, because the charge measures the deterioration under stress rather than the current shortfall. >>> THE SECOND OBSERVATION, AND IT IS THE USEFUL ONE Duration matching ALONE barely helps: 474 to 402, a reduction of only 15 per cent. That is counter-intuitive, and the reason is that the equity fall of 240 dominates the duration gap. Fixing the duration mismatch improves the bond leg by 72 and leaves the equity loss untouched. Removing the equity exposure as well takes the charge to 18. So the charge is a charge for the scheme's asset- liability mismatch, and the dominant component of that mismatch is growth-asset exposure rather than duration. A de-risking programme that hedges duration and retains equities has addressed the smaller half. >>> WHY THE CHARGE IS LARGELY A CHOICE Unlike model risk or business model risk, this charge responds directly and immediately to a decision the bank controls. That is unusual among Pillar 2A risks and it is the practical reason defined benefit schemes have been so widely de-risked.

Quantification, review and the comparability problem

The bank quantifies the risk within its internal capital assessment, using stress and scenario testing to gauge how probable and how severe a deficit could become over the assessment horizon, with credit for any admissible offsets and management actions.

The supervisor then reviews that assessment and may adjust the implied requirement, and the wiki records a specific technique: supervisors may apply a standard set of stress scenarios across banks for comparability, compare the results against each bank's internal assessment, and require justification where the two diverge. That is the same device the stress testing track described, a prescribed scenario used as a comparator rather than as the answer, and it works for the same reason: it detects an outlier assumption without the supervisor having to adjudicate every assumption.

Which puts a specific obligation on the bank's own analysis. If a bank's internal assessment produces a materially smaller charge than the supervisory standard scenario, the difference has to be explained by something identifiable, most plausibly a hedge or an allocation the standard scenario does not capture. A difference that reduces to more favourable assumptions is exactly what the comparison is designed to surface.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Three instances of one pattern: obligations with no contract

The Bank of England's definition captures payments a firm makes because of a moral obligation or because it considers it needs to. That is a striking thing for a prudential rule to say, because it makes the scope of the risk depend on what the firm will feel compelled to do rather than on what it has agreed to do.

It is the third instance of that pattern in this collection and the three are worth putting together. IRRBB's floor risk arises because a bank will not charge retail depositors a negative rate, which is a commercial and reputational constraint with no contractual basis. The deposit repricing lag is valuable because depositors do not move, which is inertia rather than agreement. And here a pension obligation extends to what the bank will pay because it considers it must.

In each case the regulation is right to reach past the contract, because the economic exposure is real and a contract-only definition would understate it. And in each case the consequence is the same: the quantity being measured depends on a behavioural judgement, so it cannot be verified and the framework must constrain it in one direction rather than specify it. That is the connection to the next point.

The asymmetric capital treatment is the same technique as the IRRBB caps

Deficits consume CET1 and surpluses do not supply it. Read as an accounting statement that is inconsistent, since the same economic quantity is recognised in one direction and not the other. Read as a prudential rule it is the technique IRRBB lecture 5 identified.

A regulator does not need to know the true value of the scheme's position. It needs to know the direction in which an error would be self-serving, and to block that direction. Recognising a surplus as capital would let a bank support its balance sheet with a claim it cannot realise on demand, because a pension surplus is not distributable and its value depends on assumptions the bank itself sets. Refusing to recognise it removes the incentive to make those assumptions optimistic, at the cost of understating the bank's true position when the scheme genuinely is in surplus.

So the asymmetry is not an oversight and it is a real cost. A bank with a well-funded scheme is worse capitalised on the regulatory measure than on any economic one, and it cannot fix that by explaining. What it can do is remove the volatility, which returns to step 4's finding that the charge is a mismatch charge, and to the observation that the regulation's shape has helped drive the closure of defined benefit provision across the industry.

What an actuary should notice, and where the discipline is genuinely ahead

This is the topic in the banking track where actuarial training transfers with no translation. The liability is a projected cash flow stream discounted at a bond yield, its sensitivities are duration, inflation and mortality, and the matching argument in step 4 is the standard one. An actuary needs no new technique here at all.

Two observations are nonetheless worth making. The first is that the standard stress set omits longevity, attacking the discount rate, equities and inflation. That is defensible for a one-year or three-year capital horizon, because longevity assumptions do not move much in a year, and it means the stressed deficit understates the exposure that an actuary would consider most serious over the scheme's life. Longevity is the sensitivity that only moves one way and the one no hedge fully removes, and a capital framework built on short-horizon stresses cannot see it.

The second is that the mitigation criteria in step 3 are stricter than a scheme funding negotiation would apply. A recovery plan agreed with trustees, which is the normal mechanism for addressing a deficit, fails the second test outright, because its efficacy depends on the future behaviour of a third party. So the bank's capital assessment cannot take credit for the very arrangement that the pensions framework treats as the answer to a deficit, and a practitioner moving between the two frameworks needs to expect that mismatch rather than be surprised by it.

The honest summary is that this is an actuarial calculation embedded in a prudential framework that measures it on a shorter horizon, with a one-sided recognition rule and a stricter view of mitigation than the pensions world uses. None of those is a defect; all of them are differences worth knowing before quoting one framework's number inside the other.

Common exam error Assuming a defined contribution scheme creates pension risk for the bank. It does not, unless benefits are not aligned to asset performance, which a minimum guarantee would breach. The related error is reasoning from the size of the current deficit to the size of the capital charge: step 4 shows the charge can be several times the base deficit, because it measures the stressed deterioration.
Exam tip The two mitigation criteria are highly examinable because they are short and consequential. Not dependent on the firm's future financial performance, and not dependent on third parties' future agreement or behaviour. If you can add that this restricts recognition to actions already taken, and that an agreed trustee recovery plan therefore fails the second test, you have shown you understand what the criteria do rather than merely what they say.
