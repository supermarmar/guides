Model, business model, reputational and legal risk

The model risk charge is a percentage of the number the models produced, so it covers a proportional error exactly equal to the percentage and no more. And the risk that actually ends banks gets a judgemental charge in basis points.

Lecture 2 of 2 Reading time, 34 minutes Source: wiki other_risks/02-other_p2a_risks.md
Step 1

The 30-second version

Model risk is the risk of loss, capital miscalculation or misstatement from errors or limitations in internal models, and it is a Pillar 2A risk for any bank using internal ratings-based or similar models. The charge is a percentage uplift to Pillar 1 requirements, with the multiplier set from validation findings, back-testing, margin of conservatism analysis and model immaturity.

That construction has a structural problem worth seeing clearly. The charge is proportional to the Pillar 1 requirement, which is itself the output of the models whose risk is being covered, so a multiplier of four per cent covers a proportional understatement of exactly four per cent. If the models could be wrong by fifteen, the multiplier must be fifteen.

Business model and strategic risk is the risk that the model becomes unviable or that senior decisions prove adverse. It is assessed through scenario analysis, earnings volatility across plan scenarios and peer comparison, and the charge is set judgementally, often in basis points of risk-weighted assets. It is also the risk most likely to end a bank, which makes the measurement asymmetry worth noticing.

Reputational and legal risk complete the set, the first assessed through stress scenarios such as a reputationally driven funding run, the second as capital against material proceedings and potential fines. Both, like pension risk in lecture 1, sit on top of an accounting recognition, which creates a boundary that has to be managed.

Feynman test Capital for the chance your models are wrong, calculated as a slice of what the models said. Capital for your strategy failing, set by argument because nobody can compute it. Capital for scandal and for lawsuits. These are the honest guesses, and putting a number on a guess is still worth doing.

Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that a credit risk modeller is directly implicated in the model risk charge. Validation findings, back-testing results and margin of conservatism analysis are the inputs to the multiplier, which means the outputs of a validation function feed a capital number. A finding is not merely a finding; it has a price.

The second reason is the circularity, which is a genuine defect rather than a quibble. The step 4 arithmetic shows the charge covers a proportional error exactly equal to its own multiplier, so a bank setting the multiplier at four per cent has implicitly asserted that its models cannot be wrong by more than four per cent, which is a much stronger claim than anyone would make in words.

The third reason is the asymmetry between measurement effort and lethality. Credit and market risk have formulas, extensive validation and years of methodological development, and a bank rarely fails from either alone. Business model failure is what actually ends institutions, and it attracts a judgemental charge expressed in basis points. That may be unavoidable and it is worth naming rather than passing over.

And a fourth reason about professional honesty. This is the lecture where the correct answer is frequently that the number is argued rather than computed, and the useful distinction is between a judgement that has been reasoned and documented and one that has been asserted. The writing track's material on unfalsifiable claims applies directly, and step 5 makes the connection.

Step 3

The mental model

Four risks, how each charge is arrived at, and the boundary problem they share.

Model risk
The risk of financial loss, regulatory capital miscalculation or misstatement of financial position arising from errors or limitations in internal models. A Pillar 2A risk for any bank using internal models.
Business model risk
The risk that the current business model becomes unviable through competitive dynamics, technological disruption or macroeconomic shifts. Distinguished from strategic risk, which concerns adverse decisions at senior level.
Margin of conservatism
Additional conservatism deliberately built into a model's parameter estimates to allow for identified estimation uncertainty. Sits underneath the model risk charge, which is meant to cover uncertainty the margin does not.
Step 4

The detail

The model risk charge covers exactly its own percentage

The formula is a percentage uplift to the Pillar 1 requirement, and that requirement is produced by the models whose risk is being covered. Working through the consequence is arithmetically trivial and the conclusion is sharper than it first appears.

SETUP Pillar 1 credit RWA R400bn K(Pillar 1) at 8% R 32.00bn alpha, from the framework 4% EC(model) = 4% x 32 R 1.28bn NOW SUPPOSE THE MODELS UNDERSTATE RISK understated by 5%: true K R33.60bn, shortfall R1.60bn alpha needed to cover it: 5% charge held R1.28bn covers 80% understated by 15%: true K R36.80bn, shortfall R4.80bn alpha needed to cover it: 15% charge held R1.28bn covers 27% understated by 30%: true K R41.60bn, shortfall R9.60bn alpha needed to cover it: 30% charge held R1.28bn covers 13% >>> THE IDENTITY A proportional understatement of x per cent requires alpha of x per cent to cover it. Exactly. Not approximately. So a bank setting alpha at 4 per cent has asserted that its models cannot understate the requirement by more than 4 per cent. Nobody would make that claim in a sentence, and the choice of alpha makes it implicitly. >>> AND THE SECOND-ORDER PROBLEM The charge is computed on the understated base. on the understated base: 4% x 32.00 = R1.280bn on the true base: 4% x 36.80 = R1.472bn So the add-on meant to cover model error is itself R0.192bn too small, for the same reason. The error does not correct itself anywhere in the chain. It scales everything, including its own remedy. >>> WHAT WOULD FIX IT A charge based on the DISPERSION of plausible outcomes rather than on a multiple of the central estimate, or an absolute add-on independent of the model output. Neither is standard practice, and a validator should at least know that the standard construction has this property. >>> THE HONEST DEFENCE The construction is not indefensible. Scaling with Pillar 1 makes the charge proportionate to the size of the modelled book, which is a reasonable exposure measure, and no better practical alternative is in general use. The defect is real and the alternative is not obvious, which is the usual position.

The margin of conservatism boundary

A credit risk practitioner should notice something uncomfortable in the list of inputs to the multiplier. Margin of conservatism analysis is one of them, and margin of conservatism is already inside the parameter estimates, because that is what it is: additional conservatism added to a probability of default or loss given default to allow for estimation uncertainty.

So is model uncertainty being capitalised twice? The careful answer is no, provided the boundary is drawn deliberately. The margin of conservatism covers uncertainty that has been identified and quantified within the model, such as sampling error, data limitations or a known deficiency with an estimable effect. The model risk charge is meant to cover what the margin cannot reach: design error, limitations nobody has identified, and the general immaturity of a model with a short performance history.

They are therefore complements rather than duplicates, and the boundary between them is itself a judgement. The practical risk is not that a bank deliberately double counts but that neither framework claims the same piece of uncertainty explicitly, so it is either counted twice by accident or missed by both. A validation function that can state which uncertainties sit in the margin and which sit in the model risk charge has done something genuinely useful, and one that cannot has left a gap in an area where both frameworks assume the other is handling it.

Business model risk, and what its three methods can and cannot see

MethodWhat it detectsWhat it cannot detect
Three assessment methods for the risk least amenable to assessment
Scenario analysis against specific strategic failure modes Failure through a route somebody has already identified and articulated as a scenario. A failure mode nobody listed, which is exactly the limitation the reverse stress test in the stress testing track exists to address.
Earnings volatility across multiple business plan scenarios How much the plan's outcome varies with assumptions, which is a reasonable proxy for strategic uncertainty and is genuinely quantitative. A structural break. Volatility measured across variations of one plan says nothing about the plan being wrong in kind rather than in degree.
Peer-group comparison of business model sustainability A bank whose model is deteriorating relative to its competitors, which is a real and useful signal. An industry-wide obsolescence. Every peer looks acceptable relative to every other peer right up to the point the shared model fails, because the benchmark is the thing failing.

The third row is the most important limitation in the table and it generalises well beyond this risk. Relative benchmarking is blind to common-mode failure by construction, so a bank that assesses its business model sustainability against peers has excluded the scenario in which branch-based retail banking, or any other shared model, is displaced. The comparison is worth making and it cannot be the whole assessment.

Which leaves an odd position worth stating. Business model risk has a genuinely appropriate analytical tool, the reverse stress test, which starts from failure and works backwards precisely because it does not depend on anybody having listed the failure mode. And it has a capital charge set judgementally in basis points. The two are not connected to each other: the reverse stress test produces no number, and the basis-point charge is not derived from it. Connecting them is available to any bank that wants to, and it is not the standard practice.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The measurement effort runs opposite to the lethality

Set this track's eight folders against the question of what actually ends banks and an uncomfortable pattern appears.

Credit risk has the deepest methodology in the collection, with parameter estimation, validation, downturn calibration and years of supervisory attention. Market risk has a rewritten global standard with two capital methodologies. Liquidity risk has two prescribed ratios. Operational risk has a standardised formula. Each of these is measured seriously, and a bank rarely fails from any one of them acting alone in a way its capital did not anticipate.

Business model risk gets scenario analysis, earnings volatility and peer comparison, and a judgemental charge in basis points. Reputational risk gets a qualitative assessment. Yet business model obsolescence and reputational collapse are among the commonest routes by which institutions actually cease to exist, and the 2023 episodes turned on funding confidence rather than on any Pillar 1 parameter.

That inversion is not evidence of regulatory failure and it is worth seeing plainly. The risks that are measured well are measured well because they are measurable, and the risks that end banks are hard to measure for the same reason they are hard to manage: they are structural, slow, and specific to the institution. A framework can only quantify what has a repeatable loss distribution. What it should not do, and sometimes does, is let the quality of the measurement be mistaken for the size of the risk.

A judgemental number needs the same discipline as an unfalsifiable claim

Several charges in this lecture are judgements expressed numerically, and the writing track's closing module supplies the right standard for them, because the problem is identical. A capital charge of fifteen basis points for business model risk cannot be falsified, so the question is not whether it is correct but whether it has been reasoned.

Three requirements carry across intact. The judgement should be owned at the level with authority over it, which for business model risk is the board rather than the risk function, because it is a judgement about the institution's strategy. It should be argued rather than asserted, so that the reasoning from the three assessment methods to the number is written down and specific. And it should name what would change it, which is the rebuttal condition: a charge with no stated circumstances under which it would move is a position rather than an assessment.

The practical test is the same one that module offered. "Management considers fifteen basis points appropriate" tells a reader nothing. "Fifteen basis points reflects the earnings volatility across our three plan scenarios, which spans four hundred million rand, discounted for the two mitigating actions already executed, and we would revisit it if the digital-only competitor share exceeded ten per cent" tells a reader something they can challenge. Both are judgements and only one is auditable.

What closing this folder actually shows

This is the last lecture in the risk measurement track, and three things recur across all eight folders in a way that only becomes visible at the end.

The first is one-sided constraint. Deposit assumptions are capped and not floored, pension surpluses are excluded while deficits are deducted, margins of conservatism may be added and never subtracted, and collateral haircuts only reduce recognised value. In each case the regulator declined to determine the true value and blocked the direction in which an error would be self-serving, which is cheaper than verification and better targeted than a symmetric rule.

The second is distrust of modelled correlation. Three prescribed correlation scenarios in the market risk standardised approach, a fifty per cent haircut on modelled diversification in internal models, a floor on tenor correlations, a hedge benefit ratio that uses no correlation input, and a supervisory standard stress set used as a comparator for pension assessments. The framework consistently prefers a crude common assumption to a bank's own estimate of how risks combine.

The third is the boundary with accounting, which appeared in this lecture, in the pension formula, and in the relationship between IFRS 9 provisions and expected loss capital. Prudential charges routinely sit on top of an accounting recognition, and the boundary has to be drawn explicitly because neither framework will draw it for the other.

Those three patterns are worth more than any individual formula in the track, because they are how prudential regulation thinks. A practitioner who recognises them can predict roughly how a new risk type will be treated before the standard is written, which is a considerably more durable skill than knowing the current parameters.

Common exam error Treating the model risk charge as a measurement of model uncertainty. It is a percentage of the Pillar 1 requirement, so it covers a proportional understatement equal to its own percentage and no more, and it is computed on the very number whose accuracy is in question. Saying that is worth more than reciting the formula.
Exam tip For business model risk, the highest-value observation is that peer-group comparison cannot detect industry-wide obsolescence, because the benchmark is the thing that fails. Add that the appropriate analytical tool is the reverse stress test and that it is not connected to the capital charge, and you have identified both the limitation and the available remedy.
