Yield curves and benchmark rates

Replacing a credit-sensitive term rate with a near risk-free overnight one did not merely change a benchmark. It changed the direction in which basis risk moves under stress, because the old reference rate widened when a bank's own funding costs widened and the new one does not.

Lecture 2 of 5 Reading time, 34 minutes Source: wiki interest_rate_risk/03-yield_curves_benchmarks.md
Step 1

The 30-second version

The yield curve is the term structure of interest rates, and it takes five recognised shapes carrying five sets of implied expectations: normal, steep, humped, flat and inverted. Four hypotheses compete to explain the shape, being market expectations, liquidity preference, the money substitute view and segmentation, and in practice participants weigh all four. The operational consequence for IRRBB is that a parallel shift cannot be assumed.

Benchmark rates are what a banking book actually references, and they have just been through the largest change in their history. LIBOR was set by polling panel banks on their borrowing costs, was manipulated, and expired at the end of 2021 for sterling with the main dollar settings following in 2023. Its replacements are overnight, backward-looking and near risk-free.

That substitution has a consequence the wiki does not draw out and which matters more than the mechanics. LIBOR contained bank credit risk, so it widened when a bank's own funding costs widened, which gave a lender referencing it a partial natural hedge. An overnight risk-free rate does not widen in stress, so a bank lending at that rate plus a fixed spread while funding at its own credit spread has acquired a new basis exposure that opens precisely when it is under pressure.

South Africa's JIBAR is built differently, from negotiable certificate of deposit bid and offer rates rather than from opinion, which made it less manipulable. It is nonetheless being reformed, with ZARONIA established as the successor overnight rate, so the same transition question is live locally.

Feynman test Banks used to lend at a rate that rose when banks were in trouble, and they were banks, so it partly protected them. Now they lend at a rate that stays put when banks are in trouble. The mechanics of the swap are boring; the fact that a hedge quietly disappeared is not.
Beyond the wiki The wiki's 03-yield_curves_benchmarks.md supplies the curve shapes and their implied expectations, the four shape hypotheses, the construction methods, the LIBOR mechanism and manipulation history, the replacement table, and JIBAR's construction and code of conduct. This lecture adds the term-rate problem created by backward-looking rates, the credit-sensitivity argument in step 4 and step 5, and the ZARONIA reform, which the wiki does not mention.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the curve is the input to everything else in this track. Economic value of equity is a present value, so it depends entirely on a discount curve, and lecture 1's yield curve risk exists because the curve moves in shapes rather than levels. A measurement framework built on parallel shocks would be answering a question the market does not ask.

The second reason is that the benchmark determines where basis risk lives, which lecture 1 identified as the form invisible to a gap report. You cannot assess a bank's basis exposure without knowing which rates each side of the balance sheet references, and after the transition the answer has changed for most banks in most currencies.

The third reason is that curve construction quality is a real constraint in the markets this collection is written for. The wiki notes that construction is harder in African markets because trading is illiquid and interest rate derivatives markets are underdeveloped. That is not a footnote: a discount curve fitted through sparse and volatile data carries estimation error straight into the economic value measure, and the resulting number is less reliable than its presentation suggests.

And a fourth reason for an ASSA candidate specifically. The JIBAR reform is live, local and examinable, and a candidate who knows only that JIBAR is the South African benchmark is describing a position that is being superseded.

Step 3

The mental model

Five curve shapes, four explanations, and the benchmark substitution with what it changed.

Term structure of interest rates
The relationship between yield and maturity for instruments of the same credit quality. Moves in shapes rather than levels, which is why parallel-shift measurement is inadequate.
Alternative reference rate
An overnight, backward-looking, near risk-free rate computed from actual transactions, replacing the polled term interbank offered rates. SONIA in sterling, SOFR in dollars, ZARONIA in rand.
Bootstrapping
Deriving a zero-coupon curve from the prices of coupon-bearing instruments, tenor by tenor, so that reinvestment assumptions do not contaminate the discount rates used for valuation.
Step 4

The detail

The hedge that disappeared

THE OLD POSITION A bank lends at LIBOR + 150bp. It funds itself in the market at its own credit spread. Now a funding stress arrives. Bank credit spreads widen generally. LIBOR is a poll of what banks say they can borrow at, so LIBOR RISES with bank credit spreads. Asset yield = LIBOR + 150bp -> rises Funding cost = own spread -> rises The two move TOGETHER, imperfectly but in the same direction. The bank is partly hedged against a system-wide funding stress by the construction of the rate it lends at. THE NEW POSITION The same bank lends at SOFR + 150bp. It still funds itself at its own credit spread. The same funding stress arrives. SOFR is an overnight secured transaction rate. It contains essentially no bank credit risk, so in a funding stress it does NOT rise with spreads. Under a flight to quality it may FALL. Asset yield = SOFR + 150bp -> flat or falling Funding cost = own spread -> rising The two now move in OPPOSITE directions. The margin compresses exactly when the bank is under pressure. WHAT THIS IS, IN LECTURE 1'S TAXONOMY A new basis risk, and one with the dangerous property identified there: benign in ordinary conditions, widening in stress, invisible to a gap report. WHY THE INDUSTRY ARGUED ABOUT IT This is the reason lenders pressed for credit-sensitive alternatives to the pure risk-free rates, and why several such rates were constructed and promoted. The official sector was unenthusiastic, on the grounds that a credit-sensitive rate built on a thin market reproduces LIBOR's original defect: a rate that has to be estimated rather than observed when the underlying market dries up, which is precisely when it matters. THE HONEST POSITION Both concerns are legitimate. Risk-free rates are more robust and less manipulable, and they removed a hedge banks genuinely relied on. The trade was made deliberately in favour of robustness, and banks are expected to manage the residual basis themselves.

Curve construction, and why the method matters for EVE

MethodWhat it givesLimitation
Three constructions, and the choice affects every present value in the EVE calculation
Yield to maturity The most commonly used measure, and a single rate summarising a bond's return Assumes coupons are reinvested at the redemption yield, which is an assumption about future rates embedded in a present-value calculation. Wrong for discounting a bank's cash flows.
Zero-coupon curve A discount rate per maturity with no reinvestment assumption, which is what a present value actually requires Requires zero-coupon instruments at the tenors you need, and most markets do not have them, so it is usually derived by bootstrapping from coupon instruments.
Par-yield curve Coupon rates at which bonds would trade at par, useful for pricing new issues Answers a pricing question rather than a discounting question. Not the right curve for valuing an existing book.

The zero-coupon curve is the correct one for economic value work, and in most markets it is a construct rather than an observation. That matters because interpolation and smoothing decisions become model assumptions: where data is incomplete the analyst interpolates, and where it is volatile a fitting model and judgement are applied. Those choices propagate into every discounted cash flow in the EVE calculation.

The wiki's observation about African markets is therefore not incidental. Where trading is illiquid and the interest rate derivatives market is underdeveloped, the curve at longer tenors rests on few observations, so the discount rates applied to the longest-dated assets, which are also the most duration-sensitive, are the least reliable ones in the calculation. A bank in that position should know how much of its EVE sensitivity is attributable to a segment of the curve it cannot observe properly, and should say so when reporting the number.

Why JIBAR was harder to manipulate

The comparison between LIBOR and JIBAR is worth making carefully, because it illustrates a general point about benchmark design that the whole transition rests on.

LIBOR asked panel banks a hypothetical question: at what rate could you borrow? A hypothetical question has no verifiable answer, so submissions could be shaded without being falsifiable, and the manipulation took both available forms. Banks understated to avoid appearing to be in funding difficulty, which is a reputational motive, and banks misstated to benefit their own loan and derivative books, which is a trading motive. Both were possible because nothing tethered the submission to a transaction.

JIBAR asks a different question. It is built from the bid and offer rates at which contributing banks buy and sell their own negotiable certificates of deposit, which are instruments they actually trade, with the two highest and two lowest submissions removed before averaging. A submission is therefore checkable against the market the bank is making, and the JSE distributes contributor rates to keep submissions close to traded levels.

That is the design principle behind the entire global reform: tether the benchmark to observable transactions. The risk-free rates go further than JIBAR by computing directly from transaction data rather than from submitted quotes, which removes discretion entirely at the cost of the term structure that step 4 discussed. JIBAR sits between the two designs, which is part of why its reform has been less urgent than LIBOR's and is happening nonetheless.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

What the transition did to IRRBB measurement, beyond the basis

The basis argument in step 4 is the largest effect and there are two others worth knowing, because both affect the numbers this track produces.

The first is the loss of a forward-looking term structure in the reference rate itself. Under LIBOR, a three-month rate was known at the start of the period, so a repricing gap analysis could take contractual repricing dates and known rates. Under a compounded overnight rate the period's rate is determined by the path of the overnight rate through the period, so a repricing bucket's contribution to earnings is not known until the bucket has passed. That does not make gap analysis impossible and it does make the near-term earnings projection more model-dependent than it was.

The second is discounting. Post-crisis practice already discounted collateralised derivatives at the overnight rate rather than a term interbank rate, for the reason market risk lecture 6 gave: the discount rate should reflect the collateral. The transition aligned the reference rate with the discount rate, which is tidier and removes a source of basis between projection and discounting curves. So the reform improved one thing while worsening another, and a summary that presents it purely as a robustness gain is incomplete in both directions.

Worth adding that a bank's own transition exposure was concentrated in legacy contracts. A twenty-year swap referencing a rate that ceases mid-life needs a fallback, and the industry solution was contractual fallback language with a spread adjustment intended to make the replacement economically neutral at the switch date. Neutral at the switch is not neutral thereafter, since the two rates behave differently in stress, which returns to step 4.

The curve as an economic signal, and the trap in it

The wiki notes that economists use the curve to predict the economy's direction, and the inverted curve's association with recession is one of the more durable empirical regularities in macroeconomics. It is worth knowing and it carries a trap for a bank manager.

An inverted curve is not merely a forecast; it is a description of the bank's immediate commercial problem. A bank borrows short and lends long, so it earns the term spread, and an inverted curve means that spread is negative. So the curve shape most associated with a coming recession is also the shape that compresses the margin in advance of it, which means a bank faces margin pressure and rising credit losses in sequence rather than independently.

That sequencing is the reason IRRBB and credit risk cannot be assessed entirely separately in a stress scenario, and it is a specific instance of the general point module 8 of the writing track made about scenario coherence: an inverted curve, compressed margins and rising defaults belong to one story. A stress test that shocks credit losses while holding the margin constant has told an incoherent story, and lecture 4's scenarios exist partly to prevent it.

What an actuary should take from the curve material

Most of this is familiar territory from a different angle, and two differences in emphasis are worth naming.

An actuary constructing a discount curve for liability valuation typically works with a prescribed or regulated curve, and the construction question is largely settled by the framework. A bank constructing a curve for economic value work has more latitude and correspondingly more responsibility, since the interpolation and smoothing choices are the bank's own and they move the answer. The validation question, whether the curve construction is documented, justified and stable, is the same question asked of any model input, and it is asked less often than it should be.

The second is extrapolation beyond the observable. Actuarial practice has argued at length about extrapolating a discount curve beyond the last liquid point, and the arguments are well developed: an ultimate forward rate, a convergence period, and an explicit acknowledgement that the extrapolated segment is an assumption rather than a market observation. Banking practice in illiquid markets faces the identical problem with less established convention, and the actuarial apparatus is directly transferable. Anyone doing EVE work on a long-dated book in a market with a short liquid curve should be borrowing that thinking rather than interpolating quietly.

Common exam error Describing the LIBOR replacement as a like-for-like substitution of one benchmark for another. The replacements differ in three structural respects: overnight rather than term, backward-looking rather than forward-looking, and near risk-free rather than credit-sensitive. Each has a distinct consequence, and the third is the one with the largest effect on a bank's risk position.
Exam tip For a South African paper, know that JIBAR is constructed from NCD bid and offer rates rather than from polled borrowing costs, and know that ZARONIA has been established as its successor. The first distinguishes JIBAR from LIBOR structurally and explains why it survived longer; the second is the live development, and a candidate who mentions it has demonstrated current knowledge rather than textbook knowledge.
