Net interest income and net interest margin

Banks are slow to raise deposit rates and quick to cut them, which is worth real money and is an option written by depositors. Lecture 1's floor risk is the same option hitting its limit, and 2022 showed what happens when it is worth less than assumed.

Lecture 3 of 5 Reading time, 36 minutes Source: wiki interest_rate_risk/04-nii_nim.md
Step 1

The 30-second version

Net interest income is interest earned less interest paid, and it is the larger of a bank's two revenue streams in developed markets, with non-interest revenue mattering more in developing ones. Deducting the impairment charge gives credit loss adjusted NII, and deducting the imputed cost of the equity held against the book gives risk-adjusted NII, which is the measure that shows whether the lending business creates value.

Net interest margin divides NII by average interest-earning assets, which makes it a gross profit margin before credit losses, since the asset rate decomposes into a funding cost, a credit premium and a profit margin. It is comparable between banks in one jurisdiction and not across jurisdictions, and it cannot separate the lending margin from the deposit margin, which is what funds transfer pricing exists to do.

The analytically interesting behaviour is asymmetric repricing. Banks pass rate rises to depositors slowly and rate cuts quickly, so NII rises in the short term when rates rise and compresses when they fall. That asymmetry is an option the bank holds and the depositor has written, and it is worth a great deal.

Which connects to lecture 1. Floor risk is this same option reaching its limit: the bank can lag deposit repricing downwards only until the rate reaches zero, after which the lag becomes a floor it cannot pass through. Deposit beta asymmetry and floor risk are one phenomenon at two rate levels.

Feynman test When the central bank raises rates your mortgage repriced last month and your savings account will get around to it eventually. That gap is most of why bank margins widen when rates rise. It stops working when rates hit zero going down, and it stops working when depositors finally notice and move their money.

Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that NII sensitivity is the number a bank is actually managed on. Economic value of equity is what the supervisory outlier test uses, and it is a valuation abstraction that does not appear in any account. NII appears in the income statement every quarter, analysts model it, and management is remunerated against it, so it is the measure that drives behaviour whatever the framework emphasises.

The second reason is the three-layer decomposition, which is the most useful piece of management information in this lecture. A lending business can have a healthy NII, an acceptable credit loss adjusted NII, and a negative risk-adjusted NII, meaning it is growing while destroying value. Only the third layer reveals that, and only the third layer has the cost of the capital tied up in it.

The third reason is that the asymmetric repricing behaviour is where a large part of a retail bank's interest rate exposure actually sits, and it is behavioural rather than contractual. The bank's ability to lag deposit repricing is not written in any contract; it is a commercial fact about customer inertia, and it can change faster than a model assumes.

And a fourth reason connecting to the earlier lectures. Lecture 1 established that behavioural assumptions are IRRBB's weak point and that the Committee kept IRRBB in Pillar 2 for that reason. Deposit repricing behaviour is the single most valuable of those assumptions, and this lecture is where its value becomes visible.

Step 3

The mental model

Three layers from income to value added, the margin that summarises them, and the asymmetry that drives the cycle.

Net interest margin
Net interest income divided by average interest-earning assets. Because the asset rate decomposes into funding cost, credit premium and profit margin, NIM measures a gross margin before credit losses rather than a profit.
Deposit beta
The proportion of a change in the policy rate passed through to deposit rates. Asymmetric in practice: lower when rates rise than when they fall, which is what makes the lag valuable to the bank.
Term liquidity premium
The charge treasury levies on a lending product for the true cost of term-matched funding, so that long-dated assets cannot be priced off cheap short-term funding. The mechanism that stops funds transfer pricing subsidising maturity transformation.
Step 4

The detail

The three layers, and what the worked example leaves out

The wiki's vehicle asset finance example reconciles exactly, and it is worth reproducing because the shape of the decomposition is more instructive than the numbers.

R100m VAF PORTFOLIO customer rate 10%, write-off rate 1% funded by R10.5m equity at 15% and R89.5m debt at 6% LAYER 1 interest income 100 x (1 - 0.01) x 10% = 9.90 interest expense 89.5 x 6% = (5.37) NET INTEREST INCOME = 4.53 NIM = 4.53 / 100 = 4.53% LAYER 2 impairment charge 100 x 1% = (1.00) CREDIT LOSS ADJUSTED NII = 3.53 LAYER 3 cost of capital 10.5 x 15% = (1.575) RISK-ADJUSTED NII = 1.955 WHAT EACH LAYER TELLS YOU Layer 1 is what the income statement reports and what NIM measures. It says nothing about whether the loans are any good. Layer 2 asks whether the margin covers the losses. A book can have a fat NIM and negative layer 2. Layer 3 asks whether it covers the cost of the equity the regulator requires against it. This is the layer that decides whether to grow the portfolio. WHAT IS ABSENT, AND IT MATTERS No operating expenses. No tax. Sanity check: layer 2 divided by equity gives 3.53 / 10.5 = 33.6% No vehicle finance business earns a 33.6% pre-capital- charge return on equity after costs. The figure is high because origination, servicing, collections, overheads and tax are all excluded. So "risk-adjusted NII" is NOT profit. It is a margin measure that has been charged for credit and capital and not for running the business. THE PRACTICAL CONSEQUENCE A positive risk-adjusted NII is necessary and not sufficient for a product to be worth writing. The full test also needs a cost-to-serve allocation, which is why product-level profitability work is harder than product-level margin work and why banks frequently stop at the margin.

What NIM can and cannot do

QuestionCan NIM answer it?Why
A single ratio doing more work than it can bear
Is this bank's lending and deposit business becoming more or less profitable over time? Yes, and this is its proper use A consistent series on one balance sheet, with composition changing slowly, tracks the direction of the gross margin well.
Is this bank more profitable than its domestic peer? Partly Comparable within a jurisdiction because rate environment and market structure are shared. Differences in asset and funding mix still drive different NIMs for reasons unrelated to skill.
Is this bank more profitable than a bank in another country? No Rate levels, inflation, market structure and the balance between interest and non-interest revenue all differ. A South African NIM and a Japanese NIM are not the same quantity.
How much of the margin comes from lending and how much from deposit-taking? No, and this is the important limitation NIM is a single number spanning both sides. Separating them requires funds transfer pricing, which prices an internal transfer between each business and treasury.
What is the margin on home loans against vehicle finance? Not objectively Requires allocating funding cost to each asset type, and any allocation rests on a transfer pricing convention rather than an observable price.
Has the margin changed because spreads moved or because the book grew? No, use net interest spread alongside it NIM has an asset base in the denominator, so it moves when the book expands or contracts even with spreads unchanged. Spread strips the size effect out.

The fourth row is the one that shapes how a bank is organised. Because NIM cannot separate the two sides, a bank that wants to know whether its deposit franchise or its lending franchise is creating the value has to build a funds transfer pricing system, and the transfer rate then becomes a politically contested internal price. The term liquidity premium is the part of that price that stops a lending business from claiming a wide margin by notionally funding a twenty-year mortgage with overnight money.

NII through the cycle

GROWTH PHASE: policy rate rising VOLUME EFFECTS, and these dominate consumer spending up -> origination up -> larger book -> higher absolute NII rising real incomes widen the addressable market retained earnings up -> more CET1 supply RATE EFFECTS on a floating-rate book Small in principle: both sides float, so the net effect is a repricing timing difference. Large in practice: the bank is SLOW to reprice deposits upwards, so NII rises in the short term until deposit rates catch up. FIXED-RATE BOOK Broadly stable if the hedging worked, subject to basis between the funding base rate and the hedge's floating leg. Front book prices at market. CREDIT Losses subdued, so risk-adjusted NII improves more than NII does. DOWNTURN PHASE: policy rate falling VOLUME EFFECTS spending down -> origination down -> shrinking book -> lower absolute NII lower real income and higher unemployment shrink the target market RATE EFFECTS The bank reprices deposits DOWN faster than it raised them, and yet NII still compresses, because asset yields fall immediately and the deposit book contains balances already at or near zero that cannot fall further. FUNDING MIX, and this cuts the other way Risk-averse customers move to deposits. Retail deposit funding is cheaper than wholesale, so the mix shift IMPROVES NII, partly offsetting the compression. CREDIT Losses rise, so risk-adjusted NII deteriorates faster than NII. THE SEQUENCING POINT Notice that in a downturn margin compression and credit losses arrive together, and lecture 2 showed that the inverted curve which precedes a recession compresses the term spread in advance of it. Three adverse effects with a common cause. A stress test that treats them as independent has understated the total.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The deposit repricing lag is an unpriced written option, and 2022 repriced it

The wiki describes asymmetric repricing behaviour accurately and treats it as a feature of pricing strategy. It is better understood as an option, and seeing it that way changes what you do about it.

A depositor holding a non-maturity account has written the bank an option to lag rate increases. The option's value depends on customer inertia, which depends on the attractiveness of alternatives and on how much attention the customer is paying. In a low-rate environment the option is worth little because the alternatives pay nothing either. In a rising-rate environment it is worth a great deal, and that value is precisely the shaded area in step 3's diagram.

The 2022 and 2023 tightening cycle tested it and found most banks' assumptions optimistic. Rate rises were unusually fast and large, money market funds and higher-paying digital accounts became visibly attractive, and deposit betas rose faster than models calibrated on the previous decade had projected. Some banks retained cheap deposits and many did not, and the ones that lost them discovered that the asset they had modelled as a stable long-duration liability was neither.

Which is lecture 1's warning arriving in concrete form. The behavioural assumption was estimated on a period in which it could not be tested, because for a decade there was no alternative worth switching for. It described inertia in the absence of temptation, and it was relied on in the presence of temptation. The direction of the error was predictable in advance and predicted by very few.

When NII and EVE disagree, and why the deposit assumption decides it

The two IRRBB perspectives can point in opposite directions on the same balance sheet, and understanding why is the substance of the discipline.

Consider a retail bank funding fixed-rate mortgages with non-maturity deposits, and suppose rates rise. On the earnings measure the bank gains, for the reason this lecture has been about: asset yields on the front book rise, deposits lag, and NII improves over the one to two year horizon the measure covers.

On the value measure the answer depends entirely on how long the deposits are assumed to last. If they are modelled as short-duration, repricing almost immediately, then the bank holds long fixed assets against short liabilities, rising rates cut the assets' present value with no offsetting liability gain, and economic value of equity falls. If they are modelled as long-duration, a stable franchise paying a rate well below market for years, then the value of that franchise rises with rates, because the spread earned on cheap funding widens, and economic value of equity can increase.

Same balance sheet, same shock, opposite sign, and the switch is a behavioural assumption that cannot be observed directly. That is the clearest possible demonstration of why the Committee declined to make IRRBB a Pillar 1 charge, which lecture 1 set out: a standardised formula would have to fix the assumption that determines the answer's sign. It is also why the framework requires both measures rather than choosing between them, since a bank optimising one can be worsening the other.

Finance and treasury measure different things, and both are right

The wiki closes on a divergence worth taking seriously rather than treating as an organisational irritation.

Finance reports headline NIM per business line on contractual balance sheet run-off, from product views submitted by the businesses. That view is accurate about what happened and it is blind to repricing structure: it will show a stable margin on a book whose rate sensitivity has changed materially, because contractual run-off does not reveal behaviour.

Treasury measures mismatch on both contractual and behavioural bases, models prepayment and withdrawal, and uses earnings at risk and economic value measures alongside sensitivity metrics because it knows the repricing gap ignores basis risk. That view is accurate about exposure and is not the view management is remunerated on.

The failure mode is not that either is wrong but that the two are reconciled rarely. A business line reporting an improving margin on a contractual view, while treasury reports a widening behavioural mismatch on the same book, is describing a situation where the margin improvement is being paid for by an increase in interest rate risk that no one has priced. Reconciling the two views is the single most useful piece of governance available in this area, and the wiki is right that banks are prone to poor NIM analytics precisely because the two functions do not meet.

Common exam error Treating NIM as a profitability measure. It is a gross margin before credit losses, before operating costs and before the cost of capital, so a bank can be growing its NIM while destroying value. If asked whether a lending business is worth writing, the answer runs through the three-layer decomposition and then notes that even risk-adjusted NII excludes cost to serve.
Exam tip The asymmetric repricing point is highly examinable and most candidates state it as a fact. State it as an option and you can then say who wrote it, what determines its value, and when it stops working, which is the difference between a mark and full marks. The link to floor risk at the zero bound is the connection examiners reward.

Test yourself

  1. Do the arithmetic. Rework the VAF example with the write-off rate raised from 1 per cent to 3 per cent. What happens to each of the three layers, and at what write-off rate does risk-adjusted NII reach zero?
  2. Define NII and name the other main revenue stream. How does the balance differ between developed and developing markets?
  3. Give the three layers of the decomposition and say what question each answers.
  4. What does the worked example omit, and why does the 33.6 per cent figure reveal it? What follows for product decisions?
  5. Define NIM and decompose the asset rate. Which components does NIM capture, and what does that make it?
  6. Give three questions NIM can answer and three it cannot.
  7. Why can NIM not separate the lending margin from the deposit margin, and what mechanism does?
  8. What is the term liquidity premium for? What behaviour does it discourage?
  9. Distinguish NIM from net interest spread. When does the difference matter?
  10. Describe NII through a growth phase and a downturn, separating volume effects from rate effects.
  11. Why does NII compress in a downturn even though banks cut deposit rates quickly? What partly offsets it?
  12. Explain the deposit repricing lag as an option: who writes it, what determines its value, and when does it stop working?
  13. What did the 2022 tightening cycle reveal about the assumption, and why was the error's direction predictable?
  14. Construct a case where rising rates improve NII and reduce EVE, and one where they improve both. What single assumption switches the sign?
  15. What do finance and treasury each measure, and what does a divergence between them indicate?
