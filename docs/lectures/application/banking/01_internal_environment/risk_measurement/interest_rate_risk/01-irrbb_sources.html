Sources and forms of IRRBB

Four sources of structural mismatch produce four distinct forms of risk, and one of them is invisible to the report most banks use to measure the others. Basis risk does not appear in a gap analysis, which is why it is the form that surprises people.

Lecture 1 of 5 Reading time, 34 minutes Source: wiki interest_rate_risk/01-context.md, 02-irrbb_sources.md; BCBS 368
Step 1

The 30-second version

A rate change hits a bank in two ways. The earnings effect changes interest income and expense, and therefore net interest income. The economic value effect changes the present value of every future cash flow, and therefore the economic value of equity. Because assets and liabilities are mismatched, neither effect is symmetric.

The mismatch has four sources. Term, short-dated liabilities funding long-dated assets. Volume, many small deposits funding large loans. Interest rate type, fixed and floating products on both sides. And product features, the early termination and prepayment rights customers hold.

Those sources produce four forms of risk that are measured differently. Gap risk from maturity mismatch, visible in a repricing gap report. Option risk, split into automatic options written into contracts and behavioural options customers exercise as rates move. Basis risk, from assets and liabilities priced off different benchmarks. And yield curve risk, from non-parallel curve moves.

The item worth carrying is that basis risk does not appear in the gap report. A South African retail bank lending at prime and funding at three-month JIBAR has a perfectly matched gap report and a live exposure to the spread between two rates that are correlated and not identical.

Feynman test You borrow short and lend long, so rate moves hurt you. That much a maturity table shows. What it does not show is that your loans track one rate and your funding tracks another, so you can be perfectly matched on paper and still lose money when the two rates move apart.
Beyond the wiki The wiki's 02-irrbb_sources.md is among the best-developed notes in the collection and supplies almost all of steps 3 and 4: the two effects, the four sources, the four forms with their sub-types, the asset and liability sensitivity distinction, the South African structure, and the naked hedge problem. This lecture adds the Committee's reasoning for keeping IRRBB in Pillar 2, the negative-rate experience that made floor risk concrete, and a correction in step 5 to the outlier test as stated in 01-context.md.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that IRRBB is the largest risk on a retail bank's balance sheet that carries no Pillar 1 capital charge. Credit risk is bigger and it is capitalised. Market risk is capitalised. IRRBB sits in Pillar 2, which means the capital held against it is whatever the bank's own assessment and its supervisor's review produce, and the quality of that assessment is not standardised.

The second reason is that it is a risk a bank can be destroyed by while running an excellent loan book. The wiki opens on exactly this point and it is worth taking seriously: the 2023 failure of Silicon Valley Bank was a banking book interest rate problem that became a liquidity problem, not a credit problem, and its loan book was not the issue.

The third reason is the taxonomy itself, which is unusually worth learning precisely. Four sources and four forms sounds like classification for its own sake and it is not, because each form is measured by a different instrument and mitigated by a different action. Gap risk is managed by writing or hedging cash flows at particular tenors; basis risk is managed by product design or a swap referencing the right benchmark; behavioural option risk is largely not hedgeable at all and is managed by modelling the run-off. Getting the diagnosis wrong means applying the wrong remedy.

And a fourth reason for actuaries. The behavioural option risks here, prepayment and early redemption, are lapse risk under different names, and the modelling discipline is the actuarial one: an experience investigation, a set of assumptions, and monitoring for drift. This is the point in banking where an actuary's existing training transfers most directly.

Step 3

The mental model

Two effects, four sources, four forms, and which measurement instrument sees which.

Gap risk
Exposure arising from the term structure of banking book instruments, where assets and liabilities reprice at different dates. The form of IRRBB a repricing gap report is designed to measure.
Basis risk
Exposure arising when assets and liabilities are priced off different benchmark rates at the same tenor, or off the same benchmark at different tenors. Invisible to a gap report because the maturities match.
Behavioural option risk
Exposure arising where customers alter cash flow timing within the flexibility the product permits, principally loan prepayment and early deposit redemption. Driven by socio-economic factors as well as rates, and largely not hedgeable.
Step 4

The detail

Basis risk, worked through the South African structure

THE POSITION A retail bank's assets are largely linked to PRIME. Its wholesale funding references 3-MONTH JIBAR. Both are short-term ZAR rates. Both move with the repo rate. Neither moves identically to the other. WHAT THE GAP REPORT SAYS Assets repricing within 3 months: large Liabilities repricing within 3 months: large Net gap in the 0-3 month bucket: small Conclusion from the gap report: well matched, little interest rate risk in the near buckets. WHAT THE GAP REPORT CANNOT SAY The margin is prime minus JIBAR, plus a spread. If prime and JIBAR move apart, the margin changes, and no line in a maturity table records this. Prime is administered: it moves when the bank moves it, conventionally in step with the policy rate. JIBAR is a market rate: it moves with interbank conditions, which include credit and liquidity premia that the policy rate does not contain. In ordinary conditions the two move together and the basis is stable. In a funding stress, JIBAR can widen against the policy rate while prime does not follow, so funding costs rise and asset yields do not. WHY THIS IS THE DANGEROUS FORM It is invisible to the primary report, it is small in ordinary conditions, and it widens in precisely the conditions where the bank is under other pressure. That combination, benign until correlated with stress, is the same shape as the wrong-way risk that makes CVA convex. THE THREE MITIGATIONS 1 A pay-fixed, receive-floating swap referencing 3-month JIBAR, offsetting the funding cash flows. 2 Product design: build products on both sides that reference the SAME underlying rate, creating a natural hedge rather than a purchased one. 3 Adjust product pricing, passing the basis through. The second is the cheapest and the least available, because customers want prime-linked mortgages and wholesale markets want JIBAR-linked funding, and a bank cannot generally choose both sides.

The two automatic options, and why floor risk stopped being theoretical

Cap riskFloor risk
Automatic options: written into the contract, and exercised whenever they are in the money
The feature A maximum rate on a loan A rate on a deposit that cannot be repriced downwards, whether by contract or in practice
Bites when Rates rise. Liabilities reprice upwards and capped assets do not. Rates fall. Loan yields fall and deposit costs do not.
Effect on margin Squeezed from the asset side Squeezed from the liability side
Mitigation Product design with a matching floor on the loan, or purchase caps in the market Very limited. The floor is often behavioural rather than contractual: a bank can charge a customer a negative deposit rate on paper and generally will not.
Why it matters now Standard textbook risk Became the defining IRRBB problem of the 2010s. Where policy rates went negative, banks' loan yields followed rates down while retail deposit rates stopped at zero, so the floor was an effective option the bank had written and not priced.

The last row is the reason floor risk deserves more attention than a textbook gives it. The floor is usually not in the contract; it is in the bank's unwillingness to charge retail depositors for holding their money, which is a commercial and reputational constraint rather than a legal one. That makes it an option the bank has written without documenting, and a standard IRRBB model that reprices deposits symmetrically will miss it entirely.

Behavioural options and the naked hedge

Prepayment and early redemption are the two behavioural options, and they are mirror images. Prepayment is a borrower repaying faster than contracted, which shortens the asset. Early redemption is a depositor withdrawing before maturity, which shortens the liability. Both remove cash flows the bank had assumed and both are influenced by rates without being determined by them, since a customer prepaying a mortgage may be moving house rather than refinancing.

That partial rate dependence is why they resist hedging, and it produces the problem the wiki names precisely. If a bank hedges an expected run-off profile and the run-off happens faster than assumed, the hedge outlives its underlying and becomes a naked position that must be unwound at market cost. Hedging a behavioural profile therefore converts an uncertain exposure into a certain instrument plus a new uncertainty about whether the instrument still matches anything.

THE NAKED HEDGE, CONCRETELY A bank holds fixed-rate mortgages with a modelled behavioural life of 7 years and hedges by paying fixed on a 7-year swap. Rates fall sharply. Borrowers refinance. The actual behavioural life collapses to 3 years. The mortgages are gone. The swap has 4 years to run, and the bank is paying fixed in a market where fixed rates have fallen, so the swap is out of the money and unwinding it crystallises a loss. The bank hedged the exposure it modelled and is left with an exposure it did not. THE ASYMMETRY THAT MAKES THIS WORSE Prepayment accelerates when rates FALL, which is exactly when a pay-fixed hedge is loss-making. The behavioural option and the hedge are adversely correlated by construction, not by bad luck. THE MANAGEMENT RESPONSE, WHICH IS ACTUARIAL Not a static hedge against a point estimate. A dynamic profile based on anticipated behavioural run-off, re-evaluated as actual experience emerges, with the hedge adjusted as the assumption updates. That is an experience investigation feeding a rolling recalibration, which is how a life office manages lapse assumptions, and the analogy is exact enough to be useful rather than decorative.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

A correction to the outlier test

The wiki's 01-context.md states that a bank is an outlier if the absolute change in economic value of equity exceeds 15 per cent of Tier 1 capital for a 200 basis point parallel shock. That sentence combines two different tests from two different frameworks, and the combination is not either of them.

The 2004 Principles for the management and supervision of interest rate risk defined an outlier by reference to a 200 basis point parallel shock, with the threshold set against the sum of Tier 1 and Tier 2 capital. BCBS 368, which replaced it in April 2016, defines the outlier test against Tier 1 capital alone at a 15 per cent threshold, and applies it to the most adverse of the six prescribed shock scenarios rather than to a parallel shock specifically.

So the 15 per cent figure and the 200 basis point parallel shock belong to different generations of the framework. The correct statement of the current test is that a bank is a potential outlier where the maximum reduction in economic value of equity under any of the six prescribed scenarios exceeds 15 per cent of Tier 1 capital. The distinction matters in practice as well as in an exam, because for many balance sheets the binding scenario is a steepener or a short-rate shock rather than a parallel move, and a bank testing only the parallel case may pass a test it would fail.

It is worth adding that some jurisdictions retain a legacy version of the older test alongside the newer one, so a bank may be subject to both. That is a reason to name the framework when quoting a threshold rather than a reason to treat the two as interchangeable.

Why the Committee left IRRBB in Pillar 2

The Committee consulted on making IRRBB a Pillar 1 charge and decided against it, and the reasoning is instructive because it is an admission about the limits of standardisation.

A Pillar 1 charge requires a formula that applies to every bank. IRRBB's magnitude depends almost entirely on behavioural assumptions, principally the assumed repricing profile of non-maturity deposits, and those assumptions differ legitimately between banks, markets and products. A standardised assumption would be badly wrong for many banks in both directions: too conservative for a bank with genuinely sticky retail deposits, and far too generous for one whose deposits are rate-sensitive corporate balances.

The alternative, letting banks set their own behavioural assumptions inside a Pillar 1 formula, hands the capital number to the least verifiable input in the calculation. Deposit stickiness is not observable in advance, it is estimated from a history that may not contain a rate cycle, and the direction of the incentive is unambiguous: a longer assumed deposit life reduces measured risk.

So the Committee kept IRRBB in Pillar 2, where a supervisor can examine the assumptions rather than accept them, and supplied a standardised framework that supervisors may impose on banks whose internal systems are inadequate. That is a considered choice rather than a gap, and it explains why the outlier test exists: it is a screening device to direct supervisory attention, not a capital formula.

What an actuary should notice about the assumptions

The behavioural assumptions in IRRBB have a property that should worry anyone trained in experience analysis, and it is worth naming because the framework does not.

Deposit stickiness and prepayment rates are estimated from history, and most banks' usable history covers a period of stable or falling rates. The assumptions therefore describe behaviour in conditions that may not recur, and the direction of the error is predictable: depositors who have never had an alternative worth moving for will look stickier than they are. When rates rise sharply and money market funds become attractive, the assumption fails in the direction that hurts, at the moment it matters.

That is not a hypothetical. The 2023 US regional bank episode was in substantial part a failure of exactly this assumption: deposits modelled as stable proved to be neither stable nor slow, and the speed of withdrawal was outside the range any behavioural model had contemplated. The interest rate risk was measured on a deposit profile that dissolved.

The actuarial response is the familiar one and worth stating in its banking form. Assumptions estimated from a single regime should be stressed to the regime that is absent from the data, not merely to a percentage variation around the fitted value. A sensitivity showing what happens if the assumed deposit life halves is more informative than a confidence interval around the estimate, because the risk is a change in behaviour rather than sampling error in the measurement of current behaviour.

Common exam error Treating basis risk as a form of gap risk, or assuming a matched gap report means matched interest rate risk. They are separate forms with separate measurement and separate mitigation, and the gap report sees only the first. If asked to identify the interest rate risks in a described balance sheet, check explicitly whether the two sides reference the same benchmark, because that is the risk the question is usually testing.
Exam tip The four sources and four forms are highly examinable because they are enumerable. Learn them as two separate lists and do not merge them: term, volume, interest rate type and product features are sources of mismatch; gap, option, basis and yield curve are forms of risk. A question asking for sources and answered with forms loses most of the marks even though the content is adjacent.
