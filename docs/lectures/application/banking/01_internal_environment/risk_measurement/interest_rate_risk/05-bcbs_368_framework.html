BCBS 368 and the supervisory framework

The Committee could not standardise the calculation, so it standardised the shocks and capped the assumption it distrusted most. That is a third way between Pillar 1 and pure Pillar 2, and the caps bind in one direction only.

Lecture 5 of 5 Reading time, 34 minutes Source: BCBS 368 (April 2016)
Step 1

The 30-second version

BCBS 368 replaced the 2004 interest rate risk principles and consists of twelve principles, nine addressed to banks and three to supervisors. It leaves IRRBB in Pillar 2, requires measurement under prescribed shocks, mandates disclosure, and supplies a standardised framework that a supervisor may impose on a bank whose internal systems are inadequate.

The design is the interesting part. Lecture 1 explained why a Pillar 1 charge was rejected: the answer depends on behavioural assumptions that differ legitimately between banks and cannot be verified. So rather than standardising the calculation, the framework standardises the shocks, calibrated by currency from historical rate data, and caps the assumptions it distrusts.

Those caps are one-sided, which is the sharpest feature of the framework. A bank may not assume its non-maturity deposits are stickier than the prescribed maximum, and there is no floor requiring it to assume they are stickier than it believes. The cap binds only in the direction that would reduce measured risk.

Enforcement runs through disclosure and the outlier test rather than capital. Exceeding fifteen per cent of Tier 1 under the most adverse of the six scenarios makes a bank an outlier, which triggers supervisory dialogue and potentially measures. It is not an automatic capital add-on, and the difference matters.

Feynman test The regulator could not write one formula that works for every bank, because the answer depends on how long each bank's deposits stay. So it fixed the shocks everyone must test, put a ceiling on how sticky you are allowed to claim your deposits are, made you publish the results, and set a line that gets you a conversation if you cross it.
Beyond the wiki The wiki's five IRRBB notes are fully consumed by lectures 1 to 4 and none of them treats the supervisory framework directly, beyond the six scenarios and the outlier test that lecture 1 corrected. Where it gives specific calibration figures, the shock sizes by currency and the deposit caps, it flags them for verification against the standard's annexes, because those are the numbers most likely to be misremembered and the ones a reader should check before relying on.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this framework is the answer to a question the whole track has been circling. Lectures 1 to 4 established that IRRBB is large, uncapitalised in Pillar 1, and dominated by an assumption nobody can verify. This lecture is what a regulator does when facing exactly that combination, and the answer is more inventive than either of the obvious options.

The second reason is that the one-sided cap is a transferable regulatory technique. Where a framework depends on an assumption the regulated party sets, and the incentive runs in a known direction, constraining only that direction is cheaper than verification and more effective than a symmetric rule. That pattern appears in downturn LGD floors, in loss given default prescriptions, and in the conservatism requirements throughout the A-IRB framework, and BCBS 368 is an unusually clean example of it.

The third reason is that disclosure is doing work here that capital does elsewhere, and it is worth being clear-eyed about how much work that is. A bank must publish its economic value and earnings sensitivities under prescribed scenarios, which makes its position comparable to peers and visible to analysts. That is a real constraint operating through market discipline rather than through a required capital buffer, and it is weaker and more flexible than a charge.

And a fourth reason for South African candidates. The shocks are calibrated by currency, so the rand shock is materially larger than the dollar or euro shock, which means a South African bank faces a more demanding test on the same balance sheet structure. That is examinable and frequently missed.

Step 3

The mental model

Twelve principles, the two things the framework standardises, and the enforcement chain that replaces a capital charge.

Outlier test
The screening threshold: a maximum reduction in economic value of equity exceeding fifteen per cent of Tier 1 capital under any of the six prescribed scenarios. A trigger for supervisory attention rather than an automatic capital requirement.
Core deposits
The portion of non-maturity deposits assumed unlikely to reprice or run off, and therefore treatable as long-dated funding. The standardised framework caps both the proportion that may be treated as core and the average maturity assigned to it.
Standardised framework
A prescribed calculation, including bucketing and behavioural assumptions, that a supervisor may require a bank to use where its internal measurement system is judged inadequate. A fallback with penal characteristics rather than an equal option.
Step 4

The detail

Shocks calibrated by currency

The framework does not apply a single shock size worldwide. It calibrates three shock parameters per currency, a parallel shock, a short-rate shock and a long-rate shock, from the historical behaviour of that currency's rates, and constructs the six scenarios from them. A currency with a volatile rate history gets a larger shock.

THE SIX SCENARIOS, BUILT FROM THREE PARAMETERS 1 parallel up the whole curve up by the parallel shock 2 parallel down the whole curve down by it 3 steepener short rates down, long rates up 4 flattener short rates up, long rates down 5 short rates up short end up by the short shock 6 short rates down short end down by it Scenarios 3 and 4 combine the short and long parameters with prescribed weights that decay along the curve, so a steepener is not simply a short-down plus long-up step function. THE CALIBRATION POINT THAT MATTERS LOCALLY Shock sizes differ substantially by currency. Major low-volatility currencies attract shocks in the region of 100 to 250 basis points on the parallel scenario. Emerging market currencies with more volatile rate histories, including the rand, attract materially larger ones, on the order of 400 basis points parallel. VERIFY THE EXACT FIGURES against the standard's annex before relying on them. The structure is what matters here and the numbers move between publications and national implementations. THE CONSEQUENCE, AND IT IS NOT SMALL Take lecture 4's portfolio: a five-year asset funded by a one-year liability, PV01 of -R28,640, and dEVE of -R5.39m at a 200bp shock, which was 13.48% of a R40m Tier 1 and inside the threshold. Now apply a 400bp shock instead. The move roughly doubles, less a convexity saving that grows with the shock size, so dEVE lands in the region of -R10m. 10 / 40 = 25%, against a 15% threshold. The same balance sheet that passes comfortably in a major currency fails decisively in rand. WHY THAT IS DEFENSIBLE The test asks whether the bank can withstand a plausible adverse move in ITS OWN rate environment. A South African bank genuinely faces larger rate moves than a Japanese one, so a common shock size would be lenient in one currency and severe in another. The framework is harder on rand books because rand rates move more, which is the point rather than a bias.

The caps on deposit assumptions

This is where the framework does its most distinctive work, and it responds directly to the problem lecture 3 identified. The value of a bank's deposit franchise depends on how long the deposits last, the bank sets that assumption, and the incentive is unambiguous: longer assumed life means a more valuable franchise and lower measured risk on some structures.

THE STRUCTURE OF THE CAPS Non-maturity deposits are split into three classes, in descending order of assumed stickiness: RETAIL TRANSACTIONAL the customer's main account, salary paid in, bills paid out RETAIL NON-TRANSACTIONAL savings balances without a transactional relationship WHOLESALE corporate and institutional For each class the framework caps TWO things: 1 the PROPORTION that may be treated as core 2 the AVERAGE MATURITY assigned to that core portion Both caps tighten as you move down the list. Retail transactional balances may be treated as substantially core with a multi-year average maturity; wholesale balances may not. Indicative magnitudes: retail transactional in the region of 90% core and around 5 years; wholesale materially lower on both. VERIFY against the annex. WHY TWO CAPS RATHER THAN ONE Capping only the proportion would let a bank assign a twenty-year maturity to a smaller core. Capping only the maturity would let it call the entire balance core. Both levers move the answer, so both are constrained. THE CLASSIFICATION IS ITSELF THE SOFT SPOT The caps are tight and the allocation between classes is the bank's. A balance classified as retail transactional attracts the loosest cap, so the incentive moves from the assumption to the classification. That is a general feature of capped-assumption regulation: constraining a parameter relocates the discretion to whatever determines which cap applies. Compare the trading book boundary in market risk lecture 1, where the same relocation happened and was answered with presumptive lists.

Disclosure, and what it is for

Principle 8 requires disclosure of the measured sensitivities, and the requirement is more pointed than it first appears. A bank publishes its change in economic value of equity and its change in net interest income under the prescribed scenarios, and it also publishes the average and longest repricing maturity it has assigned to non-maturity deposits.

That second item is the one with teeth. Publishing the sensitivity alone would let a reader see the answer without seeing the assumption that drove it. Publishing the assigned deposit maturities alongside makes the key judgement visible and comparable, so an analyst can see that one bank assumes a materially longer deposit life than its peer and can ask why. It converts an internal modelling choice into a disclosed position.

Because there is no Pillar 1 charge, this disclosure is a substantial part of the framework's actual force. The chain runs measurement, disclosure, comparability, market and supervisory questioning. That is genuine discipline and it is weaker than a capital requirement, and the Committee accepted the weakness knowingly in exchange for not fixing an assumption it could not fix.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The one-sided cap is the technique worth taking away

The caps on deposit assumptions constrain only one direction. A bank may not claim its deposits are stickier than the ceiling; nothing stops it assuming they are less sticky than it believes. That asymmetry is deliberate and it is the most transferable idea in the framework.

The reasoning is that the regulator does not need to know the true value of the assumption. It only needs to know the direction in which an error would be self-serving, and to block that direction. A bank that assumes shorter deposit lives than reality reports more risk than it runs, which is a problem for the bank and not for the supervisor. A bank that assumes longer lives reports less risk than it runs, which is the supervisor's problem exactly.

So the cap is cheaper than verification and better targeted than a symmetric prescription, and the pattern recurs across prudential regulation once you can see it. Downturn LGD requirements floor the estimate rather than fixing it. Margins of conservatism are required to be added, never subtracted. Collateral haircuts reduce recognised value and never increase it. In each case the regulator has identified the direction of the incentive and constrained only that.

The limitation is the one step 4 named. Constraining a parameter moves the discretion to whatever selects which constraint applies, so a tight cap on wholesale deposits and a loose one on retail transactional deposits makes the classification boundary the new soft spot. Market risk lecture 1 showed the same relocation at the trading book boundary and the eventual answer there was presumptive lists with penalised reclassification, which is a template available here and not currently used.

An outlier test is a screening device, and bright lines have costs

The fifteen per cent threshold is a screen, not a charge, and treating it as a charge produces two errors in opposite directions.

The first is over-reading. A bank crossing the threshold has not incurred a capital requirement; it has attracted supervisory attention, and the outcome depends on the dialogue. A bank can be an outlier for a defensible reason, such as a deliberate structural position taken with board approval and adequate capital, and remain one after review.

The second is under-reading, and it is more common. Because the consequence is a conversation rather than a formula, the threshold can be treated as soft, and it is not. Supervisors have measures available and use them, and a bank that has organised its balance sheet to sit just inside the line has told its supervisor something about its intentions.

Bright lines also create the specific problem lecture 4 quantified. The convexity approximation was worth nearly six per cent of the measured move at a two hundred basis point shock and would be worth more at four hundred, which is enough to move a bank across a threshold. So the measurement precision required is set by proximity to the line rather than by any risk management need, which is a familiar distortion of any bright-line rule and a reason to compute properly rather than approximately when near it.

What the framework cannot reach

Two limits are worth stating plainly, because the track has spent five lectures on a framework and should close honestly about it.

The first is that the framework measures a rate shock and not a run. Lecture 4's closing point was that bank liabilities are demandable, so a deposit book can leave rather than reprice, and the six scenarios all assume the balance sheet stays in place while rates move. A bank can satisfy every requirement here and be destroyed by the deposit behaviour the framework caps assumptions about, because the cap constrains the assumed repricing profile rather than protecting against wholesale withdrawal. Liquidity risk covers that, in a separate framework with separate metrics, and the two are assessed largely independently.

The second is coverage. A framework binds where it is implemented and on the institutions it captures, and national implementation of BCBS 368 has varied in timing and in the population of banks covered. A rule that would have caught a given exposure is of no use if the institution holding it sits outside the rule's scope, and the interest rate risk episodes of recent years have turned as much on which banks were in scope for which requirements as on the requirements' content.

Neither observation is an argument against the framework. Both are arguments for reading it as one instrument among several rather than as a complete answer, and for treating the outlier test as the screening device it was designed to be.

Common exam error Describing the outlier test as a capital add-on. It is a screening threshold that triggers supervisory review, and the review may or may not result in a capital measure. The related error is quoting a single shock size for all currencies: the shocks are calibrated per currency, and the rand parallel shock is roughly double the major-currency figure.
Exam tip The structure worth memorising is small: twelve principles, nine for banks and three for supervisors; six EVE scenarios and two NII scenarios; a fifteen per cent of Tier 1 outlier threshold on the worst scenario; caps on both the core proportion and the average maturity of non-maturity deposits; and a standardised framework a supervisor may impose. If you can add that the caps are one-sided and say why, you have the framework's design logic in one sentence.
