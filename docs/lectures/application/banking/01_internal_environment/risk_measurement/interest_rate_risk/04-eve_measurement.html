Economic value of equity measurement

A portfolio can have an economic value of equity of exactly zero and sit within two percentage points of the supervisory outlier threshold. The level tells you nothing; the sensitivity is the whole measure, and the worked example below shows both.

Lecture 4 of 5 Reading time, 36 minutes Source: wiki interest_rate_risk/05-irrbb_measurement.md
Step 1

The 30-second version

Measurement begins with gap analysis, bucketing assets and liabilities by when they reprice rather than when they mature, because interest rate risk is about repricing dates. From there, three instruments of increasing sophistication: PV01, the change in net present value for a one basis point shift; economic value of equity, the present value of every banking book cash flow run to maturity; and earnings at risk, a stochastic distribution of net interest income outcomes.

Everything rests on assumptions set before any of it runs. The duration of assets and liabilities under different rate scenarios, including how non-maturity deposit behaviour changes as rates move, determines the answer, which is lecture 3's point arriving as a measurement problem rather than a commercial one.

The framework requires both perspectives with different scenario counts: six prescribed shocks for economic value and two for net interest income. That asymmetry is deliberate, because value is sensitive to curve shape while near-term earnings are driven mostly by level.

And the trade-off lecture 3 set up does not resolve. Stabilising earnings and stabilising economic value require opposing structural positions, so a bank cannot immunise both and must choose a position on the spectrum and know where it is standing.

Feynman test Add up what all your future cash flows are worth today, then shock the interest rates and add them up again. The difference is the number. The hard part is not the arithmetic, it is deciding how long your deposits last, because that single guess can flip the answer's sign.
Beyond the wiki The wiki's 05-irrbb_measurement.md supplies gap analysis and its five limitations, PV01 with its bucketed enhancement, the EVE definition and the equity treatment problem, the liability-sensitive worked case with three compounding effects, the earnings at risk progression through repricing gap to NII sensitivity to a Vasicek-driven distribution, and the fully correct gap example reproduced in step 4. This lecture adds a reproducible PV01 and EVE calculation of its own, connects it to the outlier threshold, and records an arithmetic error in the wiki's PV01 table in step 5.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that economic value of equity is the measure the supervisory outlier test uses, so it is the number that determines whether a bank attracts attention. Lecture 3 argued that net interest income is what management is actually managed on, and both are true simultaneously: one drives internal behaviour and the other drives supervisory scrutiny, which is a recipe for a bank optimising the first while failing the second.

The second reason is that the measurement chain is a good illustration of increasing sophistication buying different things rather than simply more accuracy. PV01 gives one number for a parallel shift. Bucketed PV01 shows where on the curve the sensitivity sits. Full EVE handles non-parallel shocks and optionality. Earnings at risk adds a probability distribution. Each step costs modelling effort and each answers a question the previous one could not, and knowing which question you have is what determines how far up the chain you need to go.

The third reason is that the equity treatment problem is genuinely subtle and easy to get wrong. A bank's own equity is perpetual interest-free funding, and how you treat it in a present value calculation materially changes the answer, with no single correct convention.

And a fourth reason for actuaries. This is duration analysis and cash flow projection applied to a balance sheet, which is asset-liability management as an actuary would recognise it. The differences from insurance work are the behavioural assumptions and the fact that the liabilities are demandable.

Step 3

The mental model

The measurement chain, what each rung buys, and the trade-off that does not resolve.

PV01
The change in net present value from a one basis point shift in the yield curve. Also DV01, PVBP or interest rate delta. One number for the whole book, blind to where on the curve the sensitivity sits.
Economic value of equity
The present value of all banking book cash flows, assets less liabilities and including off-balance sheet items, assuming positions run off to maturity. The measure the supervisory outlier test applies six prescribed shocks to.
Earnings at risk
The value at risk of net interest income at a stated confidence level over a stated horizon, obtained by simulating a calibrated stochastic rate process. Converts a scenario answer into a probability statement.
Step 4

The detail

PV01 and EVE on a duration-mismatched book

Constructed here rather than borrowed, so every figure is reproducible. Two instruments, both priced at par, so the portfolio's economic value starts at exactly zero and only the sensitivity is interesting.

SETUP ASSET R100m, 5-year bullet, 10% annual coupon discount curve flat at 10% -> PV = 100.0000 LIABILITY R100m, 1-year bullet, 8% coupon discount curve flat at 8% -> PV = 100.0000 EVE at base = 100.0000 - 100.0000 = 0.0000 A textbook match on the balance sheet: R100m funded by R100m. And a five-year asset funded by a one-year liability, which is the mismatch. PV01: SHIFT THE WHOLE CURVE UP 1 BASIS POINT PV(asset) at 10.01% = 99.9621 change -0.0379 PV(liability) at 8.01% = 99.9907 change -0.0093 EVE = -0.0286 PV01 = -R28,640 per basis point asset leg -R37,898 (long duration, loses more) liability leg +R9,258 (short duration, gains less) The asset leg is four times the liability leg because modified duration is roughly 3.79 years against 0.93. That ratio IS the mismatch, expressed in money. NOW THE SUPERVISORY SHOCK: 200 BASIS POINTS UP PV(asset) at 12% = 92.7904 PV(liability) at 10% = 98.1818 EVE = -5.3914 dEVE = -R5,391,371 Linear estimate from PV01: -28,640 x 200 = -R5,727,957 Actual: -R5,391,371 CONVEXITY SAVES +R336,586 Convexity is 5.9% of the move at 200bp, which is why a PV01 multiplied out to a large shock overstates the loss. For an outlier test near the threshold, that difference decides the answer. THE OUTLIER TEST CONNECTION Suppose Tier 1 capital is R40m. |dEVE| / Tier 1 = 5,391,371 / 40,000,000 = 13.48% The threshold is 15%. This portfolio is inside it, and within two percentage points. Note what that means: a book whose economic value of equity is EXACTLY ZERO at base sits close to the supervisory outlier threshold. The level of EVE tells you nothing. Only its sensitivity is the measure. AND NOTICE WHICH DIRECTION Rates DOWN 200bp gives dEVE = +R6.10m, larger in magnitude than the -R5.39m from rates up, again because of convexity. The outlier test takes the most adverse scenario, so for this book the binding case is rates up, and the R6.10m gain is irrelevant to it.

Earnings at risk, step by step

The wiki's repricing gap example is arithmetically correct in every cell and total, and is reproduced here because it is the clearest illustration available of the earnings measure.

STEP 1: THE REPRICING GAP, one year in 3-month bands band total 0-3m 3-6m 6-9m 9-12m assets 90 40 10 15 25 liabilities 100 60 25 5 10 GAP -10 -20 -15 +10 +15 R10m more liabilities reprice within the year than assets, so a rate rise reduces NII over the period. Note this implicitly assumes a STATIC balance sheet: everything is replaced as it matures. STEP 2: NII SENSITIVITY, 100bp rise NII sensitivity = gap x remaining term (months) x dr / 12 band gap months open dr sensitivity 0-3m -20 12 100bp -200,000 3-6m -15 9 100bp -112,500 6-9m +10 6 100bp +50,000 9-12m +15 3 100bp +37,500 TOTAL -10 -225,000 A 100bp rise costs R225,000 of NII over the year. WHY "MONTHS OPEN" FALLS ACROSS BANDS A gap in the 0-3m band reprices almost immediately, so the new rate applies for essentially the whole 12 months. A gap in the 9-12m band reprices near the end, so the new rate applies for about 3 months. The weighting is the fraction of the year the gap is exposed, which is why the same R15m gap contributes less in the 9-12m band than the R20m gap in 0-3m despite being similar in size. STEP 3: FROM SCENARIO TO DISTRIBUTION Steps 1 and 2 answer "what if rates rise 100bp". Earnings at risk answers "how bad could NII get, with what probability". Calibrate a stochastic short rate process, Vasicek in the wiki's example, simulate many rate paths, revalue NII on each, and read the quantile. EaR at 95% = the NII outcome that only 5% of simulations fall below. This is value at risk applied to earnings, so everything market risk lecture 2 said about quantile measures applies: it is a threshold rather than a tail average, and it is not subadditive. WHAT ENHANCES THE ANALYSIS dynamic balance sheet: new business volumes, new products, funding plan changes gradual rather than instantaneous rate moves pricing time lags, which is lecture 3's deposit beta behavioural assumptions on prepayment and withdrawal

The equity treatment problem

A bank's own equity, ordinary and preference shares plus retained earnings, is perpetual funding on which no interest is paid. Dropping it into a present value calculation unmodified distorts the result, and the wiki flags this without prescribing an answer because there is not a single correct one.

The difficulty is that equity has no contractual cash flows to discount. Treated as a zero-coupon perpetual liability it has an enormous duration and its present value swamps everything. Excluded entirely, the asset side is being funded by something that does not appear, and the resulting EVE is not the value of anything. The convention the wiki describes, splitting equity into maturity buckets that roll off alongside assets with a fixed rate cost assigned to each bucket, is a reasonable middle course that makes equity behave like the term funding it economically substitutes for.

What matters more than the choice is that it is disclosed and consistent. Two banks reporting EVE sensitivity on different equity conventions are reporting different quantities, and a change of convention will move a bank's reported sensitivity without any change in its position. For a validator this is a model assumption of the first order, and it belongs in the documentation alongside the deposit repricing assumption rather than in a technical appendix.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

An arithmetic error in the wiki's PV01 table

The wiki's PV01 illustration gives a match-funded balance sheet with a base net present value of 3.0844, falling to 3.0794 on a one basis point rise and rising to 3.0894 on a one basis point fall. Its PV01 column then reports minus 0.0050 for the rise and plus 0.0100 for the fall.

The second figure is inconsistent with the table's own numbers. The fall takes the net present value from 3.0844 to 3.0894, a difference of 0.0050, not 0.0100. The plus 0.0050 the arithmetic implies is also what theory predicts, since a one basis point move in either direction produces an almost symmetric response, with convexity making the downward gain very slightly larger than the upward loss rather than twice it.

Everything else in the table reconciles. The asset and liability columns net to the stated net present values, and the stated conclusion that the net present value falls by 0.16 per cent for a one basis point rise checks out at 0.162 per cent. So the error is confined to one cell, and the correction is to read the downward PV01 as plus 0.0050.

It is worth noting separately that the asset and liability present values in that example, 17.79 and 14.71 on a hundred million rand notional each, rest on conventions the note does not state, and I could not reconstruct them. That is why the worked example in step 4 was built from scratch rather than borrowed: an illustration a reader cannot reproduce teaches the mechanics without letting them check the mechanics.

Why the level of EVE is meaningless and its sensitivity is not

The step 4 example makes a point that is easy to state and easy to forget. That portfolio's economic value of equity is exactly zero at base, and it sits at 13.48 per cent of Tier 1 under the supervisory shock, within two points of the outlier threshold. Zero level, near-threshold sensitivity.

That is not an artefact of the construction. EVE's level depends on the discount curve, the equity convention, and every behavioural assumption in the book, so comparing one bank's EVE level with another's is comparing two differently constructed quantities. Its change under a prescribed common shock is far more comparable, because the shocks are specified and much of the construction error affects base and shocked values alike and cancels.

Which is why the framework is built on delta EVE rather than EVE, and why the outlier test is a ratio to Tier 1 capital rather than an absolute amount. The measure is deliberately relative in both respects, and a bank reporting its EVE level as though it were a meaningful balance sheet quantity has misunderstood what it computed.

Convexity is small until it decides the answer

The step 4 arithmetic shows convexity saving R336,586 on a R5.7m linear estimate, which is 5.9 per cent of the move. In most contexts that is a rounding difference. In this context it can determine whether a bank is an outlier.

A bank computing its outlier ratio by multiplying PV01 by 200 would report 14.3 per cent against the correct 13.48 per cent on this book, and a bank sitting at 15.5 per cent on a linear estimate might be at 14.6 per cent properly computed. The threshold is a bright line with supervisory consequences, so the approximation error matters exactly where the number matters.

The general lesson is one that recurs across this collection. A linear sensitivity is an excellent management tool for small moves and a poor basis for a large prescribed shock, and the 200 basis point scenarios in this framework are large. That is the same reason market risk lecture 4's sensitivities-based method includes a separate curvature charge rather than relying on delta alone: at supervisory shock sizes, second-order effects stop being second-order.

What this looks like to an actuary

Three observations, of which the third is the one worth carrying.

The mechanics are asset-liability management as an actuary already knows it: project cash flows, discount, measure duration, shock the curve, measure again. Nothing in the calculation would be unfamiliar to anyone who has run a matching exercise on an annuity book, and the PV01 is a DV01 by another name.

The equity treatment problem has a close analogue in the treatment of free assets and future discretionary benefits in insurance capital work, where the same question arises about how to represent a claim with no contractual cash flow profile in a present value framework. The answers differ and the shape of the difficulty is identical.

The genuine difference is that these liabilities are demandable. An annuity liability has contractual timing that the annuitant cannot accelerate; a deposit can leave tomorrow. So the behavioural assumption in banking is not merely uncertain, it is subject to a run, and a run is not a distributional tail of the ordinary behaviour but a different regime. That is why lecture 1's warning about assumptions calibrated on a single regime has more force here than the equivalent warning about lapse rates, and why a bank's EVE can be correct on Friday and irrelevant on Monday.

Common exam error Applying six shock scenarios to net interest income. The framework prescribes six for economic value and two, parallel up and parallel down, for net interest income. The asymmetry has a reason worth stating: value depends on curve shape because it discounts long cash flows, while near-term earnings are driven mostly by the level of short rates.
Exam tip The NII sensitivity formula rewards understanding rather than memorisation. If you can explain why the "months open" weight falls across successive bands, from twelve in the first to three in the last, you can reconstruct the formula under exam pressure. It is the fraction of the year the repriced gap is actually exposed.

Test yourself

  1. Do the arithmetic. Rebuild the step 4 portfolio with the asset shortened to a 3-year bullet. Compute the new PV01, the 200 basis point delta EVE, and the outlier ratio against R40m of Tier 1.
  2. Then the gap. Rework the earnings example for a 200 basis point fall rather than a 100 basis point rise. What is the sign and magnitude?
  3. Why does gap analysis bucket by repricing date rather than maturity?
  4. Give the five limitations of gap analysis.
  5. Define PV01 and name its aliases. What can bucketed PV01 do that PV01 cannot?
  6. Write the EVE definition. What does it assume about positions?
  7. How many prescribed shocks apply to EVE and how many to NII? Explain the asymmetry.
  8. In the step 4 example, why is the asset leg of the PV01 roughly four times the liability leg?
  9. Explain why a portfolio with an EVE of exactly zero can sit close to the outlier threshold. What does that tell you about the level of EVE?
  10. How much does convexity change the 200 basis point answer, and why does that matter more than its size suggests?
  11. State the equity treatment problem, the two failing extremes, and the middle course. Why does disclosure matter more than the choice?
  12. Explain why the "months open" weight falls across successive repricing bands.
  13. What does earnings at risk add to NII sensitivity, and what property does it inherit from being a quantile measure?
  14. State the trade-off between stabilising earnings and stabilising value, and what each structural position leaves exposed.
  15. What is the arithmetic error in the wiki's PV01 table, and what should the figure be?
  16. Why does the demandability of bank liabilities make the behavioural assumption problem worse than the insurance analogue?
