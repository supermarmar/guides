Value at risk and coherent risk measures

VaR fails one of the four axioms a risk measure ought to satisfy, and the failure is not academic. Two independent bonds, four per cent default probability each: VaR at 95 per cent says the portfolio is riskier than either bond alone, and the arithmetic is nine lines.

Lecture 2 of 6 Reading time, 34 minutes Source: wiki market_risk/02-models.md; Artzner et al. (1999)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Value at risk is the loss threshold that will not be exceeded with a given confidence over a given horizon. A one-day 95 per cent VaR of 25 million says there is an estimated five per cent chance of a single-day loss above 25 million, or roughly one such day in twenty.

Artzner, Delbaen, Eber and Heath set out in 1999 the four properties a sensible risk measure should have: monotonicity, translation invariance, positive homogeneity and subadditivity. A measure satisfying all four is coherent. VaR satisfies the first three and fails the fourth in general, which means it can report that combining two exposures increases total risk.

That failure has a practical edge, because subadditivity is the axiom that encodes diversification. A risk measure that is not subadditive can penalise a bank for diversifying and can be reduced by splitting a portfolio across desks, and both of those are perverse incentives inside a capital framework.

VaR is coherent under elliptical distributions, of which the normal is the standard case, so the defence of VaR amounts to a distributional assumption. Market returns are not elliptical: they are leptokurtic, they jump, their volatility moves, and credit-sensitive assets are negatively skewed. So the assumption that rescues VaR is the assumption the data reject.

Feynman test VaR tells you how bad things get before the worst five per cent, and nothing about how bad the worst five per cent is. Worse, it can say two things together are riskier than the sum of them apart, which is nonsense, and step 4 shows it happening with two bonds and a pocket calculator.

Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this is the argument that changed the framework. FRTB replaced VaR with expected shortfall for trading book capital, and the reason was not that VaR was estimated badly. It was that VaR is the wrong functional: it reports a quantile when the regulator wants a tail expectation, and it lacks the property that makes aggregation sensible. Understanding the axioms is understanding why a global standard was rewritten.

The second reason is that the failure is demonstrable rather than theoretical. A great many candidates can recite that VaR is not subadditive and very few can produce an instance, which is the difference between having read a summary and having understood the point. The instance in step 4 takes two independent bonds and four lines of binomial arithmetic.

The third reason is that the same mathematics governs anything aggregated from parts, which includes every economic capital model an actuary builds. If your risk measure is not subadditive, the diversification benefit you compute across risk types is not guaranteed to be a benefit, and the sum of standalone capital is not an upper bound on aggregate capital. That is a real modelling constraint and not a footnote.

And a fourth reason specific to credit risk practitioners. The elliptical-distribution condition is the same condition that underwrites a great deal of standard portfolio machinery, and the empirical deviations listed in step 3 are the same deviations that make downturn calibration hard. The market risk version of the problem is better documented and the arguments are directly portable.

Step 3

The mental model

The four axioms, which one VaR fails, and what the failing axiom is for.

Value at risk
The loss threshold not exceeded with probability α over a stated horizon. Formally the relevant quantile of the loss distribution, which is why it reports where the tail begins and nothing about its contents.
Subadditivity
The requirement that the measure of a combined position never exceeds the sum of the measures of its parts. The axiom that encodes diversification, and the one VaR fails.
Coherent risk measure
A measure satisfying monotonicity, translation invariance, positive homogeneity and subadditivity. Expected shortfall is coherent; VaR is coherent only under elliptical distributions.
Step 4

The detail

Breaking subadditivity with two bonds

This is the whole argument, and it needs no distributional assumption, no simulation and no calculus. Two defaultable bonds, independent, each with a four per cent probability of default, each losing 100 on default and nothing otherwise. Confidence level 95 per cent.

SETUP Two bonds, A and B. Independent. P(default) = 0.04 each. Loss on default = 100. Confidence level = 95%. STEP 1 · VaR OF ONE BOND Loss distribution: P(L = 0) = 0.96, P(L = 100) = 0.04 P(L ≤ 0) = 0.96, which is ≥ 0.95. So the 95th percentile of loss is 0. VaR(95%) of bond A = 0. Same for bond B. Sum of standalone VaRs = 0 + 0 = 0. STEP 2 · THE PORTFOLIO OF BOTH Independent, so binomial: P(no default) = 0.96^2 = 0.9216 loss 0 P(one default) = 2(0.04)(0.96) = 0.0768 loss 100 P(both default) = 0.04^2 = 0.0016 loss 200 (check: 0.9216 + 0.0768 + 0.0016 = 1.0000) STEP 3 · VaR OF THE PORTFOLIO P(L ≤ 0) = 0.9216 < 0.95 -> 0 is not enough P(L ≤ 100) = 0.9984 ≥ 0.95 -> 100 is VaR(95%) of the portfolio = 100. STEP 4 · THE VIOLATION Subadditivity requires ρ(A + B) ≤ ρ(A) + ρ(B). Here: 100 ≤ 0 + 0 FALSE. VaR reports that holding two independent bonds is riskier than the sum of holding each alone. It reports that diversification created risk out of nothing. STEP 5 · THE SAME PORTFOLIO UNDER EXPECTED SHORTFALL ES(95%) averages the worst 5% of outcomes. One bond: the worst 5% is 4% default (loss 100) plus 1% no default (loss 0). ES = (0.04 x 100 + 0.01 x 0) / 0.05 = 4 / 0.05 = 80 Portfolio: the worst 5% is 0.16% both default (200) plus 4.84% one default (100). ES = (0.0016 x 200 + 0.0484 x 100) / 0.05 = (0.32 + 4.84) / 0.05 = 5.16 / 0.05 = 103.2 Sum of standalone ES = 80 + 80 = 160 Portfolio ES = 103.2 103.2 ≤ 160. Subadditivity holds, and the diversification benefit is 56.8, which is the number a capital framework wants. THE COMPARISON, STATED PLAINLY VaR: 0 -> 100 diversification "increased" risk ES: 160 -> 103.2 diversification reduced risk by 35% Same two bonds. One measure is usable in a capital framework and the other is not.

Notice why the failure happens, because the mechanism matters more than the example. Each bond's default probability sits just inside the tail the confidence level ignores: four per cent is less than five per cent, so a single bond's default is invisible to VaR at 95 per cent. Combining two bonds makes the event "at least one default" have probability 7.84 per cent, which is now visible. VaR did not measure more risk; it changed which events it could see.

That generalises to a warning about any concentrated portfolio of low-probability, high-severity exposures, which describes a great deal of credit and insurance risk. VaR at a fixed confidence level is discontinuous in the composition of the portfolio, and near the confidence boundary small changes in composition produce large changes in the measure.

What VaR is nonetheless good for

UseDoes VaR serve it?Why
The measure survived for good reasons and they are not all bad ones
Communicating risk to a board Yes, better than ES "A one-in-a-hundred-day loss of this size" is intelligible in one sentence. Expected shortfall requires explaining a conditional expectation, which the wiki correctly lists as its main drawback.
Setting and monitoring trading limits Yes, and it remains the primary metric Limits need a single comparable number computed daily across desks. Point-in-time VaR does this, and it is what a trader is actually managed against.
Back-testing a model Yes, and better than ES A VaR break is a binary observable event with a known expected frequency, so the test is a simple count. Back-testing a tail expectation is materially harder, which is why FRTB still back-tests VaR at desk level while capitalising on ES.
Aggregating across desks or risk types No Non-subadditive, so the sum of parts is not an upper bound and diversification benefits are not guaranteed to be benefits.
Setting regulatory capital No, and this is why FRTB changed Capital must aggregate, must not reward splitting a portfolio, and must respond to tail severity rather than tail onset.

The third row is the one most people get wrong. FRTB did not abolish VaR: it capitalises on expected shortfall and continues to require VaR back-testing at desk level, precisely because a quantile is easy to test against realised outcomes and a tail expectation is not. The framework uses each measure for what it is good at, which is a more sophisticated position than "ES replaced VaR".

Stressed VaR, and why it exists

Stressed VaR computes the same statistic on a historical window chosen to be severe rather than recent, most commonly a twelve-month period spanning the 2008 crisis, identified by searching the available history for the window that produces the highest measure. Banks run current VaR and stressed VaR together, using current VaR for limit monitoring and both for capital and economic capital.

The design reasoning is worth naming because it is the same reasoning as downturn LGD in credit risk. A measure calibrated on a recent quiet window will understate a stressed outcome, and rather than attempting to model the tail more accurately, the framework fixes the problem by changing the calibration window to one that contains the event. Stressed VaR is a window-selection answer to a distributional problem, and so is downturn LGD.

Which also imports the same weakness. A stressed window chosen from available history cannot contain a stress worse than the worst in the record, so both measures are bounded below by history and give no protection against a novel severity. The A-IRB analogue is familiar to anyone who has argued about whether 2008 is a sufficient downturn for a portfolio that did not exist in 2008.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Non-subadditivity is a capital arbitrage, not just an inelegance

The textbook treatment of subadditivity presents it as a desirable mathematical property and stops there. Inside a capital framework it is stronger than that, because a non-subadditive measure creates an exploitable asymmetry.

Run the worked example backwards. A bank holding both bonds faces a VaR of 100. Split them into two legally or organisationally separate books, measure each separately, and the total is zero. If capital were set on VaR at that confidence level and computed at book level, the bank would reduce its measured risk to nothing by drawing a line down the middle of an unchanged portfolio.

That is not a hypothetical concern about a toy example; it is the general shape of the problem. Any framework built on a non-subadditive measure rewards fragmentation, and fragmentation is the opposite of what a supervisor wants, since it obscures aggregate exposure and makes group-level risk harder to see. Coherence is therefore not an aesthetic requirement on the mathematics. It is the property that stops the capital rule from paying banks to hide.

Which is also why positive homogeneity deserves a second look, because it is the axiom that is arguably wrong in the other direction. It says doubling a position doubles the risk, and for a large concentrated position in an illiquid market that understates matters: the second half is harder to exit than the first. The coherence axioms accept this, and FRTB addresses concentration separately, through liquidity horizons and add-ons, rather than by weakening the axiom.

What this means for an actuary building an economic capital model

The constraint transfers directly and is worth stating as a rule. If you aggregate risk types with a non-subadditive measure, your diversification benefit is not reliably a benefit and the sum of standalone capital is not an upper bound on the aggregate. Anything you then say about the group's capital being lower than the sum of its parts rests on the measure's properties, not only on the correlation assumptions everyone argues about.

The practical implication is that a coherent measure should be preferred wherever aggregation is the point, which in an economic capital model it always is. That is one of the reasons expected shortfall and tail value at risk appear so widely in insurance capital work, and why the Solvency II calibration debate about a 99.5 per cent VaR over one year is a debate about a measure with this known property rather than a settled choice.

There is a subtlety worth carrying, though. Coherence does not make a measure right; it makes it well behaved under aggregation. A coherent measure estimated from a sample that does not contain the tail is still an estimate of nothing in particular, and this is exactly the downturn calibration problem in another vocabulary. The axioms discipline the functional and say nothing about the data.

The honest limitation of the whole apparatus

All of this concerns the properties of a functional applied to a known loss distribution, and the loss distribution is never known. In market risk it is estimated from a historical window whose volatility regime may not be the current one; in credit risk from a window that may contain no downturn at all.

So the ranking of measures by coherence is a genuine and useful result about a secondary question. The primary question is whether the distribution being measured resembles the distribution that will generate next quarter's losses, and no axiom addresses it. FRTB's liquidity horizons, stressed calibration windows and non-modellable risk factor treatment are all attempts on the primary question, and lectures 3 and 5 take them up.

The cleanest way to hold both facts is this. Choosing expected shortfall over value at risk is a real improvement and a cheap one, because it fixes a defect in the measure at no cost in data. It does not address the estimation problem, and a bank that moved from VaR to ES and changed nothing about its calibration window has improved its mathematics and not its risk management.

Common exam error Writing that VaR is not a coherent risk measure without qualification. VaR fails subadditivity in general, and it is coherent when losses follow an elliptical distribution, of which the normal and lognormal are the standard cases. The complete answer states the failure, names the axiom, and states the condition under which the failure does not arise, because that condition is what the defence of VaR actually rests on.
Exam tip If asked why FRTB replaced VaR with expected shortfall, two marks are available for tail severity and coherence, and a third for noticing that the framework did not abandon VaR: desk-level back-testing still uses it, because a quantile produces a countable break event and a tail expectation does not. Candidates who write that ES simply replaced VaR everywhere have missed the design.
