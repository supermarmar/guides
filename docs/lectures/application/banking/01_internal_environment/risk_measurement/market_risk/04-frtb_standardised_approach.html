The FRTB standardised approach

Three components, seven risk classes, three prescribed correlation scenarios, and a capital charge that is the worst of them. Every bank computes this whether or not it holds internal model approval, which makes it the framework's floor rather than its fallback.

Lecture 4 of 6 Reading time, 36 minutes Source: wiki market_risk/01-context.md; BCBS d457
Step 1

The 30-second version

The standardised capital requirement is the sum of three pieces. The sensitivities-based method captures price risk through delta, vega and curvature. Default risk capital captures the jump to default that a credit spread shock does not. The residual risk add-on is a crude notional-based charge for exotic and hard-to-model features.

The sensitivities-based method is the substantial part. For each of seven risk classes the bank computes sensitivities to prescribed risk factors, multiplies them by prescribed risk weights, and aggregates within and across buckets using prescribed correlations. The aggregation is a quadratic form, so offsetting positions receive hedging recognition, and the correlations decide how much.

Because correlations are the lever, the standard does not trust a single set of them. The whole calculation is run three times, under prescribed low, medium and high correlation scenarios, and the capital charge is the largest of the three. That defeats the obvious gaming strategy of constructing a portfolio whose hedge benefit depends on one correlation assumption holding.

Every bank must compute the standardised approach, including banks with internal model approval, and a desk that loses its model approval falls back to it. So it functions as a floor, a comparator and a disclosure benchmark rather than merely as the option for unsophisticated banks.

Feynman test Work out how much each position moves when each market factor moves, multiply by a weight the regulator gives you, and add them up allowing for the fact that some positions offset. Do the adding up three times with three different assumptions about how much things move together, and use the worst answer. Then add a charge for sudden defaults and another for anything too odd to model.

Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this is the approach most banks will actually use. Internal model approval under FRTB is granted desk by desk and is expensive to obtain and to keep, and a substantial part of the industry has concluded that the standardised approach is the economic choice for most or all desks. A framework's default matters more than its sophisticated option.

The second reason is that the three-scenario device is a genuinely interesting piece of regulatory design and it generalises. The problem being solved is that any capital formula giving diversification credit can be gamed by constructing exposures whose offset depends on the assumed correlation. Running the calculation under prescribed alternatives and taking the worst is a cheap and robust answer, and it is worth recognising as a pattern rather than a quirk.

The third reason is that the sensitivities-based method makes capital a function of Greeks, which changes what a trading desk optimises. Under a value-at-risk regime a desk manages a distribution; under the SBM it manages delta, vega and curvature against prescribed weights, and the two do not always point the same way. That is a real behavioural consequence of a capital rule.

And a fourth reason for credit risk specialists. Default risk capital is a credit charge computed inside the market risk framework, with prescribed loss given default values and prescribed risk weights by credit quality. Reading it alongside the A-IRB machinery is instructive: the same economic quantity appears with regulator-set parameters rather than modelled ones, and the differences show what the standardised philosophy gives up and what it protects against.

Step 3

The mental model

Three components, the seven risk classes inside the largest one, and the aggregation that runs three times.

Weighted sensitivity
A position's sensitivity to a prescribed risk factor, multiplied by the risk weight the standard assigns to that factor. The atom from which the whole sensitivities-based charge is built.
Jump-to-default risk
The loss from an obligor defaulting outright rather than its credit spread widening. Captured by default risk capital because a spread-based measure calibrated to continuous moves misses a discontinuous one.
Correlation scenario
One of three prescribed transformations of the standard's correlations, low, medium and high. The capital charge is the maximum across the three, which removes the incentive to construct hedges that depend on a single assumption.
Step 4

The detail

A worked sensitivities-based bucket

One currency's general interest rate risk delta, three tenor points, a position that is long the two-year, short the five-year and long the ten-year. The risk weights and the correlation function are the standard's.

SETUP Delta sensitivities, in currency units per unit shift: 2y: +4,000 5y: -2,500 10y: +1,800 Prescribed GIRR risk weights by tenor: 2y: 1.3% 5y: 1.1% 10y: 1.1% STEP 1 · WEIGHTED SENSITIVITIES WS(2y) = 0.013 x 4,000 = 52.0 WS(5y) = 0.011 x -2,500 = -27.5 WS(10y) = 0.011 x 1,800 = 19.8 Undiversified total |WS| = 52.0 + 27.5 + 19.8 = 99.3 That is the charge with no hedge recognition at all. STEP 2 · PRESCRIBED CORRELATIONS GIRR tenor correlations use rho(k,l) = max( exp( -theta |T(k)-T(l)| / min(T(k),T(l)) ), 40% ), theta = 3% rho(2,5) = max(exp(-0.03 x 3 / 2), 0.40) = 0.955997 rho(2,10) = max(exp(-0.03 x 8 / 2), 0.40) = 0.886920 rho(5,10) = max(exp(-0.03 x 5 / 5), 0.40) = 0.970446 Note the 40% floor: however far apart two tenors are, the standard will not let the bank treat them as independent. Another anti-gaming device. STEP 3 · MEDIUM SCENARIO sum WS^2 = 52.0^2 + 27.5^2 + 19.8^2 = 3,852.29 cross = 2(0.955997)(52.0)(-27.5) + 2(0.886920)(52.0)(19.8) + 2(0.970446)(-27.5)(19.8) = -2,734.15 + 1,826.32 - 1,056.82 = -1,964.65 K(b) = sqrt(3,852.29 - 1,964.65) = sqrt(1,887.64) = 43.4473 STEP 4 · ALL THREE SCENARIOS HIGH: rho -> min(1.25 rho, 1) = 1.0 for all three pairs With every rho at 1, the quadratic form collapses to (sum WS)^2 = (52.0 - 27.5 + 19.8)^2 = 44.3^2 K(b) = 44.3000 MEDIUM: K(b) = 43.4473 LOW: rho -> max(2 rho - 1, 0.75 rho) = 0.911994, 0.773840, 0.940892 K(b) = 42.5775 STEP 5 · THE CHARGE max(42.5775, 43.4473, 44.3000) = 44.30 Against an undiversified 99.30, so hedge recognition of 55.00, which is 55 per cent of the gross. WHY HIGH BINDS HERE The net WS across tenors is positive (+44.3) and the five-year short is the offsetting leg. Raising the correlations makes the offset less valuable, so the high scenario is the adverse one for a portfolio whose benefit comes from an internal hedge. A portfolio relying on positions moving TOGETHER would find the low scenario binding instead. That is exactly the point of running all three.

A worked default risk charge

SETUP, corporates bucket Long senior corporate bond, BBB: notional 10,000, LGD 75%, position P&L -200 Short senior corporate bond, BB (different obligor): notional 4,000, LGD 75%, position P&L +100 STEP 1 · GROSS JUMP-TO-DEFAULT JTD = LGD x notional + P&L long JTD = 0.75 x 10,000 + (-200) = 7,300 short JTD = 0.75 x (-4,000) + 100 = -2,900 Note the P&L term. A position already marked down has less left to lose on default, and the formula credits that. STEP 2 · PRESCRIBED DEFAULT RISK WEIGHTS By credit quality, a schedule running from 0.5% for AAA up to 100% for already-defaulted. Here: BBB = 6% BB = 15% RW x net long = 0.06 x 7,300 = 438.0 RW x net short = 0.15 x 2,900 = 435.0 STEP 3 · THE HEDGE BENEFIT RATIO WtS = net long / (net long + net short) = 7,300 / (7,300 + 2,900) = 7,300 / 10,200 = 0.715686 STEP 4 · THE BUCKET CHARGE DRC(b) = max( RW x net long - WtS x RW x net short , 0 ) = max( 438.0 - 0.715686 x 435.0 , 0 ) = max( 438.0 - 311.32 , 0 ) = 126.68 WHAT WtS IS DOING A short position in a DIFFERENT obligor is a partial hedge, not a perfect one. Full offset would give 438.0 - 435.0 = 3.0, which would treat two different corporate names as substitutes. No offset at all would give 438.0. The ratio scales the recognised offset by how much of the bucket is long, so a bucket that is mostly long gets more of its short positions recognised, and a balanced bucket gets less. STEP 5 · ACROSS BUCKETS Bucket charges are added as a SIMPLE SUM, with no diversification recognised between corporates, sovereigns and local government. Default risk is not treated as diversifiable across those categories.

The residual risk add-on

The RRAO is the crudest part of the framework and it is deliberately so. It is a flat charge on gross notional, at one per cent for instruments with an exotic underlying and 0.1 per cent for instruments bearing other residual risks, added on top of everything else with no netting and no diversification.

Its function is to price the risk of the framework being wrong. An instrument whose payoff depends on something the sensitivities-based method does not represent, a longevity or weather underlying, or one whose behaviour cannot be captured by delta, vega and curvature, is charged a small percentage of notional simply for being outside the model's vocabulary. Because it applies to gross notional rather than to any risk measure, it is punitive for large notional positions with small risk, which is intended: the framework is expressing a preference against instruments it cannot see properly.

The design lesson is worth taking. Rather than attempting to extend the risk-sensitive machinery to cover exotic features badly, the standard admits the limit and applies a blunt instrument outside it. That is more honest than a false precision, and the same choice appears in the non-modellable risk factor treatment on the internal models side.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The standardised approach is not the simple option any more

Under Basel II the standardised approach to market risk was genuinely simple, a set of building-block charges that a spreadsheet could produce. The FRTB standardised approach is not simple: it requires full sensitivities across seven risk classes and three risk measures, prescribed bucketing, two nested quadratic aggregations and three complete runs of the calculation.

That is a deliberate change in the relationship between the two approaches. The old standardised approach was so crude that any bank with the capability preferred internal models, so the choice was effectively determined by capability rather than by economics. The new one is risk-sensitive enough to be a credible comparator, which is what allows it to serve as a floor and as a disclosure benchmark against internal model outputs.

It also changes the industry's calculus in a way worth noticing. A great deal of the infrastructure needed for the standardised approach, a complete and consistent sensitivities production process across all trading positions, is expensive, and once a bank has built it the incremental cost of the standardised approach is low while the ongoing cost of maintaining desk-level internal model approval is not. Several large banks have concluded that standardised is the answer for most or all desks, which was not the expected outcome when FRTB was designed.

What making capital a function of Greeks does to behaviour

Under a value-at-risk capital regime, a desk reduces capital by reducing the dispersion of its profit and loss distribution, which is a reasonably direct proxy for reducing risk. Under the sensitivities-based method, a desk reduces capital by reducing weighted sensitivities against prescribed weights and correlations, and those are not the same objective.

The divergence has specific forms. A hedge that reduces economic risk but pairs positions across buckets with a low prescribed gamma receives less capital credit than its economics deserve. A position whose risk sits in a factor the standard weights lightly is cheap regardless of its actual volatility. And the forty per cent correlation floor in the worked example means a genuinely diversified tenor position cannot be recognised as such.

None of that is an argument against the framework, because the alternative, letting banks model their own correlations, is what the three-scenario device exists to avoid. It is an argument for knowing that the capital number and the risk are related but distinct, and for a risk function that manages both rather than assuming the capital charge is a risk measure. Anybody who has watched a credit portfolio be optimised against A-IRB risk weights rather than against expected loss will recognise the pattern exactly.

What a credit risk modeller should take from the DRC

Default risk capital is the most familiar part of this framework to anyone from the credit side, and the instructive part is the comparison rather than the mechanics.

The DRC computes an economic quantity you know well, exposure times loss given default times a default risk weight, and it sets every parameter by prescription. Loss given default is 75 per cent for senior debt and 100 per cent for equity and non-senior, and the risk weights come from a table indexed on credit quality. There is no modelling, no calibration window, no downturn adjustment and no representativeness question, and consequently none of the argument that consumes A-IRB validation.

What that buys is comparability and immunity from optimism. What it gives up is any recognition that this bank's senior recoveries in this jurisdiction on this collateral might genuinely differ from 75 per cent, which for a specialised portfolio can be a large error in either direction. The trade is the whole standardised-versus-modelled argument in one parameter, and it is worth being able to state it in both directions rather than assuming modelled is better because it is more sophisticated.

The hedge benefit ratio is the other thing to take away, because it is a neat solution to a problem credit models handle less elegantly. Offsetting exposures to different obligors are partial hedges, and the ratio grants recognition scaled by the bucket's directional balance rather than by any estimated correlation between the names. It is crude, it cannot be gamed, and it requires no correlation input at all.

Common exam error Describing the sensitivities-based method as producing one number. It produces three, one per correlation scenario, and the charge is the maximum. Candidates who omit the three scenarios have missed the part of the design that makes prescribed correlations workable, and it is a reliable place for marks because it is easy to state and easy to justify.
Exam tip The three components are worth memorising in order with a one-line rationale each: sensitivities for price risk, default risk capital for the discontinuity a spread shock misses, residual add-on for what the framework cannot see. If you can add that bucket-level DRC charges are summed with no cross-bucket diversification, you have said something most summaries omit.
