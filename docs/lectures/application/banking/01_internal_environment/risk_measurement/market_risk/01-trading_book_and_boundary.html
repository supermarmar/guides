The trading book and the boundary

Market risk capital applies to the trading book, so the definition of the trading book is the definition of the framework's scope. Before 2019 that definition rested on the bank's own stated intent, which is why the boundary is the part of FRTB that changed most.

Lecture 1 of 6 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: wiki market_risk/01-context.md; BCBS d457
Step 1

The 30-second version

A bank's balance sheet is divided into two regulatory books. The trading book holds positions taken with trading intent, marked to market daily, and attracts market risk capital. The banking book holds everything else, largely measured at amortised cost, and attracts credit risk capital. The same instrument can sit in either book, and the capital charge differs, often by a great deal.

That is the whole problem. Until the Fundamental Review of the Trading Book, allocation rested substantially on the bank's own declared intent, which is unobservable and revisable. A bank could place an instrument where capital was cheaper and, when conditions changed, move it. In 2008 the trading book turned out to hold a great deal of what was in substance credit risk, at market risk capital levels calibrated for liquid positions that could be exited in ten days.

FRTB's answer has three parts. Presumptive lists assign instruments to a book by their nature rather than by intent. Reclassification is punished rather than merely controlled: it needs supervisory approval, must be publicly disclosed, and any capital benefit is disallowed. And positions must be assigned to trading desks with a defined strategy, which becomes the unit at which internal model approval is granted.

The fourth piece is the internal risk transfer framework, which governs how risk legitimately moves between the books. Without it, the boundary rules would prevent banks from hedging banking book risk in the market, which is a thing they must be able to do.

Feynman test There are two boxes and the capital charge depends on which box a thing is in. Banks used to choose the box by saying what they intended to do with the instrument. Now the rules mostly choose for them, moving between boxes needs permission and is announced publicly, and you never get a capital saving from moving.

Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that scope determines everything downstream. Lectures 2 to 5 develop risk measures and capital methodologies, and all of them presuppose a defined set of positions to apply them to. A framework with a porous boundary is not a framework with a measurement problem; it is a framework with no denominator.

The second reason is that this is the clearest example in banking regulation of a rule failing because it relied on unverifiable private information. Intent is known only to the bank, cannot be audited after the fact, and changes. Any student of regulatory design should be able to say why intent-based classification was always going to be gamed, and the answer generalises well beyond market risk.

The third reason is that the 2008 experience is specific and worth knowing precisely. Losses arose in the trading book on positions that were economically credit exposures: securitisation tranches, correlation trading positions, and structured credit held at market risk capital levels. The capital held against them assumed a ten-day liquidation horizon, and in the event those positions could not be liquidated at all. That is the origin of both the boundary reform and the liquidity horizons in lecture 3.

And a reason for exam candidates. The boundary is examinable in a form that rewards precision: you can be asked which book an instrument belongs in and why, and the presumptive lists give a defensible answer where an appeal to intent does not.

Step 3

The mental model

Two books, the presumptions that allocate between them, and the one-way valve that governs movement.

Trading book
Positions held with trading intent: short-term resale, profiting from short-term price movements, locking in arbitrage profits, or hedging another trading book position. Marked to market daily and capitalised under the market risk framework.
Presumptive list
FRTB's device for replacing intent with instrument type. Named categories are presumed to belong to one book, and a bank departing from the presumption needs supervisory approval to do so.
Internal risk transfer
An internal transaction moving risk between the banking book and a trading desk, so that banking book exposures can be hedged externally without the underlying positions crossing the boundary. Governed by separate rules for credit, equity and interest rate risk.
Step 4

The detail

What the boundary decides, in capital terms

Trading bookBanking book
The same instrument, two regimes
Measurement Fair value through profit and loss, marked daily. Losses hit P&L and CET1 immediately. Amortised cost or fair value through other comprehensive income, with IFRS 9 expected credit loss provisioning.
Capital framework Market risk: sensitivities-based method plus default risk capital plus residual risk add-on, or the internal models approach. Credit risk: standardised or IRB, with risk weights from PD, LGD and EAD.
Horizon assumption Days to months. The framework assumes the position can be exited or hedged, which is what liquidity horizons formalise. One year for regulatory capital, with lifetime expected loss for IFRS 9 stage 2 and 3.
Interest rate risk Captured in the market risk charge. Captured under IRRBB in Pillar 2, not Pillar 1.
What went wrong in 2008 Structured credit and correlation positions sat here, capitalised on the assumption they were liquid. They were not. Nothing comparable, which is precisely why the incentive ran towards the trading book.

The horizon row is the substantive point and it connects to lecture 3. Trading book capital is not lower because regulators were careless about market risk; it is lower because a position that can be closed in ten days carries ten days of risk rather than a year of it. The 2008 failure was not a calibration error in the risk measure but a false premise about liquidity, and FRTB's liquidity horizons are the direct response.

The desk structure, and why it matters later

FRTB REQUIRES TRADING BOOK POSITIONS TO SIT IN DESKS. A TRADING DESK MUST HAVE a defined business strategy, documented a clear reporting line and a head trader a defined risk management structure and limits an identifiable set of risk factors it trades WHY THIS IS MORE THAN ADMINISTRATION Internal model approval under FRTB is granted DESK BY DESK, not bank-wide. A desk must pass, and keep passing: back-testing at desk level profit and loss attribution (PLA), comparing the risk model's P&L against the front office's A desk that fails moves to the standardised approach for its positions, while other desks may keep IMA approval. THE CONSEQUENCE FOR A BANK Capital becomes a function of organisational structure. How you draw desk boundaries determines which positions are pooled for back-testing, which changes the probability of failure, which changes capital. That is a genuine modelling decision made by an organisational chart, and it is one of the more peculiar features of the post-FRTB world. THE CONSEQUENCE FOR A VALIDATOR Desk definition is a model input. It should be documented, justified and stable, and a change in desk boundaries is a model change. Lecture 5 develops this.

Where the boundary still bites

Three cases are worth knowing because they recur and because the presumptive lists do not settle them by themselves.

Equity investments in funds are the clearest. A fund holding is presumed trading book if the bank has daily look-through to the fund's underlying positions, so that it can compute sensitivities, and presumed banking book if it does not. The rule is therefore about information availability rather than intent, which is a neat design: the bank cannot claim trading treatment for something it cannot measure daily.

Structured products and securitisations sit inside the trading book where they are traded, and FRTB treats them separately within the framework, with securitised exposures carved out of the correlation trading treatment and subject to their own risk weights under the sensitivities-based method. The 2008 lesson is visible in the design: the framework no longer allows a securitisation tranche to be capitalised as though it were a generic credit spread exposure.

And the most practically awkward case is a banking book position hedged externally. The underlying loan stays in the banking book, the hedge sits on a trading desk, and the internal risk transfer connects them. Whether the hedge is recognised for banking book capital purposes depends on satisfying the internal risk transfer conditions, and for credit risk transfers those conditions are strict. A bank that hedges economically but fails the conditions carries the market risk on the hedge and gets no credit relief on the exposure, which is the worst of both books.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The boundary is a supervisory design problem, not a measurement problem

It is tempting to read the boundary rules as plumbing and skip to the risk measures, and that reading misses what the reform was about. Basel 2.5 and FRTB did not primarily conclude that value at risk was mis-estimated in 2008. They concluded that the framework had been applied to the wrong set of positions, at horizons that did not hold, because the allocation rule was self-declared.

Which makes the boundary the most transferable part of this lecture. Any regulatory or internal control that depends on a firm's statement of its own intentions has the same weakness, and credit risk has its own examples: the definition of default with its unlikely-to-pay limb, the SICR assessment under IFRS 9, and the classification of a restructuring as forbearance. In each case a materially different capital or provision outcome turns on a judgement the institution makes about itself.

FRTB's three-part answer is worth generalising as a pattern. Replace the judgement with an observable presumption where you can. Where you cannot, permit the judgement but require approval and disclosure. And remove the benefit from making the judgement in your own favour, which is the part most frameworks omit and the part that actually changes behaviour.

On the implementation timetable, and why you should check it

The wiki states that FRTB goes live from 1 January 2023, and that is correct as a statement about the Basel Committee's standard. It is not a safe statement about any particular jurisdiction, and the difference matters for anyone answering an exam question or advising on a real balance sheet.

The standard was published in January 2016 as BCBS d352 with an intended 2019 start, substantially revised in January 2019 as d457, and the Committee's implementation date moved to 1 January 2022 and then to 1 January 2023 following the deferral agreed during the pandemic. National implementation has since diverged considerably, with jurisdictions adopting the market risk rules on different timetables and in some cases repeatedly deferring them, and with reporting requirements sometimes preceding own-funds requirements by years.

The honest position, and the right one for an exam, is to state the Basel date as the standard's date, note that jurisdictional implementation diverges, and check the current national timetable rather than asserting one from memory. A specific date asserted confidently and wrongly is worse than a correct statement of the structure, and this is an area where dates have moved more than once.

What an actuary should notice

Two things about this framework will look familiar and one will not.

Familiar first: the boundary problem is the classification problem that runs through insurance regulation too, where the treatment of a contract depends on whether it transfers significant insurance risk, and the answer determines the measurement model. The instinct that classification rules attract arbitrage and therefore need anti-abuse provisions is the same instinct in both fields.

Also familiar: the desk-level approval regime is a granularity decision of exactly the kind credit risk modellers argue about. Whether you pool for stability or segment for homogeneity is the segmentation question from A-IRB modelling, appearing here as an organisational question with a capital consequence.

What is not familiar is the horizon. A credit risk modeller works with annual PDs estimated over economic cycles and worries about whether a decade of data contains a downturn. A market risk modeller works with ten-day expected shortfall and has thousands of daily observations, and worries instead about whether last month's volatility regime is this month's. Both are sampling problems and they pull in opposite directions: credit risk has too little data about the tail, and market risk has plenty of data about a distribution that will not stay still.

Common exam error Describing the boundary as a choice the bank makes. Under FRTB it is a presumption the bank may rebut with permission, which is a materially different answer, and the marks are in the difference. If asked how a bank decides which book an instrument sits in, the answer is that for most instruments it does not decide: the standard's presumptive lists do, and departing from them requires supervisory approval.
Exam tip The three reclassification rules are a reliable source of marks because they are enumerable and because most candidates remember only the first. Approval, public disclosure, and disallowance of any capital benefit. If you can add that the disallowed benefit is held as an additional capital requirement for as long as the position is held, you have said the thing that distinguishes a candidate who has read the standard from one who has read a summary of it.

Test yourself

  1. Define the trading book by reference to intent, and give the four intents that qualify.
  2. Why did an intent-based boundary fail? State the answer in a form that generalises to other regulatory tests.
  3. Name three instrument categories presumed to sit in each book.
  4. Give the three reclassification rules. Which does the most work, and why?
  5. What is an internal risk transfer, and what would happen without the framework?
  6. Give four differences in treatment between the two books, including the horizon assumption.
  7. What actually went wrong in the trading book in 2008? Was it a calibration failure in the risk measure?
  8. What must a trading desk have, and why is desk definition a capital-relevant decision?
  9. On what basis is a fund holding allocated between the books, and why is that a good design?
  10. Describe the worst-of-both-books outcome for a hedged banking book exposure.
  11. State the FRTB implementation position accurately, including what you should not assert.
  12. Give two features of this framework that an actuary would find familiar and one that they would not.
