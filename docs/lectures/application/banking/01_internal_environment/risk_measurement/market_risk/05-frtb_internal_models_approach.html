The FRTB internal models approach

Approval is granted desk by desk and kept by passing two tests. Back-testing is almost impossible to fail by chance, which tells you the binding constraint is the other one: profit and loss attribution, a test of one of the bank's models against another.

Lecture 5 of 6 Reading time, 36 minutes Source: wiki market_risk/01-context.md; BCBS d457
Step 1

The 30-second version

Internal model approval is conditional on supervisory permission, and under FRTB it is granted desk by desk rather than bank-wide. A desk holds its eligibility by passing two continuing tests, and positions on a desk that fails move to the standardised approach while the rest of the bank's desks are unaffected.

Back-testing compares daily VaR against realised profit and loss over the most recent 250 days, with failure at more than twelve exceptions at 99 per cent or more than thirty at 97.5 per cent. Both thresholds sit at 4.8 times the expected count, and a correctly specified model fails by chance with probability around two in a million. This test does not bind.

Profit and loss attribution is the one that does. It compares the risk model's own profit and loss against the front office's pricing model on the same positions, using a rank correlation and a distributional test, and sorts desks into green, amber and red zones. Amber desks keep approval at a capital surcharge; red desks lose it.

That is a genuinely unusual form of validation, because it tests one of the bank's models against another rather than against reality. It is benchmarking, which credit risk does as good practice, made mandatory with quantitative thresholds and a capital consequence.

Feynman test You get permission to use your own model, one trading desk at a time. To keep it you must show that your risk model rarely underestimates a day's loss, which is easy, and that your risk model produces roughly the same daily profit and loss as the pricing model the traders actually use, which is hard. Fail the second badly and that desk goes back to the regulator's formula.

Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the desk-level architecture is the reform's real teeth. Under the old regime a bank held bank-wide model permission and losing it was so drastic that supervisors were reluctant to threaten it, which made the threat weak. Desk-level approval is revocable at a scale a supervisor will actually use, and that changes the incentive materially.

The second reason is that the arithmetic in step 4 exposes something a summary cannot. Reciting the two tests suggests two comparable hurdles. Computing the false-failure probabilities shows that back-testing is a formality at these thresholds and attribution is the gate, which tells you where a bank's model risk effort should go and where a supervisor's attention actually falls.

The third reason is that profit and loss attribution is conceptually interesting and worth understanding properly. Comparing a risk model with a pricing model is a coherence test rather than an accuracy test, and it detects a specific failure: a risk model that has been simplified for tractability until it no longer represents the positions it is measuring. That failure is invisible to back-testing, because a model can be simultaneously too crude and conservative enough to avoid exceptions.

And a fourth reason for actuaries. This is a regulator specifying a benchmarking test with numerical pass marks and a capital penalty, which is a thing credit risk validation does informally and insurance validation barely does at all. Whether that is a good idea is a live question, and the arguments transfer.

Step 3

The mental model

Three gates to eligibility, two continuing tests, and the assembly of the capital number.

Profit and loss attribution
A comparison of the risk model's valuation of static positions against the front office pricing model's valuation of the same positions. Detects a risk model that has been simplified until it no longer represents what it measures.
IMCC
The internally modelled capital charge: an equally weighted blend of expected shortfall computed across all risk factors together and the sum of expected shortfall computed risk class by risk class, so modelled diversification is credited at half weight.
Desk-level eligibility
Internal model permission granted and withdrawn at the level of an individual trading desk rather than the bank. Makes revocation a proportionate supervisory action rather than a crisis.
Step 4

The detail

How lenient is the back-testing gate?

The thresholds look demanding until you compute against them. A desk's exception count over 250 days is binomial, so the false-failure probability follows directly.

AT 99% CONFIDENCE n = 250 days, p = 0.01 Expected exceptions = 250 x 0.01 = 2.5 Failure threshold = more than 12 P(X >= 13 | model correct) = 1.94 x 10^-6 Roughly two chances in a million per desk per year. AT 97.5% CONFIDENCE n = 250 days, p = 0.025 Expected exceptions = 250 x 0.025 = 6.25 Failure threshold = more than 30 P(X >= 31 | model correct) = 3.89 x 10^-13 Four chances in ten trillion. NOTE THE SYMMETRY 12 / 2.5 = 4.8 30 / 6.25 = 4.8 The thresholds are set at the same multiple of the expected count, which is a design choice rather than a coincidence. WHAT THIS MEANS A desk fails back-testing only if its model is badly wrong, by roughly a factor of five in the tail frequency. Correct models are effectively never disqualified, and even substantially deficient models survive. Compare the older Basel traffic light on 99% VaR, where the yellow zone begins at five exceptions: P(X >= 5) = 0.1078, about a one-in-nine chance of entering the yellow zone with a correct model. So the older regime imposed a real risk of a spurious penalty, and FRTB's desk gate does not. THE CONCLUSION FOR A MODEL RISK FUNCTION Back-testing is a floor against gross failure, not a quality test. If your desk is comfortably passing it, you have learned very little. Attribution is where the information is.

That asymmetry is deliberate and defensible. Because eligibility is now per desk and revocation is a live possibility, the Committee needed a test that would not disqualify sound desks through ordinary sampling noise, since a bank cannot manage a capital framework that randomly reclassifies desks. The strictness moved into attribution, where the test is against another model rather than against a noisy realised outcome.

What attribution actually detects

Failure in the risk modelCaught by back-testing?Caught by attribution?
The two tests find different things, which is why both exist
Tail frequency badly understated: too many large losses breach the measure Yes, this is exactly what it tests Possibly not. A model can track daily profit and loss well and still misjudge the tail.
A risk factor the desk trades is missing from the model entirely Often no. The model may still be conservative enough to avoid exceptions. Yes. The missing factor's contribution appears in the pricing model's profit and loss and not in the risk model's, so the series diverge.
Valuation simplified for tractability: linear approximation of an optionality No, and it may improve exception counts by smoothing. Yes, and this is the characteristic case. The rank correlation degrades because the two series respond differently to large moves.
Model deliberately over-conservative to avoid exceptions No. Conservatism improves the back-test. Yes, through the distributional test: a systematically wider risk-theoretical distribution fails the Kolmogorov-Smirnov comparison.
Front office pricing model itself is wrong Possibly, via realised outcomes No. Attribution measures agreement between two models and is silent on whether both are wrong together.

The last row is the honest limitation and it is worth stating plainly. Attribution is a coherence test, so two models that agree with each other and both misrepresent the market pass it comfortably. The framework relies on back-testing and on independent validation to catch that case, and back-testing is, as step 4 showed, extremely lenient.

What happens when a desk fails

The consequences are graduated, which is the point of the traffic light. A green desk keeps its approval. An amber desk keeps it and pays a capital surcharge, which is intended to price the model's demonstrated imprecision rather than to punish it. A red desk loses internal model treatment for its positions, which move to the standardised approach.

Two operational consequences follow that a bank has to plan for. The first is that the standardised approach must be maintained in production for every desk regardless of model status, because a desk can require it at short notice. That is one of the reasons the standardised approach is not optional infrastructure, as lecture 4 argued.

The second is that desk boundaries become a capital-relevant modelling choice, which lecture 1 flagged. A widely drawn desk pools more positions, which stabilises the attribution statistics and reduces the chance of failure, and also means a single failure moves more positions to the standardised approach. A narrowly drawn desk is more likely to fail and fails smaller. Choosing between those is a genuine optimisation with no clean answer, made by an organisational chart, and it should be documented as the model decision it is.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Attribution is mandatory benchmarking, and that is a significant innovation

Model validation traditionally compares a model against outcomes. Attribution does something different: it compares a model against another model built by a different function inside the same bank for a different purpose, and requires them to agree within numerical tolerances.

Credit risk validation has an informal version of this, which is benchmarking a rating model against an external rating, a challenger model or a vendor score. It is regarded as good practice, its thresholds are set by the bank, and failing it produces a finding rather than a capital consequence. FRTB's attribution test is the same idea with the discretion removed: the statistics are specified, the thresholds are specified, and the outcome is capital.

Whether that is a good development is genuinely arguable, and both sides are worth holding. In favour: it detects the specific failure that outcome-based testing cannot see, namely a risk model simplified into unreality, and it is objective in a way that bank-set benchmark thresholds are not. Against: it makes a coherence property into a capital requirement, so a bank can be penalised for a disagreement between two models without any evidence that either is wrong about the world, and it creates an incentive to converge the risk model onto the pricing model whether or not that improves risk measurement.

That last incentive is the one to watch. The cheapest route to a green zone is to make the risk model reuse the front office's valuation library, which guarantees agreement and eliminates the independent view that the risk function was supposed to provide. The test intended to prevent simplification can, applied without care, remove independence instead.

Why so few banks are using this

The internal models approach was expected to be the destination for large trading banks and has turned out not to be, and the reasons are worth assembling because they are all in this lecture.

Approval is per desk, so the cost is incurred repeatedly rather than once. Eligibility must be re-earned continuously, so the cost is ongoing rather than up-front. The standardised approach must be maintained in parallel regardless, so internal models are an addition to the infrastructure rather than a substitute for it. Non-modellable risk factors attract a punitive add-on, so the desks with the most complex positions gain least. And the diversification credit is halved by the rho blend, which removes much of the benefit that made internal models attractive under the old regime.

Put together, the internal models approach offers a capital reduction that is smaller than it used to be, at a cost that is larger and recurring, with a risk of losing it at short notice. Several major banks have concluded that the standardised approach is the answer for most or all desks. That is a reasonable commercial judgement and it is also, arguably, the framework working: if internal models are only worth having where a bank's modelling genuinely adds measurement value, then a framework that prices them properly should reduce their use.

What a credit risk modeller should take from this

Three things transfer, and one of them is uncomfortable.

The transferable technique is the rho blend. Faced with a bank's claim of diversification across risk classes, the Committee did not attempt to verify the correlations or replace them with prescribed ones; it credited half the claimed benefit. That is a robust response to unverifiable diversification and it is directly applicable to economic capital aggregation, where the same problem arises and is usually handled either by accepting the bank's correlation matrix or by arguing about it at length.

The transferable diagnosis is the distinction in step 4's table between a model that is wrong in the tail and a model that is wrong in its representation. A-IRB validation is heavily weighted towards the first, through discriminatory power and calibration testing against realised default rates, and has fewer instruments for the second. A PD model that has been simplified until it no longer distinguishes the risk drivers a credit officer would use can pass calibration testing comfortably, and the FRTB analogue would be an attribution failure.

The uncomfortable item is the leniency calculation. If you have never computed the false-failure rate of your own back-test or calibration test, you do not know whether it is a quality control or a formality, and the FRTB numbers suggest that thresholds set to avoid spurious failures can end up detecting almost nothing. That calculation takes ten minutes for any binomial test and it is worth doing for every threshold in a monitoring framework you rely on.

Common exam error Treating back-testing and attribution as two equivalent hurdles. They test different failures, they differ enormously in strictness, and only attribution has a graduated consequence through the traffic light. A candidate who says both must be passed has stated the rule; one who can say which binds and why has understood the design.
Exam tip The four components of the internal models charge are worth memorising with rationales: the blended expected shortfall, the stressed add-on for non-modellable factors, default risk capital, and the maximum-of-latest-and-averaged construction. If asked why default risk capital is separate, the answer is that a jump to default is not a continuous risk factor move and an expected shortfall over a liquidity horizon cannot represent it.
