CVA, XVA and proprietary trading

A credit exposure whose value moves with market factors belongs to neither framework, which is why CVA has its own. And a bank may recognise the benefit of its own default in its accounts and may not count it as capital, which is the sharpest divergence between the two rulebooks.

Lecture 6 of 6 Reading time, 34 minutes Source: wiki market_risk/03-proprietary_trading_xva.md; BCBS d507
Step 1

The 30-second version

A derivative's fair value is not the textbook discounted expectation. It is adjusted for a family of costs the pre-crisis framework ignored, collectively XVA. CVA is the cost of the counterparty defaulting, DVA the benefit of your own default, FVA the cost of funding the position, MVA the cost of funding initial margin, and KVA the cost of the regulatory capital the trade consumes for its life.

CVA is the one with a capital framework of its own, and the reason is structural. CVA is a credit exposure whose mark-to-market moves with credit spreads and with every market factor driving the underlying exposure, so it is a credit risk that behaves like a market risk. Neither Pillar 1 framework accommodates it, so it has its own, revised in 2020 into a basic and a standardised approach.

DVA exposes the sharpest accounting-prudential divergence in the framework. Fair value accounting requires a bank to recognise the gain from a deterioration in its own creditworthiness, because its liabilities are worth less. Regulatory capital filters that gain out, because a bank cannot be better capitalised for being closer to failure.

Proprietary trading is the other half of the lecture, and the post-crisis response was structural rather than quantitative. The moral hazard argument, that a bank with implicit taxpayer support has asymmetric incentives, produced the Volcker Rule in the United States and ring-fencing in the United Kingdom, both separating deposit-taking from speculative trading rather than merely capitalising it more heavily.

Feynman test The price of a swap has to include the chance your counterparty fails, the cost of funding it, and the capital it ties up. One of those adjustments, the benefit of your own possible failure, is real in the accounts and struck out for capital, because getting closer to bankruptcy cannot make you better capitalised.

Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that CVA is where the two halves of a bank's risk apparatus meet, and an actuary from the credit side will find it the most legible part of market risk. It is a credit calculation, using exposure profiles and default probabilities, whose result is a mark-to-market that moves daily. Everything difficult about it comes from that combination.

The second reason is that the CVA losses of 2008 were large and were not what anyone had capitalised for. A substantial share of trading losses came not from defaults but from the deterioration in the market value of counterparty credit, on positions where no counterparty had actually failed. Basel III's CVA charge exists because the exposure was live, material and outside both frameworks.

The third reason is the DVA filter, which is worth understanding because it is the clearest case in banking regulation of accounting and prudential rules deliberately diverging, and the divergence is correct. It is a good illustration of why regulatory capital is not equity and why reading one from the other misleads.

And a fourth reason about proprietary trading. The structural response is the most consequential regulatory reform in this track and the least quantitative. It is worth being able to state the moral hazard argument precisely, because it is the argument that justifies bank regulation generally, and this is where it was applied most directly.

Step 3

The mental model

The adjustment family, why CVA needs its own framework, and where DVA is struck out.

CVA
The credit value adjustment: the market value of the expected loss from a counterparty defaulting before a derivative matures. A credit quantity that is marked to market, and therefore volatile.
DVA filter
The prudential requirement to derecognise from regulatory capital any gain or loss arising from changes in the bank's own credit risk, reversing an accounting entry that fair value measurement requires.
Moral hazard
The asymmetry created when a bank retains the upside of risk-taking while an implicit public guarantee absorbs the downside. The argument underlying both the Volcker Rule and ring-fencing.
Step 4

The detail

What CVA actually computes

THE UNILATERAL CVA, IN STRUCTURE CVA = LGD x SUM over time buckets of [ discounted expected exposure at t ] x [ marginal probability of default in t ] IN WORDS For each future time bucket, ask two questions: how much would the counterparty owe us if it defaulted then? -> expected exposure how likely is it to default then? -> marginal PD Multiply, apply loss given default, discount, and sum. THE THREE INPUTS AND WHERE THEY COME FROM EXPECTED EXPOSURE from a Monte Carlo simulation of the derivative's value across future paths, floored at zero because a counterparty owing nothing costs nothing on default. This is the market risk input. MARGINAL PD from credit spreads where the counterparty trades, so market-implied rather than through-the-cycle. This is why CVA moves daily. LGD usually a market convention rather than a modelled recovery, for consistency with the spread used. WHY IT MOVES SO MUCH Two of the three inputs are market-observed and move together in stress: exposure rises as volatility rises, and spreads widen at the same time. So CVA is convex in stress, which is what made 2008 CVA losses so large without a single default occurring. WHAT A CREDIT MODELLER SHOULD NOTICE The PD here is market-implied, not the through-the-cycle or point-in-time estimate an A-IRB or IFRS 9 model produces. The same obligor therefore carries two different default probabilities inside one bank, for two legitimate purposes, and reconciling them is a standing source of confusion. The distinction is real: a spread-implied PD contains a risk premium and a liquidity component, so it is not an estimate of the physical default probability at all.

The XVA family, with what is contested

AdjustmentWhat it pricesStatus
Not all of these are equally settled
CVA Counterparty default before maturity Settled. Universally applied, accounting-recognised, and separately capitalised.
DVA The benefit of the bank's own default Accounting-required and prudentially filtered. Also uncomfortable in principle: it books a gain from a deterioration the bank's shareholders would not welcome.
FVA Funding cost of the position and of collateral posted Contested theoretically, universal in practice. Hull and White argued in 2012 that FVA should not exist, on the grounds that a firm's funding cost reflects the risk of its assets and adjusting valuations for it double counts. The market adopted it regardless, and large one-off FVA charges were taken by major dealers.
MVA Funding cost of initial margin Increasingly material, because mandatory clearing and the uncleared margin rules have pushed initial margin requirements up substantially since 2008.
KVA Cost of regulatory capital over the trade's life Applied by many dealers and conceptually awkward: it prices a regulatory requirement into a market value, so a change in the capital rules changes the fair value of an unchanged trade.

The FVA row is worth dwelling on because it is a genuine and unresolved disagreement between theory and practice. Hull and White's argument runs from the Modigliani-Miller logic: a firm's funding spread compensates its creditors for the risk of its assets, so charging a trade for the firm's funding spread double counts the risk already in the trade's own valuation. The counter-argument from practitioners is that the funding cost is a real cash outflow that someone has to bear, and that a desk which ignores it will systematically write trades that lose money for the bank. Both are right about different things, which is why the debate persists.

Proprietary trading and the structural response

The wiki's account of the moral hazard argument is worth restating precisely because it is the general justification for bank regulation applied at its sharpest. A bank whose failure would be socially costly enjoys an implicit expectation of rescue. That expectation lowers its funding cost, and it means the bank keeps the gains from risk-taking while a public guarantee absorbs part of the losses. Speculative trading funded by insured deposits is the purest form of the asymmetry, which is what produced the phrase casino banking.

TWO JURISDICTIONS, TWO STRUCTURAL ANSWERS UNITED STATES · THE VOLCKER RULE Part of the Dodd-Frank Act, July 2010. Prohibits banking entities from proprietary trading and from certain relationships with hedge funds and private equity funds. PERMITTED, with conditions: market-making, underwriting, risk-mitigating hedging, and trading in US government securities. Effect: reverses part of the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act of 1999 and restores an element of the Glass-Steagall separation of 1933. The compliance difficulty is definitional: market- making and proprietary trading look identical from outside, because both involve holding inventory. The rule was substantially simplified in amendments around 2019 and 2020 for exactly that reason. UNITED KINGDOM · RING-FENCING Recommended by the Independent Commission on Banking (the Vickers Commission), reporting 2011. Legislated in the Financial Services (Banking Reform) Act 2013, in force from 1 January 2019. Applies to banking groups above a core deposits threshold, set at GBP 25 billion. Requires retail and small-business deposit-taking to sit in a separate legal entity with its own board, its own capital, and constrained dealings with the rest of the group. Effect: separation by structure rather than by activity, so the investment bank may still trade, but not with the ring-fenced bank's balance sheet. THE DESIGN DIFFERENCE, WHICH IS THE EXAMINABLE POINT Volcker bans an ACTIVITY and must therefore define it. Ring-fencing separates an ENTITY and must therefore define the perimeter. Defining an activity is harder, because intent is unobservable, which is the same problem lecture 1 found in the trading book boundary. Defining a perimeter is easier to enforce and permits the group to continue the activity elsewhere.

That last comparison is the substantive insight and it connects the whole track. The Volcker Rule attempts to regulate on the basis of what a bank is trying to do, which is exactly the failed premise of the pre-FRTB trading book boundary. Ring-fencing regulates on the basis of where an activity sits, which is observable. Two decades of regulatory experience point consistently in one direction: rules that turn on structure are enforceable and rules that turn on intent are negotiated.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Two corrections and an update to the wiki

The wiki's XVA table describes KVA as "to be replaced by SA-CCR", and that needs untangling because the two things are different in kind. KVA is a valuation adjustment: a price charged to a client for the capital a trade will consume. SA-CCR, the standardised approach to counterparty credit risk, is an exposure measurement method, replacing the earlier current exposure method and standardised method for computing exposure at default on derivatives.

SA-CCR therefore does not replace KVA. What it does is change one of KVA's inputs: if the exposure measure changes, the capital the trade consumes changes, so the KVA changes. The relationship is that SA-CCR feeds KVA rather than supersedes it, and the distinction matters because a candidate who says SA-CCR replaced KVA has confused a pricing adjustment with a regulatory exposure calculation.

The second item is an update rather than a correction. The wiki explains the multi-curve framework in terms of the spread between three-month and six-month LIBOR, which was the right explanation when written and describes a benchmark that no longer exists. Sterling LIBOR panels ceased at the end of 2021 and the main US dollar LIBOR settings ceased in mid-2023, with markets transitioning to overnight risk-free rates, SONIA in sterling and SOFR in dollars, compounded in arrears.

The multi-curve insight survives the transition intact and its illustration changes. The pre-crisis error was using one curve to both project and discount cash flows; the post-crisis correction was recognising that the discount rate depends on the collateral, so a collateralised trade discounts at the overnight rate rather than at a term interbank rate. That reasoning is now expressed through risk-free rate curves plus credit-sensitive spreads rather than through LIBOR tenor basis, and the underlying point, that projection and discounting are different jobs requiring different curves, is unchanged.

Why the DVA filter is the right answer

The DVA filter looks at first like regulators overriding accountants for convenience, and it is worth seeing why it is principled.

Accounting measures a firm's position at fair value, and it is simply true that a firm whose credit has deteriorated could extinguish its liabilities more cheaply. Recognising that is internally consistent, and refusing to recognise it would make the fair value of a liability depend on who held it.

Regulatory capital measures something else: the resources available to absorb losses while continuing as a going concern. A gain arising from the bank's own approach to failure is not available for that purpose, because realising it requires either buying back debt with cash the bank does not have in stress, or actually defaulting. So counting it would make the capital ratio rise as the bank deteriorated, which is the precise opposite of what a capital ratio is for.

The general lesson is worth carrying beyond this case. Accounting and prudential frameworks answer different questions, and the temptation to treat one as a version of the other produces errors in both directions. The same divergence appears in the IFRS 9 and expected loss capital relationship, in the treatment of unrealised gains on securities, and in the pension surplus deductions, and in each case the resolution comes from asking what the number is for rather than which rulebook is correct.

Where this track ends up

Six lectures on market risk produce a consistent picture worth stating as a whole, because the pieces reinforce each other.

The framework's failures in 2008 were failures of scope and of premise rather than of measurement: the wrong positions were in the trading book, and their assumed exit horizons did not hold. The reforms addressed the measure as well, and the measure reform is the intellectually satisfying part while the horizon and boundary reforms are the economically significant ones. Throughout, the Committee has consistently refused to trust modelled correlation: three prescribed scenarios in the standardised approach, a fifty per cent haircut on modelled diversification in the internal models approach, a forty per cent floor on tenor correlations, and a hedge benefit ratio in default risk capital that uses no correlation input at all.

And the structural reforms sit outside all of it. No amount of capital calibration addresses the moral hazard of speculative trading funded by insured deposits, which is why the most consequential post-crisis change to trading was not a risk weight but a legal perimeter. That is worth remembering when reading any quantitative framework: the calibration answers a question that has already been narrowed by a structural decision made elsewhere.

Common exam error Describing CVA as a credit risk charge or as a market risk charge. It is neither: it is a credit exposure measured at fair value, capitalised under its own framework precisely because the two Pillar 1 frameworks each capture half of it. Saying which half each misses, the default event for market risk and the daily mark for credit risk, is what earns the marks.
Exam tip On proprietary trading, the marks are in the comparison rather than the descriptions. Volcker prohibits an activity and must define it; ring-fencing separates an entity and must define a perimeter. Add that defining an activity founders on unobservable intent, the same failure as the pre-FRTB trading book boundary, and you have connected two parts of the syllabus in one sentence.
