Expected shortfall and the move to tail measures

FRTB chose 97.5 per cent expected shortfall rather than 99 per cent, and the choice was not arbitrary. Under the normal distribution the two are the same number to within half a per cent, so the change of measure smuggles in no change of level. Under fat tails they diverge, which is the entire point.

Lecture 3 of 6 Reading time, 34 minutes Source: wiki market_risk/02-models.md; BCBS d457
Step 1

The 30-second version

Expected shortfall is the average loss in the worst tail rather than the threshold at which the tail begins. Where VaR reports a quantile, ES reports a conditional expectation, and that single change repairs the coherence failure lecture 2 demonstrated: ES satisfies all four axioms, including subadditivity, so it aggregates sensibly and cannot be reduced by splitting a portfolio.

FRTB adopted ES at a 97.5 per cent one-tailed confidence level in place of 99 per cent VaR, and the calibration is deliberate. Under a normal distribution, ES at 97.5 per cent is 2.3378 standard deviations and VaR at 99 per cent is 2.3264, a difference of half a per cent. The measure changed and the nominal severity did not.

What changes is behaviour under fat tails. The shortfall-to-quantile ratio is 1.193 under the normal and rises monotonically as tails thicken, reaching 1.58 for a Student-t with three degrees of freedom. So the new measure is calibrated to agree with the old one where the old one was defensible, and to exceed it precisely where the old one understated.

Two further FRTB pieces matter as much as the measure. Liquidity horizons assign risk factors to buckets from ten to one hundred and twenty days, which is the direct answer to the 2008 failure lecture 1 described. And the ES must be calibrated to a period of stress rather than the recent past, using a reduced set of risk factors with a long history.

Feynman test Instead of asking how bad things get before the worst 2.5 per cent, ask how bad the worst 2.5 per cent actually is on average. The number they picked comes out the same as the old one for a normal bell curve and bigger for a fat-tailed one, which is exactly the behaviour you want from a replacement.
Beyond the wiki The wiki's 02-models.md gives the ES definition, its advantages and disadvantages, the TVaR formula for the normal case, and the qualitative claim that the shortfall-to-quantile ratio rises as Student-t degrees of freedom fall. This lecture computes that ratio, establishes the 97.5 per cent calibration equivalence which the wiki does not mention, and adds the liquidity horizons, the stressed calibration mechanism, non-modellable risk factors, and the elicitability problem that makes ES hard to back-test. Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the calibration point defeats the commonest misreading of FRTB. Practitioners often assume that moving from 99 per cent VaR to 97.5 per cent ES was a covert tightening, or alternatively a loosening because the percentile fell. Neither is right: the Committee picked the pair that coincides under normality, so any capital change is attributable to tail fatness rather than to the redefinition. That is a well-designed reform and worth recognising as one.

The second reason is that the liquidity horizons are where the 2008 lesson actually lands. Lecture 1 established that the trading book failure was a false premise about exit times rather than a mis-estimated risk measure. A framework that fixed only the measure would have missed the point. The horizons are the fix, and they are why a securitisation position now attracts a much longer assumed holding period than a major currency pair.

The third reason is the estimation cost, which is rarely stated and matters to anyone who has to build the thing. A tail expectation has higher estimation variance than a quantile from the same sample, because it depends on the magnitude of a small number of extreme observations rather than on their position. Switching to ES improves the functional and makes the estimate noisier, and both facts are true simultaneously.

And a fourth reason for actuaries. Expected shortfall is tail value at risk under another name, and TVaR is standard equipment in insurance capital work. The differences in usage between the two industries are worth knowing: banking capitalises a ten-day trading horizon and insurance a one-year underwriting horizon, and the estimation problems are correspondingly opposite.

Step 3

The mental model

The measure, the calibration that makes it comparable, and the two FRTB additions that do as much work as the measure itself.

Expected shortfall
The expected loss conditional on the loss exceeding the VaR threshold, so the average of the tail rather than its starting point. Coherent, and therefore usable for aggregation and capital.
Liquidity horizon
The assumed time to exit or hedge a risk factor without materially moving the market. FRTB assigns every risk factor to one of five buckets from 10 to 120 days, and capital scales with the assignment.
Shortfall-to-quantile ratio
ES divided by VaR at the same confidence level. A pure measure of tail fatness: 1.193 under the normal, rising to 1.582 for a Student-t with three degrees of freedom.
Step 4

The detail

Computing ES for a normal, and the calibration check

THE CLOSED FORM For losses X ~ N(mu, sigma^2), at confidence alpha: ES(alpha) = mu + sigma * phi(z) / (1 - alpha) where z = Phi^-1(alpha), phi is the standard normal density and Phi^-1 the inverse standard normal CDF. (The wiki gives this as the TVaR formula; ES and TVaR coincide for continuous distributions.) WORKED, AT 97.5% z = Phi^-1(0.975) = 1.95996 phi(z) = exp(-z^2/2)/sqrt(2 pi) = 0.058440 ES/sigma = 0.058440 / 0.025 = 2.33780 WORKED, VaR AT 99% FOR COMPARISON Phi^-1(0.99) = 2.32635 THE CALIBRATION ES(97.5%) = 2.33780 sigma VaR(99%) = 2.32635 sigma Difference = 0.01145 sigma, or 0.49 per cent. This is why FRTB paired 97.5% ES with the outgoing 99% VaR. A bank whose returns were genuinely normal would see essentially no change in capital from the switch. NOW THE SAME PAIR UNDER A FAT TAIL Student-t, 4 degrees of freedom: VaR(99%) is larger than the normal's, and ES(97.5%) = 3.9934 against VaR(97.5%) = 2.7765, a ratio of 1.438 rather than 1.193. The measures agree where the assumption holds and diverge where it fails. That is what a well-designed replacement looks like: it binds only in the cases that motivated it. A NUMERICAL EXAMPLE A desk with daily P&L standard deviation of GBP 4m: old: 99% VaR = 2.32635 x 4 = GBP 9.31m new: 97.5% ES = 2.33780 x 4 = GBP 9.35m Under normality the switch costs GBP 40,000 of measured risk. Under a t with 4 df on the same sigma the ES rises to 3.9934 x 4 = GBP 15.97m. The capital consequence of FRTB is therefore a statement about the bank's return distribution, not about the Committee's severity preference.

The last line is the takeaway and it reframes a common complaint. Banks whose FRTB capital rose substantially on the measure change are banks whose trading returns are materially non-normal, which is most banks, and the increase is the framework recognising something that was previously invisible rather than an arbitrary tightening.

Stressed calibration, and the reduced set of risk factors

An ES estimated on the last twelve months of data measures the last twelve months. FRTB requires the ES to be calibrated to a period of significant stress, and it faces an immediate practical obstacle: many current risk factors have no data going back to a stress period, because the instruments or markets did not exist.

The standard's solution is a scaling construction. The bank identifies a reduced set of risk factors, chosen so that they have a long history covering the stress period and so that they explain the bulk of the variation in the full set. It then computes the ES three ways and scales.

THE STRESSED ES CONSTRUCTION ES = ES(reduced set, stress period) x ES(full set, current period) --------------------------- ES(reduced set, current period) READ THE RATIO ON THE RIGHT AS AN UPLIFT FACTOR It asks: how much more risk does the full set of risk factors show than the reduced set, measured over a period where both are observable? That uplift is then applied to the reduced set's stressed measure, which is observable over the stress period. THE CONDITION THAT MAKES IT WORK The reduced set must explain a substantial proportion of the variation of the full set, and the standard imposes a floor on that proportion. If the reduced set is a poor proxy, the uplift factor is unreliable and the whole construction fails. WHAT A CREDIT RISK MODELLER WILL RECOGNISE This is proxy-based extension of a short history using a long-history benchmark, which is exactly what is done when a portfolio without 2008 data is calibrated using a related portfolio that has it. The same weakness applies: the proxy relationship is estimated in benign conditions and assumed to hold in stress, which is the assumption most likely to fail when it matters.

Non-modellable risk factors

Some risk factors cannot be modelled credibly because there are too few observable prices. FRTB tests each risk factor for modellability, essentially by requiring a sufficient number of real, verifiable price observations over a period, and a factor that fails the test is a non-modellable risk factor.

Non-modellable factors do not simply drop out. They attract a separate stressed capital add-on, computed factor by factor with limited diversification recognition, and that add-on sits alongside the modelled ES in the internal models capital requirement. The design intent is to remove the incentive to leave illiquid exposures out of the model, which under the old regime could reduce measured risk.

The practical consequence is worth knowing because it drives behaviour. The add-on is punitive relative to modelled treatment, so a desk trading instruments with sparse price data may find the internal models approach uneconomic even where it holds approval, and may prefer the standardised approach for those positions. That is intentional, and it is one of the mechanisms by which FRTB pushes illiquid trading towards higher capital.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

What ES gives up: elicitability and back-testing

The switch to expected shortfall has a genuine cost, and it is not the interpretability point the wiki lists, which is real but minor. The serious cost is that ES is hard to validate against outcomes, and the reason is a specific technical property.

A statistical functional is elicitable if it is the minimiser of an expected loss function, which is what allows you to score competing forecasts and say which was better. Quantiles are elicitable, so VaR forecasts can be compared and back-tested straightforwardly: count the breaches and compare with the expected count. Gneiting showed in 2011 that expected shortfall is not elicitable, which means there is no scoring function whose minimiser is ES, and comparative back-testing of ES forecasts is correspondingly awkward.

That is precisely why FRTB back-tests VaR at desk level while capitalising on ES, which lecture 2 flagged as the design point most candidates miss. The framework uses a quantile for validation because quantiles can be validated, and a tail expectation for capital because tail expectations aggregate. It is a division of labour forced by the mathematics rather than an inconsistency.

There is a second cost that practitioners feel more directly. ES depends on the magnitude of the few observations beyond the threshold, so its estimation variance is higher than a quantile's from the same sample. A bank that switched to ES with an unchanged data window improved its functional and worsened its estimation error, and the net effect on the quality of the number is not obviously positive at short windows.

The horizons matter more than the measure

If you had to rank FRTB's changes by how much capital they move, the change of measure would not come first. Under normality it moves nothing, and even under realistic tail fatness the ratio is between 1.2 and 1.5 on the same volatility. The liquidity horizons multiply risk by the square root of a horizon that rises from ten days to a hundred and twenty, which is a much larger factor.

That ordering is worth carrying because it corrects the emphasis in most summaries, including the wiki's, which lists the VaR-to-ES change first among the FRTB key points. The measure change is the intellectually interesting reform and the horizon change is the economically significant one, and they answer different failures: coherence answers a mathematical defect, and horizons answer the false premise that killed the old framework in practice.

It also explains the pattern of complaint from the industry. Banks objected less to expected shortfall than to the horizon assignments and to the non-modellable risk factor treatment, because those are where the capital is, and both are judgements about liquidity rather than about statistics. Whether a particular credit spread factor belongs in the forty-day or the sixty-day bucket is a supervisory determination with a direct capital consequence and no clean empirical answer.

Reading this as an actuary

Expected shortfall is TVaR, and TVaR is the measure a great deal of insurance capital work already uses, so the concept requires no adjustment. Three differences in application are worth noting.

First, the horizon. Banking market risk capitalises exit horizons of days to months and insurance capitalises a year, which reverses the data problem. A market risk modeller has thousands of daily observations of a distribution that will not stay still; a general insurance modeller has a handful of annual observations of a distribution that may be stable. Both are estimating a tail and the binding constraint is different.

Second, the confidence level. Solvency II's calibration is a 99.5 per cent VaR over one year, which is a quantile and therefore carries the coherence property lecture 2 discussed, while the Swiss Solvency Test uses expected shortfall. The banking framework has moved to the coherent measure and the largest insurance framework in Europe has not, which is a live inconsistency and a reasonable thing to have an opinion about.

Third, the stressed calibration construction in step 4 is the same proxy-extension technique used throughout actuarial work when a short data history has to be extended using a longer related series, and it carries the same risk: the proxy relationship is fitted in ordinary conditions and relied on in stress. An actuary reading FRTB's reduced-set formula should recognise an old problem in new notation and should be sceptical of it for the same reasons.

Common exam error Stating that FRTB reduced the confidence level from 99 per cent to 97.5 per cent and therefore relaxed the standard. The confidence level fell and the measure changed at the same time, and the two were chosen so that the resulting severity is essentially unchanged under normality. Describing the level change without the measure change, or the measure change without the calibration, both produce the wrong conclusion.
Exam tip If asked to compare VaR and ES, the marks are in the properties rather than the definitions: coherence, tail sensitivity, back-testability, and estimation error. A candidate who can say that ES is coherent but not elicitable, and that this is why the framework capitalises on ES while back-testing VaR, has produced the answer that distinguishes reading the standard from reading a summary of it.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Do the arithmetic. Compute ES at 97.5 per cent for a standard normal from the closed form, and compare with VaR at 99 per cent. State the difference as a percentage.
  2. Define expected shortfall and say how it differs functionally from VaR.
  3. Why did FRTB pair 97.5 per cent ES with the outgoing 99 per cent VaR? What does that imply about the source of any capital change?
  4. What is the shortfall-to-quantile ratio, and what does its behaviour across Student-t degrees of freedom tell you? Give the normal value and the value at three degrees of freedom.
  5. What are liquidity horizons, how many buckets are there, and what failure do they answer?
  6. Write out the stressed ES construction and explain the ratio on the right as an uplift factor. What condition must the reduced set satisfy?
  7. What credit risk technique is the stressed calibration structurally identical to, and what weakness do they share?
  8. What is a non-modellable risk factor, how is it capitalised, and what behaviour is that designed to discourage?
  9. Define elicitability. Why does ES's failure of it explain FRTB's use of two different measures?
  10. Give the second, practitioner-level cost of moving to ES, and say why a bank that switched with an unchanged window is only half improved.
  11. Rank the measure change against the horizon change by capital impact, and justify the ranking.
  12. Give three differences between how ES is used in banking and how TVaR is used in insurance capital work.
