Notation

The symbol table for everything that follows, built on a survival model rather than on convention. Every later credit-risk lecture (PD, LGD, EAD, A-IRB capital, IFRS 9 ECL) reuses these definitions, so this is the file to bookmark and the file to argue about first.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A credit-risk model is a survival model with money attached. Everything in it is one of three things: a probability that a facility leaves the performing state, an amount conditional on it having left, or a rule for aggregating the two. Almost every dispute in a modelling team is, on inspection, a dispute about what a symbol means. This lecture is the single place those definitions live, and it defines them as random variables on a stated probability space rather than as formulae that happen to work.

Six families of symbol carry the weight. The probabilistic foundation fixes a probability space, a default time \(T^{\mathsf{def}}_i\), and two information sets, so that words like "conditional" and "point-in-time" have referents. The Lexis coordinates pin a facility to three time indices, loan age \(t\), vintage \(v_i\), and calendar month \(\tau\), of which only two are free. Delinquency and state mechanics turn raw days-past-due into the state process whose first-passage time is the default event. The survival layer supplies the actuarial machinery, one-month decrement rates, forces, and competing risks, on which the entire PD family sits as an alias. EAD and LGD turn a default event into a monetary loss. The loss and capital objects at the top of the stack consume all of the above.

Feynman test A credit model is a translation device. On one side: a borrower who pays or does not pay, an account balance that rises and falls, an economy that booms and slumps. On the other side: a single number called expected loss. Every symbol here is a step in that translation. Reading any credit model comes down to asking, at every symbol, three questions. Is this about one facility, a pool, or the economy? Which of the three time coordinates is the index? And is this a random variable, a probability, an estimator, or a realised number? Answer those and the algebra becomes mechanical. Fail to answer them and you get formulae that are individually plausible and jointly wrong.
Step 2

Why this matters

Inconsistent notation is one of the few sources of model error that survives every layer of validation. A documentation slip that swaps the order of two arguments will not fail a unit test, will not show up in a Q-Q plot, and will not break a backtest. It surfaces when a regulator asks a junior analyst to define a term and gets an answer that contradicts the model technical document. By then the audit trail is months deep and the rebuild is expensive.

The risk is concrete, and the earlier version of this very file demonstrated it. Five defect classes recur, and each is represented in the wiki notation file this lecture descends from. Symbol collisions: nine letters carried two meanings each, including \(p\) for both the probation period and the macro-vector dimension, \(R\) for both a recovery cash flow and the Basel asset correlation, and \(s\) for both the arrears stickiness parameter and a one-period survival probability. Type errors: a scalar credit limit declared a subset of a covariate vector, a design vector typed as an unordered set, an expectation operator subscripted with a realisation. Unit errors: an annual effective interest rate raised to a monthly power, so that a twelve-month ECL discounted by twelve years. Definitional errors: an outcome operator that measured default status at the horizon while its prose claimed default at any point within the horizon, which are different events whenever cure is possible. Structural errors: a loan-level unexpected loss defined as a loan-level 99.9% quantile minus expected loss, which for any facility with PD below 0.1% returns a negative number.

Two of those were material rather than cosmetic. The expected credit loss rows summed conditional one-month default probabilities without weighting by survival, which overstates ECL by roughly a quarter of itself over a five-year horizon at plausible hazard levels. That is the same error the file's own commentary warned against two sections later. Adopting survival notation as the primitive layer, rather than bolting it on as an explanatory aside, makes that error impossible to write: \({}_{v}p\) is definitionally a product, and a marginal decrement probability definitionally carries a survival factor.

The supervisor cares for a specific reason. The IRB use test under CRR Article 144, the IFRS 9 governance expectations under PRA SS3/24, and the BCBS 239 data lineage requirement all hinge on the bank being able to explain, end to end, what each number means. Notation is the bottom rung of that ladder. If the symbol table is wrong, every higher rung wobbles.

Step 3

The mental model

Three time coordinates, one constraint

The standard framing in credit modelling is that a loan lives on two clocks, its own age and the calendar. That framing is incomplete, and the incompleteness matters. A facility has three time coordinates: loan age \(t\), measured in months since drawdown; vintage \(v_i\), the calendar month of origination; and calendar month \(\tau\), the wall-clock date. They satisfy an exact identity.

$$ \tau \;=\; v_i + t $$

Three coordinates and one linear constraint leave two degrees of freedom. This is the actuary's Lexis diagram, and it imports the age-period-cohort identification problem wholesale from mortality projection. Seasoning (an age effect), the credit cycle (a period effect), and origination quality (a cohort effect) are perfectly collinear, so no model that enters all three linearly can identify them separately. Every credit model resolves this by imposing a constraint, usually without saying so.

The identification problem has a practical face. A behavioural scorecard fitted on a portfolio originated during a tightening credit cycle cannot distinguish a genuine seasoning hump from the fact that later vintages were underwritten more tightly. A-IRB resolves this by fixing the period effect, calibrating to a long-run average that is deliberately cycle-neutral and letting age and vintage absorb the rest. IFRS 9 resolves it the other way, holding a seasoning curve fixed and letting the forward-looking overlay carry the period effect. Neither is more correct. What is incorrect is failing to state which constraint the model imposes, because the two produce different answers on the same data.

The state process and its first-passage time

A facility occupies one of four states at each month: performing, in default, written off, or closed. Default is defined by delinquency and unlikeliness-to-pay triggers; cure returns a facility to performing but only after a probation period has elapsed with no new trigger. That probation requirement is what makes the process semi-Markov rather than Markov: the transition intensity out of default depends on how long the facility has already been there, not only on the fact that it is there.

Two exits compete for a performing facility. It can default, or it can prepay and close. This is a multiple-decrement model in the actuarial sense, and treating it as a single-decrement model is a common source of lifetime ECL error, because the two exits are not independent and the observed default rate is a dependent rate.

The last line of that figure is worth stating as an identity, because it is the point where actuarial notation earns its place. Lifetime expected credit loss has exactly the structure of the expected present value of a term assurance, with the sum assured equal to loss given default multiplied by exposure at default, and the decrement of interest being default rather than death. Written that way, the survival factor cannot be dropped, because it is part of the assurance function itself.

The definition that was quietly wrong A twelve-month PD is the probability of entering default at some point in the next twelve months, not the probability of being in default at month twelve. In first-passage terms it is \(\mathbb{P}(t < T^{\mathsf{def}}_i \leq t + 12 \mid \cdot)\), not \(\mathbb{P}(J_{i,t+12} = \mathsf{D} \mid \cdot)\). The two coincide only when cure is impossible or when the probation period exceeds the outcome horizon. On a retail book with a three-month probation period and meaningful cure rates, using the occupancy definition understates the observed default rate, which then propagates into a low calibration target and an under-stated capital requirement. Any model document that defines the target flag as "in default at month twelve" should be read carefully, because that is usually a coding convenience rather than a considered choice.

Why the translation earns its place Three recurring errors become unwriteable once the PD family is expressed in survival notation. A cumulative PD cannot be built as a product of survival probabilities, because \({}_{t}p\) is already the product. A lifetime ECL cannot drop the survival weight, because the marginal \(m_{i,t}(v)\) carries it by construction. And a PD term structure cannot be silently confused between conditional hazards and marginals, because \(q\) and \(m\) are different letters. The cost is one layer of unfamiliar notation. The benefit is that the two most expensive errors in the previous version become syntactically impossible rather than merely discouraged.

7. Population aggregates and estimators

Estimators carry a hat. Estimands do not. The earlier version wrote a through-the-cycle PD as approximately equal to a long-run average default rate, which conflates the two: the average is a statistic computed from a sample, the through-the-cycle PD is a property of the distribution. Whether the statistic is a good estimator of the parameter is a question that can only be asked once they are distinguished.

8. Systematic conditioning and forward-looking adjustment

The single-factor model is stated as a latent-variable model and the conditional PD derived from it, rather than asserted. Deriving it settles the sign question that the earlier version resolved by declaration.

Let \(Z_{\tau} \sim N(0,1)\) be the systematic stress factor at calendar month \(\tau\), signed so that positive means stress. Let \(\varepsilon_i \sim N(0,1)\) be an idiosyncratic shock, independent of \(Z_{\tau}\) and across facilities. Define the latent asset value

$$ A_{i,\tau} \;=\; -\sqrt{R}\, Z_{\tau} \;+\; \sqrt{1 - R}\; \varepsilon_i, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(A_{i,\tau}) = R + (1 - R) = 1 $$

with default in the period defined as \(A_{i,\tau} \leq N^{-1}(\text{PD})\), where \(\text{PD}\) is the unconditional through-the-cycle probability. Conditioning on \(Z_{\tau} = z\) and rearranging gives the conditional default probability directly.

$$ \mathbb{P}\!\left( A_{i,\tau} \leq N^{-1}(\text{PD}) \;\middle|\; Z_{\tau} = z \right) \;=\; N\!\left( \frac{N^{-1}(\text{PD}) + \sqrt{R}\, z}{\sqrt{1 - R}} \right) $$

The plus sign in the numerator is a consequence of the minus sign in the latent model, not an independent convention. Setting \(z = 0\) recovers the unconditional PD, confirming the calibration. Setting \(z = N^{-1}(0.999) \approx 3.09\) gives the Basel worst-case default rate. If instead the systematic factor is signed so that positive means benign, the latent model carries \(+\sqrt{R}\,Y\), the conditional PD carries \(-\sqrt{R}\,y\), and Basel evaluates it at \(y = N^{-1}(0.001)\). The two are identical. What is never acceptable is presenting the conditional PD formula without stating which latent model generated it.

12. Capital objects

The A-IRB capital formula is the asymptotic single risk factor result: as a portfolio becomes infinitely granular under a single systematic factor, the 99.9% quantile of the loss rate equals the loss rate evaluated at the 99.9% quantile of that factor, and the portfolio requirement decomposes additively into facility contributions. Both conditions are assumptions, and Pillar 2A exists largely to charge for their failure.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Three coordinates, and the constraint nobody writes down

The usual advice is to keep loan age and calendar date straight. That is necessary but not sufficient, because the third coordinate is what makes the first two ambiguous. Once \(\tau = v_i + t\) is on the page, it is obvious that a seasoning curve estimated on a portfolio whose origination standards tightened over time is partly measuring underwriting change, and that a macro overlay fitted on the same data is partly measuring seasoning. The constraint that resolves the collinearity is a modelling choice, and it should appear in the model document as a stated assumption rather than as a side effect of which variables happened to be in the design matrix. A-IRB pins the period effect by construction, since a long-run average is deliberately cycle-neutral. IFRS 9 pins the age effect by fixing a seasoning curve and letting the overlay move. Reconciling the two parameter sets means reconciling two different resolutions of the same identification problem, which is why the reconciliations are always harder than they look.

A PD is a first-passage probability, not an occupancy probability

This is the correction most likely to change a number in production. "Defaults within twelve months" and "is in default at month twelve" are different events, and they diverge exactly on the population that defaults and cures inside the window. On a retail unsecured book with a three-month probation period, that population is not small. The first-passage form is what CRR Article 178 and every default-rate disclosure mean. Any target flag built as a point-in-time status lookup at the horizon is measuring the wrong thing, and it will bias the calibration target downwards without producing any visible symptom in a discrimination test, because the accounts it misclassifies are genuinely high-risk accounts and the model will still rank them correctly.

Default and prepayment are competing risks

Every observed default rate on a real portfolio is a dependent decrement rate, because the facilities that prepaid are no longer available to default. This matters in two places. Building a lifetime PD curve by chaining independent rates, obtained for instance from a survival model fitted with prepayments treated as censoring, produces a curve that is too steep unless the prepayment decrement is reinstated in the survival factor. And projecting an ECL over a balance profile that already amortises for expected prepayment, while also using PDs estimated on a prepayment-censored basis, double-counts attrition. The clean discipline is to build the multiple-decrement table once, take both dependent rates from it, and use the same survival factor everywhere. The actuarial machinery for this is standard and sits in the multiple-decrements lecture; the credit-risk literature tends to reinvent it, badly.

Unexpected loss does not exist at facility level

The loss on a single facility over one year is zero with probability \(1 - \text{PD}\) and roughly \(\text{LGD} \cdot \text{EAD}\) otherwise. Its 99.9% quantile is therefore zero whenever the PD is below ten basis points, and equals the full loss otherwise: a step function of PD, not a smooth risk measure. Facility-level unexpected loss defined as that quantile minus expected loss is negative for good-quality exposures and discontinuous everywhere. The reason \(K\) looks like a facility-level quantity is the asymptotic single risk factor result, which says that under infinite granularity and a single factor, the portfolio quantile decomposes into additive facility contributions. \(K\) is a contribution. Concentration risk, which is the failure of the granularity assumption, and multi-factor risk, which is the failure of the single-factor assumption, are both charged under Pillar 2A precisely because the decomposition is only asymptotically valid.

The product of three parameters is not the expected loss

Writing \(\text{EL} = \text{PD} \cdot \text{LGD} \cdot \text{EAD}\) is exact only if loss given default and exposure at default are conditionally independent given default. They are not: exposures that default after heavy limit utilisation tend to recover worse, and both deteriorate together in a downturn. The regulatory framework patches this by requiring downturn LGD rather than long-run average LGD in the capital formula, which is a conservative loading standing in for an unmodelled dependence rather than a modelling of it. Under IFRS 9 no such patch exists, which is one reason accounting and regulatory loss estimates on the same exposure legitimately differ by a wide margin.

Derive the sign, do not declare it

The recurring argument about whether the Vasicek numerator carries a plus or a minus is settled by writing the latent asset model down. If the systematic factor is signed so that positive means stress, it enters the asset value with a minus, and the conditional PD therefore carries a plus. If it is signed the other way, both flip. The conditional PD is not a free-standing formula with a convention attached; it is a consequence of a two-line derivation. Model documents that state the formula and assert the convention have skipped the step where the convention is determined, and are one careless standardisation of a forward-looking index away from a model that responds to stress by lowering provisions.

Jensen sits between the average scenario and the average outcome

The conditional PD is a strictly convex-then-concave function of the systematic factor, so the PD at the average economic state is not the average PD across states. Calibrating a through-the-cycle level by feeding a mean macro path into a forward-looking model returns the wrong number, and the error grows with the volatility of the factor. The same point is why IFRS 9 requires an explicit probability-weighted expectation across scenarios rather than a single central scenario: paragraph 5.5.17(a) is a statement about Jensen's inequality dressed as an accounting requirement.

LGD has more flavours than any other parameter

Workout LGD comes from observed recovery cash flows on closed workouts. Market LGD comes from secondary-market prices on defaulted debt. Long-run average LGD is the default-weighted historical mean. Downturn LGD adds a margin for adverse periods and is what the capital formula consumes. Point-in-time LGD carries the current-state adjustment and is what ECL consumes. Best estimate of expected loss is the loss rate on exposures that have already defaulted. The accounting and regulatory loss rates on the same facility can differ by twenty percentage points with both correct, because they answer different questions under different conditioning. A model document that says only "LGD" has not said anything.

Provisions against expected loss: the regulatory-accounting bridge

The shortfall mechanic under CRR Article 159 connects the two worlds. Where regulatory expected loss exceeds accounting provisions, the excess is deducted from CET1, because the loss is not yet on the books and capital must absorb it. Where provisions exceed regulatory expected loss, the excess may be added back to Tier 2 up to 0.6% of credit risk-weighted assets. This is why IFRS 9 expected credit loss and IRB expected loss must both be produced and reconciled at every reporting date even though they are built on different PDs, different LGDs and different horizons. The reconciliation is not a tidying exercise; it moves capital.

Step 6

Test yourself

  1. State the Lexis identity linking \(t\), \(v_i\) and \(\tau\). Explain why a model that enters seasoning, cycle and vintage effects linearly cannot identify all three, and state the constraint that A-IRB imposes and the different constraint that IFRS 9 imposes.
  2. Distinguish \(\mathbb{P}(t < T^{\mathsf{def}}_i \leq t+12 \mid \cdot)\) from \(\mathbb{P}(J_{i,t+12} = \mathsf{D} \mid \cdot)\). On what population do they differ, and in which direction does using the second as a target flag bias the calibrated PD?
  3. Write the marginal probability of default in month \(v\) in terms of a survival factor and a one-month dependent rate. Show why summing conditional hazards without the survival factor overstates a sixty-month lifetime ECL by roughly fifteen percentage points at a 1% monthly hazard.
  4. Given monthly forces \(\mu^{\mathsf{def}} = 0.004\) and \(\mu^{\mathsf{pre}} = 0.012\), compute the independent and dependent one-month default probabilities. Explain which one an observed default rate on a live portfolio estimates.
  5. Derive the Basel conditional default probability from the latent asset model \(A = -\sqrt{R}Z + \sqrt{1-R}\,\varepsilon\). State what changes if \(Z\) is signed so that positive means benign, and what must then be true of the forward-looking index.
  6. A facility has a twelve-month PD of five basis points. Compute the 99.9% quantile of its own one-year loss distribution and hence its facility-level "unexpected loss" on the naive definition. Explain what the number tells you about that definition, and state the two assumptions under which \(K\) is nonetheless a valid facility-level charge.
  7. State the condition under which \(\text{EL} = \text{PD} \cdot \text{LGD} \cdot \text{EAD}\) is exact rather than approximate, explain why it fails in practice, and identify the regulatory device that compensates.
  8. Define exposure at default as a conditional expectation. Explain why defining it as an indicator multiplied by the balance at the end of the outcome window is wrong on two separate counts.
  9. Define workout LGD, market LGD, long-run average LGD, downturn LGD, point-in-time LGD, ELBE and LGD-in-default. State which enters the capital formula, which enters the ECL formula, and write the capital charge on a defaulted exposure.
  10. Explain the expected loss shortfall under CRR Article 159. When does it hit CET1, when can excess provisions be added to Tier 2, and what is the cap?
  11. State the conditions on the calibration window \(\mathcal{T}\) under which the long-run average observed default rate is a reasonable estimator of the through-the-cycle PD. Explain why the standard error of that estimator does not shrink with the number of facilities.
  12. Write lifetime ECL as an actuarial term assurance. Identify the sum assured, the decrement, the discount function, and the term.
