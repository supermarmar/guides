Counterparty credit risk: bilateral exposure, netting, and two charges for one trade

Credit risk in the trading book differs from credit risk in the banking book in one structural respect that changes everything downstream: either party can be the one owed money, so the exposure has no fixed sign and no fixed size. The probability of default and the loss given default are borrowed from existing machinery. The whole difficulty is the exposure, and a bank ends up holding capital against it twice.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A mortgage is a unilateral credit exposure: the borrower can default on the bank and never the reverse. A derivative is bilateral, because the mark-to-market can favour either side, so which party carries credit exposure depends on where the market has moved. That single asymmetry is why counterparty credit risk needs its own machinery: the PD and LGD come from existing models, and the exposure does not.

Exposure is measured at the level of a netting set, meaning all transactions under one enforceable netting agreement, usually an ISDA Master Agreement or, for repo, an ICMA Global Master Repurchase Agreement. Within a netting set, hedging sets group transactions by asset class for the purpose of allowing offsets in the capital formula. The first is a legal construct, the second a computational one, and confusing them is the most common error in this area.

Two capital charges then apply to the same trade. The CCR default risk charge covers the loss if the counterparty actually defaults, computed from an exposure of $1.4 \times (\text{RC} + \text{PFE})$ under SA-CCR or $1.4 \times \text{Effective EPE}$ under the internal model method. The CVA charge covers mark-to-market losses from the counterparty's credit spread widening without any default at all, which in the 2007 to 2009 crisis were roughly twice the size of default losses.

Three computed results. The netting benefit is not a matter of documentation quality but an exact correlation result: with $n$ trades of equal volatility and pairwise correlation $\rho$, net exposure is $\sqrt{(1 + (n-1)\rho)/n}$ of gross, so ten uncorrelated trades net to 31.6 per cent while fifty highly correlated ones net only to 89.7 per cent. The BA-CVA formula as the source states it would charge a bank with no hedges 54 per cent more for electing the hedging-aware approach, which cannot be right and shows a term has been dropped. And a bank moving from the supervisory alpha of 1.4 to the own-estimate floor of 1.2 cuts its CCR exposure, and hence its risk-weighted assets, by 14.3 per cent.

Feynman test We have a deal with another bank. If the market moves our way they owe us, and if they go bust before paying we lose that. So we need to know how much they might come to owe us, not just what they owe us today. And separately, if the market merely decides they are less likely to pay, our deal is worth less right now.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this is the one place in credit risk where the exposure is the hard part. Everywhere else in this track the exposure was close to observable: a drawn balance, an amortisation schedule, a limit. Here the exposure is the positive part of a mark-to-market that will not exist until a future date, under market conditions nobody knows, netted against other trades whose values move together. The PD comes from the bank's existing rating models and the LGD from market spreads. All the modelling effort goes into a quantity that in the banking book was arithmetic.

The second reason is that the two charges are genuinely for different things and are frequently confused. Default risk capital answers what happens if the counterparty fails. CVA capital answers what happens if the market merely reprices the counterparty's credit. A bank can lose heavily on the second while every counterparty remains solvent, which is precisely what happened in 2008, and the fact that CVA losses exceeded default losses by roughly a factor of two is the historical justification for the whole framework.

The third reason is that this is the largest F107 gap in the credit risk subtree that is also examinable in detail. The syllabus map in the repository lists counterparty credit risk capital approaches, SA-CCR, the internal model method and CVA capital, as a priority gap. The two wiki notes here are substantive, around 160 and 140 lines, so unusually the constraint is not source material but the need to separate what the notes get right from the two places they slip.

And a fourth reason worth naming because it reframes the topic. Counterparty credit risk is where the banking book and the trading book stop being separable. The exposure is driven by market risk factors, the default is a credit event, the charge is calibrated by credit machinery, and the hedge is a credit derivative priced in the market. A bank organised into a market risk function and a credit risk function has to decide which one owns this, and whichever answer it picks leaves something unowned.

The mental model

One trade, one boundary test, two grouping constructs, and two capital charges that run in parallel.

Step 4

The detail

Bilateral, and what follows from it

Counterparty credit risk sits on the border between market and credit risk, and the source puts the defining property precisely: it applies where there is a bilateral risk of loss, meaning either party to the transaction could default and either could be the one owed money. In a derivative, if the market moves against one side, that side may fail and the other side loses a gain it had accrued but not received.

Three consequences follow, and they are the reason the rest of the machinery exists. The exposure has no fixed sign, so it must be computed as the positive part of a value that may be negative. It has no fixed size, so a potential future component is needed alongside the current one. And because both parties face the same problem, the mitigants are symmetric: collateral flows both ways, and a netting agreement protects both sides at once.

It is worth separating counterparty credit risk from settlement risk, which is the risk of delivering one leg and not receiving the other. Settlement risk is largely engineered away: most securities trade delivery-versus-payment, so the legs are simultaneous, and central counterparties interpose themselves and guarantee performance. Counterparty credit risk is what remains once settlement risk has been removed, which is why it concentrates in over-the-counter derivatives that neither mechanism touches.

Netting sets, hedging sets, and why the distinction matters operationally

A netting set is all transactions covered by one enforceable netting agreement. The capital calculation is performed at that level, so the boundary of the netting set determines the size of the exposure. Its enforceability is a legal question: the ISDA has obtained opinions from major jurisdictions confirming that close-out netting works, and where no such opinion exists the netting benefit is not available regardless of what the contract says.

The source lists four benefits and they are of different kinds. Lower capital requirements and the ability to do more business within regulatory limits are economic. Fewer and smaller payments reduce operational cost. And simpler recoveries on default, since only one net amount is owed, is a resolution benefit that matters most in exactly the circumstances where everything else is going wrong.

A hedging set is different in kind. It groups transactions within a single asset class inside a netting set so that the capital formula can offset them: one hedging set per currency for interest rate transactions, per currency pair for foreign exchange, then credit, equity, commodity by type, basis and volatility. No agreement creates a hedging set and no counterparty can be persuaded to widen one. It exists only as a rule inside SA-CCR, and the practical consequence is that a bank cannot improve its hedging set treatment by negotiation, only by changing which trades it does.

The two exposure methods

Under the standardised approach, exposure at default is $1.4 \times (\text{RC} + \text{PFE})$. The replacement cost is what it would cost to replace the netting set immediately on default, computed at netting set level. The potential future exposure is the additional exposure that could build between default and replacement, computed per hedging set and aggregated. Replacement cost depends on whether the netting set is margined: unmargined sets hold little or no collateral and any collateral held does not move with the market, while margined sets have collateral that is topped up as values move, which limits losses without eliminating them.

Under the internal model method, exposure at default is $1.4 \times \text{Effective EPE}$, and the modelling approach is not prescribed. Two conditions apply: regulatory approval, and use of the internal model method for market risk as well, though the credit risk side may remain on the standardised or IRB approach. The mathematics of Effective EPE is the subject of the next lecture, and there is a labelling error in the source's treatment of it that is worth being warned about in advance.

One point the source omits and it is worth having. The alpha of 1.4 is the supervisory value, and the framework permits banks to use own estimates of alpha subject to a floor of 1.2. That is not a technicality: it is a fourteen per cent reduction in exposure and therefore in risk-weighted assets, available to a bank that can demonstrate its own joint simulation of exposure and default. Which explains why sophisticated houses invest in modelling wrong-way risk explicitly rather than accepting the supervisory scalar.

The CVA charge, and the loss it exists to cover

Credit valuation adjustment capital covers something the default risk charge does not: the mark-to-market loss when a counterparty's creditworthiness deteriorates without default. A derivative receivable from a weakening counterparty is worth less today, and that reduction in value is a loss now, not a contingent loss later. The framework exists because in the 2007 to 2009 crisis these losses were roughly twice the size of losses from actual counterparty defaults.

Three approaches exist, and the ordering is not simply by sophistication. The reduced BA-CVA recognises no hedges and every bank must compute it, because it feeds the others. The full BA-CVA recognises credit default swap hedges of counterparty spread. The SA-CVA is a sensitivities-based approach adapted from the market risk standardised approach and requires both monthly sensitivity reporting and a dedicated CVA desk with a mandate to manage and hedge the risk. Trades cleared through a recognised central counterparty are exempt from the charge entirely.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The netting benefit is a correlation result, exactly, and it can be written down

Netting is usually discussed as a legal and operational matter: get the agreement signed, secure the opinion, book the trades into the right set. That is necessary and it obscures the quantitative question, which is how much netting is actually worth. The answer has a closed form and it depends on one thing.

HOW MUCH IS NETTING WORTH? SET UP n trades in one netting set each with mark-to-market V_i, zero mean, standard deviation s, pairwise correlation rho WITHOUT NETTING, exposure is the sum of the positive parts E[max(V_i, 0)] = s / sqrt(2 pi) for each trade gross exposure = n . s / sqrt(2 pi) WITH NETTING, exposure is the positive part of the sum sd of the sum = s . sqrt( n + n(n-1) rho ) net exposure = s . sqrt( n + n(n-1) rho ) / sqrt(2 pi) SO THE RATIO IS PURELY A FUNCTION OF n AND rho netting factor = sqrt( (1 + (n-1) rho) / n ) n rho=0.0 rho=0.2 rho=0.5 rho=0.8 rho=1.0 2 70.7% 77.5% 86.6% 94.9% 100.0% 5 44.7% 60.0% 77.5% 91.7% 100.0% 10 31.6% 52.9% 74.2% 90.6% 100.0% 20 22.4% 49.0% 72.5% 90.0% 100.0% 50 14.1% 46.5% 71.4% 89.7% 100.0% READ THE TWO EXTREMES OF THE BOTTOM ROW Fifty independent trades net down to 14.1% of gross exposure, a saving of six sevenths. Fifty trades at a correlation of 0.8 net down to 89.7%, a saving of one tenth, and adding more of them barely helps because the limit as n grows is sqrt(rho) = 89.4%. THE CONSEQUENCE FOR A TRADING BUSINESS Netting is worth most on a DIVERSE book and almost nothing on a DIRECTIONAL one. A dealer running two-way flow across many risk factors captures nearly the whole benefit. A bank whose derivative book is a hundred interest rate swaps all in the same direction, hedging the same balance sheet exposure, has a netting agreement that saves it about a tenth. Which means the netting benefit cannot be forecast from the number of trades or the quality of the paperwork. It has to be computed from the correlation structure, and a bank whose book is becoming more directional is losing netting benefit without anything in its documentation changing. AND THE LIMIT IS WORTH REMEMBERING as n grows, the netting factor tends to sqrt(rho) rho = 0.25 -> 50% rho = 0.5 -> 70.7% rho = 0.8 -> 89.4% so the average pairwise correlation of the book sets a floor on the exposure that no amount of trade volume can beat.

The full BA-CVA formula in the source cannot be right, and the proof needs no reference

The source gives the two basic-approach charges as $C_{\text{reduced}} = 0.65 \times K_{\text{reduced}}$ and $C_{\text{full}} = 0.25 \times K_{\text{reduced}} + 0.75 \times K_{\text{hedged}}$. The first carries the discount scalar of 0.65 and the second does not. That asymmetry produces an absurdity, and it can be exposed without consulting the standard at all.

A ONE-LINE CONSISTENCY PROOF CONSIDER A BANK WITH NO CVA HEDGES AT ALL then by construction K_hedged = K_reduced UNDER THE SOURCE'S TWO FORMULAS, with K_reduced = 100 C_reduced = 0.65 x 100 = 65.00 C_full = 0.25 x 100 + 0.75 x 100 = 100.00 ratio = 1.538 SO A BANK HOLDING ZERO HEDGES WOULD PAY 54% MORE FOR ELECTING THE HEDGING-AWARE APPROACH THAN FOR THE APPROACH THAT IGNORES HEDGES. That is not a conservative calibration, it is incoherent: the full approach is supposed to RECOGNISE hedges, so at zero hedges it must collapse to the reduced approach. THE MISSING TERM IS THE SCALAR C_full = 0.65 x ( 0.25 K_reduced + 0.75 K_hedged ) at K_hedged = K_reduced this returns 0.65 x 100 = 65.00, exactly C_reduced. The two approaches now agree at the boundary, which is what a well-formed pair of formulas has to do. WHAT THE 25 / 75 BLEND IS ACTUALLY FOR It is a floor on hedge recognition. Even a perfect hedge reducing K_hedged to zero leaves 25% of the unhedged charge standing, so the maximum benefit from hedging is 75%. The source describes this correctly. The scalar is a separate device applied to both. THE GENERAL LESSON, WHICH IS THE USEFUL PART Any pair of regulatory formulas offering an election must agree at the boundary where the election makes no difference. That boundary test costs one substitution and it catches transcription errors in a way that re-reading the text does not, because a dropped factor reads perfectly well.

Two charges for one trade, and why that is not double counting

A bank holds default risk capital and CVA capital against the same derivative, which looks like being charged twice for one exposure. It is not, and being able to say why crisply is worth having, because the question is asked in every discussion of the framework.

The two charges cover disjoint losses. Default risk capital covers the event in which the counterparty fails and the bank recovers less than the replacement cost of the netting set. CVA capital covers the change in the fair value of the receivable caused by a change in the counterparty's credit spread while it continues to perform. One is a jump-to-default loss and the other is a spread loss, and a bank can suffer either without the other. The historical evidence is that they arrive in different proportions from what intuition suggests: in the crisis the spread losses were about twice the default losses, which is why the second charge was added after the first had existed for years.

There is a deeper point about how the framework treats the same risk twice more generally, and it recurs across this whole track. Regulatory capital is built as a set of charges for named loss mechanisms, not as a single estimate of a portfolio loss distribution. That produces overlaps and gaps, both of which are visible if you look, and it is a deliberate trade of coherence for auditability. The same design shows up in Pillar 2A, where charges for named risks are added on top of Pillar 1 rather than integrated with it, and in the relationship between the IFRS 9 provision and expected loss capital, where two frameworks measure overlapping quantities and neither will draw the boundary for the other.

The through-line from the rest of the track Counterparty credit risk is the clearest case in the collection of a risk that no single function owns. The exposure is driven by market factors, the loss is a credit event, the capital is calibrated with credit machinery, and the hedge is a traded credit instrument. Every boundary error this track has found lived between two owners. This is a risk that is entirely boundary.
Where this goes wrong in practice The characteristic failure is a netting set that is narrower than the legal agreement, because trades were booked to the wrong entity, the wrong agreement identifier, or a legacy system that predates the current documentation. The capital consequence is silent and large: on a diverse book, splitting one netting set of twenty trades into two of ten raises exposure from 22.4 to 31.6 per cent of gross, a 41 per cent increase, with no change in market risk, no change in the counterparty and nothing visible in any risk report. Reconciling booked netting sets against executed agreements is unglamorous and it is where the money is.
Exam tip If asked why counterparty credit risk needs its own exposure methodology, the marks are for bilateralism and its consequences, not for listing formulas. Either party can be owed, so the exposure is the positive part of a signed value; the value moves with the market, so a potential future component is needed; and the exposure is measured per netting set rather than per trade, so the legal perimeter is part of the calculation. A further mark is available for distinguishing counterparty credit risk from settlement risk, and noting that delivery-versus-payment and central clearing have largely removed the latter.
