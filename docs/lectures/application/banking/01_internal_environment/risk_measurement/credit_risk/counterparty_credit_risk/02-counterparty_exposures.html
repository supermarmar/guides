Counterparty exposures: four metrics, one scalar, and a quantity that is not identified

The exposure measures that feed the internal model method are four, not three, and the difference between two of them is where the framework's treatment of short-dated trades lives. The 1.4 scalar that sits in front of them is not arbitrary: it can be inverted into the exposure-default correlation it assumes, which turns out to depend on the book. And the market-implied default probability everyone quotes is not separately identified from the recovery rate.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Four quantities, in a strict order. Expected exposure $EE(t)$ is the average positive mark-to-market at a future date. Expected positive exposure is the time-weighted average of $EE$ over the first year. Effective expected exposure is the running maximum of $EE$, which prevents the profile from declining. Effective expected positive exposure is the time-weighted average of that running maximum, and it is what the capital formula consumes: $\text{EAD} = \alpha \times \text{Effective EPE}$.

The source collapses two of these, defining expected positive exposure as the running maximum. That object is Effective $EE$ under Basel CRE53, and the mislabelling matters for a specific reason developed in step 4: it makes it impossible to see that the effective adjustment does nothing on a long-dated swap and bites only on early-peaking profiles, which is what it exists for.

The $\alpha$ of 1.4 can be inverted. Modelling exposure as $\exp(\sigma Z)$ and default as a correlated threshold event gives $\mathbb{E}[E \mid \text{default}] / \mathbb{E}[E] = \Phi(\rho\sigma - z_q)/\text{PD}$, so a 1.4 uplift corresponds to a definite correlation. At a 1 per cent probability of default that correlation is 0.43 on a low-volatility book and 0.16 on a high-volatility one. One scalar, embedding assumptions that differ by a factor of nearly three depending on whose book it is applied to.

And the market-implied probability of default is not what it appears. The credit triangle $\text{spread} \approx \lambda \times \text{LGD}$ has two unknowns and one observation. Varying the assumed recovery across a plausible range moves the five-year default probability from 8.0 to 22.1 per cent, a range of 94 per cent, while moving the product PD times LGD by only 8.1 per cent. The spread identifies expected loss and says almost nothing about either factor separately.

Feynman test Simulate thousands of futures. In each one, work out what the trades would be worth. Keep the ones where they owe us, average those, and do it for every future date. Then multiply by a fudge factor, because in the futures where they owe us most they are also most likely to be in trouble.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the four metrics are examinable and are easy to confuse, and the confusion is not harmless. A candidate who defines expected positive exposure as a running maximum has the Basel definition of effective expected exposure and will lose the mark. Worse, the collapse hides the design logic: the two averages exist to convert a profile into a single number, and the running maximum exists to stop a declining profile understating credit risk. Those are different jobs.

The second reason is that the exposure profile is a genuinely different object from anything else in credit risk. A loan exposure declines predictably. A derivative exposure typically rises then falls, because diffusion widens the distribution of future values while amortisation shrinks the remaining cash flows, and the peak of that hump is where the risk sits. Whether the peak falls inside or outside the first year determines whether the framework's effective adjustment does anything at all.

The third reason is that the 1.4 scalar is the single largest number in the calculation that nobody derives. It is described in the source, correctly, as covering model uncertainty and wrong-way risk. Being able to invert it into an implied correlation converts it from a regulatory constant into a testable assumption, which is the difference between accepting a floor and knowing whether it binds on your portfolio.

And a fourth reason with a practical edge. Market-implied default probabilities are used for two purposes in most banks: pricing and capitalising credit valuation adjustment, which needs expected loss, and setting counterparty limits, which needs a probability. The first use is sound and the second is not, and the reason is identification rather than data quality, so no amount of better market data fixes it.

The mental model

One exposure profile with four metrics drawn on it, one survival curve, and the correlation that makes the two interact.

Step 4

The detail

The four metrics, correctly ordered

Start from the definition that is uncontroversial. The expected exposure at a future date is $EE(t) = \mathbb{E}[\max(V(t), 0)]$, where $V(t)$ is the mark-to-market of the netting set from the bank's perspective. Only the positive part matters: if the netting set is worth less than nothing to the bank, the counterparty's default costs the bank nothing on credit grounds. The expectation is taken over the joint distribution of all relevant market factors, which is why simulation is the norm.

From $EE$, three further quantities are built and the Basel framework keeps them distinct. Expected positive exposure is the time-weighted average of $EE$ over the first year, or to maturity if the netting set matures sooner. Effective expected exposure at each date is the maximum of $EE$ at that date or any earlier one, a running maximum that prevents a declining profile from reducing measured credit risk. Effective expected positive exposure is the time-weighted average of the effective expected exposure over the same first year, and it is the quantity the capital formula uses.

TWO PROFILES, FOUR METRICS, AND WHY THE ORDER MATTERS PROFILE A, a five-year swap. Diffusion dominates early and amortisation late, so the hump peaks around year 2. quarterly EE over year 1 1.80 2.60 3.10 3.40 Effective EE over year 1 1.80 2.60 3.10 3.40 EPE = 2.7250 Effective EPE = 2.7250 ratio = 1.0000 EAD at 1.4 = 3.8150 PROFILE B, a one-year FX forward. The peak is at month 5 and the profile then declines to zero at maturity. quarterly EE over year 1 2.40 3.60 4.10 3.70 Effective EE over year 1 2.40 3.60 4.10 4.10 EPE = 3.4500 Effective EPE = 3.5500 ratio = 1.0290 EAD at 1.4 = 4.9700 THE POINT In profile A the EE rises throughout the first year, so the running maximum equals the EE at every point and Effective EPE equals EPE EXACTLY. The effective adjustment does nothing at all. In profile B the peak falls inside the year, so the running maximum holds the peak while the EE declines, and Effective EPE exceeds EPE by 2.9%. WHICH TELLS YOU WHAT THE ADJUSTMENT IS FOR Short-dated and early-peaking netting sets. On a long swap book it is inert, and a bank that has implemented it and cannot demonstrate any difference has probably implemented it correctly. That inference is unavailable if the running maximum is called EPE, because the two concepts have then been merged and there is nothing to compare.
Correction to the source The wiki defines expected positive exposure as $EPE(t) = \max_{s \leq t} EE(s)$. Under Basel CRE53, and Annex 4 of the original Basel II text, that object is effective expected exposure; expected positive exposure is the plain time-weighted average of $EE$. The source then defines effective expected positive exposure as the time-weighted average of its own "EPE", which given its definition arrives at the correct formula for effective EPE. So the capital number is right and the vocabulary is wrong, which is the most awkward kind of error to inherit: it produces the right answer in practice and the wrong answer in an exam.

Survival curves, and what a credit spread actually contains

For the standardised approach to credit valuation adjustment, the probabilities of default must be market-implied rather than historical, and they come from bonds or credit default swaps. The default intensity or hazard rate $\lambda(t)$ is the instantaneous probability of default per unit time, and from it follow the cumulative default probability $PD(t) = 1 - \exp(-\int_0^t \lambda)$ and the survival curve $S(t) = \exp(-\int_0^t \lambda)$, which are exactly complementary.

Bootstrapping the hazard curve from observed prices requires four assumptions the source lists and they are worth taking seriously: linearity when interpolating between sparse maturities, the risk-free curve used for discounting, the recovery rate used to separate default probability from the spread, and the treatment of liquidity and volatility premia, which are not default risk and must be stripped out. Credit default swaps are preferred to bonds precisely because they are instruments whose purpose is to trade credit, so fewer of these confounds are present, and proxy spreads from comparable names are used where no swap trades.

The source also makes a shape observation worth keeping: investment grade names typically show hazard rates that increase with maturity, because there is more room to deteriorate than to improve, while distressed names can show declining hazard rates if the market expects an early resolution one way or the other.

Closed form against simulation, and what simulation costs

Early counterparty risk systems used closed-form approximations built on existing value-at-risk infrastructure: fast, implementable with what a market risk function already had, and adequate for simple low-dimensional portfolios. What they could not do was handle correlation, diversification and credit migration simultaneously, particularly for path-dependent payoffs. Monte Carlo simulation replaced them because it can carry multiple risk factors, their correlations, rating migration, uncertain recoveries, and mitigants including netting and collateral, all at once.

The cost is worth quantifying, because it explains both why the transition took so long and why closed forms survive for some purposes.

WHAT THIS EXPLAINS Why the overnight batch exists, why exposure numbers are a day old when limits are checked against them, and why banks still run closed-form approximations intraday for pre-deal limit checks while the simulation runs overnight for capital. It also explains the pressure to reduce time points and paths, which is a precision decision usually made by an infrastructure team on cost grounds rather than by a risk team on accuracy grounds.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The alpha of 1.4 is an assumption about correlation, and it can be inverted

The source describes the 1.4 as a conservative regulatory scaling factor covering model uncertainty and the tendency for exposures to be correlated with default, which is right. It is more useful than that, because the wrong-way risk component can be written down and the scalar inverted into the correlation it assumes.

INVERTING THE SCALAR A MINIMAL MODEL exposure E = exp(sigma Z), Z ~ N(0,1) default occurs when Y > z_q, Y ~ N(0,1), P = PD wrong-way risk corr(Z, Y) = rho > 0 Then, using the standard bivariate normal tilt, E[E | default] / E[E] = Phi(rho.sigma - z_q) / PD SANITY CHECK at rho = 0 this is Phi(-z_q)/PD = PD/PD = 1.000 exactly, as independence requires. SO THE SCALAR IS REGRESSIVE IN EXPOSURE VOLATILITY A quiet netting set gets credit for a substantial wrong-way correlation. A volatile one gets credit for almost none, because the same multiplicative uplift represents a smaller correlation when the exposure distribution is already wide. Since volatile books are exactly the ones where wrong-way risk is most damaging, a single fixed alpha is weakest where it is needed most. AND IT PRICES THE OWN-ESTIMATE OPTION PROPERLY Lecture 1 noted that own estimates of alpha are permitted with a floor of 1.2, worth 14.3% of exposure. This table says what a bank has to demonstrate to claim it: that its actual exposure-default correlation is below the level the supervisory scalar assumes. On a volatile book, where 1.4 implies only rho = 0.16, that is a demanding claim. On a quiet book, where it implies 0.43, it may be straightforward. Which is the opposite of how the option is usually discussed, as a reward for modelling sophistication. It is available in proportion to how little wrong-way risk you actually have.

Two caveats to keep the result honest. The model is deliberately minimal: a single lognormal exposure factor and a single-threshold default, so it captures the direction and rough magnitude of wrong-way risk and not the path dependence of a real netting set. And the 1.4 covers model uncertainty as well as wrong-way risk, so the implied correlations above are upper bounds on the wrong-way component. Neither caveat affects the comparative point, which is that the implied assumption varies with the book while the scalar does not.

The market-implied default probability is not identified

The source states the credit triangle plainly: a credit spread is approximately the product of default probability and loss given default, so estimating one implies the other. That is exactly right, and the consequence deserves more emphasis than it usually gets, because it determines which uses of a market-implied probability are legitimate.

THE PRODUCT SPANS A RANGE OF 8.1%. WHAT IS AND IS NOT IDENTIFIED The spread pins down expected loss. It does not pin down either factor, and the recovery rate that would separate them is not observable ex ante for a name that has not defaulted. WHICH USES SURVIVE CVA pricing and CVA capital depend on the product, so the recovery assumption is nearly immaterial and the market-implied approach is sound. A counterparty LIMIT framework expressed in probability of default depends on the factor. The same 150 basis point spread supports a five-year default probability of 8% or 22% according to an assumption the bank has made and typically not documented. THE PRACTICAL RULE Use market-implied quantities for the product and historical or rating-based quantities for the factor. Mixing them, for instance setting a limit in market- implied PD terms and a provision in historical PD terms, produces two numbers that cannot be reconciled and a long argument about which is wrong. Neither is. AND NOTE WHAT WOULD NOT HELP Better market data, more liquid names, tighter bid-offer. None of it identifies a second parameter from one equation. This is an identification problem, not a measurement problem.

Wrong-way risk, and why netting can make it worse

Wrong-way risk is the adverse correlation between exposure and counterparty credit quality: the bank's exposure is largest exactly when the counterparty is least able to pay. The source's two examples are well chosen. A bank that has sold credit protection to a counterparty on a reference entity linked to that counterparty's own fortunes faces the extreme case, and a bank with a foreign exchange swap against a sovereign faces the systemic version, since a sharp currency depreciation both increases the exposure and stresses the sovereign.

There is a structural point worth adding, and it connects to the netting result from lecture 1. Netting reduces exposure by exploiting the imperfect correlation between trade values. Wrong-way risk arises from correlation between exposure and default. A netting set constructed to maximise offsets is selected for internal diversification, but nothing in that selection reduces the correlation of the net exposure with the counterparty's credit. Indeed a netting set dominated by one directional position, which is the case where netting is worth least, is also the case where the net exposure most closely tracks a single market factor and therefore most plausibly tracks the counterparty's fortunes.

So the two effects are not independent, and they point the same way for the same book. A directional netting set gets little netting benefit and carries more concentrated wrong-way risk. A diverse one gets a large netting benefit and its net exposure is a blend of factors less likely to align with any one counterparty's distress. That is a reason to think about netting sets in terms of what drives them rather than how many trades they contain.

What closes the counterparty track Both lectures ended in the same place from different directions. Lecture 1 found that the netting benefit is a function of correlation and not of documentation. This one found that the regulatory scalar is a statement about correlation and that the market-implied default probability is not identified without an assumption. In each case a number that looks like a fact turns out to be an assumption about joint behaviour, which is the defining feature of counterparty credit risk: nothing about it is a property of one instrument.
Exam tip The four exposure metrics are a favourite because they are easy to state wrongly. Give them in order and say what each step does: $EE$ is the profile, $EPE$ averages it over the first year, effective $EE$ is the running maximum that stops the profile declining, and effective $EPE$ averages that. A further mark is available for saying why the running maximum exists, which is that credit risk does not fall simply because a trade is closer to maturity, and another for noting the one-year cap and that it is the shorter of one year and maturity.
