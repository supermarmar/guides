Data and sampling: the word "successful", and what it costs

The source describes the training data for an application scorecard in one sentence, and the sentence contains the whole problem: historical data on past successful applicants. Outcomes exist only for the people the old policy approved, which biases the calibration in one direction and the measured discrimination in the other. Both effects are large, and only two things in the world actually fix them.

Step 1

The 30-second version

An application scorecard is trained on the attributes of past applicants and their subsequent repayment behaviour. Repayment behaviour only exists for applicants who were approved, so the model never sees an outcome for anyone the previous policy declined. That is the reject inference problem, and it is the single most important fact about the data.

It does two things, both large and running in opposite directions. Calibrating on approvals understates the through-the-door probability of default by 46.6 per cent on the worked figures, because the sample excludes the worst risks. And measuring discrimination on approvals attenuates the Gini by 35.7 per cent, from 0.500 to 0.322, because the score range has been truncated. The first makes the portfolio look safer than it is; the second makes the model look weaker than it is.

The source specifies a 50 / 30 / 20 split into training, testing and holdout, and says regulators require evidence that the model was tested on data untouched during training or tuning. That third dataset earns its keep arithmetically: on a 30,000-account test set the Hanley-McNeil standard error of the area under the curve is 0.0053, so selecting the best of twenty candidate specifications inflates the reported figure by about 0.010 of area, or 0.020 of Gini, purely by selection.

And the two scorecards differ at the dataset level, not just the feature level. An application scorecard is cross-sectional: one row per person, a photograph. A behavioural scorecard is panel: twelve rows per account, a film. But rows are not observations. At an intra-account correlation of 0.6, twelve monthly snapshots are worth 1.58 independent observations, so panel sampling multiplies rows by twelve and information by about 1.6.

Feynman test We only find out whether someone repays if we lend to them. So the only people we can learn from are the ones the old rules liked, which means we are studying a class we selected for passing the exam and then wondering why everyone passes.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this problem has now appeared three times in the track without being confronted. Lecture 2 raised it as a reason application scorecards fall short for IRB, and again inside swap-set analysis, where the bad rate quoted for previously declined applicants is unobservable. Lecture 3 noted behavioural scorecards are less exposed because performance is observed on the booked population. It is the same problem each time and it belongs to the data, not to any of those uses.

The second reason is that the direction of each bias determines who notices. An understated probability of default flatters the portfolio, so nobody complains until losses arrive. An attenuated Gini understates the model, so the modelling team notices immediately and may respond by rebuilding a model that was never the problem. The biases are asymmetric in visibility as well as in sign.

The third reason is that the split percentages look like a convention and encode a real trade. Half the data trains, three tenths tunes, one fifth is held back. The holdout is pure cost in fitting terms and pure evidence in validation terms, and step 5 puts a number on what it buys so that the trade can be argued rather than asserted.

And a fourth reason about panel data that is routinely got wrong. A behavioural dataset with a million rows sounds like a large sample and is not, because the rows come from far fewer accounts and are heavily correlated within account. Every standard error computed as though the rows were independent is too small, every significance test is too generous, and the design effect that corrects it is one line of arithmetic.

The mental model

Step 4

The detail

What the data requirement actually says

The source's statement of the data requirement is brief and exact: to train an application scorecard, use historical data capturing the characteristics of past successful applicants and their subsequent repayment behaviour. Every word of that is correct and the word "successful" is the entire problem. Characteristics are available for everyone who applied, because they were collected at application. Repayment behaviour is available only for those who were booked. So the target variable is missing, not at random, for exactly the applicants whose risk the model most needs to distinguish.

Two things worth noting before the quantification. This is a selection on the outcome's cause rather than ordinary missing data: applicants were declined because of characteristics correlated with the outcome, which is what makes the missingness informative. And the selection was made by a previous model or policy, so the bias in the new model is inherited from the old one, which means every generation of scorecard is trained on a population shaped by its predecessor.

The three-way split, and the sampling methods behind it

The source specifies training at 50 per cent to fit parameters, testing at 30 per cent to tune hyperparameters and compare intermediate iterations, and a holdout at 20 per cent reserved until the final stages as a proxy for future data. Its justification for three rather than two is regulatory: banks must demonstrate that the model has been tested on independent data not used in any way during training or tuning, and the holdout is the evidence. It adds a second use that is easy to overlook, which is that a holdout drawn from a later period tests stability over time as well as independence, since credit models often remain in use for years.

On sampling method the source distinguishes probability sampling, where every member of the population has a known non-zero chance of selection, covering simple random and stratified designs, from non-probability sampling, where some members have no chance at all, which is faster and cheaper and introduces bias.

That distinction is worth pausing on, because it names the reject inference problem in general terms without connecting the two. A training sample restricted to approved applicants is a non-probability sample of the through-the-door population: declined applicants have zero probability of appearing. So the framework the source sets out already classifies the scorecard's own dataset as the biased kind, and the classification is not incidental. It is the reason no reweighting of the sample can fix it, since reweighting requires a known non-zero selection probability for every stratum and there is none for the rejects.

Cross-sectional against panel

The two scorecards differ in dataset shape, and the source's metaphor is a good one. Cross-sectional sampling takes one snapshot: every observation is a different person observed once, so you see everyone as they are now with no knowledge of how they got there. A hundred thousand applicants from one quarter, attributes recorded at application, outcome observed over the following twelve months, each person appearing exactly once.

Panel sampling tracks the same accounts over time, producing multiple observations per account. An account opened in January might contribute a row for every month of that year, each row carrying that month's utilisation, payment behaviour and delinquency status, and the outcome over the following twelve months. A film rather than a photograph.

The consequence the source does not draw is that the film has far less information than its frame count suggests, and step 5 quantifies it.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Reject inference does two things, and they point in opposite directions

SIZING BOTH BIASES SETUP goods ~ N(0,1), bads ~ N(-0.9539, 1), so the true AUC on the full applicant population is 0.75 and the Gini 0.500 through-the-door bad rate 10% the old policy approved the top 70%, cutting at -0.6318 BIAS 1, THE CALIBRATION bad rate through-the-door 10.000% bad rate among approvals 5.339% understatement 46.6% a model calibrated on the booked population assigns probabilities roughly half what the applicant population warrants. Direction: it makes the PORTFOLIO LOOK SAFE. BIAS 2, THE MEASURED DISCRIMINATION Gini on the full applicant population 0.500 Gini within the approved population 0.322 attenuation 35.7% this is classical RANGE RESTRICTION. Truncating the score distribution compresses the separation between goods and bads that remains measurable. Direction: it makes the MODEL LOOK WEAK. THE ASYMMETRY THAT MATTERS The two biases flatter opposite things, so they are noticed by opposite people. An understated PD pleases everyone until the losses arrive twelve to twenty-four months later. An attenuated Gini annoys the modelling team immediately, and the standard reaction is to rebuild the model. The model was never the problem; the measurement window was. AND ONE THING SURVIVES TRUNCATION INTACT the ORDERING. Range restriction compresses the measured spread; it does not reverse the ranking. So a scorecard built on approvals still ranks the approved population correctly, which is why it works perfectly well for moving a cut-off within the approved range and fails for anything that needs an absolute level. That is the same discrimination-against-calibration split from lecture 2, arriving from the data side: the property the underwriter needs is the one the sampling problem barely damages, and the property the regulator needs is the one it wrecks. WHICH EXPLAINS THE HISTORICAL SEQUENCE Scorecards were built for underwriting for decades with this bias present and unaddressed, because underwriting needs ordering. It became urgent when the same scores were pointed at IRB capital and IFRS 9 provisions, which need levels. The bias did not change; the use did.

What can and cannot fix it

A large literature exists on reject inference and Hand and Henley's 1997 review in the Journal of the Royal Statistical Society remains the honest summary of it. The methods divide into two kinds and the division is the practical point.

Statistical augmentation covers reweighting the accepted sample to resemble the through-the-door population, parcelling rejects into good and bad in assumed proportions, fuzzy augmentation that assigns each reject fractional membership of both classes, and formal sample-selection models in the tradition of Heckman's 1979 correction. Every one of these imposes an assumption about the missing outcomes: that the relationship between characteristics and outcome estimated on accepts extends to rejects, or that a specified selection equation is correctly specified. None of them recovers information, because the information is not present. They can make the model consistent with a stated assumption, which is worth something, and they cannot test the assumption on the data that lack it.

New information covers exactly two things. Bureau performance on rejected applicants: an applicant declined here may have borrowed elsewhere, and their bureau record shows how that went, which is a genuine observation of the missing outcome on a partially overlapping definition. And a random-approval window: deliberately approving a small random sample above a hard policy floor, accepting the losses as the price of an unbiased sample. That is the only method that produces the data the problem requires, and it is expensive, which is why it is rare and why it is worth budgeting explicitly rather than hoping augmentation will substitute.

The honest position to hold in a model committee is therefore narrow and defensible: augmentation is documentation of an assumption, bureau data is partial evidence, and a random-approval window is the only fix. A validation report that describes fuzzy augmentation as having "addressed" reject bias has mislabelled an assumption as a correction.

What the holdout is worth, in area under the curve

The case for a third dataset is usually made as a compliance point, which is how the source makes it. It can be made as an arithmetic one, and the arithmetic is more persuasive because it says how much.

SELECTION BIAS IN A TEST STATISTIC A TEST SET OF 30,000 ACCOUNTS, 3,000 OF THEM BAD, AUC 0.75 the Hanley-McNeil standard error of an AUC estimate is SE = sqrt( [ A(1-A) + (n_b - 1)(Q1 - A^2) + (n_g - 1)(Q2 - A^2) ] / (n_b n_g) ) with Q1 = A/(2-A) and Q2 = 2A^2/(1+A) = 0.00531 NOW TUNE k CANDIDATE SPECIFICATIONS AND KEEP THE BEST the reported figure is then the MAXIMUM of k noisy draws, which exceeds the truth by about E[max of k standard normals] times the standard error k E[max] AUC inflation Gini inflation 1 0.013 +0.0001 +0.0001 5 1.164 +0.0062 +0.0124 10 1.535 +0.0081 +0.0163 20 1.863 +0.0099 +0.0198 50 2.248 +0.0119 +0.0239 SO TWENTY CANDIDATES INFLATE THE REPORTED GINI BY ABOUT 0.020, AND THAT IS PRECISELY WHAT THE HOLDOUT RECOVERS. THREE THINGS FOLLOW 1 THE NUMBER OF CANDIDATES IS THE THING TO DISCLOSE. The inflation depends on k, so a model document that reports the winning specification without saying how many were tried has withheld the one input needed to interpret its own headline statistic. 2 THE HOLDOUT MUST BE UNTOUCHED, NOT MERELY UNSEEN. Looking at the holdout once and then adjusting anything converts it into a second test set, and the bias returns with k counted from that point. 3 A 20% HOLDOUT COSTS LITTLE AND BUYS A LOT. The training set falls from 70% to 50% of the data, which on samples of this size moves the fitted model hardly at all, and it buys an estimate whose bias is zero rather than two Gini points.

Panel data multiplies rows by twelve and information by 1.6

A behavioural dataset looks abundant. One account contributing twelve monthly rows turns a hundred thousand accounts into 1.2 million observations, and the temptation is to treat that as a twelvefold gain in statistical power. It is not, because the twelve rows describe the same borrower in adjacent months and are therefore heavily correlated.

THE DESIGN EFFECT for clusters of size m with intra-cluster correlation rho, the effective number of independent observations is n_effective = n_rows / (1 + (m - 1) rho) rows per account rho=0.3 rho=0.5 rho=0.6 rho=0.8 3 1.88 1.50 1.36 1.15 6 2.40 1.71 1.50 1.20 12 2.79 1.85 1.58 1.22 24 3.04 1.92 1.62 1.24 AT TWELVE MONTHLY SNAPSHOTS AND rho = 0.6, TWELVE ROWS ARE WORTH 1.58 INDEPENDENT OBSERVATIONS. AND THE RETURNS TO MORE ROWS COLLAPSE going from 12 rows to 24 buys 0.04 of an observation. The limit as m grows is 1/rho, so at rho = 0.6 no amount of monthly sampling on one account will ever be worth more than 1.67 independent accounts. THREE CONSEQUENCES 1 EVERY NAIVE STANDARD ERROR IS TOO SMALL, by a factor of about sqrt(1 + (m-1)rho), which at m = 12 and rho = 0.6 is 2.76. So a t-statistic computed on 1.2 million rows as though they were independent is nearly three times too large, and variables will appear significant that are not. 2 SAMPLE ONE ROW PER ACCOUNT PER OUTCOME WINDOW unless you have a reason not to. The information cost is small and the inferential clarity is large. 3 SPLIT BY ACCOUNT, NEVER BY ROW. If rows from one account land in both the training and holdout sets, the holdout is contaminated in the worst way: the model has seen the same borrower under a different date stamp, and the independence the regulator asked for is gone while the documentation says it is present.
The pattern across the track Three lectures in this track have now turned on the same asymmetry. Discrimination and calibration are different properties, they are damaged by different things, and the property a bank happens to need determines whether a known flaw is tolerable. The reject bias was survivable for forty years of underwriting and became urgent the week the score was pointed at a capital calculation. Nothing about the data changed.
Where this goes wrong in practice A random split on a panel dataset, done in one line, in good faith. It looks like the standard practice from every textbook and it silently destroys the holdout, because the same account appears on both sides of the partition. The symptom is a holdout performance suspiciously close to the test performance, which reads as reassuring and is the opposite. The check takes one query: count the accounts appearing in more than one partition, and expect zero.
Exam tip If asked about reject inference, the answer that earns full marks names the direction of the bias and not just its existence: the sample excludes the worst risks, so the estimated probability of default is too low and the portfolio looks safer than it is. A second mark is available for the range restriction effect on measured discrimination, and a third for the crucial practical point that no purely statistical method recovers the missing information, so the only real fixes are bureau performance on rejects and a deliberate random-approval sample.
