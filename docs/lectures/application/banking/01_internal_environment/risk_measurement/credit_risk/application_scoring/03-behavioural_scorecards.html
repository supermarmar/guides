Behavioural scorecards: the handover, and six models that are four problems

Once an account has performance, a second scorecard takes over from the first, and the transition is managed by a blend weight that ramps over months on book. That weight can be derived rather than chosen, and the practitioner ramp turns out to sit within a few points of the variance-optimal answer. The six variants a large bank runs simultaneously turn out to be four modelling problems and two calibrations of one of them.

Step 1

The 30-second version

An application score is fixed at booking and describes an applicant who no longer exists. A behavioural scorecard refreshes the estimate monthly from observed account data: utilisation, payment behaviour, delinquency status, time in a performing spell. The handover between the two is staged over months on book, with a hybrid blend $w(t)\,B + (1-w(t))\,A$ in the middle where the weight ramps from roughly 0.3 to roughly 0.8.

That weight can be derived. Treating both scores as noisy readings of the same latent risk, the variance-minimising weight on the behavioural score is $\text{var}_A / (\text{var}_A + \text{var}_B)$, and on plausible discrimination profiles it is 0.356 at month six and 0.898 at month twelve. The source's ramp of 0.3 to 0.8 is within 0.06 and 0.10 of the optimum, which is a good result for a heuristic. The blend also beats both individual scores by most where they are closest, at month six, which is exactly where the source places the hybrid band.

A large retail bank then runs four to six behavioural variants on the same raw data, differing in default definition, outcome window and rating philosophy. The IRB variant needs the full regulatory default definition, ninety days past due plus unlikeness to pay, and the source notes that the unlikeness-to-pay component is often missing from pure ninety-day scorecards.

Quantifying that omission produced the result I did not expect. If unlikeness-to-pay-only cases are 15 per cent of all defaults, expected loss is understated by the full 15 per cent while the capital requirement is understated by only 1.5 per cent, a factor of ten, because retail capital is nearly insensitive to the default rate while expected loss is exactly linear in it. So the case for a separate IRB build is a compliance and provisioning argument, not a capital argument.

Feynman test What we knew when they applied stops being useful once we can watch them pay. For the first few months we mostly trust the application, then mostly trust the behaviour, and in between we mix the two in a ratio that shifts each month.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this is the model that actually runs the retail bank. The application scorecard makes one decision per customer; the behavioural scorecard makes decisions every month for the life of every account: credit line increases and decreases, transaction authorisations at sub-second latency, cross-sell, retention, risk-based repricing, and automatic renewal that avoids a fresh application altogether. Its volume of decisions exceeds the application scorecard's by orders of magnitude.

The second reason is that it carries the regulatory numbers. Lecture 2 established that the application score cannot serve ongoing capital or provisioning; the behavioural score is what does. So the IRB grade that drives risk-weighted assets, the point-in-time probability that sizes expected credit loss, and the current-against-origination comparison that drives staging all come from here.

The third reason is that the handover is a genuine design decision with a cost on both sides, and it is usually made by convention. Too early and the estimate is driven by a few months of noisy behaviour; too late and it ignores visible deterioration. Step 5 shows the blend weight is not a matter of taste, since the variance-optimal answer is computable from the two scores' own discrimination, and it shows what the source's conventional ramp costs.

And a fourth reason about model inventory. Six behavioural variants on one portfolio is six validation cycles, six sets of documentation and six recalibration schedules, all on one data pipeline. Step 5 argues that the count can be reduced without losing anything, because two of the six differ only in calibration and one differs only in a definitional refinement. That is a governance saving available for free.

Step 3

The mental model

One timeline, one weight that ramps along it, and six variants that reduce to four problems.

Step 4

The detail

The handover schedule

The source sets out five phases by months on book and the rationale for each. From zero to three there is no internal performance, so the application score is all there is. From three to six there is limited but informative behaviour, so the application score remains primary with emerging-behaviour overlays. From six to twelve a hybrid blend runs, with the weight on the behavioural score ramping from about 0.3 to about 0.8, which the source says smooths the transition and controls overweighting of noisy early behaviour. From twelve to eighteen behaviour dominates with the application score as a backstop, and beyond eighteen the application variables are fully stale and the behavioural score runs alone.

Two properties of that schedule deserve naming because they are easy to miss. The application score does not deteriorate as a model; it deteriorates as a description, because the account holder's circumstances move away from what was recorded at application. And the behavioural score does not become available at a single moment; its discrimination improves month by month as the performance window lengthens, which is why the handover is a ramp rather than a switch.

The blend is also a volatility control, and it is not free

The source says the blend controls overweighting of noisy early behaviour, which is exactly right and has a precise arithmetic form. The application score is static, contributing no month-on-month movement at all, so the variance of the blended score's monthly change is $w^2$ times the variance of the behavioural score's monthly change. At a weight of 0.3 the monthly movement is thirty per cent of what a pure behavioural score would produce; at 0.8 it is eighty per cent.

What follows is a trade rather than a free lunch. The same factor that damps noise also damps signal: a genuine deterioration at month seven registers at thirty per cent of its true size in a blend weighted 0.3, so recognition is delayed. That is defensible when early behavioural data is mostly noise, which is the source's argument, and it becomes indefensible as soon as it is not. The empirical test is whether the account-level score volatility at each month on book is dominated by reversals, which look like noise, or by persistent moves, which look like signal.

The six variants, and their parameter triples

The source's central observation about the variants is that they share the same raw input data and diverge fundamentally in how they are built and what they are used for. Each is fixed by a triple: default definition, outcome window, and rating philosophy.

Account management is the workhorse: ninety days past due, a twelve-month window, point-in-time, with discrimination as the primary objective because the output is used ordinally to rank accounts rather than as an absolute probability. It drives automated line increases for top deciles and decreases on deterioration, real-time authorisations at sub-second latency, cross-sell and retention, risk-based repricing on revolving products, and automatic renewal that obviates fresh application scoring.

IRB PD grades follow EBA/GL/2016/07 precisely, meaning ninety days past due plus the unlikeness-to-pay triggers: distressed restructuring, write-off, specific credit adjustment, bankruptcy. Twelve-month window for retail, recalibrated annually to the long-run average default rate, with full model risk management validation and regulatory sign-off before any change goes live.

IFRS 9 uses the same definition for consistency and the opposite philosophy, since the estimate should respond to current conditions. The window stretches to remaining lifetime for stage 2, requiring a survival model or marginal probability term structure. The source notes that banks often implement it as a macro-conditional overlay on the through-the-cycle IRB probability, applying a Z-shift derived from a macroeconomic model, rather than as a separate scorecard.

SICR targets deterioration rather than default, over a three to six month window because the point is early warning rather than full-cycle prediction. Collections inverts the target once an account is delinquent, predicting failure to cure within ninety days or a roll to charge-off, with a feature space shifted to broken promises to pay, partial payment counts, contact responsiveness and hardship flags. Recovery predicts the probability of recovery at and beyond charge-off and shapes placement between in-house workout, agency, legal action or debt sale.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The missing unlikeness-to-pay component costs ten times more in provisions than in capital

The source makes a specific claim: the unlikeness-to-pay component is often missing from pure ninety-day scorecards, which is why a separate IRB build is needed. The claim is correct and the reasoning usually offered for it, that capital would be understated, turns out to be the weaker half of the argument.

AND A CONSEQUENCE WORTH GENERALISING Whenever a definitional change alters a probability of default, work out its effect on EL and on K separately. In retail they differ by an order of magnitude, and an impact assessment that reports only the capital effect will conclude the change is immaterial when the accounting effect is ten times larger.

Six variants, four problems, and a governance saving

Running four to six behavioural scorecards on one portfolio is standard and it is worth asking how many genuinely distinct modelling problems that represents, because each additional model carries a validation cycle, a documentation set and a recalibration schedule.

Laying the triples side by side, three of the six predict the same event. Account management predicts ninety-day delinquency; IRB predicts the same event plus unlikeness to pay, which is a refinement of the target definition rather than a different target; and IFRS 9 predicts the IRB event over a longer horizon with the opposite calibration philosophy. Lecture 2 established that philosophy is a property of the calibration and not of the scorecard, and the source itself notes that banks commonly implement IFRS 9 as a macro-conditional Z-shift on the through-the-cycle probability rather than as a separate model. So those three collapse towards one modelling problem with three calibrations and one definitional variant.

The other three do not collapse. SICR predicts deterioration, which is a different event and not merely an earlier one. Collections predicts cure, which inverts the target and changes the population to accounts already delinquent. Recovery predicts recovery amount on a post-charge-off population. Each has a different target, a different feature space and a different population, so each is a genuine build.

Which gives a defensible inventory: four modelling problems, several calibrations of the first. The practical consequence is that consolidating the default-prediction family reduces the validation burden without reducing coverage, provided the calibrations are separately documented and separately monitored. That is worth doing before adding a fifth variant, and it is the kind of saving that only becomes visible when the variants are tabulated by their triples rather than listed by their uses.

The distinction that keeps recurring Three lectures in this track have now turned on the same separation: a model produces an ordering, and a calibration turns the ordering into a number. Discrimination and calibration have different diagnostics, the through-the-cycle question belongs entirely to the calibration, and two of the six behavioural variants are one model calibrated twice. Confusing the two is what produces both unnecessary model inventory and unnecessary arguments about rating philosophy.
Where this goes wrong in practice A behavioural scorecard whose features include the account's own recent delinquency at high weight, used to drive the significant-increase-in-credit-risk test. It will be an excellent predictor and a useless early warning, because it fires at roughly the same time as the thirty-day backstop it is supposed to pre-empt. The source's separate SICR classifier with a three to six month window exists precisely to avoid this, and the test is the coherence condition from IFRS 9 lecture 9: the staging signal must lead the delinquency signal, and a model built to predict delinquency accurately will not.
Exam tip If asked why a bank needs both an application and a behavioural scorecard, do not answer that the behavioural one is better. Answer that they measure different things: the application score is the only estimate available at origination and must be preserved unchanged as the staging reference point, while the behavioural score is the only estimate that reflects current risk and is therefore the one capital and provisions require. A further mark is available for the handover, and another for noting that the blend weight in the transition is a variance-weighting problem with a computable answer.
