Modelling: a fifty-year-old technique that keeps winning, and why

A credit score is a linear transformation of the log-odds, so the number itself carries no information and only differences mean anything. The technique that produces it is logistic regression on binned variables, and both limitations the source attributes to it turn out to be either removed by the standard methodology or smaller than advertised. The closing lecture of the track, and of the credit risk collection.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The scaling is the whole of the score's meaning. A probability becomes a score through $\text{Score} = \text{Offset} + \text{Factor} \times \ln(\text{odds})$, where the factor is fixed by the points to double the odds: $\text{Factor} = \text{PDO}/\ln 2$. Change the two presentation constants and the same portfolio scores from 418 to 733, or from 609 to 766, or from 372 to 593. The ordering never moves. The score's range carries no information at all, and only differences in score mean anything.

One precision point worth having. The PDO is points to double the odds, not the probability, and the two coincide only when the probability is small. Doubling from 1 to 2 per cent moves the odds by 2.01 and the score by 20.15 points; doubling from 16 to 32 per cent moves the odds by 2.47 and the score by 26.10.

The source lists two limitations of logistic regression, and both need adjusting. It assumes a linear relationship between features and the log-odds, which is true of the raw variables and false after weight-of-evidence coding, because replacing a value with the log-odds of its own bin makes the relationship linear by construction. That is what the standard scorecard methodology is for.

And on rare defaults, resampling turns out to change almost nothing that matters. Fitting the same data naturally and balanced gives slopes of 1.1008 and 1.0909, an intercept shifted by exactly $\ln k$ where $k$ is the sampling ratio, and an area under the curve identical to five decimal places. Resampling is a computational convenience, not a statistical improvement, and it demands an offset correction that is not optional if the output is a probability.

Feynman test Work out the odds that someone repays, take the logarithm, multiply by a number that makes it look like a credit score, and add another number so it lands in a familiar range. Both of those last numbers are decoration.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the score's arbitrariness is routinely misunderstood, including by people who use it daily. A bank comparing its scores against a bureau score, or against a competitor's cut-off, or across two of its own products, is comparing numbers whose scales were chosen for presentation. The only comparable quantities are the probability of default and the differences within one scale.

The second reason is that the two limitations named in the source are the standard reasons given for reaching past logistic regression toward machine learning. If one of them is removed by the binning step that every scorecard already performs and the other is a calibration offset with a closed form, the case for the more complex model has to rest on something else, namely interactions, and that case should be made on its own terms.

The third reason is that the additive points structure is not a presentational nicety. It is what makes an adverse-action reason code computable without approximation: the applicant lost forty points at this characteristic, and that is a fact about the model rather than an interpretation of it. A model without that structure has to reconstruct explanations after the fact, and the reconstruction is an approximation whose stability is itself a question.

And a fourth reason, since this closes the collection. Fifty-four lectures of credit risk have found errors in covariances, in benchmarks, in aggregation shortcuts and in sampling frames. Almost none of them were in the model-fitting step. That is worth stating explicitly at the end, because the instinct when a number is wrong is to rebuild the model, and the evidence across this collection is that the model is rarely where the problem lives.

Step 4

The detail

The scaling, and the constant that is not arbitrary

The source gives the transformation as $\text{Score} = \text{Base Score} + \text{Factor} \times \ln((1-\text{PD})/\text{PD})$, with a typical range of 300 to 900. The quantity inside the logarithm is the good-to-bad odds, so a higher score means a safer applicant, and the whole construction is a monotone transformation of the probability. Nothing is added by the transformation and nothing is lost.

Two constants fix it. The factor is determined by the points to double the odds: if adding PDO points doubles the odds then $\text{Factor} \times \ln 2 = \text{PDO}$, so $\text{Factor} = \text{PDO}/\ln 2$. The offset is fixed by anchoring a chosen score at chosen odds. Neither is estimated from data and neither affects any decision.

Why the points are additive, and what that buys

With weight-of-evidence coding the linear predictor is $z = \alpha + \sum_i \beta_i \, \text{WoE}_i$, and the score is an affine function of $z$. So the score decomposes into a constant plus a contribution from each characteristic, and each contribution is a fixed number of points attached to the bin the applicant falls in. That structure is why a scorecard can be printed as a table and why an applicant can be told which characteristics cost them points.

It also makes an adverse-action reason code exact rather than approximate. The characteristics on which the applicant lost the most points relative to a reference profile are the reasons for decline, and that statement is a fact about the arithmetic of the model. No reconstruction, no attribution method, no dependence on a baseline choice. Step 5 returns to what happens when the model class does not have this property.

Logistic regression, and the two limitations the source names

The source's treatment is accurate and worth recording. The model builds a linear predictor $z = \alpha + \beta_1 X_1 + \ldots + \beta_n X_n$, transforms it through the logistic function to a probability, and is fitted by minimising log-loss, the negative log-likelihood. It is chosen for simplicity, interpretability and effectiveness on binary outcomes, gives a continuous score rather than a binary result so that risk-based pricing is possible, and is easy to explain to stakeholders. Different models may be built for different segments.

The two limitations are given as the linearity of the relationship between features and log-odds, and poor performance on imbalanced data without adjustments such as oversampling or weighting. Both are worth examining rather than accepting, and step 5 does that.

Gradient boosting, and the check the source insists on

The source's description of gradient boosting is correct: an ensemble that builds decision trees sequentially, each correcting the errors of its predecessors, using the gradient and the Hessian of the loss to optimise at each step. It captures complex non-linear relationships and interactions that logistic regression must be told about, and it produces feature importances as a by-product. Its costs are interpretability, computation, and sensitivity to tuning of the learning rate, tree count and depth.

The most interesting line in the note is a practical instruction rather than a property: it "can make some incorrect inferences so you have to do some spot checks e.g. Actuary vs Nurse and their PDs". That sentence is doing more work than it appears to. It is saying that a flexible model can learn an ordering between two occupations that the bank could not defend, and that a human must therefore inspect the fitted relationship for plausibility. The reason the check is necessary is that the model class provides no guarantee the scorecard methodology provides by construction, which step 5 develops.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The linearity limitation is removed by the step before the model

Logistic regression does assume the log-odds are linear in the covariates. That is a real restriction on raw variables and it is not a restriction on the model as actually built, because weight-of-evidence coding replaces each raw value with the log-odds of its own bin before fitting.

WHICH REFRAMES THE LIMITATION "logistic regression assumes linearity in the log-odds" is true and is a limitation of logistic regression on RAW variables. The scorecard methodology has already removed it before the model is fitted, which is precisely why that methodology exists and why it has survived fifty years of alternatives. WHAT IS NOT REMOVED INTERACTIONS. WoE linearises each variable separately and says nothing about their joint behaviour, so an interaction still has to be specified by hand. That is the genuine remaining advantage of a tree ensemble, and it is a much narrower claim than "logistic regression cannot handle non-linearity".

The imbalance limitation is an intercept, and it has a closed form

The claim that rare events degrade logistic regression is widely repeated and mostly wrong in the way that matters. King and Zeng's 2001 treatment of rare-events logistic regression establishes that the small-sample bias falls on the intercept, and that the effect of case-control style sampling is a known offset rather than a distortion of the slopes. It is worth demonstrating rather than citing.

Two large errors in opposite directions, in the same pipeline, neither documented, is how a scorecard can appear calibrated while both of its calibration problems are live.

What the actuary-against-nurse check is really testing

The source's spot check deserves the last word on model choice. A gradient boosted model can place two occupations in an order the bank cannot defend, and the recommended response is that a human inspect the fitted relationships for plausibility. That is sound advice and it is worth being precise about why the check is needed for one model class and not the other.

A weight-of-evidence scorecard is built with an explicit directionality step: each characteristic's bins are examined, their ordering is checked against economic reasoning, and bins that violate the expected direction are merged or the variable is rejected. So the plausibility constraint is imposed during construction and is visible in the scorecard table afterwards. A tree ensemble fits whatever pattern the data contain, including patterns that reflect historical selection rather than credit risk, and nothing in its construction rules any of them out.

Which means the trade is not really interpretability against accuracy. It is that one model class lets you impose a constraint and the other only lets you detect a violation, after the fact, if someone thinks to look. Monotonicity constraints in modern boosting libraries narrow that gap and do not close it, because they constrain the direction of a single feature and not the plausibility of an interaction. For a lending decision that must be explained to a declined applicant and defended to a regulator, the ability to impose is worth a good deal more than a small gain in discrimination.

Why the old technique keeps winning Logistic regression on binned variables survives not because it is the most accurate available method but because its structure delivers three things the alternatives must reconstruct: an exact additive decomposition for reason codes, an enforceable direction for every characteristic, and a recalibration that is a single intercept. A method that is slightly less accurate and structurally explicable beats one that is slightly more accurate and explicable only by approximation, whenever the output has to be defended to someone who was declined.
Where this goes wrong in practice A challenger model reported as a headline Gini improvement with no statement of what it gives up. The comparison that matters is not two Gini figures; it is two Gini figures alongside the reason-code method, the monotonicity guarantee, the recalibration procedure and the number of specifications tried before the winner emerged. Lecture 4 showed the last of those is worth two Gini points on its own, which is frequently the entire claimed improvement.
Exam tip If asked to compare logistic regression with machine learning for credit scoring, the weak answer is that one is interpretable and the other is accurate. The strong answer separates three things: weight-of-evidence binning already handles non-linearity, so the remaining statistical advantage of a tree ensemble is interactions specifically; interpretability differs in kind rather than degree, since one model permits an exact reason code and the other requires an attribution method; and the plausibility constraint can be imposed in one and only detected in the other.
