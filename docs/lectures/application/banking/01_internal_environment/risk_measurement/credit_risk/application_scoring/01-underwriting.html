Underwriting: judgement, what it costs, and the frameworks that discipline it

Before a scorecard exists there is a lending decision, and for most of banking history it was made by a person. The frameworks that structured that judgement are still in use and still examinable, and the case for replacing judgement with a model in retail is usually made qualitatively. It can be quantified, and the numbers are larger than the argument usually claims.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Extending credit is the core activity of a bank and the decision to extend it has two halves. Ability to pay is measurable from financial data. Willingness to pay is not, and the frameworks that structure it, the five Cs of credit and CAMPARI, exist to make a judgement about willingness as consistent as a judgement about ability.

Two portfolios are watched separately. The front book is newly originated lending under current policy, and it shows whether recent strategy and model changes are working. The back book is legacy lending under superseded policy, and it supplies the performance history that validates and calibrates the models. One tells you about the future, the other supplies the evidence.

The case against subjective retail lending is normally made on grounds of inconsistency and bias, and both can be measured. If a scorecard on the same data reaches a Gini-equivalent AUC of 0.75 and an underwriter reaches 0.65, then at an unchanged 70 per cent approval rate the scorecard cuts the approved bad rate from 7.37 to 5.34 per cent, a 27.5 per cent reduction, and cuts the share of all bad applicants let through from 51.6 to 37.4 per cent.

The inconsistency is starker. Two independent underwriters of that quality, assessing the same applicants, agree on only 60.6 per cent of approve-or-decline decisions at a 50 per cent approval rate, against 50 per cent from two coin flips. And an incentive bias worth half a standard deviation of score raises the approved bad rate by 13.2 per cent while raising the approval rate by 20.7 per cent, which is the basis of the monitoring point in step 5: bias shows up in approval rates immediately and in bad rates only after the outcome window.

Feynman test Two questions: can they pay, and will they? The first you can read off their accounts. The second you have to judge, and two people judging the same applicant will disagree about two times in five, which is why banks eventually wrote the judgement down as a formula.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this is the layer everything else in the credit risk track sits on. The A-IRB and IFRS 9 chains estimate the risk of exposures that already exist; the concentration and counterparty tracks measure portfolio properties of what has been written. Underwriting decides what gets written in the first place, so it is the only stage at which risk is chosen rather than measured.

The second reason is that the qualitative frameworks are not historical curiosities. They remain the operative structure for wholesale and specialised lending, where the source is explicit that historical data is often neither available nor comparable, and they remain in retail as the vocabulary in which policy rules and scorecard characteristics are justified. A modeller who cannot say which of the five Cs a characteristic belongs to will struggle to defend it to a credit committee.

The third reason is that the bias problem is a live conduct and legal exposure, not only a modelling one. The source names discrimination on the basis of race, religion, nationality, gender or marital status as a documented consequence of purely subjective retail decisions, alongside irrational preferences about types of employment. Objectivity here is a compliance requirement as much as an accuracy improvement.

And a fourth reason worth being direct about. The usual framing is that models replaced judgement because they are better. The numbers in step 5 support that and they also show what it cost: a committee of five underwriters closes 64 per cent of the gap to the scorecard, so judgement was not incapable, it was noisy and expensive. Understanding the trade that was made is more useful than treating the outcome as inevitable.

The mental model

Step 4

The detail

Front book and back book

The distinction looks administrative and is not. The front book is exposures approved under current credit policy and pricing standards, and it is where a bank sees whether its current underwriting is working, whether new lending sits inside risk appetite, and whether its models remain predictive under current origination practice. The back book is exposures written under superseded policy, potentially with different risk characteristics, pricing and criteria.

The tension between them is a permanent modelling difficulty rather than a temporary one. Validation, back-testing and calibration all require observed performance, which only the back book has, but the population the model will be applied to is the front book, written under different policy. So every retail credit model is calibrated on a population it will never be used on, and the size of that mismatch is a function of how much policy has changed since the development window. That is the representativeness problem the A-IRB sampling lectures treat formally, appearing here in its origin.

Qualitative factors, and where they are strongest

The source's framing of the lending decision is worth keeping. Part of the decision rests not on a borrower's ability to pay but on their sense of obligation to pay, and qualitative factors exist to carry the second. They are less tangible, harder to quantify and, the source is blunt, particularly susceptible to subjectivity and bias when a banker's own success is tied to landing the best deal.

Their strength varies systematically by borrower type, and the reason is data availability. Wholesale borrowers, particularly listed companies, are obliged to disclose, so both the quantitative record and valuable qualitative material such as stated strategy are available and verifiable. Management quality, business characteristics and structure are the broad soft factors, and the working rule is that a factor belongs in the qualitative assessment when it is relevant to credit risk but cannot be captured quantitatively or has no viable data source.

Specialised lending is the case where qualitative assessment is not a supplement but the substance. The source notes that these exposures have distinctive loss distributions, with both high default and high loss rates in distress, and that historical data is often neither available nor comparable to the exposure being assessed. The qualitative factors are also different in kind: financial strength and flexibility, control over collateral, the phase of the project, and marketability. Project phase in particular has no analogue in a retail scorecard, because a half-built asset is not a fractionally impaired one.

Retail is where the history turns. For many years retail decisions were made by local relationship managers on qualitative grounds, because the wholesale models could not be applied to small unrated borrowers and data was expensive to collect and verify against a small potential profit. The source makes an observation worth noticing: sound retail frameworks were first developed by smaller banks, because dedicated resources, local knowledge and relationships were available and manual data collection forced thoroughness. Improved data collection then let larger banks build more precise frameworks, with verification remaining the weak point.

The five Cs, taken collectively

The framework's own instruction is the important part: the five Cs must be taken collectively, no single C in isolation suffices, and strength in one can offset weakness in another. Character is willingness to pay, proxied for individuals by credit history and a bureau score, and for corporates by a substantial list the source sets out, covering track record under stress, strategic consistency, succession planning, governance, ownership stability, incentive alignment, transparency and reporting integrity. The source adds a caution worth carrying: character assessment is not a substitute for know-your-customer procedures.

Capacity and Capital divide the ability question by economic state, and the split is the neat part of the framework. Capacity asks whether the borrower can service the debt in good times, measured by ratios such as total debt service and debt service coverage, and assessed through business model, sector, competitive moat, market share and sustainability. Capital asks whether they can pay in bad times, from unencumbered assets and the capital structure, so a borrower already highly leveraged has less of it regardless of current cash flow. Collateral is the plan B, whose nature, condition and desirability set the loan-to-value the lender will extend. Conditions covers the purpose of the credit and the external environment, including macroeconomics, sector volatility and buyer concentration.

CAMPARI, and the letter that is not a risk factor

CAMPARI is the common alternative in UK and European banking, and the source explains its appeal precisely: it includes the profitability of the deal for the bank, not just the risk of the borrower. That deserves emphasis, because it changes what kind of framework it is. Character, Ability, Purpose, Amount, Repayment and Insurance are all risk assessments and map onto the five Cs closely. Margin is not a risk factor at all. It asks whether the return justifies the risk, which is a pricing question.

So CAMPARI is not a more granular five Cs, it is a risk-and-return framework, and its natural home is relationship banking where the credit decision and the deal economics are settled together. The five Cs answer whether to lend; CAMPARI answers whether to lend on these terms. A bank that has separated its credit function from its pricing function has, in effect, split CAMPARI back into its two halves and given them to different committees.

The framework also contains one item that reads oddly and is genuinely useful: Amount, which asks whether the request is sufficient rather than merely affordable. A facility too small to solve the borrower's problem leaves them struggling and the bank exposed, which is a failure mode no risk model detects because the model only ever sees the amount that was actually requested.

Internal and external ratings

Ratings carry the quantitative side, and the source is careful that the boundary is porous: ratings do incorporate qualitative factors where quantitative information is thin, and the assessment of those factors is made as quantitative as possible through qualitative scorecards or overlays. Internal ratings are produced by the bank on its own clients using internal or external models, and their advantage is access to private information. External ratings come from agencies with in-house models and offer an arguably unbiased view of an entity the bank has no private information on.

Two observations from the source are worth holding. External ratings are a black box from the bank's side, mitigated but not removed by published methodology that omits proprietary detail. And the issuer-pays model gives some grounds to suspect an upward bias, which agencies have addressed with greater disclosure over the years without eliminating the structural incentive. The dating is also instructive: external agencies date from the nineteenth century, largely to analyse US railroads, while bank internal ratings only took off in the 1990s with Basel II. Internal rating is the recent innovation, not the traditional practice.

The grandmaster's perspective

The case for scoring retail credit is usually made in the terms the source uses: subjective decisions allow inconsistency and permit bias. Both claims are true and both are measurable, which makes the argument considerably stronger than the qualitative version.

Judgement was noisy rather than incapable, and averaging proves it

The three results above read as a case for replacing people with a formula, and they are, but the mechanism matters. In the model above the underwriter sees the right score with noise added, so their failure is variance rather than bias in the statistical sense. That has a testable consequence: averaging independent assessors should recover the signal, and it does, at a rate that can be computed.

WHICH EXPLAINS THE HISTORICAL SEQUENCE Small banks with local knowledge and relationships could afford thorough manual assessment on small volumes. Large banks could not, and had no adequate model either, so retail lending was either careful and small or large and crude. Cheap data resolved the dilemma by making the model possible, not by making judgement worse. AND IT PREDICTS WHERE JUDGEMENT SURVIVES Judgement remains where the ticket is large enough to fund several expert opinions and the data too thin to model: wholesale, specialised lending, and the refer band of a retail scorecard. Those are exactly the three places the source says qualitative assessment still dominates.

The five Cs are a scorecard written in prose

There is a structural continuity between the old framework and the modern architecture that is usually missed because the two are taught as successive eras. The five Cs are explicitly compensatory: the framework's own instruction is that the Cs are taken collectively and that strength in one can offset weakness in another. That is an additive assumption about how evidence combines, and an additive scorecard is exactly that assumption made explicit, weighted by data rather than by the assessor, and calibrated to an outcome.

What did not survive the transition is the part that was never compensatory. Undischarged bankruptcy, a sanctions hit, a know-your-customer failure, an age below the product minimum: no strength elsewhere offsets any of them, and they are not statistical judgements at all. Those became policy rules, which sit upstream of the score and outside it, and which lecture 2 finds occupying step 2 of the decision chain for exactly this reason.

So the modern retail decision is not a model replacing a framework. It is the framework split along a line the framework itself implied: the compensatory part became a fitted additive model, and the non-compensatory part became hard-coded rules. Seeing it that way makes two things easier. It explains why policy rules are not derived statistically and should not be, since they encode conditions under which the score should not be trusted rather than evidence to be weighed. And it gives a test for whether a proposed characteristic belongs in the scorecard or in the rules: ask whether any amount of strength elsewhere should be allowed to offset it.

The pattern this shares with the rest of the track Every framework in the credit risk collection turns out to encode an assumption about how evidence combines. The ASRF assumed independence across obligors, the sector model assumed a single common factor, the aggregation shortcut assumed perfect dependence across sectors. The five Cs assume additive compensation between factors. In each case the assumption is invisible in the framework's own vocabulary and decisive for what the framework gets wrong.
Where this goes wrong in practice A qualitative overlay with no ceiling on how much it may move the decision. Once the compensatory principle is granted, an assessor can in principle justify any approval by asserting sufficient strength somewhere unmeasured, and the language of the five Cs supplies the vocabulary to do it. The discipline that works is not banning the overlay but bounding it: state in policy the maximum number of notches or score points a qualitative factor may contribute, require the specific factor and its magnitude to be recorded, and monitor override rates and their subsequent performance by assessor. Unbounded compensation is how a documented framework becomes a subjective decision with better paperwork.
Exam tip If asked to compare the five Cs with CAMPARI, the marks are not for reciting both lists. Say that they overlap almost completely on risk and that CAMPARI adds Margin, which is a return factor rather than a risk factor, so CAMPARI is a risk-and-return framework suited to relationship banking where price and credit are decided together. A further mark is available for Amount, which asks whether the facility is large enough to solve the borrower's problem, a failure mode no risk model can see because the model only observes the amount requested.
