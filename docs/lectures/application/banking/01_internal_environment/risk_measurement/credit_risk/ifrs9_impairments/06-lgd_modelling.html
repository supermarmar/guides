LGD modelling: the cure decomposition, the completion threshold, and the scenario product

The IFRS 9 loss given default is not built as one model. It is decomposed into the probability of writing off given default and two conditional severities, one for write-off and one for cure. That decomposition puts the largest sensitivity in an unexpected place, forces a censoring problem the standard says nothing about, and interacts with the scenario weighting in a way that averaging parameters gets wrong by roughly eight per cent.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A defaulted account has two possible destinations and they carry very different losses. It can cure, in which case the loss is collection and counselling cost and nothing more, or it can be written off, in which case the loss is whatever the exposure exceeds the present value of recoveries. So the IFRS 9 LGD is built as a probability-weighted blend of the two:

$$\text{LGD} = \text{PWGD} \times \text{LGW} + (1 - \text{PWGD}) \times \text{LGC}$$

The PWGD is the probability of write-off given default, estimated eventually rather than at a fixed horizon. LGW is loss given write-off, driven mainly by collateral. LGC is loss given cure, small and driven by costs and payment delay.

Three things fall out of the algebra and each is the subject of a section below. First, when the cure rate exceeds one half, LGD is more sensitive per percentage point to the small LGC than to the large LGW, which inverts where modelling effort usually goes. Second, cures resolve faster than write-offs, so any cohort that has not fully resolved understates the write-off rate, and the completion threshold that manages this is a pure judgement the standard does not prescribe. Third, because PD and LGD move together across macroeconomic scenarios, averaging the parameters and then multiplying understates ECL by about eight per cent on the figures worked below.

Feynman test Ask two questions rather than one. Will this defaulted borrower recover, and if not, how much will we get back from selling what secures the loan? Multiply the second answer by the chance of the first going badly, add the cost of the recoveries that do work out, and you have the loss.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the decomposition is not presentational. A direct LGD model fitted to realised losses on all defaults blends two populations whose loss distributions barely overlap: cured accounts cluster near zero and written-off accounts spread widely towards one. A single regression on that mixture fits neither well, and its residuals are structurally bimodal. Splitting on the destination converts one badly specified model into two reasonable ones.

The second reason is that the split relocates where the risk drivers live. Collateral value explains almost nothing about whether an account cures and almost everything about the loss if it does not. Repayment capacity explains the reverse. Fitted jointly, each driver's coefficient is contaminated by the other population; fitted separately, both are interpretable.

The third reason is the one an auditor will find. IFRS 9 paragraph 5.5.17 requires the expected credit loss to be an unbiased and probability-weighted amount, and the completion threshold introduces a bias with a known sign. Every incomplete cohort understates write-off, therefore understates LGD, therefore understates the provision. A parameter with a directional bias in a standard that demands the absence of one is exactly what a challenge session should land on, and the threshold has no regulatory anchor to hide behind.

And a fourth reason that is easy to miss. The LGD carries the consequence of a choice made in the PD model. Lecture 5's term structure counts re-defaults, which means the same economic loss can be counted twice unless the LGD declines to attach principal loss to a cure. That reconciliation is worked through in step 4, and it turns out to explain why LGC is a cost concept rather than a loss concept.

Step 3

The mental model

One default, two destinations, three parameters, and two curves whose shapes come from the same underlying mechanism.

Step 4

The detail

Where the sensitivity actually sits

Differentiate the decomposition and the answer is immediate. With $L$ for LGD, $P$ for PWGD, $W$ for LGW and $C$ for LGC:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial P} = W - C \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial W} = P \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial C} = 1 - P$$

The derivative with respect to LGC is $1-P$, the cure rate. On any portfolio where more than half of defaults cure, and most retail portfolios are such portfolios, a percentage point of error in LGC costs more LGD than a percentage point of error in LGW. That is the opposite of where effort goes, because LGW is the large number and the one with the collateral machinery attached.

SENSITIVITY, WORKED ON A SECURED RETAIL PORTFOLIO BASE PARAMETERS PWGD = 35% so the cure rate is 65% LGW = 28% LGC = 2.5% collection and counselling cost LGD = 0.35 x 0.28 + 0.65 x 0.025 = 0.0980 + 0.01625 = 11.425% DERIVATIVES AT THAT POINT d/dPWGD = W - C = 0.280 - 0.025 = 0.255 d/dLGW = P = 0.350 d/dLGC = 1 - P = 0.650 <-- the largest NOW COMPARE TWO PLAUSIBLE ERRORS A LGW wrong by 10% RELATIVE: 28% -> 30.8% LGD = 0.35 x 0.308 + 0.65 x 0.025 = 12.405% LGD rises by 0.98pp, i.e. 8.6% relative B LGC wrong by 100% RELATIVE: 2.5% -> 5.0% LGD = 0.35 x 0.28 + 0.65 x 0.050 = 13.050% LGD rises by 1.63pp, i.e. 14.2% relative WHY B IS THE REALISTIC ERROR, NOT A LGW rests on observed recoveries and observed collateral sale proceeds. Both are cash flows with dates and amounts in the recoveries data. LGC rests on COLLECTION COSTS, which are almost never observed per account. They are a departmental cost base allocated to accounts by a driver someone chose. A factor-of-two error in that allocation is entirely ordinary, and it moves LGD by half again as much as a ten per cent error in the collateral-driven parameter. THE CONCLUSION On a high-cure portfolio the least-modelled parameter carries the largest derivative and the largest plausible relative error at the same time.

The completion threshold, and a bias with a known sign

The PWGD is defined on completed accounts, that is those which have reached a final state of cure or write-off, because an account still in collections has no observed destination. The write-off rate is therefore the number written off over the number completed, and the completion rate is the number completed over the total in the cohort.

The source is explicit that a completion threshold, for example eighty per cent, is used to exclude cohorts too unresolved to be informative, and equally explicit that this is not prescribed by IFRS. What the source does not do is quantify what the threshold buys, and the answer is less than one might hope.

CENSORING BIAS IN AN UNRESOLVED COHORT A COHORT OF 1,000 DEFAULTS, TRUE ULTIMATE OUTCOME 600 cure, 400 write off, so the true PWGD is 40.0% RESOLUTION SPEEDS DIFFER, WHICH IS THE WHOLE POINT cures completed by month 6 / 12 / 18 : 80% / 95% / 100% write-offs completed by 6 / 12 / 18 / 24 : 15% / 45% / 75% / 100% WHAT THE NAIVE ESTIMATE SEES AT EACH OBSERVATION DATE month cured written off completion naive PWGD 6 480 60 54.0% 11.11% 12 570 180 75.0% 24.00% 18 600 300 90.0% 33.33% 24 600 400 100.0% 40.00% The estimate approaches the truth FROM BELOW at every date. That is not a sampling accident. It follows directly from cures completing faster than write-offs, so the completed subset is always over-representative of cures. SO HOW MUCH DOES AN 80% THRESHOLD BUY? Let c be the completion rate and w-hat the naive estimate. The incomplete accounts are all destined for one state or the other, so the true rate is bounded: w-hat . c <= w <= w-hat . c + (1 - c) lower bound: every incomplete account eventually cures upper bound: every incomplete account is written off At w-hat = 30%: c = 0.80 w in [24.0%, 44.0%] width 20.0pp c = 0.90 w in [27.0%, 37.0%] width 10.0pp c = 0.95 w in [28.5%, 33.5%] width 5.0pp THE POINT An 80% threshold leaves a 20 percentage point interval around a 30 per cent point estimate. The bound width is exactly 1 - c, so the threshold's value is linear in it and 80% is a loose setting for a parameter that scales the whole LGD. And the threshold cannot simply be raised, because a higher threshold admits only OLDER cohorts. That is the tension developed in step 5.

Lifetime LGW and the recovery curve

LGW is calibrated as one minus the recovery rate, where the recovery rate is the present value of recoveries, inclusive of collection costs, over the exposure. It can be balance-weighted or number-weighted, and the two answers differ whenever recovery performance correlates with exposure size, which on secured lending it does.

The recovery curve plots the rate against time in collections and rises with a decelerating slope, because some recovery arrives immediately on default and the remainder arrives through legal process over years. Longer recovery periods reduce the overall recovery rate, both because the discounting bites and because the accounts still recovering at month thirty are the difficult ones.

For secured exposures the loss is modelled in two components, the loss on the secured part driven by collateral value and the loss on the unsecured part driven by recoveries, which is the same structure the A-IRB LGD uses. For unsecured exposures such as credit cards there is no collateral leg and the calibration rests on historical recovery rates from comparable accounts. A useful consistency check the source gives: plotting LGW against the write-off date should produce an average that reconciles to the average of the LGW curve by TIC.

Reconciling the zero-loss-on-cure requirement from lecture 5

Lecture 5 established that the PD term structure counts re-default events, and that the same treatment must be carried into the LGD by assigning a zero loss assumption to cure events. That sits uneasily beside a decomposition in which LGC is explicitly non-zero. The resolution is a distinction the arithmetic makes unavoidable.

WHY LGC IS A COST CONCEPT, NOT A LOSS CONCEPT ONE ACCOUNT, EXPOSURE 100, TWO DEFAULT EVENTS t = 1 defaults, then cures. Collection cost 2.5. t = 3 defaults again, written off. Loss on write-off 28. THE PD TERM STRUCTURE COUNTS BOTH EVENTS, so the ECL calculation will attach an LGD to each of them. TREATMENT A, CORRECT default 1 gets LGC = 2.5 (cost only) default 2 gets LGW = 28.0 (principal loss) total loss recognised = 30.5 The 100 of principal is at risk once and is lost once. TREATMENT B, WRONG default 1 gets a "loss given default" that includes some principal write-down, say 8.0 default 2 gets LGW = 28.0 total loss recognised = 36.0 The same principal has been impaired twice. SO THE INSTRUCTION RESOLVES CLEANLY "Zero loss on cure" means zero CREDIT loss, not zero cost. The principal loss belongs entirely to the terminal write-off, because that is the only event at which principal is actually forgone. The cure carries only the cash costs it genuinely caused. THE VALIDATOR'S TEST Sum the LGD assigned across all default events for a single account over its life. Under treatment A the principal component sums to at most one write-off. Under treatment B it does not. This is a check on the interaction of two models, so neither modelling team will run it unless someone asks.

The A-IRB delta, decomposed

The IFRS 9 LGD and its A-IRB counterpart differ in three separable ways and it is worth seeing the sizes rather than the list. IFRS 9 requires an unbiased probability-weighted amount discounted at the effective interest rate, with cash flows from collateral limited to those expected from obtaining and selling it, per paragraph 5.5.17 and B5.5.29. The regulatory parameter is an economic loss concept: CRR Article 181(1)(a) requires all relevant costs including material indirect costs, Article 181(1)(b) requires a downturn LGD where LGD is cyclical, and the EBA guidelines on PD and LGD estimation set the discount rate as the primary interest rate plus a five percentage point spread.

THE SAME DEFAULTED ACCOUNT UNDER BOTH FRAMEWORKS FACTS exposure at default 100 recovery expected, base 70, received 3 years on recovery expected, downturn 55, same timing effective interest rate 6% risk-free rate 2%, so the regulatory discount rate is 7% material indirect costs 3% of exposure IFRS 9 BASE PV = 70 / 1.06^3 = 58.77 LGD = 41.23% STEP TO A-IRB, ONE CHANGE AT A TIME 1 discount rate 6% -> 7% PV = 70 / 1.07^3 = 57.14 LGD = 42.86% +1.63pp 2 add material indirect costs LGD = 45.86% +3.00pp 3 downturn recovery 70 -> 55 PV = 55 / 1.07^3 = 44.90 LGD = 55.10% plus indirect costs LGD = 58.10% +12.24pp TOTAL GAP 41.23% -> 58.10% = 16.87pp RATIO 58.10 / 41.23 = 1.41x DECOMPOSITION OF THE GAP downturn recovery haircut 12.24pp 72.6% material indirect costs 3.00pp 17.8% discount rate spread 1.63pp 9.7% READ THIS RESULT PROPERLY Roughly three quarters of the gap is the downturn condition. The other two differences are real, both appear in exam answers, and together they account for barely a quarter of the difference. If you are explaining why the two numbers diverge, lead with the downturn and mention the rest.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The scenario product, and an eight per cent error nobody sees

IFRS 9 requires the LGD under each macroeconomic scenario, probability-weighted, and the natural implementation is to weight each parameter and then multiply the averages. Because the LGD is linear in the recovery amount, weighting the LGDs gives exactly the same answer as computing the LGD of the weighted recovery, which makes the shortcut look harmless. It is not, and the reason is that PD and LGD are correlated across scenarios by construction: the downside scenario raises both.

MULTIPLY THEN AVERAGE, NOT AVERAGE THEN MULTIPLY THREE SCENARIOS, SAME ACCOUNT AS ABOVE, EIR 6% scenario weight recovery LGD PD upside 15% 78 34.51% 2.0% base 60% 70 41.23% 3.0% downside 25% 55 53.82% 8.0% ROUTE 1, AVERAGE THE PARAMETERS THEN MULTIPLY E[PD] = 0.15(2.0) + 0.60(3.0) + 0.25(8.0) = 4.100% E[LGD] = 0.15(34.51) + 0.60(41.23) + 0.25(53.82) = 43.368% E[PD] x E[LGD] = 1.7781% of exposure ROUTE 2, MULTIPLY WITHIN EACH SCENARIO THEN AVERAGE upside 0.15 x 2.0% x 34.51% = 0.1035% base 0.60 x 3.0% x 41.23% = 0.7421% downside 0.25 x 8.0% x 53.82% = 1.0764% E[PD x LGD] = 1.9220% of exposure THE DIFFERENCE 1.9220 / 1.7781 = 1.0810 Route 1 understates the loss by 8.1%. WHERE IT COMES FROM Cov(PD, LGD) > 0 across scenarios. The scenario that makes more accounts default is the same scenario that depresses collateral values and lengthens recovery timelines. E[XY] = E[X]E[Y] + Cov(X, Y), and the covariance term is the whole 8.1%. Note that the LGD's own linearity in recovery hides this. Scenario-weighting the LGD in isolation is exactly right. The error appears only in the PRODUCT, which is why it survives a review that checks each parameter model separately and never checks the aggregation. THE GENERAL FORM A probability-weighted expectation of a product is never the product of probability-weighted expectations unless the factors are independent. IFRS 9 asks for the expectation of the loss, which is a product of three correlated factors. Weight at the level of the loss, not at the level of the parameters.

The LGD cannot be as point-in-time as the PD, and the reason is structural

Lecture 5's estimator achieves something specific: by sliding the observation window back one month for each additional month of horizon, every point on the PD term structure draws its defaults from the same recent outcome months. The estimate is point-in-time at every horizon simultaneously.

The PWGD cannot do this and the obstruction is not a modelling deficiency. A default cohort must resolve before its write-off rate is observable, and resolution takes two years or more on secured lending. The completion threshold therefore forces the estimate onto cohorts that defaulted long enough ago to have completed, which is precisely the opposite of the reference-period design. Raising the threshold to tighten the censoring bound pushes the cohorts further into the past and makes the estimate less current; lowering it keeps the estimate current and reintroduces a downward bias. There is no setting at which both are satisfied.

So the IFRS 9 LGD is structurally staler than the IFRS 9 PD, and the tension is a genuine one rather than a failure of implementation. Two consequences follow. The forward-looking information overlay of lecture 8 carries more of the burden on the LGD side than on the PD side, because it is the only route by which current conditions enter a parameter estimated on old cohorts. And the completion threshold deserves to be documented as a bias-recency trade-off with the bound width stated, not as a data-quality filter, because calling it a filter conceals a choice that moves the provision.

Where this goes wrong in practice A bank that sets the completion threshold once, at eighty per cent, and never revisits it has fixed the width of its censoring bound at twenty percentage points of the write-off rate and stopped thinking about it. The threshold should be reviewed whenever collections strategy, legal process duration or portfolio mix changes, because all three move the resolution speeds that the bound depends on.

Why the PWGD curve dips, and what the dip is worth

The non-monotone shape of PWGD against time in collections is easy to state and worth understanding mechanically. Early in collections the population still contains the hopeless cases, that is fraud, deceased borrowers, and accounts with no engagement at all, which resolve to write-off almost immediately. As they exit, the surviving pool is enriched in accounts with genuine short-term distress, so the conditional probability of write-off falls. Beyond that point continued failure to cure is informative in the other direction, and the probability climbs towards one hundred per cent past about twelve months.

This is a selection effect, not a change in any individual account's prospects, and it is the same two-speed resolution that produces the censoring bias in the completion threshold. One mechanism, two consequences, which is worth carrying because it means an intervention that changes resolution speeds moves both at once.

The dip has a practical use the source names: the curing rule can be inferred from the point where accounts stop recovering, or where the lower PWGD is observed. A bank setting the number of sufficient payments required for a cure is usually choosing that number from collections policy or convention. The trough of the empirical PWGD curve is a data-driven answer to the same question, and where the two disagree, the policy definition is calling accounts cured that the data say will write off.

The pattern this shares with the rest of the track Three times now a boundary between two models has turned out to be where the error lives: the re-default double-count between PD and LGD, the scenario product between the parameter models and the aggregation, and the curing rule between collections policy and the PWGD model. Each is invisible from inside either model. Each requires someone whose remit spans both.
