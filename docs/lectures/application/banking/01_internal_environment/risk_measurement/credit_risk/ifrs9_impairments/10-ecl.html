ECL: the aggregation, the cliff, and four measurement bases

Five lectures of parameter work now collapse into one number. The assembly looks like bookkeeping and contains three things worth knowing: the stage determines the window over which defaults are counted and not the window over which losses land, the covariance error identified separately in lectures 6 and 7 turns out to be almost exactly additive across the three factors, and one asset class in the standard can carry a negative allowance.

IFRS 9 10 of 12 Reading time, 40 minutes Sources: IFRS 9 paragraphs 5.4.1, 5.5.1 to 5.5.17, Appendix A, B5.5.28 to B5.5.35 and B5.5.44. The wiki 07_ecl/ folder is empty.
Step 1

The 30-second version

The quantity IFRS 9 actually defines is a discounted cash flow shortfall. Appendix A sets a credit loss as the difference between the contractual cash flows due and the cash flows expected, discounted at the original effective interest rate, and the expected credit loss as the probability-weighted version of that. The three-factor product is a way of computing it, not the definition of it, and every lecture in this track has been building the inputs to that computation.

Assembly is a sum over horizons of $\text{PD}^m_t \times \text{LGD}_t \times \text{EAD}_t$ discounted from period $t$ back to the reporting date. What the stage changes is only the upper limit of that sum. Stage 1 stops at twelve months, stage 2 runs to the end of the expected life, stage 3 is the same lifetime sum on an already-defaulted asset, and purchased or originated credit-impaired assets are a fourth basis with its own rule.

Three results, computed below. On one amortising loan at one moment, moving between stage 1 and stage 2 multiplies the provision by 3.75, and nothing about the account has changed except a flag. Aggregating with averaged parameters rather than averaging the assembled loss understates the answer by 11.68 per cent, which decomposes almost exactly into the three pairwise covariances at 11.41 per cent plus a negligible third-order term. And transferring to stage 3 cuts interest revenue by 40 per cent on the worked figures, because paragraph 5.4.1 switches the interest calculation from the gross carrying amount to the net.

One correction to a very common misreading. Appendix A limits twelve-month ECL to losses from default events possible within twelve months. It does not limit the cash flows those defaults generate. A default in month eleven followed by a three-year recovery process produces cash flows out to month forty-seven, and all of them sit inside the twelve-month number.

Feynman test For each future period, work out the chance they default then, how much you would lose if they did, and how big the loan would be at that point. Multiply the three, pull the answer back to today's money, and add up the periods. Stage 1 means you only add up the first year.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this is the only place the whole model is visible at once. Each parameter team can validate its own output against its own back-test and every one of them can pass while the assembled number is wrong, which is exactly what lectures 6 through 9 kept finding. Aggregation is where independence assumptions between models either get respected or get quietly violated, and it is usually owned by whoever runs the calculation engine rather than by a modelling team.

The second reason is the discontinuity. A staged model produces a provision that jumps when a flag flips, and the jump is large. That is a deliberate feature of IFRS 9 rather than a defect, but it means the provision is not a smooth function of credit quality, and it makes the number sensitive to the staging boundary in a way a continuous measure would not be. The American standard took the other route, and the contrast in step 5 is the clearest way to see what the stages cost and buy.

The third reason is that two of the four measurement bases get almost no attention and both can bite. Stage 3 changes how interest revenue is recognised as well as how the allowance is measured, so a transfer hits profit twice. And purchased credit-impaired assets follow a rule that inverts the usual intuition: their day-one allowance is zero however impaired they are, and their allowance can subsequently be negative.

And a fourth reason particular to this lecture. The wiki's 07_ecl/ folder is empty, as are 08_monitoring/ and most of 06_testing_results/. So the last three lectures in this track rest on the standard itself rather than on internal notes, which is the right way round for the aggregation step because the standard specifies it tightly. It is the wrong way round for testing and monitoring, and step 7 flags that as the gap it is.

Step 3

The mental model

Three term structures, one switch, one set of weights, four measurement bases, and two discounting steps of which only one is visible.

Step 4

The detail

The formula, and the one thing the stage changes

Paragraph 5.5.17 requires the estimate to be an unbiased probability-weighted amount reflecting the range of possible outcomes, to reflect the time value of money, and to use reasonable and supportable information available without undue cost or effort. The three-factor assembly satisfies the first two as follows, with $T$ the horizon limit set by the stage:

$$\text{ECL} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{\text{PD}^m_t \times \text{LGD}_t \times \text{EAD}_t}{(1 + \text{EIR})^t}$$

The marginal PD is the right factor here, not the cumulative, because each period's loss must be attached to the period in which the default occurs so that it can be discounted from that period. Using cumulative PDs and a single discount factor is a common shortcut and it overstates the provision, because it discounts early losses as though they arrived late.

On a long-dated book at a higher EIR the figure is much larger, and the error is one-directional.

The default window and the loss window

Appendix A defines twelve-month expected credit losses as the portion of lifetime expected credit losses representing losses that result from default events possible within the twelve months after the reporting date. The constraint is on when the default may happen. It says nothing about when the resulting cash flows arrive, and it cannot, because the loss from a default is a shortfall realised over the whole recovery process.

So a default in month eleven of a secured exposure, followed by three years of legal recovery, produces cash flows out to month forty-seven, and every one of them is inside the twelve-month number. Two consequences follow. The twelve-month provision depends on collateral valuations and legal timelines four years out, which is not what the label suggests. And the discounting happens twice: once inside the LGD to bring recoveries back to the default date, and once inside the ECL sum to bring the loss from the default date back to the reporting date. Only the second appears in the formula, so the first is the one that gets omitted or double-counted.

Stage 3, and the transfer that hits profit twice

Stage 3 measures the same lifetime sum on an asset that is already credit-impaired, so the allowance change on transfer is often modest. The larger effect is elsewhere. Paragraph 5.4.1 requires interest revenue to be calculated by applying the effective interest rate to the gross carrying amount for stages 1 and 2, and to the amortised cost, meaning gross less the loss allowance, once the asset becomes credit-impaired.

THE INTEREST REVENUE SWITCH gross carrying amount 100 loss allowance 40 effective interest rate 6% stages 1 and 2 interest on gross = 6.00 stage 3 interest on net = 3.60 a 40% reduction in interest revenue, which is exactly the coverage ratio WHY THIS IS THE PART THAT SURPRISES PEOPLE The provision movement on transfer to stage 3 may be small, because a stage 2 lifetime measure already anticipated much of the loss. The interest effect is immediate, mechanical and proportional to the coverage ratio, so on a heavily provisioned book it dominates. It is also permanent while the asset stays impaired, so it feeds through to net interest margin rather than to a one-off impairment line. A bank reporting stable impairment charges and deteriorating NIM may be describing the same underlying migration twice.

The simplified route, and why the standard offers one

Paragraph 5.5.15 requires a lifetime ECL measure at all times for trade receivables without a significant financing component, and B5.5.35 permits a provision matrix: historical loss rates by ageing bucket, applied to current balances, adjusted for forward-looking information. No PD, no LGD, no staging assessment and no discounting.

A PROVISION MATRIX bucket balance loss rate ECL current 12,000 0.3% 36.00 1 to 30 days 5,000 1.6% 80.00 31 to 60 3,000 3.6% 108.00 61 to 90 2,000 6.8% 136.00 over 90 1,000 10.9% 109.00 ------ ------ 23,000 469.00 = 2.04% of the book WHY THIS IS NOT A LOWER STANDARD The instruments are short-dated, so discounting is immaterial and there is no meaningful term structure to build. Staging is unnecessary because the measure is already lifetime. The forward-looking requirement still applies and is met by adjusting the loss rates. THE POINT WORTH TAKING FROM IT The elaborate machinery in lectures 5 to 9 exists because long-dated instruments need it, not because IFRS 9 requires elaborateness. Where the term structure is trivial the standard permits the calculation to be trivial. A bank applying full ECL machinery to a thirty-day receivables book has misread which requirement it is meeting.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The covariance error is additive, which makes it diagnosable

Lectures 6 and 7 each found that averaging parameters across scenarios and then multiplying understates the loss, by 8.09 per cent for the PD and LGD pair and 2.57 per cent for the PD and EAD pair. The obvious next question is what happens with all three factors at once, and the answer is more useful than a single bigger number.

THE THREE-FACTOR SCENARIO PRODUCT scenario weight PD LGD EAD upside 15% 2.0% 34.51% 7,800 base 60% 3.0% 41.23% 8,500 downside 25% 8.0% 53.82% 9,200 ROUTE 1, AVERAGE THE PARAMETERS THEN MULTIPLY E[PD] = 4.100% E[LGD] = 43.369% E[EAD] = 8,570 product 152.39 ROUTE 2, ASSEMBLE THE LOSS THEN AVERAGE E[PD x LGD x EAD] 170.19 UNDERSTATEMENT 11.68% NOW DECOMPOSE IT PD with LGD 8.09% PD with EAD 2.57% LGD with EAD 0.74% ------ sum 11.41% actual three-factor result 11.68% third-order term 0.27% THE RESULT THAT MATTERS The three-factor error is, to within a quarter of a percentage point, the SUM of the three pairwise covariance effects. The genuinely three-way interaction contributes almost nothing. WHY THAT IS USEFUL RATHER THAN MERELY TIDY It means the aggregation error is diagnosable pair by pair. A bank need not simulate a joint distribution over three factors to find out where its error lives; it can compute three pairwise numbers, each of which is a two-column calculation, and know that they account for 97.7% of the total. It also ranks the remediation. Getting the PD and LGD scenarios consistent with each other recovers 8.09 of the 11.68 points, which is 69% of the error, and it is the easiest of the three to fix because both parameters already respond to the same macro drivers. AND THE ORDER OF MAGNITUDE IS WORTH HOLDING An 11.68% understatement of the provision is not a rounding difference. It is larger than most model changes that trigger a formal revalidation, and it is produced by an implementation shortcut rather than by a modelling assumption anyone wrote down.

What the stages cost, and the standard that declined to have them

The 3.75-times cliff is not a defect in the calculation. It is the direct consequence of a design decision: IFRS 9 recognises twelve-month losses at origination and lifetime losses only after a significant deterioration, which defers the recognition of most expected loss until something happens. The alternative was available and the American standard took it. FASB's current expected credit loss model, introduced by ASU 2016-13, requires lifetime expected credit losses on all in-scope instruments from the day they are recognised, with no stages and no transfer test.

Setting the two side by side isolates what the stages buy and what they cost. They buy a smaller day-one loss on new lending, which is what the objection to a full lifetime measure was about: recognising a loss on a loan the moment it is written looks like reporting a loss on a profitable transaction. They cost a discontinuity, a transfer test that consumed all of lecture 9, and a provision that is insensitive to deterioration up to the boundary and then jumps.

Which produces the sharpest way to see the SICR problem. Under CECL there is no threshold to choose, because there is no boundary; the provision responds continuously to a change in lifetime expectations. Every difficulty in lecture 9, the arbitrary threshold, the crossover between relative and absolute rules, the outcome period that silently sets the stage 2 population, exists because IFRS 9 chose a step function over a continuous one. That is a defensible choice with a stated benefit, and the difficulties are its price rather than evidence that anyone implemented it badly.

The pattern across five lectures Lectures 6 through 10 have found the same class of error five times: a positive covariance between parameters, prepayment correlated with credit quality, amplitude compression in a fitted series, a cut-off sensitive to a prevalence nobody supplied, and now an aggregation shortcut worth 11.68 per cent. Every one of them is invisible from inside a single model, every one of them reduces the provision, and every one is found by looking at a boundary between two pieces of work owned by different people. That is where the review effort belongs, and it is not where a model-by-model validation plan puts it.

The asset class that can carry a negative allowance

Purchased or originated credit-impaired assets are the fourth measurement basis and they invert the usual intuition completely. Paragraph 5.5.13 requires the loss allowance to be the cumulative change in lifetime expected credit losses since initial recognition, and 5.5.14 states explicitly that a favourable change is recognised as an impairment gain even if it exceeds the amount previously recognised as a loss.

POCI, WORKED A CREDIT-IMPAIRED LOAN BOUGHT AT A DISCOUNT contractual cash flows 40 a year for 3 years = 120 EXPECTED cash flows 22 a year for 3 years = 66 purchase price 55 THE DISCOUNT RATE IS THE CREDIT-ADJUSTED EIR solve 55 = PV of the EXPECTED cash flows credit-adjusted EIR = 9.70% for contrast, the contractual EIR on the same price would be 52.03%, which is why a normal EIR would be meaningless here DAY ONE: THE ALLOWANCE IS ZERO The 54 shortfall between contractual and expected cash flows is already inside the discount rate. Recognising an allowance as well would impair the same expectation twice, which is the same double-counting logic as the re-default question in lecture 6. SUBSEQUENTLY, THE ALLOWANCE IS THE CHANGE expectations fall to 19 a year PV 47.50 allowance +7.50 expectations rise to 25 a year PV 62.50 allowance -7.50 A NEGATIVE ALLOWANCE, PERMITTED EXPLICITLY No other asset class in the standard can do this. A stage 1 or stage 2 asset's allowance is bounded below by zero because its measurement starts from contractual cash flows. A POCI asset's measurement starts from the expectation embedded in its price, so improvement is a gain rather than a smaller loss. WHERE THIS ACTUALLY ARISES Acquired loan portfolios and business combinations, which is precisely when a bank is least likely to have the modelling infrastructure ready and most likely to be under time pressure. The rule is simple and the implementation is not, because it requires tracking a separate credit-adjusted EIR per acquired tranche for the life of the assets.
Where this goes wrong in practice The most damaging aggregation error is not any of the covariance effects. It is a calculation engine that applies the scenario weights at parameter level because that is how the parameters arrive from the modelling teams, in a weighted form, with the individual scenario values discarded upstream. Once the per-scenario parameters are gone the correct calculation is impossible and no amount of care in the engine recovers it. The fix belongs in the data contract between the parameter models and the engine, not in the engine.
Exam tip If asked to contrast IFRS 9 with the American model, the marks are for naming the mechanism rather than the acronym. IFRS 9 recognises twelve-month losses initially and lifetime losses after a significant increase in credit risk, so it needs a transfer test and produces a discontinuity. CECL recognises lifetime losses from day one, so it needs no transfer test, produces a larger day-one charge on new lending, and responds continuously. A further mark is available for noting that the IFRS 9 approach makes the provision sensitive to a threshold the standard does not specify.

Test yourself

  1. Give the Appendix A definition of a credit loss, and explain why the three-factor product is a computation rather than a definition.
  2. Write the ECL sum and state precisely what the stage changes about it.
  3. Why must the marginal rather than the cumulative PD be used, and which way does the cumulative shortcut err?
  4. Do the arithmetic. For the five-period loan in step 4, compute the stage 1 and stage 2 ECL and the ratio between them. Why would the ratio be larger on a mortgage?
  5. How much does discounting remove from the lifetime figure on that loan, and why is the direction of the error worth stating?
  6. What does Appendix A limit to twelve months, and what does it not limit? Give the consequence for a secured exposure.
  7. Identify the two discounting steps and say which one is invisible in the formula.
  8. Do the arithmetic. With a gross carrying amount of 100, an allowance of 40 and an EIR of 6 per cent, compute interest revenue in stage 2 and in stage 3. Express the reduction as a function of the coverage ratio.
  9. Why might a transfer to stage 3 show up in net interest margin rather than in the impairment charge?
  10. Describe the provision matrix and say why permitting it is not a relaxation of the standard.
  11. Do the arithmetic. Using step 5's three scenarios, compute the ECL both ways and the understatement.
  12. Do the arithmetic. Decompose that understatement into its three pairwise components and the third-order term. What share does the genuinely three-way interaction contribute?
  13. Why does the additivity of the covariance effects make the error easier to remediate, and which pair should be fixed first?
  14. State the design decision that produces the staging cliff and the objection it was answering.
  15. Contrast IFRS 9 with the CECL model on three dimensions: the initial measure, the need for a transfer test, and the response to deterioration.
  16. Explain why every difficulty in the SICR lecture is a consequence of the choice between a step function and a continuous one.
  17. State the POCI measurement rule and explain why the day-one allowance is zero.
  18. Do the arithmetic. For the POCI loan in step 5, solve for the credit-adjusted EIR and compute the allowance under both revised expectations.
  19. Why can a POCI allowance be negative when no other asset class's can?
  20. What is the one aggregation error that cannot be fixed inside the calculation engine, and where does the fix belong?
