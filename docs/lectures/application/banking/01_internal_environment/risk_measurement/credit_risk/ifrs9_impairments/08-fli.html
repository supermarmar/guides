Forward-looking information: scenarios, weights, and the amplitude nobody checks

The first component in this track with no regulatory counterpart at all. A regression links default rates to macroeconomic variables, forecasts under three scenarios produce three adjustment factors, and a probability weighting collapses them into one number. Each step is straightforward. The composition contains a mathematical certainty that works against the provision, and the diagnostic the method recommends cannot see it.

IFRS 9 8 of 12 Reading time, 40 minutes Sources: docs/wiki/.../ifrs9_impairments/05_modelling/fli/01-model_methodology.md, IFRS 9 paragraphs 5.5.17 and B5.5.42 to B5.5.52, Mincer and Zarnowitz (1969)
Step 1

The 30-second version

IFRS 9 paragraph 5.5.17 requires the expected credit loss to be probability-weighted and to reflect reasonable and supportable information about future economic conditions. Nothing in the A-IRB framework asks for this: a through-the-cycle regulatory PD is deliberately insensitive to where in the cycle you are standing. Forward-looking information is the component with no regulatory analogue, which means there is no supervisory calibration to fall back on and every choice is the bank's own.

The mechanism is a four-step chain. Regress historical default rates on standardised macroeconomic variables. Forecast those variables under a baseline, an upside and a downside. Convert each scenario's predicted default rate into a multiplicative FLI factor relative to the base. Weight the factors by scenario probabilities.

Three findings, all computed below, and they do not point the same way. Scenario asymmetry alone raises the PD by 4.9 per cent above the baseline, splitting 57 per cent from the downside being further away than the upside and 43 per cent from it carrying more weight. The convexity that justifies having three scenarios at all is worth 5.4 per cent, and three points capture it exactly if you choose them as quadrature nodes rather than as round numbers.

And the one that dominates. Because ordinary least squares fitted values satisfy $\text{var}(\hat{y}) = R^2 \, \text{var}(y)$ identically, every regression-based FLI model produces a default-rate series compressed toward its own mean. At an $R^2$ of 0.60 the amplitude is 77.5 per cent of the truth, so downside deviations are understated by 22.5 per cent. That is four times the convexity uplift and runs the other way. The visual peaks-and-troughs test the source recommends has a correlation of exactly 1.000 on such a model.

Feynman test Work out how much worse people are at repaying when the economy is bad. Guess how bad the economy will be. Multiply. Then check that your guesses swing as widely as reality does, because a model fitted to the past always swings less.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is the absence of an anchor. Every other parameter in this track has a regulatory sibling to argue against: the reference period against through-the-cycle calibration, the LGD against the downturn requirement, the exposure against a conversion factor. Forward-looking information has none, so a bank cannot answer the question "is this reasonable?" by pointing at what a supervisor prescribed. The only available answers are the historical relationship and the published macroeconomic forecast.

The second reason is that this component is where the provision becomes procyclical or fails to. The whole point of a point-in-time framework is that provisions rise before losses arrive. If the FLI model is under-dispersed, provisions rise a little when they should rise a lot, and the counter-cyclical benefit the standard was designed to deliver is diluted by a property of ordinary least squares that nobody chose.

The third reason is the audit interface. The source notes that in an audit typically only the twelve-month regression is examined. On a front-loaded portfolio, 61.5 per cent of the lifetime expected credit loss comes from horizons beyond twelve months, so the horizons that carry most of the stage 2 provision are the ones nobody looks at. That is a statement about where challenge is applied rather than about where the money is.

And a fourth reason specific to this source. The wiki note contains two headings, Discrete Scenarios and Stochastic Scenarios, with no content beneath either. They are the right two headings and the choice between them is a real one, so step 4 fills them, and the answer turns out to be more precise than "three scenarios or a simulation": three scenarios reproduce a full simulation to four decimal places provided the nodes and weights are chosen as a quadrature rule rather than as round numbers.

Step 4

The detail

The regression, the standardisation, and what the intercept does and does not give you

The specification is a regression of the average default rate at a given horizon on macroeconomic variables, one model per outcome period, so that a twelve-month regression sits alongside a one-month, a twenty-four-month and so on. Variables are standardised to $Z = (X - \mu_X)/\sigma_X$, which removes units, makes coefficients comparable across variables on different scales, and improves numerical conditioning.

Standardisation has a second consequence the source draws out: setting every $Z$ to zero puts every macro variable at its historical mean, so the prediction collapses to the intercept, and the intercept should therefore equal the through-the-cycle default rate. That is a genuinely useful sanity check and it is worth knowing exactly when it holds.

THE INTERCEPT CHECK, AND WHERE IT BREAKS UNDER AN IDENTITY LINK, y = b0 + b1 Z + e OLS with an intercept forces the residuals to sum to zero, so b0 = mean(y) whenever mean(Z) = 0. The check holds EXACTLY. It is an algebraic property of OLS, not an empirical finding. BUT A LINEAR MODEL OF A RATE IS THE WRONG SPECIFICATION A default rate lies in [0, 1] and a linear predictor does not. Fitted values can go negative in a benign scenario and above one in a severe one. The standard fix is a logit link, regressing log(p / (1 - p)). UNDER A LOGIT LINK THE CHECK FAILS, AND IN A KNOWN DIRECTION ten years of default rates 2.5 3.0 3.5 4.5 7.0 8.0 6.0 4.0 3.5 3.0 (%) mean = 4.5000% mean of the logits = -3.126299 inverse-logit of that mean = 4.2035% So plugging in Z = 0 returns 4.2035%, not 4.5000%. Understated by 0.2965pp, or 6.59% relative. WHY, AND WHICH WAY IT ALWAYS RUNS The logistic function is CONVEX below 0.5, so by Jensen's inequality the mean of the transformed rates exceeds the transform of the mean logit. Every portfolio with a default rate under 50% is affected, which is every portfolio. THE UNCOMFORTABLE PAIRING The diagnostic the note recommends works only with the link function you should not use, and breaks with the link function you should. Both statements are correct and neither is a reason to abandon the check. Compute the expected discrepancy from the historical series first, then test the intercept against THAT rather than against the arithmetic mean.

From predicted rates to factors, and the gap between the baseline and the answer

The factor construction keeps the model modular, which is its real merit. Rather than rebuilding the PD term structure inside each scenario, the FLI model produces a scalar per scenario and per horizon, and the base term structure from lecture 5 is scaled by it. So $\text{Factor}_X = \text{PD}^{\text{FLI}}_X / \text{PD}^{\text{base}}$ and $\text{PD}_X = \text{PD}^{\text{base}} \times \text{Factor}_X$, with the weighted PD following from the scenario probabilities.

THE SOURCE'S OWN NUMBERS, AND WHAT THEY IMPLY PREDICTED TWELVE-MONTH PDs baseline 4.5% weight 50% upside 3.8% weight 20% downside 5.7% weight 30% FACTORS, taking the base PD as 4.5% baseline 1.0000 upside 0.8444 downside 1.2667 WEIGHTED PD 4.5(0.50) + 3.8(0.20) + 5.7(0.30) = 4.7200% THE THING WORTH NOTICING The weighted PD EXCEEDS the baseline by 0.22pp, which is 4.89% relative, even though the weights sum to one and the baseline is the single most likely path. DECOMPOSING THE 0.22pp uplift = sum of weight x deviation from baseline = 0.50(0.0) + 0.20(-0.7) + 0.30(+1.2) = -0.14 + 0.36 = +0.22pp with symmetric 25 / 50 / 25 weights the uplift would be 0.25(-0.7) + 0.25(+1.2) = +0.125pp so deviation asymmetry 0.125pp 56.8% weight asymmetry 0.095pp 43.2% WHY THIS MATTERS FOR REPORTING A bank that describes the baseline as "our central view" and books the weighted number is booking a figure 4.9% higher than the view it has described, for two reasons that are separately defensible and separately arguable. Both belong in the disclosure. Stating only the weighted result conceals which of the two is doing the work.

Discrete or stochastic: the two headings the wiki leaves empty

The source has headings for discrete and stochastic scenarios with nothing under either. They are the right division. Discrete means three to five named paths with assigned probabilities; stochastic means simulating the macro distribution, computing the loss along each path, and averaging. The usual framing is that the discrete approach is explicable and the stochastic one is accurate, and you choose your trade-off.

That framing is wrong, and the reason multiple scenarios exist at all shows why. If the loss were linear in the macro variable, a single scenario at the forecast mean would give the exactly correct answer and nobody would need three. Paragraph B5.5.42 requires the estimate to reflect the possibility that a credit loss occurs even where the most likely outcome is no loss, which is a statement about convexity. The scenarios exist to integrate a non-linear function against a distribution, and integrating a smooth function against a normal distribution using a small number of points is a solved problem with a name.

HOW MANY SCENARIOS DOES CONVEXITY ACTUALLY NEED? THE CONVEXITY UPLIFT PD at the mean, z = 0 4.5000% exact E[PD] 4.7429% uplift +5.40% relative captured by C 100.0% of the truth captured by B 112.4% of the truth READ B CAREFULLY, BECAUSE IT FAILS IN TWO DIRECTIONS Its nodes are too close to the centre, which understates the variance at 0.500 against 1.000 and therefore understates convexity. But its weights put more mass on the downside, so its implied mean is -0.10 rather than 0, which overstates the PD. The mean error is larger than the variance error, so the net result overshoots. THE PRACTICAL RECOMMENDATION Separate the two things a scenario set is doing. Its CENTRE should be the bank's macro forecast, which is a view and belongs to the economists. Its SPREAD should integrate the distribution around that centre, which is arithmetic and belongs to the modellers. Round-number scenarios conflate them, so you cannot tell whether an implied mean of -0.25 is a deliberate pessimistic view or an accident of weights chosen for other reasons. Three nodes at the forecast mean and the mean plus or minus sqrt(3) standard deviations, weighted 2/3, 1/6 and 1/6, give the accuracy of a simulation with the narratability of a discrete scenario set. There is no trade-off to make.

Reverting to the mean, and what happens to the lifetime factor

Paragraph B5.5.50 acknowledges that detailed information becomes less available the further into the future the estimate reaches, and permits extrapolation from the detailed information that exists. In practice this means macro forecasts revert to historical averages after three to five years, so the FLI factor decays toward one. The source concludes that the lifetime FLI PD will be the same as the base PD because the adjustment averages out. That deserves testing, because it is the step at which the whole overlay could be argued away.

DOES THE LIFETIME FACTOR AVERAGE OUT? NO. FLI FACTOR BY YEAR, reverting over three years yr1 1.30 yr2 1.18 yr3 1.06 yr4 1.00 yr5 1.00 simple mean of the five factors = 1.108 BUT THE LIFETIME FACTOR IS WEIGHTED BY THE MARGINAL PD, NOT BY TIME lifetime factor = sum(marginal PD_t x factor_t) divided by sum(marginal PD_t) ON A MORTGAGE PROFILE peaking at seasoning marginal PDs 0.8 / 1.4 / 1.2 / 0.9 / 0.6 lifetime factor = 1.1151 lifetime PD 11.5% above unadjusted ON A CARD PROFILE, front-loaded marginal PDs 2.0 / 1.5 / 0.9 / 0.5 / 0.3 lifetime factor = 1.1777 lifetime PD 17.8% above unadjusted WHY THE CLAIM FAILS Mean reversion makes the FORECAST average out. It does not make the ADJUSTMENT average out, because the adjustment is weighted by where the defaults are, and the defaults are concentrated in exactly the early years where the factor is furthest from one. The more front-loaded the marginal PD profile, the larger the lifetime factor. This is the same front- loading identified in lecture 7, appearing again with the opposite implication: there it reduced the exposure contribution, here it increases the FLI adjustment. THE FAIR READING OF THE SOURCE If the claim means the macro forecast reverts, it is correct and unremarkable. If it means the lifetime PD needs no FLI adjustment, it is wrong by between 11 and 18 per cent on these profiles. Given how the sentence would be used in a model document, the distinction is worth stating explicitly.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Every regression-based FLI model is under-dispersed, and the R-squared tells you by how much

This is the most important thing in the lecture and it is not an empirical risk. For ordinary least squares with an intercept, the fitted values satisfy $\text{var}(\hat{y}) = R^2 \, \text{var}(y)$ as an identity. So the standard deviation of the fitted default-rate series is $\sqrt{R^2}$ times the standard deviation of the actual series, always, with no exceptions and no dependence on the data.

THE COMPRESSION TABLE R-squared amplitude of the fitted series deviations as a share of the actual compressed by 0.80 89.4% 10.6% 0.70 83.7% 16.3% 0.60 77.5% 22.5% 0.50 70.7% 29.3% 0.40 63.2% 36.8% A macro-to-default-rate regression on ten to fifteen years of annual or quarterly data, with two or three variables, typically lands between 0.40 and 0.70. So the fitted downturn default rate is between 16% and 37% closer to the mean than the real one. PUT THAT AGAINST THE CONVEXITY UPLIFT convexity adds +5.4% under-dispersion at R2 = 0.60 removes, of every deviation -22.5% The effect that receives the modelling attention is four times smaller than the effect that receives none, and the two run in opposite directions. WHY THE DOWNSIDE SCENARIO IS WHERE IT BITES The baseline sits near the centre of the distribution where compression barely matters, because a small deviation shrunk by 22.5% is still small. The downside scenario is a large deviation, and 22.5% of a large number is a large number. So the error is concentrated in precisely the scenario that carries the provision. AND THE SIGN IS ALWAYS THE SAME Compression pulls the downside toward the mean, which lowers the downside PD, which lowers the weighted PD, which lowers the provision. There is no configuration in which this error is conservative.

The fix is not to abandon regression. It is to test the dispersion and correct it if it fails, which is a one-line regression rather than a research programme. Fit $\text{actual} = a + b \times \text{fitted}$ and require $b = 1$; this is the Mincer and Zarnowitz forecast-evaluation regression from 1969 and it is the standard test for exactly this failure. A slope materially above one says the forecast is under-dispersed, and rescaling the fitted deviations by the estimated slope restores the amplitude.

Why the recommended diagnostic cannot find it

The source proposes plotting expected PDs against historical default rates and checking visually that the peaks and troughs align. That test is worth running and it will pass on a badly under-dispersed model, for a reason that is a property of correlation rather than of the data: correlation is invariant under affine rescaling. Shrink a series toward its own mean by any factor and its correlation with the original is exactly one.

A MODEL THAT PASSES THE VISUAL TEST PERFECTLY AND UNDERSTATES THE DOWNTURN BY A SIXTH actual 2.50 3.00 3.50 4.50 7.00 8.00 6.00 4.00 3.50 3.00 fitted 3.30 3.60 3.90 4.50 6.00 6.60 5.40 4.20 3.90 3.60 The fitted series is the actual series shrunk 40% toward its mean of 4.5%, which is what an OLS fit with an R-squared of 0.36 produces. THE VISUAL TEST correlation of levels 1.000000 direction of change agreement 9 of 9 peak in the same year yes, year 6 trough in the same year yes Every criterion the source names is satisfied perfectly. WHAT THE TEST DID NOT SEE peak default rate actual 8.00%, fitted 6.60% understated by 17.5% downturn years 5 and 6 actual 7.50%, fitted 6.30% understated by 16.0% THE SLOPE TEST, ON THE SAME NUMBERS regress actual on fitted: b = 1.6667, a = -3.0000 b is nowhere near 1, and 1/1.6667 = 0.60 recovers the compression factor exactly. THE GENERAL LESSON Timing and amplitude are separate properties of a forecast and they need separate tests. A correlation or a visual overlay tests timing. Only a slope test, or an explicit comparison of standard deviations, tests amplitude. A validation framework that checks the first and not the second will approve a model that is right about when downturns happen and wrong about how bad they are, which for a provision is the worse of the two errors to make.

The twelve-month audit shortcut, and where the money actually is

The source records that in an audit typically only the twelve-month regression is examined. That is understandable, because the twelve-month model is the one with the most data behind it and the clearest interpretation, and it drives the whole stage 1 provision. It is also where the challenge is least needed.

On the front-loaded profile used above, 61.5 per cent of the lifetime expected credit loss arises at horizons beyond twelve months. Those horizons are estimated from regressions with progressively fewer non-overlapping observations, progressively wider confidence intervals, and no examination. So the pattern is that the best-evidenced model receives the scrutiny and the worst-evidenced models carry most of the stage 2 balance.

The practical response is not to demand that every horizon be audited, which is not proportionate. It is to make the multi-horizon models' sensitivity visible in one place: report the FLI factor at each horizon alongside the number of effective observations behind it, so that a reviewer can see immediately that the sixty-month factor rests on a fifth of the evidence supporting the twelve-month one. That converts an invisible weakness into a stated limitation, which is where model risk is supposed to live.

Three lectures, one class of error Lecture 6 found a positive covariance between PD and LGD that parameter-level validation cannot see. Lecture 7 found prepayment correlated with credit quality, invisible to any test on portfolio averages. This lecture finds an amplitude compression invisible to any test based on correlation. In all three the individual parameter is right and the composition is wrong, and in all three the error reduces the provision. That is not coincidence: an error that increased the provision would have been found, because someone would have asked why the number was so large.
Where this goes wrong in practice The most damaging version of this is a management overlay applied to fix the symptom. A bank whose FLI model under-predicts downturn losses discovers the shortfall in a downturn and adds a post-model adjustment. The adjustment is judgemental, unauditable and permanent, and it substitutes for the one-line slope test that would have diagnosed the cause. Overlays are the right answer to a genuinely unmodellable risk and the wrong answer to an under-dispersed regression.
