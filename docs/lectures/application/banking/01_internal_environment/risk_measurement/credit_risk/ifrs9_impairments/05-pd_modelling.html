PD modelling: point-in-time term structures and the reference period

IFRS 9 needs a point-in-time probability of default at every horizon out to the instrument's life, which a through-the-cycle twelve-month PD cannot supply. Four candidate methodologies exist and the empirical term structure approach is the one specified here in full. Its cleverness sits in a single parameter: the reference period constrains the outcome months rather than the observation months, which is what makes the estimate point-in-time at long horizons as well as short.

Step 1

The 30-second version

IFRS 9 needs something A-IRB does not provide: a point-in-time probability of default at every horizon from one month to the end of the instrument's expected life. The literature on how to produce it is thin, and four approaches are candidates: binary logistic regression, empirical term structures, run-off triangles, and Markov chains on migration matrices.

The approach developed here is the empirical term structure. It builds a defaults table of accounts performing at each observation month and the months in which they subsequently defaulted, converts that directly into marginal and cumulative PDs, and requires no forecasting, no survival model and no explicit modelling of cures or closures.

The reference period $R$ is the parameter that does the work. It restricts the estimate to the most recent $R$ outcome months, and because the observation window for horizon $t$ ends at $M-(t-1)$, every horizon draws on the same recent economic window. That is what makes the twelve-month and the forty-eight-month estimate equally point-in-time.

Which yields an observation worth carrying: the same estimator produces an IFRS 9 PD or a through-the-cycle PD depending on $R$ alone. A short reference period is point-in-time and volatile; one spanning a full economic cycle is through-the-cycle. The difference between the two frameworks' probabilities of default is, in this method, one number.

Feynman test Count how many accounts that were healthy in January had defaulted by February, by March, by April. Do that for the last few months only, so the answer reflects the economy now rather than the economy on average. Widen the window to a whole cycle and you have built a regulatory PD instead.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the A-IRB PD is the wrong quantity for this purpose in two separate respects. It is calibrated through-the-cycle, so it deliberately does not respond to current conditions, and it is a twelve-month probability, so it says nothing about years two through twenty of a mortgage. IFRS 9 stage 2 requires lifetime expected credit loss, which needs a PD at every future horizon.

The second reason is that the significant increase in credit risk assessment depends on PD granularity. A staging decision compares credit risk at reporting date against origination, so the PD must be granular enough to move when an individual account deteriorates. A methodology producing only segment averages cannot support the assessment that drives the largest single judgement in the standard.

The third reason is that the reference period is a genuine modelling choice with a visible consequence, and it is one of the few places in IFRS 9 where a bank's judgement translates directly into provision volatility. A short window tracks the cycle and produces provisions that move; a long one smooths and produces provisions that do not. Both are defensible and they are not the same accounting outcome.

And a fourth reason about the literature itself. The wiki is candid that very few IFRS 9 PD methodologies exist in published academic work and that the field remains in its infancy in developing countries. This is an area where a practitioner is building on thin foundations, and knowing that is part of using the method responsibly.

Step 4

The detail

The defaults table and the empirical estimator

Let $M_1, M_2, \ldots, M_K$ be the observation months. For observation month $M_k$, let $n_{k,0}$ be the number of accounts performing at that month, and $d_{k,t}$ the number of those accounts that defaulted in the period $(t-1, t]$ months afterwards. The empirical marginal and cumulative PDs follow directly:

$$p^m_{k,t} = \frac{d_{k,t}}{n_{k,0}} \qquad\qquad p^c_{k,t} = \sum_{i=0}^{t} \frac{d_{k,i}}{n_{k,0}}$$

No forecasting is involved. Because the denominator is fixed at the accounts performing on the observation date, accounts that close from a performing status simply stop appearing in the numerator, so attrition is handled implicitly and needs no separate adjustment in the ECL calculation. Re-defaults are included, so a lifetime cumulative PD can exceed 100 per cent for a high-risk account on a long-lived product, which is intentional.

The estimate used in the model is a weighted average across the most recent $R$ observation months with an available outcome horizon of at least $t$, weighted by the number of observations so that small and volatile segments are not driven by a single month:

$$\tilde{p}^m_t(R, M) = \frac{\sum_{i=M-(t-1)-(R-1)}^{M-(t-1)} d_{i,t}}{\sum_{i=M-(t-1)-(R-1)}^{M-(t-1)} n_{i,0}}$$

That is the whole design. The reference period constrains the OUTCOME window, not the observation window, by sliding the observation window back one month for each additional month of horizon. So the three-year marginal PD reflects the same recent economic conditions as the one-month PD. The estimate is point-in-time at every horizon simultaneously, which is precisely what IFRS 9 requires and what a naive long-horizon estimate cannot deliver, because a naive estimate of the 36-month PD would have to use observation months from three years ago.

Segmentation, and the three tests

A single term structure will not fit a revolving retail portfolio, because the portfolio is not homogeneous. Left unsegmented, the curve is a blend of low-risk and high-risk shapes, and it therefore understates the risk of high-risk customers and overstates the risk of low-risk customers simultaneously.

The shapes differ structurally, not just in level. Young accounts carry significant default risk in the first year or two after origination and improve markedly thereafter; matured accounts show no such improvement. Customers with recent irregular payment behaviour default soon after observation rather than later, while very low risk customers show a constant or even rising hazard over time. So a high-risk segment needs a steeply rising cumulative curve and a low-risk segment a more linear one, and no single curve is both.

THREE TESTS FOR WHETHER A SEGMENTATION IS MATERIAL 1 VISUAL COMPARISON against the unsegmented curve Segmented curves should show no crossings and give clear risk differentiation. 2 STEEPNESS RATIO, which is the quantitative one Define, for T = 24, 36, 48 months: Ratio(T) = cumulative PD at T / cumulative PD at 12 This measures the curve's steepness independently of its level, so it isolates SHAPE. It also shows the size of the over- or understatement that using the unsegmented curve would produce, and it can be tracked over time by varying the reference month M. 3 CONSISTENCY OVER HORIZONS AND TIME Compare segmented against unsegmented cumulative PDs at T = 12, 24, 36, 48 months, and vary M. Confirms the difference is stable rather than an artefact of one reference month. WHY TEST 2 IS THE USEFUL ONE Levels differ between segments by construction, since that is what segmenting on risk does. Test 2 asks whether the SHAPES differ, which is the question that determines whether one curve can serve both segments. Two segments with different levels and identical shapes can be handled by scaling a single curve. Two with different shapes cannot.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

One estimator, two frameworks, one parameter

The reference period is described in the source as a key parameter and it is more than that. A short $R$ makes the estimate point-in-time and volatile. An $R$ spanning an entire economic cycle makes it through-the-cycle. Nothing else in the estimator changes.

So the structural difference between an IFRS 9 PD and an A-IRB PD, which is usually presented as a difference of philosophy between two frameworks, reduces in this method to the width of a window. That is worth holding for two reasons. It makes the relationship between the two model families concrete rather than conceptual, and it means a bank running both can and should be able to explain its two PDs as the same estimator at two settings, which is a far more defensible position in a validation meeting than maintaining two unrelated methodologies.

It also locates the judgement precisely. A bank choosing $R$ is choosing how much of the economic cycle its provisions will reflect, and therefore how much they will move. There is no correct answer, and there is a clear obligation to state the choice and its consequence rather than to present the resulting PD as an estimate with no dial attached.

Re-defaults create a double-counting requirement in the LGD model

The decision to include re-default events in the PD term structure, rather than modelling on a worst-ever basis as regulatory models typically do, has a consequence outside the PD model, and the source states it explicitly: the same approach must be applied in the LGD model, for example by assigning a zero loss assumption to cure events.

The reason is worth being clear about. If an account defaults, cures, and defaults again, and the PD term structure counts both defaults, then the loss must be attributed to only one of them or the same economic loss enters the expected credit loss calculation twice. Assigning zero loss to the cured first default resolves it: the PD counts the event and the LGD declines to attach a loss to it.

That is the same pattern that recurred throughout the risk measurement track, where a Pillar 2A charge sits on top of an accounting recognition and the boundary must be drawn deliberately. Here it sits between two models the same bank builds, which makes it easier to get wrong, because each modelling team can reasonably assume the other is handling it. A validator's question is simple and worth asking every time: does the LGD model know what the PD model counted?

Why the Markov chain approach disappoints, and what that teaches

Migration matrices are the most theoretically attractive of the four candidates. A single transition matrix, raised to the power $k$, yields a PD at any horizon, forecasts the future portfolio risk profile as a by-product, and is genuinely useful for budgeting and stress testing. The state space is clean, with transient communicating states for performing and defaulted accounts and absorbing states for settled and written-off, and the transition probabilities have straightforward maximum likelihood estimates $\hat{p}_{ml} = n_{ml} / n_m$.

It disappoints for two reasons, and both generalise. The time-invariance assumption fits actual multi-period default behaviour badly, so acceptable fit requires time-specific matrices, at which point the model has become complex without offering more than direct estimation of the term structure would. And minor deviations in monthly transition probabilities compound over multi-year horizons into large errors in the multi-year PD, because the matrix is being multiplied by itself many times.

The lesson is one worth taking beyond credit risk. A parsimonious model that requires per-period parameters to fit is no longer parsimonious, and a multiplicative structure propagates small parameter errors geometrically. Both are reasons to prefer direct estimation of the quantity you actually need over an elegant generating mechanism, when the data permit direct estimation at all. Here it does, which is why the empirical approach was chosen.

Exam tip If asked why an A-IRB PD cannot be used for IFRS 9, two marks are available for the horizon, since twelve months does not support lifetime expected loss, and two for the calibration, since through-the-cycle deliberately does not respond to current conditions. A third mark is available for noting that a single estimator can produce both, with the reference period as the only difference.
