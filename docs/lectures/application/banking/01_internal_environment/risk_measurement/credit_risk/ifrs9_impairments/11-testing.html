Testing: discrimination, calibration, and why they select different models

The A-IRB battery carries over almost intact and misses everything that is specific to IFRS 9. Three additions are needed: twelve metrics on stage transfers, four evaluation factors for a staging definition, and a set of boundary tests that no single parameter model can run. The lecture's central result is that the threshold with the best discriminatory power has portfolio calibration nearly nine times worse than the threshold with almost the worst.

IFRS 9 11 of 12 Reading time, 40 minutes Sources: docs/wiki/.../ifrs9_impairments/06_testing_results/sicr/model-evalution.md, IFRS 9 5.5.9 and 5.5.11, EBA (2018) as cited there
Step 1

The 30-second version

Most of an IFRS 9 validation battery is the A-IRB battery. Discriminatory power, population stability, calibration accuracy, monotonicity, homogeneity and benchmarking all transfer unchanged from lectures 15, 22 and 29 of the capital track, because a logistic regression on retail data has the same failure modes whichever framework consumes its output. Restating them would waste the lecture.

What does not transfer is everything attached to the parts of IFRS 9 that have no regulatory counterpart. Three additions are needed. Stage transfer metrics, of which the source gives twelve, interrogate the staging machinery rather than any parameter. Four evaluation factors assess a staging definition itself: the accuracy and dynamicity of its predictions, the instability of the implied portfolio rate, and its responsiveness to economic distress. And boundary tests catch the class of error that lectures 6 to 10 kept finding, which is invisible from inside any single model.

The result that organises the lecture is a demonstration that two ordinary objectives select different models. Using the source's own candidate thresholds, the value of $u$ that maximises discriminatory power flags 38.8 per cent of the portfolio against an actual SICR rate of 6.97 per cent, a calibration gap of 31.8 percentage points. The EBA candidate at 200 per cent flags 10.6 per cent, a gap of 3.6 points. So the better-discriminating threshold calibrates 8.7 times worse, and the source reports the same effect at more than seven times from real data.

Which yields the discipline this lecture argues for: a provisioning model is selected on calibration and monitored on discrimination, not the other way round. The provision is a level, not a ranking, and no amount of correct ordering fixes a level that is five times too high.

Feynman test Two questions, not one. Does the model put the right accounts at the top of the list, and does it put the right number of accounts on the wrong side of the line? A model can ace the first and fail the second, and it is the second that sets the provision.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the validation habits of credit risk modelling were formed on regulatory capital, where discrimination genuinely is the priority. An A-IRB PD model's Gini matters because the risk weight function is applied grade by grade and misranking obligors misallocates capital across the portfolio while leaving the total roughly intact. An IFRS 9 model's job is to produce a number for the balance sheet, and a misranking that preserves the total is nearly harmless while a correct ranking at the wrong level is a misstatement.

The second reason is that the staging machinery has no parameter to validate. There is no back-test for a threshold, because the threshold is a definition rather than an estimate. So the twelve transfer metrics work by a different logic: they look for patterns that would only appear if the staging were behaving badly, such as exposures reaching stage 3 without passing through stage 2, or a stage 2 population that empties as fast as it fills.

The third reason is that boundary errors are the ones this track kept finding, and no existing validation framework looks for them. A covariance between two parameter models, a prepayment assumption inconsistent with a PD segmentation, an amplitude compression that correlation cannot see: each is a property of a pair of models and each sits outside the remit of both teams. Putting them in a battery is the only way they get run.

And a fourth reason worth being direct about. The wiki has one testing note, on SICR, and it is excellent. The pd, lgd, ead and fli testing folders are empty apart from a single PD notebook. So the coverage in this lecture is deliberately weighted towards staging, which is where the source is strong, and step 7 records the rest as a gap rather than inventing content to fill it.

Step 4

The detail

The four rate quantities, and one that behaves differently from the rest

The source defines a small set of quantities that the whole staging evaluation rests on, and keeping them apart is most of the work. The actual SICR rate $A_t$ estimates, at reporting time $t$ and over the accounts currently in stage 1, the transition probability $P(Y_{t+k} = 1 \mid Y_t = 0)$ of becoming flagged $k$ months later. The expected SICR rate $B_t$ is the same average taken over the model's probability scores rather than over realised outcomes. The discretised expected rate $C_t$ counts how many of those scores exceed the cut-off.

Separately, the prevalence $\phi_{d,s,k}$ is the proportion of positive outcomes in the modelling dataset, which measures class imbalance rather than portfolio behaviour. The distinction is easy to lose and the two quantities need not move together. The source states that prevalence falls as $k$ rises, signifying increased rarity, while its companion note states that the SICR rate rises with $k$. Both cannot follow from the hazard, so it is worth checking which is a property of the portfolio and which of the sample.

Rather that prevalence is a property of the SAMPLE and the SICR rate is a property of the PORTFOLIO, and a validation pack that reports one under the other's name will support opposite conclusions about k. The test is cheap: compute both, plot both against k, and if they diverge, the divergence is telling you something about how the dataset was assembled.

Discrimination and calibration select different thresholds

The source runs the comparison that matters. It evaluates the PD comparison classifier $H$ at six thresholds including the EBA candidate of 200 per cent, finds its discriminatory power substantially inferior to any SICR model and monotonically decreasing in $u$, and then reports something that cuts the other way: at portfolio level, the mean absolute error of the EBA threshold is more than seven times lower than that of the best-discriminating threshold. The threshold that ranks best over-provisions massively.

The source reports this without explaining the mechanism, and the mechanism is worth having because it generalises.

The two mean absolute errors say the same thing again

The source reports two portfolio-level errors. Comparing the actual rate against the probabilistic expected rate gives a mean of 0.44 per cent across values of $k$. Comparing it against the dichotomised expected rate gives 1.11 per cent, which is 2.5 times larger. So dichotomising a well-calibrated probability costs more calibration than the model itself contributes in error.

That locates the bottleneck precisely. If the aggregate of the probability scores tracks the aggregate of the outcomes to within 0.44 per cent, the model is not the problem; the cut-off is. And a cut-off chosen on a discrimination statistic is chosen on the one criterion that cannot see the aggregate. On the source's own figures the smallest error of both kinds occurs at $k = 9$, which is what makes that the prudential choice rather than any accuracy argument.

What the twelve transfer metrics are actually looking for

Read as a list the twelve metrics look miscellaneous. Grouped by the failure each detects they become a coherent diagnostic, and four groups cover all of them.

The first three attribute stage 2 entries to their cause, splitting quantitative PD movement from qualitative criteria and from the backstop alone. A bank whose stage 2 population arrives predominantly through the thirty-day backstop has a quantitative trigger that is not working, because the backstop exists to catch what the model misses rather than to be the model.

The next two ask whether the boundary discriminates at all: the PD difference between the two stages, and their realised default rates. If stage 1 and stage 2 default at similar rates the boundary is decorative, whatever its threshold.

The next four ask whether stage 2 functions as an early warning. Exposures reaching stage 3 having spent under six or twelve months in stage 2, or arriving directly from stage 1, are all cases where the warning came too late or not at all, and the average time in stage 2 before stage 3 puts a number on the lead time. This is the same coherence condition lecture 9 identified: staging must precede default, and these four metrics test it retrospectively on the realised population rather than on a definition.

The last three test stability: the outflow from stage 2 back to stage 1 on a three-month average, the full transition matrix, and the transfer rate from stage 1 to stage 3 against stage 2 to stage 3. A high outflow rate means accounts are oscillating, which step 5 shows is expensive in a way the balance sheet conceals.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The source reports the evidence that undercuts its own conclusion, which is to its credit

Lecture 9 argued that the finding of an insignificant PD ratio was real but that the conclusion drawn from it, that the direct modelling approach is objectively superior, was too strong, because the comparison scores both candidates against a delinquency target that one of them was built to predict. The testing note sharpens that considerably, and it does so with the source's own numbers.

At account level the note is unambiguous: the $H$ classifier's discriminatory power is substantially inferior at every threshold, and its account-level accuracy is described as atrocious. At portfolio level the note says something different. The EBA threshold's mean absolute error is reasonably close to that of the SICR model, albeit still worse. So on the metric that is nearest to neutral between the two approaches, because it compares each against realised portfolio outcomes rather than against either one's chosen target, the gap narrows from decisive to modest.

That is not a criticism of the note. Reporting a portfolio-level result that qualifies your account-level conclusion is exactly what a careful evaluation does, and a less careful one would have stopped at the AUC table. The point for a practitioner is about which number to carry into a model committee. The account-level comparison favours the direct model heavily and is measured against a target that favours it. The portfolio-level comparison is closer to neutral and shows the approaches close. A bank choosing between them should weight the second, because the provision is a portfolio-level quantity.

The general form Whenever two modelling approaches are compared, ask who chose the target. If the target was constructed by one of the approaches, the comparison is measuring fit to a definition rather than accuracy about the world. The fix is not to abandon the comparison but to add a metric that neither approach's definition touches, which here is the realised portfolio rate.

Oscillation is cheap on the balance sheet and expensive in the profit and loss

The source identifies a subtle effect in the dynamicity factor: at small $k$ the account-level predictions become so dynamic that accounts oscillate rapidly between stages, and this oscillation dampens the aggregate transition into stage 2, making the portfolio rate less responsive. So account-level dynamicity and portfolio-level responsiveness move in opposite directions at the short end, which is why the most stable rates are found at both extremes of $k$ and why stability is not the recommendation it looks like.

There is a second cost of oscillation that the note does not price, and it is the one a finance director notices.

Five per cent of the book oscillating monthly injects 8.6% of a full year's charge as noise into each month's result. WHICH REFRAMES THE STICKINESS PARAMETER The source treats s as a modelling choice, stabilising but costly because it makes SICR events scarcer and the portfolio rate less responsive. That is right, and incomplete. Stickiness is also a PROFIT AND LOSS CONTROL, and it is the only one available: there is no other lever in the framework that reduces transfer-driven volatility in the charge. Which suggests the choice of s should be argued on reported-earnings volatility alongside accuracy, and that transfer metric 10, the stage 2 outflow to stage 1, is the diagnostic that tells you whether s is set too low.

What a proportionate battery actually looks like

Three tiers is more tests than most banks run and the temptation is to treat the list as an aspiration. It is worth being concrete about cost, because the tiers differ enormously in what they demand.

Tier 1 is already built. It is the A-IRB battery running on IFRS 9 parameters, and the marginal cost is configuration rather than development.

Tier 2 needs data the bank already has. All twelve transfer metrics are computable from the staging history and the transition matrix, which every IFRS 9 engine produces as a by-product of the monthly run. The four evaluation factors need a definition study, which is a one-off exercise rather than a recurring test, and once $(d,s,k)$ is chosen the recurring version is only the accuracy and instability monitoring.

Tier 3 is five tests and every one is a small calculation. The pairwise covariances are three two-column sums. The dispersion slope is one regression. The principal-sum check is one query. The flagged-share plot is one chart. The prepayment segmentation check is a comparison of two segmentation schemes. None requires new modelling, new data or new infrastructure, and between them they found errors worth 11.68 per cent, 22.5 per cent, 5.10 per cent and a provision that did not move in a downturn.

So the honest conclusion is that the tier which finds the largest errors is also the cheapest to run and the least likely to be run, and the reason is organisational rather than technical. Each tier 3 test spans two models, so it appears in neither model's validation plan, and it requires someone whose remit covers both to notice it is missing.

Where this goes wrong in practice A validation report that leads with Gini and PSI for each parameter, reports them all as green, and contains no portfolio-level comparison of expected against actual staging rates is not a weak report. It is a report that has tested the wrong property. Discrimination should appear in the monitoring pack as a stability check, because a Gini that falls has told you something changed. Selection and sign-off belong to calibration, because that is the property the balance sheet depends on.
Exam tip If asked how to validate an IFRS 9 model, the weak answer lists discrimination statistics. The strong answer separates three questions. Does each parameter rank correctly, which is the inherited battery. Does the staging behave, which is the transfer metrics and specifically whether stage 2 precedes stage 3. And does the aggregate match reality, which is expected against actual at portfolio level and is the one the provision depends on. A further mark is available for noting that discrimination and calibration can be optimised by different thresholds, so the choice of selection criterion is itself a decision.

Test yourself

  1. Why does most of the A-IRB testing battery transfer to IFRS 9 unchanged, and what does it fail to cover?
  2. Explain why discrimination is the right priority for a regulatory capital model and the wrong one for a provisioning model.
  3. Why is there no back-test for a SICR threshold, and what do the transfer metrics do instead?
  4. Define the actual, expected and discretised expected SICR rates, and say what each is averaged over.
  5. Distinguish the prevalence from the SICR rate. Which is a property of the sample and which of the portfolio?
  6. Do the arithmetic. With a 0.8 per cent monthly hazard, compute the probability of a SICR event within k months for k of 3, 9 and 24. What does that imply about the claim that prevalence falls as k rises?
  7. Do the arithmetic. Using the lognormal construction in step 4, compute sensitivity, specificity, discrete AUC and the flagged rate at u of 120 and 200 per cent, and the ratio of the calibration gaps.
  8. Why does the area under the curve for a discrete classifier reduce to balanced accuracy, and what does that statistic ignore?
  9. Explain mechanically why maximising balanced accuracy at a 7 per cent prevalence over-flags so heavily.
  10. Do the arithmetic. Solve for the threshold that calibrates the flagged rate to the actual rate. How far is it from the accuracy-optimal threshold, in u and in share of the book?

  12. Group the twelve transfer metrics by the failure each detects, and give the four groups.

  19. Do the arithmetic. With 5 per cent of the book flipping monthly, compute the allowance swing and express it against a 25 basis point allowance and a 30 basis point annual charge. Why does the charge suffer more than the balance?
