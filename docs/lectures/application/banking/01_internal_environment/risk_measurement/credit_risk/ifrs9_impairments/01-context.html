IFRS 9: expected credit losses, three stages and forward-looking information

The opening lecture of the IFRS 9 impairments track. The accounting framework that sits alongside A-IRB: it uses the same PD, LGD, EAD parameters but in a different conditioning set (forward-looking, multi-scenario, best-estimate rather than stressed). The three-stage model determines whether ECL is computed over 12 months or the lifetime; the forward-looking information overlay generates the macroeconomic scenarios that the parameters are conditioned on.

Step 1

The 30-second version

IFRS 9 is the accounting standard for impairments. It replaced IAS 39's incurred-loss model with an expected-loss model in 2018, requiring banks to provide for credit losses on every loan from origination rather than waiting for a loss event. The mechanism: an expected credit loss (ECL) is computed per exposure and recognised on the balance sheet as a loss allowance offsetting the loan's gross carrying value. Changes in ECL flow through the income statement as impairment charges or releases. The ECL is the probability-weighted present value of cash shortfalls from credit default over a defined horizon, computed across multiple macroeconomic scenarios.

The horizon depends on the three-stage model. Performing loans without significant credit deterioration are in Stage 1 and carry a 12-month ECL (the loss expected from defaults in the next 12 months). Loans whose credit risk has materially increased since origination (a SICR trigger) move to Stage 2 and carry a lifetime ECL (over the entire remaining contractual life). Defaulted loans are in Stage 3 and also carry a lifetime ECL, with PD set to 1 by construction. Stages can transition: a deteriorating loan moves 1 → 2, a recovering loan moves 2 → 1, a defaulted-and-cured loan moves 3 → 2.

The IFRS 9 framework reuses the bank's IRB PD, LGD and EAD machinery but with three structural differences. First, the parameters are PiT not TTC: the IFRS 9 PD reflects current economic conditions rather than the long-run average. Second, the framework is forward-looking: a forward-looking-information (FLI) overlay generates scenarios (typically base, upside, downside) and the ECL is the probability-weighted aggregation. Third, the calibration is best-estimate rather than conservative: there is no MoC and no regulatory floor; the bank's expected outcome is what gets booked. The result is an ECL that is more volatile than IRB capital but closer to the realised loss in any given period.

Feynman test IRB capital and IFRS 9 provisions are two different answers to two different questions on the same loan. IRB asks "how much equity must we hold against the worst-case loss?", uses TTC parameters and a 99.9% confidence level, and produces a stable capital number. IFRS 9 asks "how much should we provide for the expected loss?", uses PiT parameters and a probability-weighted scenario aggregation, and produces a volatile but accurate provision. The same PD model can feed both; the post-processing differs entirely.
Step 2

Why this matters

Provisions and capital interact mechanically but differ in purpose. Provisions sit on the income statement and the balance sheet, reducing both reported earnings and the gross carrying value of the loan book. Capital sits on the balance sheet only, reducing the equity that absorbs unexpected losses. The bank holds both: provisions for the expected loss, capital for the unexpected loss above expectation. A loan defaulting consumes the provision first (smoothing the P&L hit) and capital only if losses exceed the provision. The supervisor allows the bank to use IFRS 9 Stage 3 ECL as a proxy for the expected portion of the loss when computing the K formula for defaulted exposures (the ELBE subtraction in lecture 16 and lecture 21).

The shift from IAS 39 to IFRS 9 in 2018 was the largest accounting reform in retail banking in a generation. Under IAS 39's incurred-loss model, a bank only provisioned for losses on loans where impairment had already happened (a missed payment, a covenant breach). The 2008 financial crisis exposed the procyclicality of this: banks ran with thin provisions through the boom, then had to recognise huge losses suddenly as the cycle turned. IFRS 9's expected-loss model addresses this by requiring banks to recognise lifetime ECL on Stage 2 loans (those with deteriorating but not yet defaulted credit), which builds up provisions earlier in the cycle.

For F107 the IFRS 9 framework is core syllabus. The exam-classic questions cover the three stages, the SICR trigger, the ECL formula with FLI overlay, and the parameter differences from IRB. Candidates who treat IFRS 9 as "IRB without the floors" miss the structural distinctions (PiT not TTC, best-estimate not stressed, forward-looking not LRA-anchored).

Step 3

The mental model

The three-stage model determines which formula applies; the FLI overlay determines the scenario-weighted aggregation. The diagram below shows both.

Three properties of the architecture to internalise. First, the stages are conditional on credit quality, not on default status alone: Stage 2 captures loans that have not defaulted but have deteriorated. Second, the switch from 12-month to lifetime ECL at the 1→2 transition is the most consequential mechanism in the framework: the same loan can move from a tiny ECL to a much larger ECL in a single reporting period. Third, the FLI overlay is what makes IFRS 9 forward-looking: the bank generates macroeconomic scenarios, conditions PD / LGD / EAD on each, and weights the resulting ECLs.

Step 4

The detail

Asset classification

IFRS 9 classifies financial assets into three categories based on (1) the bank's business model for managing the asset and (2) the contractual cash-flow characteristics. The categories drive whether ECL applies and at what measurement basis:

  • Amortised cost (AC): assets held to collect contractual principal and interest cash flows (the "hold to collect" business model). The dominant category for retail loan books. ECL applies.
  • Fair value through OCI (FVOCI): assets held for both collection and sale. Less common for retail loans; relevant for some debt securities. ECL applies, recognised in OCI.
  • Fair value through P&L (FVTPL): assets held for trading, or that fail the contractual cash-flow test. ECL does not apply (fair value changes capture credit risk directly).

A standard retail credit card or mortgage book is at amortised cost and is fully in scope of the three-stage ECL model.

The ECL formula in detail

The expected credit loss over a horizon T, conditional on a forward-looking scenario FLIi:

ECL(FLI^i)  =  sum over t = 1 to T  of  EAD_t * LGD_t * PD_t(FLI^i) * (1 + EIR)^-t

       where  PD_t       = forward-in-time PD at horizon t under scenario i
              LGD_t      = forward-looking LGD at horizon t
              EAD_t      = forward-looking EAD at horizon t
              EIR        = effective interest rate (discount rate)
              T          = 12 (Stage 1) or remaining lifetime (Stages 2 and 3)

The aggregate ECL is the probability-weighted average across N scenarios:

Aggregate ECL  =  sum over scenarios i = 1 to N  of  w_i * ECL(FLI^i)

       where  w_i = probability of scenario i, sum w_i = 1
              typically N = 3: base, upside, downside

The discount rate is the effective interest rate (EIR) on the loan, not the SOFR-plus-spread convention used in IRB downturn LGD. This is one of the IFRS 9-specific subtleties: discounting reflects the bank's contractual yield on the loan, not its funding cost in stress.

The three stages in detail

Stage 1: Performing assets

Assets where credit risk has not increased significantly since origination. The ECL is the 12-month expected loss:

ECL_Stage1 (per scenario) = sum_{k=1}^{12}  PD_k^FiT  *  LGD_k^PiT  *  EAD_k^PiT  *  (1 + EIR)^-k

The 12-month framing captures the loss expected from defaults occurring in the next 12 months only. The total loss given default still spans the full workout (lifetime LGD captures the recovery profile), but the probability of default is measured over a 12-month window. This is the conceptual symmetry with IRB.

Stage 2: Underperforming assets

Assets where a significant increase in credit risk (SICR) has occurred since origination but the loan has not yet defaulted. The ECL becomes lifetime:

ECL_Stage2 (per scenario) = sum_{k=1}^{n}  PD_k^FiT  *  LGD_k^PiT  *  EAD_k^PiT  *  (1 + EIR)^-k

       where  n = remaining contractual lifetime (in months)

The PD term structure (PD_1^FiT, PD_2^FiT, ..., PD_n^FiT) captures the per-period marginal default probability across the remaining life. The construction of this term structure is the subject of lecture 5 (PD modelling). The transition from Stage 1 to Stage 2 produces a step-change in ECL: a loan with a £500 12-month ECL might have a £4,000 lifetime ECL depending on its remaining tenor and PD profile.

Stage 3: Non-performing assets

Assets that have defaulted. PD is set to 1; ECL is the lifetime expected loss given default:

ECL_Stage3 (per scenario) = sum_{k=1}^{n}  LGD_k^PiT  *  EAD_k^PiT  *  (1 + EIR)^-k

The carrying amount is now net of the loss allowance (rather than gross, as in Stages 1 and 2), reflecting that the loan is impaired. This is the IFRS 9 analogue of the IRB defaulted-asset treatment via ELBE.

SICR: the Stage 1 → 2 trigger

IFRS 9 paragraph 5.5.3 requires recognition of lifetime ECL when "the credit risk on a financial instrument has increased significantly since initial recognition". The standard deliberately does not specify what "significantly" means quantitatively; the bank must set its own SICR criteria. Industry practice typically uses three trigger types:

  1. Quantitative: a doubling (or other relative threshold) of lifetime PD versus origination; a 30+ days-past-due backstop trigger; a credit-score deterioration of N notches.
  2. Qualitative: forbearance flag; watch-list addition; covenant breach; a step-down in external rating.
  3. Operational: the 30-DPD backstop in IFRS 9 paragraph 5.5.11 (a rebuttable presumption that 30 DPD indicates SICR).

SICR is the most consequential mechanism in the framework because of the step-change in ECL it triggers. Lecture 9 of this track covers SICR construction in detail.

FLI: forward-looking information and scenarios

IFRS 9 paragraph 5.5.17 requires the ECL calculation to "reflect an unbiased and probability-weighted amount, the time value of money, and reasonable and supportable information about past events, current conditions and forecasts of future economic conditions". The forward-looking-information element is what distinguishes IFRS 9 from a purely backward-looking PiT model.

The standard practice:

  • The bank generates 3 (or sometimes 5) macroeconomic scenarios: typically a base case aligned with the consensus forecast, an upside scenario assuming better-than-base outcomes, and a downside scenario assuming worse-than-base.
  • Each scenario specifies a time-path for the key macroeconomic variables relevant to the portfolio's credit risk: unemployment, GDP, inflation, house-price index, interest rates.
  • Probabilities are assigned to each scenario; the central tendency (base case) typically carries 40-60% probability, with the tails sharing the remainder.
  • The PD, LGD and EAD models are conditioned on the scenario macros to produce scenario-specific parameter paths.
  • The ECL per scenario is computed via the formula above; the aggregate ECL is the probability-weighted sum.

The probability weighting introduces a structural conservatism via Jensen's inequality: even if the scenarios are symmetric around the base case, the ECL function is convex in the macro variables (PDs rise more in downturns than they fall in upturns), so the probability-weighted ECL exceeds the ECL at the base-case macro. Lecture 8 of this track covers FLI construction in detail.

IFRS 9 vs IRB: the comprehensive comparison

The same bank uses the same underlying PD, LGD and EAD machinery for both, but the calibration and use differ across ten dimensions:

The key practical insight: most banks operate one combined PD modelling pipeline (the IRB scorecard from lecture 13) and apply different post-processing for IRB versus IFRS 9. The IRB path applies LRA calibration, MoC overlay and the 0.03% floor; the IFRS 9 path applies FLI conditioning and probability weighting. The same underlying ranking; different absolute calibrations and different downstream uses.

The ECL accounting flow

Once the ECL is computed, the accounting mechanics:

  1. At initial recognition, a Stage 1 12-month ECL is computed and recognised as a loss allowance.
  2. The loss allowance reduces the gross carrying amount of the loan on the balance sheet (the loan is shown net of allowance).
  3. The same allowance amount is recognised as an impairment expense in the income statement, reducing reported earnings.
  4. At every subsequent reporting date (typically monthly for IFRS 9), the ECL is recomputed. The difference between the new ECL and the prior allowance flows through the income statement as an additional impairment (if ECL increased) or a release (if ECL decreased).
  5. When a loan defaults (transitions to Stage 3), the allowance becomes lifetime; on write-off the allowance is consumed.

This is the mechanism that smooths income-statement volatility: provisions absorb expected losses gradually before defaults occur, rather than reporting a sudden impairment at the default event.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first lesson is that IFRS 9 is the operational reverse of IRB. IRB uses TTC parameters to produce a stable capital number; IFRS 9 uses PiT parameters to produce a volatile but accurate provision number. The same loan can have a 1.5% IRB regulatory PD and a 0.5% IFRS 9 Stage 1 PD during a benign year, then a 1.5% IRB and a 3.0% IFRS 9 in a downturn. The two paths intentionally diverge through the cycle; banks that try to reconcile them at every point find themselves fighting the framework's design.

The second lesson is on the SICR step-change. A loan transitioning from Stage 1 to Stage 2 sees its ECL multiply by 5x to 20x in a single reporting period (depending on remaining tenor and PD profile). For a £100 billion loan book with 5% migration from Stage 1 to Stage 2 in a quarter, that is a £500 million to £2 billion incremental impairment charge in a single quarter. SICR triggers are therefore not just a technical mechanism; they are the single most consequential trigger of P&L volatility in the framework. The supervisor probes SICR criteria carefully because aggressive SICR (rare migration) suppresses ECL while conservative SICR (early migration) inflates it.

The third lesson is on FLI and Jensen's inequality. The ECL function is convex in the macroeconomic variables: a 1 percentage point rise in unemployment increases PD by more than a 1 percentage point fall in unemployment decreases it. The probability-weighted ECL across symmetric scenarios is therefore higher than the ECL at the central scenario, even if the scenarios are unbiased forecasts. This convexity is the framework's built-in conservatism; banks that try to flatten it by using fewer scenarios or compressed weights generally end up with provisions that the auditor finds inadequate.

The fourth lesson, structurally most important: IFRS 9 is the bank's accounting truth; IRB is the regulator's capital floor. The accountants book the IFRS 9 number; the supervisor inspects the IRB number. Both numbers come from the same underlying credit-risk machinery but serve different purposes and answer different questions. A practitioner who can articulate both perspectives simultaneously understands the framework; one who reduces IFRS 9 to "IRB without floors" misses the conceptual distinction.

  7. Why does IFRS 9 use the effective interest rate as the discount factor while IRB downturn LGD uses risk-free + 5% floored at 9%?
