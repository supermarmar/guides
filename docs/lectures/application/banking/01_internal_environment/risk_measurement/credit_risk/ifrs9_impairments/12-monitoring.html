Monitoring: the reconciliation, the attribution, and what the charge cannot tell you

Twelve runs a year, four external reporting dates, one audit opinion. Monitoring an IFRS 9 model is a different activity from monitoring a capital model, and the artefact that forces it to happen is a disclosure requirement rather than a supervisory one. The closing lecture of the track, which also draws together the pattern the previous eleven kept finding.

IFRS 9 12 of 12 Reading time, 40 minutes Sources: IFRS 7 paragraphs 35F to 35N, IAS 8 paragraphs 36 and 41 to 42, IAS 34, BCBS d350, ISA 540 (Revised). The wiki 08_monitoring/ folder is empty.
Step 1

The 30-second version

A capital model is recalibrated annually and challenged by a supervisor. An IFRS 9 model is run monthly, reported quarterly under IAS 34, and challenged by an auditor working to ISA 540 (Revised), which requires the method, the assumptions and the data to be evaluated separately. Twelve chances a year to move the number, four to explain it, one to have it examined.

The central artefact is not a supervisory return. It is IFRS 7 paragraph 35H, which requires a reconciliation of the loss allowance from opening to closing balance, by stage. That disclosure is what forces a bank to be able to say why its provision moved, and everything useful in monitoring is organised around producing it honestly.

Three results, computed below. On a worked reconciliation the stage 2 to stage 3 transfer line alone accounts for 73.8 per cent of the impairment charge, because the cliff between those two measurement bases is far larger than any remeasurement effect. Because write-offs reduce the allowance without touching profit or loss, the allowance can fall while the charge is strongly positive, which is the single most misread pattern in an impairment disclosure.

And the one that should change how a monitoring pack is built. The allowance is a level and the charge is its difference, so the charge carries twice the estimation noise and only one unit of signal. On plausible parameters the allowance is 97.8 per cent signal and the monthly charge is 47.1 per cent signal. More than half of what a monthly impairment charge moves by is noise, and no improvement to any model fixes that, because it is a property of differencing.

Feynman test Every month the number changes. Was it the customers, the economy, the model, or the calendar? If you cannot say which, you cannot say anything, and the reconciliation exists to make you say which.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the challenger is different in kind. A supervisor asks whether a model is prudent and can require a floor or an add-on if it is not. An auditor asks whether a number is materially correct, which is a two-sided question, and under ISA 540 (Revised) evaluates the method, the assumptions and the data as three separate matters rather than accepting a model as a package. That changes what documentation is for: it has to support each of the three independently.

The second reason is the cadence. Twelve runs a year means twelve opportunities for a change to enter, and the changes come from four different places: the portfolio, the parameters rolling forward, a recalibration, and a scenario update. Unless the movement is attributed to those four, nobody can distinguish an economy that deteriorated from an engine that was rebuilt, and both look like a rising charge.

The third reason is that one accounting classification decision moves real money between reporting periods. A recalibration is a change in estimate under IAS 8 paragraph 36 and is recognised prospectively; a defect is an error under paragraphs 41 to 42 and is restated retrospectively if material. On the worked figures the classification moves 115 between two years, which is 24 per cent of an annual charge, and the party making the classification is the party whose current-year result depends on it.

And a fourth reason worth stating because it closes the track. This is the twelfth lecture, the wiki's 08_monitoring/ folder is empty, and the material therefore comes from the disclosure standards and the auditing standard rather than from internal notes. That turns out to suit the subject, because monitoring under IFRS 9 genuinely is driven by external reporting obligations rather than by internal model governance, and a bank that treats it as the latter will produce a pack that satisfies nobody.

The mental model

One monthly cycle, one disclosure that anchors it, four sources of movement, and three challengers who want different things.

Step 4

The detail

The reconciliation, and the line that dominates it

IFRS 7 paragraph 35H requires a reconciliation of the loss allowance from opening to closing balance, presented separately for twelve-month ECL, lifetime ECL on non-impaired assets and lifetime ECL on impaired assets, with paragraph 35I requiring the equivalent for gross carrying amounts and 35L requiring information about amounts written off. Together they make the movement auditable rather than asserted.

The FLI remeasurement, which will absorb most of the discussion in the audit, is 15.2%. 2 THE ALLOWANCE FELL AND THE CHARGE WAS POSITIVE Closing 1,066.88 against opening 1,145.00, a reduction of 78.12, alongside a charge of +521.88. Nothing is wrong. Write-offs consumed more allowance than the charge added. This is the most misread pattern in an impairment disclosure. A falling allowance is consistent with improving credit AND with heavy write-offs, and the two have opposite implications. Only the reconciliation separates them, which is precisely why the standard requires one rather than a coverage ratio.

Attributing the movement to four sources

The reconciliation says what changed in accounting terms. It does not say why, and the four candidate causes have completely different implications. A movement caused by the portfolio is information about borrowers. A movement caused by the reference period rolling forward is the mechanical consequence of a design choice from lecture 5, since a fixed window $R$ produces a different PD as the reference month $M$ advances even with no change in the economy. A movement caused by recalibration is a governance event. A movement caused by a scenario update is a judgement about the future.

A monitoring pack that reports only the reconciliation cannot distinguish these, and the practical consequence is specific: a bank cannot answer the question an audit committee always asks, which is whether the increase will continue. Portfolio deterioration usually continues. A roll-forward effect does not. A recalibration is a step change that will not repeat. A scenario update repeats only if the forecast moves again.

The attribution is a sequence of single-factor revaluations: hold everything at last month's values, change one input, record the difference, and move to the next. It is not elegant, the order matters slightly and the residual has to be disclosed, but it is the only decomposition an auditor can follow, and a residual that is large is itself the finding.

Change in estimate or error, and why the distinction has teeth

When monitoring finds that the provision has been wrong, IAS 8 forces a classification with real consequences. A revision arising from new information or new developments is a change in accounting estimate under paragraph 36 and is recognised prospectively, in the current period. A misstatement arising from a failure to use information that was available is an error under paragraphs 41 to 42 and, if material, requires retrospective restatement of comparatives.

THE DISTINCTION IS GENUINE, NOT A LOOPHOLE A macro forecast that turned out wrong is new information and is properly a change in estimate. A segmentation that ignored a variable sitting in the dataset is a failure to use available information and is properly an error. Most real cases are somewhere between, which is exactly why the boundary is contested. WHAT MAKES IT DEFENSIBLE The classification is much easier to argue if the monitoring pack recorded, at the time, what information was available and what was excluded and why. A limitations register maintained contemporaneously turns a contested judgement into a documented one. A register written after the finding is worth nothing, for the obvious reason.

The auditor's three questions

ISA 540 (Revised) restructured the audit of accounting estimates around three separable matters: the method, including the appropriateness of the model and its assumptions in the circumstances; the assumptions, including whether they are reasonable individually and consistent with each other and with other assumptions used elsewhere in the entity; and the data, including its relevance, reliability and completeness. It also requires the auditor to consider inherent risk factors of estimation uncertainty, complexity and subjectivity, and to evaluate whether management's point estimate is reasonable given a range of possible outcomes.

Two consequences follow that matter more than they sound. Consistency of assumptions across the entity is explicitly in scope, which means the macro scenarios used for provisioning, for stress testing and for business planning are compared, and an unexplained difference is a finding. And the audit is two-sided. An over-provision is as much a misstatement as an under-provision, which distinguishes an auditor from a supervisor and is why the prudence argument that satisfies a supervisor can fail an audit.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The impairment charge is half noise, and that is arithmetic rather than a modelling failure

The allowance is a level and the charge is its first difference. Differencing two noisy estimates carries the noise of both, so the charge has twice the noise variance while the signal it carries is only the true monthly movement, which on a slowly-varying credit portfolio is small.

SIGNAL SHARE OF THE LEVEL AND OF THE CHANGE observed level = true level + e_t var = var(true level) + var(e) observed change = true change + (e_t - e_t-1) var = var(true change) + 2 var(e) sd(level) sd(monthly change) sd(noise) signal share level change 10.0 2.0 1.0 99.0% 66.7% 10.0 2.0 1.5 97.8% 47.1% 10.0 2.0 2.0 96.2% 33.3% 10.0 1.0 1.5 97.8% 18.2% 10.0 4.0 1.5 97.8% 78.0% AT sd(noise) = 1.5 THE LEVEL IS 97.8% SIGNAL AND THE MONTHLY CHANGE IS 47.1% SIGNAL. READ THE FOURTH ROW Halve the true monthly movement, which is what a benign and stable period looks like, and the signal share of the charge falls to 18.2%. So the calmer the credit environment, the more of the monthly charge is noise. The metric is least informative precisely when it is most closely watched. WHAT DOES AND DOES NOT FIX THIS A better model reduces var(e) and helps both columns, but the factor of two is structural and survives any improvement. Lengthening the reporting interval helps a great deal, because var(true change) grows roughly linearly with the interval while var(e) does not. This is a real argument for judging model performance on a rolling quarterly or annual charge rather than month to month, and it does not conflict with monthly measurement, which IAS 34 and the monthly close require regardless. The practical remedy is the attribution. If each month's movement is decomposed into portfolio, roll-forward, recalibration and scenario, the noise concentrates in the residual and in the roll-forward line where it can be seen and discounted, rather than being read as portfolio deterioration.

Which connects directly to lecture 11's oscillation result and completes it. There, stage transfers were shown to inject volatility into the charge that the balance sheet conceals, and stickiness was the only available control. Here the same asymmetry appears for estimation noise rather than for transfers, from the same cause: the balance sheet reports a level and the income statement reports a difference. Any noise source at all is amplified by the second and absorbed by the first.

What twelve lectures found, and what it says about where to look

WHICH GIVES THE CLOSING RECOMMENDATION A validation plan organised model by model cannot find any of these, and a validation plan organised by boundary finds all seven. The tests are cheap: three pairwise sums, one regression slope, one query, one chart, one comparison of two segmentation schemes. The obstacle is not analytical difficulty. It is that each test belongs to nobody, so somebody has to be given it explicitly.

The other thread running through the track is worth naming too, because it is the thing that distinguishes IFRS 9 from the capital framework at every point. A-IRB is a one-sided problem: the supervisor wants prudence, conservatism is safe, and a margin of conservatism is a formal component of the estimate. IFRS 9 is two-sided: paragraph 5.5.17 requires an unbiased estimate, the auditor tests it in both directions, and over-provisioning is a misstatement rather than a virtue. Every difference the track examined follows from that, from the absence of a downturn add-on in lecture 6 to the removal of the margin of conservatism to the fact that the best-discriminating threshold in lecture 11 fails not because it is imprudent but because it is wrong.

If you keep one sentence from the track The regulatory question is whether the number is prudent and the accounting question is whether it is right, and a bank that answers the first when asked the second will be corrected by its auditor rather than praised by its supervisor.
Where this goes wrong in practice The characteristic monitoring failure is a pack that grows. Each month a new exhibit is added in response to a question, nothing is ever removed, and after three years the pack is ninety pages and nobody reads the four exhibits that would catch a problem. The discipline is to state, in advance, what each exhibit would have to show for someone to act, and to delete any exhibit for which the answer is nothing. An exhibit with no action attached is decoration with a governance cost.
Exam tip If asked how IFRS 9 monitoring differs from regulatory model monitoring, three marks are available. The cadence is monthly rather than annual, with quarterly external reporting under IAS 34. The challenger is an auditor rather than a supervisor, so the test is two-sided and over-provisioning is a finding. And the anchoring artefact is a disclosure, the IFRS 7 paragraph 35H reconciliation, rather than a supervisory return. A fourth mark is available for the IAS 8 distinction between a change in estimate and an error.
