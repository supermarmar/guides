Definitions: carrying amounts, SICR, FiT PD, EIR

The vocabulary the rest of the IFRS 9 track inherits. Six families of definitions: balance-sheet quantities (gross and net carrying amount), credit-quality concepts (SICR, impairment, definition of default), lifecycle states (cure, write-off, PWOR), parameter definitions (FiT PD, lifetime PD, EIR), interest-revenue mechanics per stage, and key portfolio metrics. Many overlap with the IRB definitions in lecture 03; this lecture flags the IFRS 9-specific distinctions explicitly.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Six families of definitions structure the IFRS 9 framework. Balance-sheet quantities: the gross carrying amount is the loan's book value before any impairment allowance; the net carrying amount equals gross minus ECL. Credit-quality concepts: impairment is the judgement that an asset has deteriorated to the point that scheduled collection is doubtful; significant increase in credit risk (SICR) is the operational trigger that moves a loan from Stage 1 to Stage 2; definition of default mirrors IRB with a 90-DPD rebuttable presumption that the bank can extend if it has reasonable and supportable evidence. Lifecycle states: cure, write-off and post-write-off recoveries (PWOR), with the accounting subtlety that write-off is balance-sheet neutral at execution. Parameter definitions: forward-in-time (FiT) PD conditioned on FLI scenarios, lifetime PD as a term structure of one-period FiT PDs, effective interest rate (EIR) as the discount factor. Interest revenue mechanics: computed on gross carrying amount for Stages 1 and 2, on net for Stage 3 (the "interest in suspense" convention). Metrics: coverage ratio and NPL ratio.

Several of these definitions overlap with the IRB definitions in lecture 03 but with IFRS 9-specific twists. Cure is the most consequential: the IRB framework specifies a 12-month probation period; IFRS 9 paragraph 5.5.7 is silent on cure timing, leaving the bank to choose between "instant cure" (return to performing as soon as the trigger clears) and a probation period analogous to IRB. EIR is another: where IRB downturn LGD uses a discount rate of max(SOFR + 5%, 9%), IFRS 9 uses the loan's contractual EIR — the rate that equates discounted contractual cash flows to the initial gross carrying amount. The discount-rate difference is one of the largest drivers of numerical divergence between IRB and IFRS 9 LGDs on the same loan.

The parameter definitions formalise the conceptual differences introduced in lecture 01. FiT PD is the Forward-in-Time PD: a PiT PD conditioned on a specific FLI scenario; the IFRS 9 ECL is built from FiT PDs at every horizon t weighted by the scenario probabilities. Lifetime PD is the term structure {PD_1^FiT, PD_2^FiT, ..., PD_n^FiT} of marginal one-period FiT PDs across the remaining contractual life. The construction of this term structure via the Vasicek TTC-to-SysPiT mapping and migration-matrix extension is the subject of lecture 05.

Feynman test The IRB definitions in lecture 03 told you what to count and when. The IFRS 9 definitions in this lecture tell you what to book and when. The two vocabularies overlap because they reference the same underlying credit events (default, cure, write-off, recovery) but they answer different questions: IRB asks "how much capital must we hold against this exposure", IFRS 9 asks "how much provision must we book against this exposure". The same loan can have the same default event drive both a regulatory capital number and an accounting impairment charge, with the two numbers differing because the calibration and discount rate differ.
Step 2

Why this matters

The carrying-amount distinction matters because it controls how Stage 1/2 and Stage 3 loans look on the balance sheet. A Stage 1 or 2 loan is shown net of its ECL allowance, but interest income on it is computed on the gross amount (still earning interest on the full nominal). A Stage 3 loan is shown net (gross minus lifetime ECL), and interest income is also computed on the net amount — the "interest in suspense" convention. This switch at Stage 3 has a real cost: a defaulted loan stops contributing the full contractual interest to the income statement, just at the moment when the bank most needs the cash. Practitioners who understand this can explain why a bank's net interest margin compresses in a downturn even when its average gross yield is unchanged.

The SICR definition matters because it determines when the framework's biggest step-change happens. As discussed in lecture 01, a loan moving from Stage 1 to Stage 2 sees its ECL multiply by 5x to 20x. The SICR criteria are therefore not just a technical definition; they are the largest single lever the bank's modelling team has over its provisions. IFRS 9 paragraph 5.5.11 sets a 30-DPD rebuttable presumption (i.e. SICR is presumed at 30 DPD unless the bank has evidence that a longer arrears period is the right trigger); the bank's documented SICR criteria typically include quantitative thresholds (relative PD increases since origination), qualitative triggers (forbearance flags, watch-list addition) and the 30-DPD backstop.

The FiT PD definition matters because it formally distinguishes IFRS 9 PDs from IRB PDs. The IRB PD is calibrated to the long-run average (TTC) and stable through the cycle. The IFRS 9 FiT PD is conditioned on a forward-looking scenario, so the same loan has different FiT PDs under base, upside and downside scenarios in the same reporting period. The bank uses both: IRB capital uses the TTC PD; IFRS 9 ECL uses the probability-weighted aggregation across FiT PDs.

Step 4

The detail

Family 1: Balance-sheet quantities

Gross carrying amount (IFRS 9 §5.1.1)

The original or amortised cost of the loan before any deductions for ECL. Includes:

  • Principal outstanding
  • Accrued interest income (earned but not yet received)
  • Transaction costs (for amortised-cost assets): fees directly attributable to issuing or acquiring the asset
  • Amortisation adjustments via the EIR method

Example: a bank issues a £100,000 loan with £500 in legal fees at 10% interest. The initial gross carrying amount is £100,500.

Net carrying amount (IFRS 9 §5.2.2)

Net carrying amount  =  Gross carrying amount  −  ECL allowance

This is the value at which the loan is presented on the balance sheet. For Stages 1 and 2, the ECL allowance is a small deduction; for Stage 3, it can be a large fraction of the gross amount. Off-balance-sheet exposures (undrawn commitments, financial guarantees) have their ECL presented as a provision (liability) rather than as a deduction from an asset.

Movements in ECL pass through the income statement: an increase in ECL is an impairment expense (reducing earnings); a decrease is an impairment reversal (increasing earnings). The chain: ECL up → loss provision up → assets down → impairment loss in P&L → income down → retained earnings down → equity down.

Family 2: Credit-quality concepts

Impairment

The judgement that an exposure has deteriorated to the point where there is no longer a reasonable expectation of collecting the full contractual amount as scheduled. Impairment is a credit-quality concept, not an accounting trigger: a Stage 2 loan is impaired in the IFRS 9 sense even though it has not defaulted. The accounting consequence is the move to lifetime ECL.

Significant Increase in Credit Risk (SICR)

The trigger that moves a loan from Stage 1 (12m ECL) to Stage 2 (lifetime ECL). IFRS 9 paragraph 5.5.3 requires the assessment "since initial recognition" — the comparison is to the credit risk at origination, not to a static threshold. The bank must define its SICR criteria; lecture 09 of this track covers them in detail. Three broad trigger families:

  • Macroeconomic deterioration: national or local economic decline, GDP reduction, unemployment rise, property-price falls, sector decline.
  • Company / borrower deterioration: forbearance request, covenant breach, credit-rating decline, cashflow issues, reduced net worth, customer / market-share loss, declining collateral value.
  • Portfolio-specific signals: for mortgages, falling rents and limited refinancing options; for retail, early delinquency, high utilisation, increasing total debt, income below debt service, life events (deceased estate, fraud, abscondence, vehicle theft).

IFRS 9 paragraph 5.5.11 provides a 30-DPD rebuttable presumption: SICR is presumed when the loan is 30 days past due, unless the bank has reasonable and supportable evidence that a more lagging trigger is appropriate.

Definition of default

IFRS 9 paragraph B5.5.37 requires the bank to apply a default definition that is consistent with the one used for internal credit risk management. In practice this means alignment with the IRB CRR Article 178 definition: 90 DPD on a material credit obligation, or any of the UTP indicators (see lecture 03). A 90-DPD rebuttable presumption applies: a bank that has evidence that a more lagging definition is appropriate can rebut, but in practice almost no bank does (alignment with IRB is operationally cleaner).

Family 3: Lifecycle states

Cure (IFRS 9 §5.5.7)

The transition from defaulted back to performing. IFRS 9 paragraph 5.5.7 is silent on cure timing. Two implementation choices:

  • Instant cure: the account returns to performing immediately when the default trigger clears (the 90+ DPD obligation is paid down; the UTP condition is resolved).
  • Probation period: a specified period (typically 3 to 12 months) during which the account must remain non-default before returning to performing. Aligned with the IRB 12-month probation from lecture 03.

Most banks align IFRS 9 cure with IRB cure (12-month probation) to keep the two frameworks operationally consistent.

Return to default and redefault

An account that cures and then defaults again is a redefault. PD modelling must treat redefaults as distinct observations to avoid underestimating segment-specific risk. The standard practice tracks four lifecycle states in PD training data:

  • Performing (no arrears)
  • Curing (recovered from default, in probation or post-cure)
  • Defaulted (currently in default)
  • Redefaulting (cured then defaulted again)

Features such as "time since cure" are typically informative because the redefault hazard rate decreases with time since cure.

Write-off

The derecognition of a financial asset (or portion) when the bank has no reasonable expectation of recovery. The gross carrying amount is removed from the balance sheet. The critical accounting subtlety:

The P&L impact of a write-off happens earlier, when the ECL provision is raised, not at the write-off itself. By the time write-off occurs, a Stage 3 loss allowance equal to the write-off amount should already be sitting on the balance sheet, having been expensed through P&L when it was booked. The write-off simply collapses both the gross asset and the provision simultaneously. 
Accounting entry at write-off:
Dr  Loss Allowance (contra-asset)     X
Cr  Gross Loan Asset                  X

No new P&L entry; the balance sheet shrinks on both sides but net carrying amount (already zero) is unchanged. Equity was reduced earlier when the impairment expense was recognised. If the write-off exceeds the existing provision (i.e. the bank was under-provisioned), the excess does hit P&L as an additional impairment charge at the point of write-off.

Post-write-off recoveries (PWOR)

Collections received after a loan has been written off. These flow directly to P&L as recovery income; the gross carrying amount is not reinstated.

Accounting entry for PWOR:
Dr  Cash
Cr  Impairment Reversal (P&L income)

Persistently high PWOR rates indicate the bank's write-off policy may be too aggressive (writing off too early before recovery efforts are exhausted); this inflates the LGD and ECL and signals a policy review is needed.

Family 4: Parameter definitions

Forward-in-Time (FiT) PD

Lifted from lecture 09 via the wiki's 02_probability_of_default.md. The Forward-in-Time PD is the IFRS 9 analogue of the IRB PiT PD, but with an additional FLI conditioning. Formally:

PD^FiT_i,t(k, x_i, x_{i,t}, FLI^s_{t'})
   =  P( facility i defaults in period [t + k - 1, t + k]
         | static obligor features x_i,
           behavioural features at observation x_{i,t},
           macro-economic scenario FLI^s )

Three structural differences from IRB PiT PD:

  • Forward-looking: the conditioning set includes macroeconomic scenarios; IRB PiT PD conditions on intrinsic obligor quality only.
  • Per-scenario: a single facility has multiple FiT PDs at any given t, one per scenario (base, upside, downside); IRB PiT PD is single-valued.
  • Multi-horizon: FiT PD is defined at every horizon k = 1, 2, ..., n where n is the remaining contractual life; IRB PiT PD is defined for k = 12 only.

Lifetime PD

The cumulative probability of default over the remaining contractual life, built from the term structure of one-period FiT PDs:

PD^Lifetime_i,t  =  1  −  S_i,t(n)
                =  1  −  prod_{v=1}^{n}  (1 − PD^FiT_i,t(v))

       where  n = remaining contractual life (months)
              S_i,t(v) = survival probability through v months

The lifetime PD is what makes Stage 2 ECL materially larger than Stage 1 ECL: instead of summing default contributions over 12 months, the bank sums over the full remaining life. Lecture 05 of this track covers the construction of the term structure in detail, including the Vasicek mapping from TTC PD to systemically conditioned PiT and the migration-matrix extension to lifetime.

Effective Interest Rate (EIR)

The rate that equates the present value of expected future cash flows to the loan's initial gross carrying amount. Found by solving:

Initial Loan Amount  =  sum_{t=1}^{n}  CF_t / (1 + EIR)^t

where CF_t is the expected cash flow at time t (interest, principal, fees) and the initial loan amount is the net amount disbursed after fees. The EIR captures:

  • Contractual interest rate (the nominal rate in the loan agreement)
  • Transaction costs (initiation fees directly attributable to issuance)
  • Premiums, discounts, deferred fees

The EIR is typically slightly higher than the contractual rate due to upfront fees being amortised over the loan's life. Solved numerically (Newton-Raphson or financial-software root-finding) on a per-facility basis at origination, then held constant for IFRS 9 purposes.

The EIR is what discounts ECL cash flows in IFRS 9 — fundamentally different from the IRB downturn LGD discount rate (max(SOFR + 5%, 9%)). The EIR is loan-specific and reflects the bank's contractual yield; the IRB discount rate is a regulatory convention. On the same loan, IFRS 9 LGD using EIR can be 3 to 8 percentage points different from IRB LGD using the 9% floor.

Family 5: Interest revenue mechanics (IFRS 9 §5.4.1)

Interest revenue is recognised at the EIR but on a different base depending on the stage:

  • Stages 1 and 2 (performing and underperforming): interest revenue calculated on the gross carrying amount. The full contractual yield is recognised even though some ECL has been provided.
  • Stage 3 (credit-impaired): interest revenue calculated on the net carrying amount (gross minus lifetime ECL). The portion of nominal interest that the bank does not expect to recover is not recognised. This is the "interest in suspense" (ISP) treatment.

Worked example: a £1,000,000 loan at 10% EIR for 5 years. In Stage 1 or 2 the bank books £100,000 per year as interest income. If the loan moves to Stage 3 with a £200,000 lifetime ECL, interest income switches to 10% × (£1,000,000 − £200,000) = £80,000 per year. The £20,000 difference is the interest the bank no longer expects to collect.

Family 6: Portfolio metrics

Coverage ratio

The aggregate ECL across all stages divided by the outstanding gross balance:

Coverage ratio  =  total_ECL_i,t  /  Balance_i,t

       where  total_ECL = ECL_Stage1 + ECL_Stage2 + ECL_Stage3

Used to compare portfolios, track portfolio quality over time, and assess strategic-change effectiveness. Often split between new business (recently originated) and existing business to evaluate underwriting changes.

NPL ratio

The non-performing-loan ratio: Stage 3 ECL over total balance, or alternatively Stage 3 gross balance over total balance:

NPL ratio  =  Stage 3 ECL  /  Balance        (provision-based variant)
       or  =  Stage 3 gross balance  /  Total gross balance        (exposure-based variant)

The most-watched portfolio-health metric externally. Investors compare NPL ratios across banks; supervisors track it as a risk-appetite indicator; the board sees it monthly. A persistently rising NPL ratio is the most reliable signal of underlying portfolio deterioration.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first lesson is that write-off is operationally less consequential than its name suggests. The temptation is to view write-off as the moment of loss. The accounting reality is that the loss was already recognised when the Stage 3 ECL was raised; the write-off is a balance-sheet contraction with no new P&L impact. The mechanism that smooths bank earnings is therefore not write-off but the gradual recognition of ECL as loans deteriorate through the stages. Practitioners who understand this can explain why a bank's reported earnings reflect credit-cycle conditions a year ahead of when defaults actually crystallise.

The second lesson is on the interest-revenue switch at Stage 3. The drop from gross-based to net-based interest income on impaired loans is one of the framework's most subtle but consequential features. For a large book, the switch can compress reported NII materially in a downturn even before any cash defaults actually occur, just because more loans are sitting in Stage 3. The board-level financial narrative that "the cycle has turned" usually shows up in net interest margin two to three quarters before it shows up in actual writedowns.

The third lesson is on the EIR vs IRB-discount-rate gap. The same loan with the same realised cash flows produces materially different LGDs under IRB downturn (discounted at max(SOFR + 5%, 9%)) and IFRS 9 (discounted at EIR, typically 6 to 12%). For a typical retail credit card with an EIR of 18 to 24%, the IFRS 9 LGD is materially lower than the IRB LGD because high-EIR discounting collapses the present value of late-arriving recoveries. For a typical mortgage with an EIR of 4 to 6%, the IFRS 9 LGD is higher than the IRB LGD for the opposite reason. Practitioners who understand this can explain why credit-card IFRS 9 LGDs are typically below 70% while mortgage IFRS 9 LGDs are typically above their IRB counterparts.

The fourth lesson, structurally most important: the FiT PD is the bridge between the IRB scorecard and the IFRS 9 ECL. The bank's IRB PD scorecard ranks facilities; the FiT PD takes the same scorecard, conditions it on FLI scenarios, and produces multiple PiT PDs per facility (one per scenario, per horizon). The migration-matrix extension to lifetime PD then builds the term structure. The whole IFRS 9 PD machinery is therefore a sequence of transformations applied to the IRB scorecard output; the underlying ranking is the same. Lecture 05 unpacks the transformations; this lecture has defined the vocabulary.
