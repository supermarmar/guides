EAD modelling: amortisation, prepayment, and who holds the option

IFRS 9 never defines exposure at default. It defines a cash flow shortfall, and the exposure is whatever a modeller has to construct to decompose that shortfall into three factors. What the construction has to produce is a term structure, and its direction of travel is set by a single question: which party holds the option to change the balance. Term loans amortise down, revolvers draw up, and in both cases the option is exercised against the bank.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Start from what the standard actually says. IFRS 9 Appendix A defines a credit loss as the difference between all contractual cash flows due and all cash flows expected to be received, discounted at the original effective interest rate. There is no exposure at default anywhere in that definition. The familiar $\text{ECL} = \text{PD} \times \text{LGD} \times \text{EAD}$ is a decomposition of the shortfall, not a definition of it, and it is only correct if the three factors are defined consistently with one another.

What the decomposition needs is an expected exposure at each future default date, which makes EAD a term structure exactly as the PD is. For an amortising term loan that term structure declines twice over: once because the contractual schedule repays principal, and again because some borrowers prepay and leave the book entirely.

For a revolving facility it moves the other way. A credit card's contractual balance is whatever the customer chooses, and customers choose to draw as distress mounts, so the expected exposure at default exceeds the balance observed today. On the figures below a card with a 3,000 balance against a 10,000 limit carries an accounting exposure of 8,500 against a Basel standardised treatment of 3,700, a factor of 2.3.

Three quantified results follow. Prepayment at 10 per cent a year cuts the lifetime ECL on a five-year amortising loan by 12.7 per cent. Almost none of that comes from the discount rate, which moves only 10 basis points and 0.2 per cent of the provision. And roughly a third of the benefit is an artefact, because prepayment is credit-selective: the borrowers who refinance away are the ones who can, so the surviving pool is worse than the pool you started with.

Feynman test How much will we be owed at the moment things go wrong? On a mortgage, less than today, because they have been paying it down and some will have moved. On a credit card, more than today, because someone in trouble spends the rest of their limit first.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the exposure is the only one of the three factors that a bank can compute almost exactly and still get wrong. A contractual amortisation schedule is arithmetic, not estimation, and yet the exposure profile that goes into the ECL calculation depends on behavioural assumptions layered on top of the arithmetic. Getting the arithmetic right creates a false sense that the parameter is not a model.

The second reason is that the direction of the behavioural adjustment is not neutral. Prepayment reduces the provision and drawdown increases it, and the products where prepayment dominates are the large secured books that carry most of the balance sheet. So the aggregate effect of behavioural modelling on a retail bank's provision is downward, which means the assumptions that reduce it are the ones requiring the most evidence and receiving the least challenge.

The third reason is that the IFRS 9 and regulatory exposures diverge most sharply here, more sharply than for PD or LGD, and the divergence is structural rather than calibrational. The Basel standardised framework applies a 10 per cent conversion factor to an unconditionally cancellable commitment because the bank has the contractual right to cancel. IFRS 9 paragraph 5.5.20 requires measurement over the period the entity is exposed to credit risk where the loss is not mitigated by credit risk management actions, and a bank that has not yet noticed the deterioration has not exercised the right. The two numbers answer different questions and both answers are correct.

And a fourth reason particular to this note. The wiki source for this topic is sixteen lines and covers only pipeline accounts. It also asserts that the pipeline is such a small component of ECL that it is not material, and that claim is tested numerically in step 4. It survives a test of scale and fails a test of mechanism, which is a useful distinction to be able to draw.

The mental model

Two product families, two exposure profiles pointing in opposite directions, and one question that determines which you are looking at.

Step 4

The detail

The amortising profile, and the prepayment overlay

The contractual exposure at the start of period $t$ is the outstanding balance from the amortisation schedule, which for a level-payment loan of principal $B_0$ at rate $r$ over $n$ periods with payment $A$ satisfies $B_t = B_{t-1}(1+r) - A$. The behavioural overlay is a constant prepayment rate, so the expected exposure multiplies the contractual balance by the probability that the account has not prepaid: $\mathbb{E}[\text{EAD}_t] = B_{t-1}(1 - \text{CPR})^{t-1}$.

NOTICE WHERE THE ECL IS CONCENTRATED Year 2 is the largest contribution in both columns, because the marginal PD peaks at seasoning while the exposure is still near its opening level. The product of a rising-then-falling PD and a monotonically falling exposure is more front-loaded than either factor alone. That matters for staging: the ECL a stage transition releases or captures is concentrated in the first two or three years even on a long-dated loan.

The second channel: prepayment changes the effective interest rate

Prepayment enters the calculation twice, and this is the part most easily missed. IFRS 9 defines the effective interest rate using estimated future cash flows, and paragraph B5.4.1 requires fees, points, transaction costs, premiums and discounts to be amortised over the expected life of the instrument. So if an instrument was originated with an upfront fee, a prepayment assumption shortens the expected life, spreads the fee over fewer periods, and raises the effective interest rate. A higher effective interest rate discounts the ECL more heavily, reducing the provision a second time.

It is worth knowing how large that second channel is, because the answer is surprising.

THE DISCOUNT-RATE CHANNEL, MEASURED SAME LOAN, PLUS AN ORIGINATION FEE OF 2,000 RECEIVED initial net carrying amount = 100,000 - 2,000 = 98,000 EFFECTIVE INTEREST RATE, no prepayment assumed solve 98,000 = sum of 23,739.64 discounted over 5 yrs EIR = 6.7471% EFFECTIVE INTEREST RATE, 10% CPR assumed expected cash flows shorten and thicken: 31,965.68 / 27,076.75 / 22,754.56 / 18,938.86 / 15,575.58 EIR = 6.8494% uplift = 10.2 basis points EFFECT ON THE PROVISION PV of ECL at 6.7471% 612.41 PV of ECL at 6.8494% 611.15 difference -1.26, i.e. -0.21% THE HONEST CONCLUSION The channel is real and it is second order. Ten basis points on the discount rate moves the provision by two tenths of one per cent, against 12.7% for the exposure channel. If you have limited challenge time, spend it on the CPR's effect on the exposure profile and not on its effect on the EIR. The exception is a book originated at a material discount or premium, for instance an acquired portfolio, where the amount being amortised is a large fraction of the carrying amount rather than 2%. There the EIR channel scales up proportionately and the ordering can reverse.

Revolving facilities, and the undrawn commitment

For an undrawn commitment IFRS 9 paragraph B5.5.30 measures the expected credit loss as the present value of the difference between the contractual cash flows due if the holder draws down and the cash flows expected if it is drawn, and B5.5.31 requires the estimate of expected drawdowns to be consistent with past experience and expectations. So the modelling task is precisely a conversion factor, and it is the same object the regulatory framework uses with a different calibration target.

A CREDIT CARD, THREE ANSWERS TO ONE QUESTION FACTS limit 10,000, drawn 3,000, undrawn 7,000 observed utilisation at default on this book: 85% BASEL III STANDARDISED, unconditionally cancellable exposure = 3,000 + 10% x 7,000 = 3,700 BASEL III STANDARDISED, other commitment, 40% CCF exposure = 3,000 + 40% x 7,000 = 5,800 IFRS 9 BEHAVIOURAL, 85% utilisation at default exposure = 85% x 10,000 = 8,500 implied conversion factor on the undrawn = 78.6% ratio to the 10% treatment = 2.30x WHY ALL THREE ARE DEFENSIBLE The 10% reflects the bank's contractual RIGHT to cancel. The 78.6% reflects the bank's observed FAILURE to exercise that right before the customer had already drawn. Paragraph 5.5.20 asks for the period over which the entity is exposed and where the loss would not be mitigated by credit risk management action, which is a question about what the bank does, not what it may do. THE TRAP A bank that argues its cancellation rights justify a low conversion factor is asserting an operational capability. That assertion is testable against the utilisation data it already holds, and on most card books the data contradict it.

Pipeline accounts, and testing the wiki's materiality claim

The source's whole treatment concerns pipeline accounts, meaning facilities offered and committed but not yet drawn. A conversion factor is applied as a multiplier and it declines as months since offer increase, starting around 80 to 90 per cent and reaching zero after about six months, with a schedule such as 90, 70, 50, 30, 10 and 0.01 per cent. The logic is survivorship: an offer still undrawn after four months is unlikely ever to draw.

The note then asserts that the pipeline is usually so small a component of ECL that it is not material. That is a testable claim and it is worth testing rather than repeating.

DOES THE PIPELINE MATTER? TWO TESTS SETUP 6,000 offers outstanding, average 150,000, spread evenly across the six months-since-offer buckets on-book portfolio 20bn with a 100m provision twelve-month PD 0.8%, LGD 25%, so a 20bp loss rate TEST 1, SCALE offers outstanding 900m expected drawdown at the CCF schedule 375m effective average CCF 41.7% twelve-month ECL on the pipeline 0.75m as a share of the provision 0.75% Even concentrating the whole pipeline in the newest bucket at a 90% CCF gives 810m of expected drawdown and 1.62m of ECL, or 1.6% of the provision. VERDICT: the claim survives. The pipeline cannot move the provision by a material amount at any plausible scale. TEST 2, MECHANISM The CCF schedule declines with age because offers that have not drawn are progressively less likely to draw. That empirical relationship was estimated over some historical rate environment. Now suppose rates have risen 200bp since those offers were made. The offer is a rate lock and the lock is now valuable, so conversion on OLD offers rises rather than falls. The declining schedule is not merely imprecise, it has the wrong sign, and it errs in the direction of understating exposure. VERDICT: the claim fails. The schedule is conditional on a rate path that is never stated, and its error is directional and adverse. RECONCILING THE TWO A parameter can be immaterial to the provision and still be wrong in a way worth documenting, because the same schedule is used for volume forecasting and capital planning where the exposure itself is the answer rather than an input to a 20bp loss rate.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The prepayment calculation in step 4 is wrong, and by a third

Scaling the contractual balance by a prepayment survival probability while leaving the PD term structure untouched contains an assumption that is stated nowhere: that prepayment is independent of credit quality. It is not, and the mechanism is not subtle. Refinancing elsewhere requires qualifying elsewhere, so the borrowers who prepay are disproportionately the creditworthy ones. The pool that survives is worse than the pool that was measured.

PREPAYMENT IS CREDIT-SELECTIVE, QUANTIFIED SPLIT THE POOL, PRESERVING BOTH AVERAGES good 70% of accounts, PD multiplier 0.5000, CPR 13% bad 30% of accounts, PD multiplier 2.1667, CPR 3% weighted PD multiplier = 0.7(0.5) + 0.3(2.1667) = 1.0000 blended CPR = 0.7(13%) + 0.3(3%) = 10.0% So this pool is IDENTICAL to step 4's on both averages. Nothing has been made worse by assumption. THE MIX DRIFTS, BECAUSE THE GOOD ONES LEAVE FASTER good share of the surviving pool, by year yr1 70.00% yr2 67.67% yr3 65.24% yr4 62.74% yr5 60.16% ECL, THREE WAYS no prepayment at all 809.80 single pool at 10% CPR (step 4) 706.75 two segments, same averages 742.79 The single-pool figure understates by 36.04, or 5.10%. READ AS A REDUCTION IN THE PROVISION claimed benefit of prepayment 12.73% true benefit 8.28% So 4.45 percentage points of the 12.73 claimed, which is 35% of the benefit, is an artefact of treating prepayment as credit-neutral. WHY THIS IS HARD TO CATCH Both inputs are right. The CPR is 10% and the average PD multiplier is 1.00, so every parameter reconciles to the portfolio average and every back-test on the average passes. The error is in the INDEPENDENCE, which no single-parameter test examines.

The correction has a practical implication beyond the number. A bank that models prepayment at portfolio level and applies the result as a uniform exposure haircut is understating its provision, and the understatement grows with the horizon because the mix drift compounds. Segmenting the CPR by the same risk grades the PD model already uses fixes it at essentially no cost, since both models sit on the same dataset.

The covariance discipline extends to the exposure, at a tenth of the size

Lecture 6 established that averaging PD and LGD across scenarios and then multiplying understates ECL by 8.1 per cent, because the two are positively correlated across scenarios. The same logic applies to the exposure, since a downside scenario draws revolvers harder. It is worth measuring rather than assuming, and the measurement changes where effort should go.

THE SAME CARD, THREE SCENARIOS scenario weight utilisation EAD PD upside 15% 78% 7,800 2.0% base 60% 85% 8,500 3.0% downside 25% 92% 9,200 8.0% ROUTE 1, AVERAGE THEN MULTIPLY E[PD] = 4.100% E[EAD] = 8,570 product = 351.37 ROUTE 2, MULTIPLY THEN AVERAGE E[PD x EAD] = 360.40 UNDERSTATEMENT 2.57% COMPARE WITH THE LGD RESULT FROM LECTURE 6: 8.10% WHY THE EAD TERM IS SMALLER Utilisation at default varies from 78% to 92% across scenarios, a range of 18% of the base. LGD varied from 34.5% to 53.8%, a range of 47% of the base. The covariance term scales with the dispersion of the factor, and the exposure is the least scenario-sensitive of the three because it is anchored to a contractual limit. THE ALLOCATION THIS IMPLIES All three covariance terms are positive, so ignoring them always understates. But if you are choosing where to spend validation effort, the ordering is LGD first at 8.1%, exposure second at 2.6%. Say so with numbers rather than treating them as equally important.

The exposure is the factor where the accounting and the regulation genuinely disagree

Across the IFRS 9 track the differences from A-IRB have mostly been differences of calibration on a shared concept. The PD differs by the width of a reference period. The LGD differs by a downturn condition, a cost definition and a discount rate, all adjustments to one quantity. The exposure is different in kind: the standardised 10 per cent conversion factor and the 78.6 per cent behavioural factor are not two estimates of the same number with different conservatism, they are answers to two different questions.

The regulatory question is what the bank is contractually committed to advance, because capital must cover an obligation the bank cannot walk away from. The accounting question is what the bank expects to be owed, because the provision must reflect an expectation. A bank with excellent early-warning systems and fast cancellation genuinely has a lower accounting exposure and the same regulatory one, and that is not an inconsistency to be reconciled away.

Which is worth carrying into a validation meeting for a specific reason. The instinct when two numbers for the same-sounding parameter differ by a factor of two is to look for an error. Here there is none, and the right response is to be able to name the two questions crisply and show that each number answers its own. The pattern recurred in the stress testing and IRRBB tracks, where a regulatory constraint and an internal estimate of the same exposure sat side by side without either being wrong.

The pattern this shares with lecture 6 Three errors in this lecture and the last live in an assumption of independence that no parameter model states: prepayment independent of credit quality, LGD independent of PD across scenarios, exposure independent of PD across scenarios. Each parameter reconciles perfectly to its own average. Each product does not. A validation framework organised model by model cannot find any of them.
Where this goes wrong in practice The most common implementation shortcut is to hold the exposure flat at the current balance for the whole lifetime, on the grounds that it is conservative. On an amortising book it is conservative and it also destroys the term structure, which means the staging transfer between stage 1 and stage 2 releases the wrong amount and the sensitivity to the scenario weights disappears. Conservative and uninformative is not a safe combination when the number has to be explained to an audit committee.

Test yourself

  1. State the IFRS 9 definition of a credit loss. Where does exposure at default appear in it?
  2. Why is the three-factor product a decomposition rather than a definition, and what condition must hold for it to be correct?
  3. Write the recursion for the contractual balance of a level-payment loan and the expected exposure under a constant prepayment rate.
  4. Do the arithmetic. Reproduce the balance schedule for 100,000 at 6 per cent over five annual payments, then compute the lifetime ECL with and without a 10 per cent CPR.
  5. Why is the ECL concentrated in the early years even on a long-dated loan, and what does that imply for staging transfers?
  6. Explain the two distinct channels through which a prepayment assumption reduces the provision.
  7. Do the arithmetic. With a 2,000 origination fee, the effective interest rate rises 10 basis points once prepayment is assumed. How much of the provision does that move, and when would the ordering of the two channels reverse?
  8. Contrast paragraphs 5.5.19 and 5.5.20. Which products does the second one exist for, and why is the first insufficient for them?
  9. Do the arithmetic. A card has a 10,000 limit, 3,000 drawn, and 85 per cent utilisation at default. Give the Basel standardised exposure under both conversion factors and the IFRS 9 exposure, and state the implied conversion factor on the undrawn.
  10. A bank argues its cancellation rights justify a low conversion factor. What is it asserting, and how would you test it?
  11. Describe the pipeline CCF schedule and the survivorship logic behind its decline.
  12. Do the arithmetic. Test the materiality of the pipeline at scale, then explain the mechanism test that the same schedule fails and which direction the error runs in.
  13. Explain why prepayment is credit-selective and in which direction that biases a portfolio-level CPR.
  14. Do the arithmetic. Using step 5's two segments, show that both portfolio averages are preserved, then compute the ECL and the share of the claimed prepayment benefit that is an artefact.
  15. Why does no single-parameter back-test detect the adverse-selection error?
  16. Compute the scenario-product understatement for the exposure and explain why it is smaller than the LGD one.
  17. Explain why the standardised 10 per cent factor and the behavioural 78.6 per cent factor are not two estimates of the same quantity.
  18. What is wrong with holding the exposure flat at the current balance, given that it is conservative?
