The IFRS 9 process: eight stages, monthly cadence, audit interface

A roadmap of the rest of this track. The eight stages of an IFRS 9 model lifecycle, the monthly cadence at which they run in production, the use-test applications that consume the ECL outputs, and the audit relationship that distinguishes IFRS 9 from the supervisor-facing IRB regime.

Step 1

The 30-second version

An IFRS 9 ECL model is built once and operates at a monthly cadence thereafter. The lifecycle has eight stages, parallel to the A-IRB nine-stage lifecycle but with two IFRS 9-specific additions (FLI and SICR) and one IFRS 9-specific subtraction (no MoC or output-floor mechanism). The eight stages: data engineering, portfolio description, feature engineering for the five parameters (PD, LGD, EAD, FLI, SICR), modelling per parameter, testing, ECL assembly, and ongoing monitoring. Each parameter then plugs into the ECL formula from lecture 01 to produce per-facility provision numbers that the finance team books in the income statement and balance sheet.

Two structural features distinguish the IFRS 9 lifecycle from the A-IRB lifecycle. First, the monthly cadence: where A-IRB runs the test battery quarterly and recalibrates annually, IFRS 9 reruns the ECL calculation every month-end and feeds the result into the bank's financial statements. The monthly drumbeat is what makes IFRS 9 a finance-function discipline as much as a risk-modelling discipline. Second, the audit interface: where IRB outputs are inspected by the prudential supervisor, IFRS 9 outputs are inspected by the bank's external auditors as part of the financial statement audit. The audit relationship has different acceptance criteria (best-estimate-with-explainable-judgement vs supervisor-acceptable-conservatism) and different escalation pathways (audit qualification vs supervisory remediation).

The use-test applications are broader for IFRS 9 than for IRB. Beyond the direct use as the provisioning input, IFRS 9 outputs feed planning, stress testing, portfolio management, risk-adjusted pricing, RoRWA-equivalent calculations, and external reporting. The accounting nature of IFRS 9 means the outputs are scrutinised by a wider stakeholder group (auditors, investors, the board's Audit Committee) than the IRB-only ones. The lecture sequence that follows builds out each stage; this lecture is the map of where each subsequent lecture sits in the overall lifecycle.

Feynman test The A-IRB lifecycle in lecture 4 was the regulator's process: build the model, get supervisory approval, recalibrate annually, monitor quarterly. The IFRS 9 lifecycle is the accountant's process: build the model, get audit comfort, recompute every month, report every quarter. The same underlying risk models feed both; the operating cadence, the assurance interface, and the consequences of failure differ. A practitioner who understands both can articulate why a single risk-modelling team often produces outputs for two distinct downstream functions.
Step 2

Why this matters

The monthly cadence is the operational headline. IFRS 9 ECL feeds the income statement; the income statement is published quarterly (in most jurisdictions) and audited annually. The bank therefore needs an ECL number at every month-end, recomputed on the latest portfolio composition and the latest macroeconomic scenarios. The infrastructure needed to support a monthly drumbeat is materially different from the infrastructure that supports A-IRB's quarterly cadence: automated data feeds, automated scenario refresh, automated model execution, automated downstream booking. Banks that have invested in this infrastructure produce a credible monthly number; those that have not produce a monthly number with manual overrides that the auditor flags at year-end.

The audit interface is the second consequential feature. The external auditors form an opinion on whether the IFRS 9 ECL is materially correct as part of the financial-statement audit. Their inspection covers: the model methodology (does it produce unbiased best-estimate outputs?), the FLI scenario design (are the scenarios reasonable and supportable?), the SICR criteria (are they applied consistently?), the data quality (are the inputs auditable?), the governance (is the process well-controlled?), and the disclosures (are the notes to the accounts informative?). The audit relationship has a different rhythm from the supervisory relationship: quarterly interim reviews, deep-dive year-end audit, audit committee challenge, and the possibility of an audit qualification that the supervisor would not impose. The audit-readiness work is parallel to but distinct from the supervisor-readiness work that A-IRB requires.

For F107 the IFRS 9 process is testable as the operational counterpart to the A-IRB lifecycle. Candidates who can articulate the differences in cadence, assurance interface, and use cases score well; candidates who treat IFRS 9 as "A-IRB with different formulas" miss the operational and governance distinctions that make IFRS 9 its own discipline.

Step 3

The mental model

The eight-stage IFRS 9 lifecycle maps closely onto the nine-stage A-IRB lifecycle from lecture 04, with the two IFRS 9-specific stages (FLI and SICR modelling) replacing the A-IRB-specific MoC and RWA stages.

Three properties of the lifecycle to internalise. First, FLI and SICR are first-class modelling stages in IFRS 9, not bolt-ons. Each has its own feature engineering, model fitting and testing stage, parallel to PD / LGD / EAD. The lecture sequence in this track gives FLI (lecture 08) and SICR (lecture 09) their own dedicated lectures for the same reason. Second, the ECL assembly is its own stage (Block 3), distinct from the parameter modelling. The ECL formula combines the five parameters via the lecture-01 equation; lecture 10 of this track unpacks the assembly. Third, the monitoring stage interfaces with the external audit, not just the model risk committee. The audit relationship runs in parallel to the internal validation function and operates on different acceptance criteria.

The five parameter pipelines

Block 2 of the lifecycle runs five parameter pipelines in parallel:

  • PD (lecture 05): FiT PD construction. Starts with the IRB PiT scorecard from lecture 13; applies the Vasicek TTC-to-SysPiT mapping to condition on FLI scenarios; extends to lifetime via migration matrices.
  • LGD (lecture 06): IFRS 9 LGD. Same workout-LGD machinery as IRB LGD, but with direct costs only (no indirect), no downturn add-on, multi-scenario FLI conditioning, EIR discount rate.
  • EAD (lecture 07): IFRS 9 EAD. Same EADF target as IRB but with lifetime amortisation, repayments, prepayments included. FLI overlay rather than max-of-paths downturn.
  • FLI (lecture 08): forward-looking information. Construction of macro scenarios (base, upside, downside), MEV time-paths, scenario weights. Conditions PD / LGD / EAD on the scenarios.
  • SICR (lecture 09): significant increase in credit risk. The trigger for Stage 1 → 2 transitions. Quantitative (PD-relative thresholds), qualitative (watch-list, forbearance), and the 30-DPD backstop from lecture 02.

The monthly cadence

The IFRS 9 lifecycle's operational cadence is a monthly drumbeat. Each month-end, the bank:

  1. Refreshes the data (MRD update with the latest portfolio composition and balance information).
  2. Refreshes the macroeconomic forecasts (FLI scenarios are typically updated quarterly but the weights may be adjusted monthly).
  3. Reruns the SICR assessment for every facility, producing stage assignments.
  4. Reruns the PD / LGD / EAD models on the refreshed data.
  5. Aggregates per-facility ECL across stages and scenarios.
  6. Compares the new ECL to the prior month's allowance; the difference flows through P&L as impairment expense (increase) or release (decrease).
  7. Books the new allowance to the balance sheet, reducing the gross carrying amount.

The monthly cycle takes typically 5 to 10 working days after month-end to complete. The first half of the cycle is automated (data refresh, model execution); the second half is governed (model risk committee review, finance team booking, audit interim review). Banks that have invested in automation produce a credible monthly ECL within a week; banks that have not produce a number with manual workarounds that the auditor flags.

The audit interface

External auditors form an opinion on the IFRS 9 ECL as part of the financial-statement audit. Three substantive interactions:

  • Interim reviews (quarterly): the audit team conducts limited-scope reviews of the ECL methodology, model outputs and key judgements at quarterly reporting dates.
  • Year-end audit (annual): deep-dive on the methodology, governance, FLI scenario design, SICR criteria, data quality and provisioning. The most substantive interaction; typically running 2 to 3 months between fieldwork and final sign-off.
  • Audit committee challenge: the board's Audit Committee (independent non-executive directors) challenges the management's IFRS 9 judgements based on the audit team's findings and management's representations. Material disagreements escalate to potential audit qualification.

The audit acceptance criteria differ from supervisory criteria. The auditor focuses on best-estimate accuracy ("is the ECL number unbiased?") and reasonable judgement ("would another competent auditor reach the same conclusion?"). The supervisor focuses on conservatism ("is the IRB number prudent enough?") and methodology robustness ("does the model meet regulatory minimum standards?"). A model that satisfies both is the dual-purpose IRB-and-IFRS-9 pipeline; the post-processing differences (as covered in lectures 04 to 12) are what separate the outputs for the two audiences.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first lesson is that the monthly cadence determines the bank's IFRS 9 infrastructure investment. A bank that produces a credible monthly ECL with automation has invested £20-50m in data infrastructure, model execution pipelines, scenario refresh automation, and audit-ready evidence registers. A bank that produces a monthly ECL with manual overrides has not made the investment and is one auditor finding away from a qualification. The IFRS 9 lifecycle is therefore as much an infrastructure-investment question as a modelling question.

The second lesson is on the audit relationship. The external auditor operates on different acceptance criteria from the supervisor and brings different incentives. The auditor is paid by the bank but reports to shareholders; the supervisor reports to government and protects depositors. The two interfaces sometimes give conflicting feedback on the same model: the supervisor wants more conservatism (higher IRB capital); the auditor wants less conservatism (lower IFRS 9 provisions, because over-provisioning understates earnings). A practitioner who can articulate both perspectives can serve both audiences; one who tries to use the same outputs for both ends up in conflict with whichever audience they did not optimise for.

The third lesson is on FLI and SICR as first-class modelling stages. The A-IRB lifecycle has three parameter pipelines (PD, LGD, EAD). The IFRS 9 lifecycle has five because FLI and SICR are not derived from PD / LGD / EAD; they are standalone models with their own data, features, modelling and testing. Practitioners new to IFRS 9 sometimes treat FLI as "the macro overlay" and SICR as "the stage trigger" without recognising that each requires the full modelling discipline. Lectures 08 and 09 of this track unpack the modelling.

The fourth lesson, structurally most important: the IFRS 9 lifecycle is the accounting team's IRB-equivalent. A bank that runs A-IRB has a complete risk-modelling discipline; a bank that runs IFRS 9 has an equally complete accounting-impairment discipline; a bank that runs both (most large banks) has two parallel disciplines with shared upstream inputs and diverging downstream outputs. The 12 lectures of this IFRS 9 track build the second discipline alongside the 32 lectures of A-IRB; reading both gives the practitioner the full picture of how a modern bank's credit-risk-modelling function operates.
