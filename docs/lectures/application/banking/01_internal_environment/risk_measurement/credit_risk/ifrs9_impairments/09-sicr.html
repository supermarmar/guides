SICR: the threshold, the target, and the outcome period nobody argues about

The largest single judgement in IFRS 9, and the standard prescribes no method for it. Two families of approach exist: compare the probability of default now against origination, or model the deterioration event directly. The second predicts better and quietly changes what is being predicted. And in both families the parameter that determines the size of the stage 2 book turns out not to be the threshold everyone debates.

IFRS 9 9 of 12 Reading time, 45 minutes Sources: docs/wiki/.../ifrs9_impairments/05_modelling/sicr/01-model_methodology.md, IFRS 9 paragraphs 5.5.4, 5.5.9 to 5.5.11, B5.5.2 and B5.5.17
Step 1

The 30-second version

Stage 2 is where lifetime expected credit loss starts being recognised, so the test that moves an account into it decides how much provision a bank holds. IFRS 9 paragraph 5.5.9 defines the test as a significant increase in the risk of a default occurring since initial recognition, and then prescribes no method, no metric and no threshold. Paragraph 5.5.11 adds a rebuttable presumption at thirty days past due, which is a backstop rather than a definition.

The direct reading of 5.5.9 is the PD comparison approach: compute a residual lifetime PD at origination and at the reporting date, form either the difference or the ratio, and compare against a threshold $u$. Migration to stage 2 is then the indicator $H(m,u) = [m > u]$, and migration back when the magnitude falls below $u$ again.

The alternative is to model the deterioration event directly. Define a delinquency measure, define a SICR event as that measure breaching a threshold $d$ for $s$ consecutive months, look forward $k$ months, and fit a classifier to predict it. The source's research programme finds the PD ratio statistically insignificant in such a model, and calls this a rebuttal of the intuition behind 5.5.9. Step 5 argues the finding is real and the conclusion overstated, for a reason about targets rather than about statistics.

Three computed results frame the whole thing. A relative and an absolute threshold cross at exactly $u_{\text{abs}}/(u_{\text{rel}}-1)$, which for a doubling rule with a two-point floor is a PD of 2.00 per cent, so the two rules bind on opposite halves of the book. A fixed roll-rate quota understates the provision by 25 per cent in a downturn and by nothing at all in benign conditions, which inverts exactly the behaviour the standard was written to produce. And holding the cost ratio fixed at six to one, the flagged share of the portfolio moves from 2.8 per cent to 68 per cent as the SICR prevalence moves from 3 to 30 per cent, which means the outcome period $k$ governs the size of the stage 2 book far more forcefully than the cost ratio or the threshold.

Feynman test Has this borrower got noticeably riskier since we lent to them? Nobody will tell you how much riskier counts, or how to measure riskier, so you choose both, and your choice sets how much money the bank sets aside.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is leverage. Every other model in this track estimates a quantity; this one operates a switch. An account on the stage 1 side of the boundary carries twelve-month expected loss and on the stage 2 side carries lifetime expected loss, and on a long-dated amortising product those differ by a factor of five or six. So the staging test multiplies a large number by a binary decision, and the decision rests on a threshold the standard declines to set.

The second reason is that the resulting variability is acknowledged rather than hidden. The source records that the PRA observed multiple threshold values in use across UK banks and even across portfolios within a bank, and was unsurprised, attributing the spread to genuine differences in risk appetite, strategy and portfolio composition. That is a supervisor saying the dispersion is legitimate. It is also a supervisor saying that two banks with identical books can hold materially different provisions and both be compliant.

The third reason is that the failure modes are asymmetric and the source is explicit about it. A false negative means the bank has not raised its provision on an account whose risk is genuinely rising, which is the failure the standard exists to prevent. A false positive means an unnecessarily large provision, which is costly but not a compliance breach. The research programme the note follows assigns costs of six and one accordingly, and step 4 shows what that ratio does and, more importantly, what it does not control.

And a fourth reason worth stating plainly. This is the component where a modelling choice most directly changes reported profit, because the stage 2 population is both large and discretionary. That makes it the component where the discipline of writing down the choice and its consequence matters most, and where an unexamined default inherited from a vendor or a peer benchmark does the most damage.

The mental model

One switch, two routes to operating it, and four parameters of which the last is the one that matters.

Step 4

The detail

The PD comparison approach, stated precisely

Write $p_t(x, t')$ for the $t$-month PD given risk information $x$ observed at time $t'$. Paragraph 5.5.9 compares the risk of default over the remaining life at the reporting date $t'_r$ against the risk over that same remaining period as assessed at initial recognition $t'_i$. The residual qualifier matters: comparing a twenty-year lifetime PD at origination against a nineteen-year lifetime PD today would confuse the passage of time with deterioration. A twelve-month PD is a permitted proxy only where changes in it reasonably approximate changes in the lifetime PD.

The magnitude $m(x, t'_r)$ is either the difference or the ratio, and the migration rule is the indicator $H(m,u) = [m > u]$. The transfer is symmetric: an account returns to stage 1 once $m$ falls back below $u$, which the source notes is not merely permitted but cost-efficient, since over-eager stage 2 classification is expensive even when it is prudent.

Two difficulties follow immediately and the source names both. Selecting $u$ is subjective and unguided, and the note reports that the EBA specifies 200 per cent without explanation while the PRA has observed multiple values across UK banks and across portfolios. Separately, any reliance on a point-estimate PD imports the accuracy of two PD models, one of which may have been superseded since the account was written.

THE CROSSOVER, DERIVED A RELATIVE RULE flags when PD_r > PD_o x u_rel AN ABSOLUTE RULE flags when PD_r > PD_o + u_abs These coincide when PD_o x u_rel = PD_o + u_abs, so PD_o = u_abs / (u_rel - 1) With a doubling rule and a 2pp floor: PD_o = 2.00%. BELOW the crossover the relative rule binds and is the stricter test. ABOVE it the absolute rule binds. So the two rules are not alternatives competing on the same ground; each governs one half of the book. Under the absolute rule, grade A needs a twenty-one-fold deterioration. Each rule has a blind spot and they are at opposite ends. WHICH IS WHY 'COMBINATION' IS NOT A COMPROMISE It is a repair. A relative rule with an absolute FLOOR suppresses trivial flags at the low end. A relative rule with an absolute BACKSTOP catches the high end the ratio cannot reach. These fix different failures and a bank that needs both should say so rather than describing the combination as a balanced choice.

Why a relative rule cannot stand alone, in one identity

There is a second and stronger argument for the absolute floor, and it is about estimation rather than economics. The relative standard error of a default-rate estimate from $n$ observations is $\sqrt{(1-p)/np}$, which grows without bound as $p$ falls. So the same relative move means something quite different at the two ends of the grade scale.

RELATIVE NOISE BY PD LEVEL relative standard error = sqrt((1 - p) / (n p)) p n=1,000 n=10,000 n=100,000 0.10% 99.9% 31.6% 10.0% 0.50% 44.6% 14.1% 4.5% 2.00% 22.1% 7.0% 2.2% 8.00% 10.7% 3.4% 1.1% The relative noise at p = 0.10% is 9.3 times that at p = 8.00%, for any given n. WHAT THAT DOES TO A 2x TRIGGER A doubling is a +100% relative move, so the number of relative standard errors it represents is 1 / rse. at n = 5,000 p = 0.10% a doubling is 2.24 rse false flag 1.26% p = 8.00% a doubling is 20.85 rse false flag ~0% So a uniform relative threshold asks a materially weaker statistical question of the best grades than of the worst. On a large retail portfolio the best grades hold most of the accounts, so a relative rule without a floor generates a stage 2 population substantially composed of estimation noise. THIS IS NOT AN ARGUMENT AGAINST RELATIVE THRESHOLDS It is an argument that the floor is structural rather than cosmetic. The floor should be set from the estimation error of the PD model at the relevant PD level, which is computable, rather than from a round number, which is what it usually is.

Forward roll rates, and the drawback that matters

A common alternative sets $u$ so that the flagged population is a predetermined percentage of the portfolio, taken from the historical transition rate into thirty days past due. If ten of a hundred current accounts rolled into the one-to-thirty-day bucket over the last twelve months, the roll rate is ten per cent and ten per cent of accounts are migrated. Which ten per cent is then decided on other evidence, and the source gives a genuinely useful example: a borrower under strain misses the credit card payment before the mortgage payment, because the mortgage secures the house, so credit card delinquency should trigger SICR on the mortgage.

The source lists three drawbacks: the roll rate calculation is sensitive to the length and recency of the sampling window, the fixed percentage presumes the delinquent proportion stays static, and stakeholders disagree about adjusting the percentage across scenarios. The second is not merely a limitation. It reverses the behaviour the standard was designed to produce.

Its error is zero in the state where accuracy is cheap and largest in the state where it is expensive. AND NOTICE WHAT THE PROVISION DOES Under the quota the provision is 0.00375 in all three states. It does not move at all. A framework whose entire purpose is to raise provisions before losses arrive has been configured to hold them constant through a downturn. THE DIAGNOSTIC Plot the flagged share of the portfolio against the observed default rate over several years. Under a correctly specified SICR rule the two move together and the flagged share leads. Under a quota the flagged share is a horizontal line. That plot takes an afternoon and it is not a standard validation exhibit.

The direct modelling route: the delinquency measure and three parameters

The second family abandons the two-point PD comparison and models deterioration directly. The measure is $g_0$, the unweighted number of payments in arrears, constructed from days past due and chosen for intuitive appeal and ubiquity. A preliminary SICR event occurs at time $t$ if $g_0(v) \geq d$ holds across the whole window $v \in [t-(s-1), t]$, which the source formalises with Iverson brackets as the Boolean function $G(d,s,t)$. The parameter $s$ is stickiness: it smooths away rapid flip-flopping in the SICR status, and larger values make the flag harder to acquire and harder to lose.

To turn that into a prediction target, the process $Z_t(d,s,k) = G(d,s,t+k)$ evaluates the event $k$ months ahead and assigns the answer back to time $t$, giving a cross-sectional dataset of present-day features against a future SICR outcome. Varying $(d,s,k)$ over $d \in \{1,2\}$, $s \in \{1,2,3\}$ and $k \in \{3,6,9,12\}$ generates twenty-four candidate definitions, each of which can be a target for a separate model.

Three feature families enter: account-level information such as repayment method, macroeconomic information such as the prevailing inflation rate, and time-dependent behavioural information such as time spent in a performing spell or the PD ratio itself. The technique is binary logistic regression, and the resulting probability is dichotomised at a cut-off $c$ chosen from the misclassification costs.

The cut-off, and the thing the cost ratio does not control

Assigning six to a false negative and one to a false positive is a defensible expression of the asymmetry, and the source finds the cut-off from the generalised Youden index given that ratio. It is worth deriving the optimum from first principles, because doing so reveals a dependence the ratio alone conceals.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The insignificant PD ratio: the finding is real and the conclusion is too strong

The source's most striking claim is worth quoting in substance. The variable capturing the change in lifetime PD since initial recognition, which is the quantity paragraph 5.5.9 names, turns out statistically insignificant across all SICR definitions tested, and the note reads this as rebutting the intuition behind 5.5.9 on the grounds that the broader input space already captures whatever information it holds.

Take the finding seriously first, because it is a real result and it is uncomfortable in a useful way. If a richer feature set predicts future deterioration better and more parsimoniously than the PD ratio, then a bank building the PD comparison approach is doing more work for a worse prediction, and paragraph 5.5.4's permission to use all reasonable and supportable information gives it every right to use the better model. The note is also honest about the mechanism, observing that the PD model's input space is a subset of the SICR model's, so redundancy is expected rather than surprising.

Two objections, and the second is the substantive one.

The statistical objection is narrow. Insignificance among collinear predictors establishes that the incremental information is small given the rest, not that the variable is uninformative. That is what the note itself says, so describing the same result as a rebuttal of 5.5.9 is a stronger claim than the evidence carries. Redundancy given a richer model and absence of signal are different findings.

The substantive objection is that the comparison is not measuring what it appears to. Paragraph 5.5.9 is definitional, not predictive. It specifies which quantity constitutes a significant increase in credit risk; it does not hypothesise that this quantity best predicts something else. The direct modelling route defines the target as $G(d,s,t+k)$, a delinquency event, and then finds that features built to predict delinquency beat a feature built to predict default. That ordering is close to guaranteed, and it would be guaranteed if the comparison were run the other way: score both approaches against a twelve-month default outcome rather than against a delinquency outcome and the ranking may well reverse. The note does not report that test, and it is the test that would settle the question.

What is actually going on The direct route substitutes the target. A delinquency event at $t+k$ is an operationalisation of deterioration, and a defensible one, since 5.5.11's thirty-day presumption is itself delinquency-based and B5.5.2 states that credit risk generally increases before an asset becomes past due. But an operationalisation that predicts itself better than a rival predicts it is not evidence about the standard. It is evidence that the target was chosen to suit one of the candidates.

Where that leaves a practitioner is more constructive than it sounds. Both routes are legitimate, and the honest framing is that they answer different questions: the PD comparison implements 5.5.9 literally and pays for it in accuracy, while the direct model predicts observable deterioration accurately and pays for it in the need to argue that its target is a faithful proxy for the standard's quantity. Neither argument is free, and a model document that presents only the accuracy comparison has made the harder argument disappear rather than won it.

The outcome period contaminates the target, and the source's own heuristic agrees

There is an independent reason to bound $k$, and it follows from the definitions rather than from any dataset. If SICR is $g_0 \geq 1$ and default is $g_0 \geq 3$, then every account that defaults by $t+k$ has necessarily passed through the SICR condition. So the SICR flag at horizon $k$ is bounded below by the default rate at horizon $k$, and as $k$ grows the flag increasingly identifies accounts that have already defaulted rather than accounts about to deteriorate.

THE SOURCE REACHES THE SAME PLACE BY A DIFFERENT ROUTE It disqualifies k > 24 because those SICR rates fail to track the twelve-month default rate, and notes that k >= 18 adds little to the SICR mean while increasingly selecting default instances. It disqualifies k <= 3 for the opposite reason, that the rate is too stable to respond to an unfolding crisis. Two independent arguments converging on the same window is worth more than either alone. The contamination calculation needs no data at all, only the definitions, so it can be run before a dataset is built. THE WORKING PRINCIPLE WORTH KEEPING The SICR rate should exceed the default rate, because staging should precede default. That is a coherence condition, it is cheap to check, and a definition that fails it is not a candidate regardless of its AUC.

Where the boundary problem really comes from

The source makes an observation in passing that deserves more than a passing mention: choosing the stage 1 and stage 2 boundary is a special case of a problem that goes back to radar in the 1930s, the question of whether the return is a bird or a bomber. That framing is exactly right and it carries a warning the SICR literature tends not to state.

In the radar problem the two hypotheses are mutually exclusive states of the world and the classifier's job is to discriminate between them. In the SICR problem there is no bird and no bomber. Credit risk is continuous, the boundary is an accounting construct, and there is no true label to discriminate against; the label $G(d,s,t+k)$ was manufactured by choosing three parameters. So the machinery of ROC curves, Youden indices and misclassification costs is being applied to a problem whose ground truth is itself a modelling choice. All the discrimination statistics remain valid conditional on that choice, and none of them can evaluate it.

Which is why the parameter ordering in this lecture matters. The threshold $u$, the cut-off $c$ and the cost ratio $a$ are all decisions made inside a chosen definition and all are testable against it. The definition parameters $d$, $s$ and $k$ are decisions about what the ground truth is, and no accuracy statistic computed against that ground truth can assess them. They have to be justified from the standard, from the contamination arithmetic, and from the coherence condition that SICR should precede default. A model committee that spends its time on $u$ and inherits $k$ from whoever built the dataset has allocated its attention precisely backwards.

Where this goes wrong in practice The most common version is a stage 2 population that is stable through a downturn, produced by a quota rule, a stale roll-rate window, or a cut-off calibrated on a benign-period prevalence and never revisited. All three produce the same symptom and the symptom looks like model stability, which is normally something to want. Here it is the failure. The single most useful exhibit in a SICR monitoring pack is the flagged share plotted against the realised default rate over as many years as the data allow, with the expectation stated in advance that the first series leads the second.
Exam tip If asked why IFRS 9 staging varies so much between banks, three marks are available and most candidates get one. The standard is principles-based and sets no threshold, which is the mark everyone gets. The second is that the quantity itself is unspecified, so banks compare different things, whether lifetime PD ratios, PD differences, delinquency predictions or ratings migrations. The third is that the thirty-day presumption in 5.5.11 is a rebuttable backstop rather than a definition, so it bounds the divergence from below without removing it.
