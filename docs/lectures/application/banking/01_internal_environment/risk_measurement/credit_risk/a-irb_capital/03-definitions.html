Definitions: obligor, facility, default, cure, loss

The vocabulary every later A-IRB lecture relies on. Who the obligor is, what counts as a facility, when an exposure has defaulted under CRR Article 178, when it has cured, and how economic loss is measured. Every PD, LGD and EAD model in the rest of the track inherits these definitions.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Default is the single most consequential boundary in credit-risk modelling, and the regulator defines it explicitly so banks cannot define their way out of risk. CRR Article 178 sets two limbs: an exposure is in default if it is more than 90 days past due on a material credit obligation, or if the institution considers the obligor unlikely to pay without recourse to security. Either limb is sufficient on its own. The same definition must be used across PD, LGD and EAD, and across booking, modelling and reporting.

Around this central rule sits a small cluster of dependent definitions. An obligor is the legal entity liable for the credit obligation. A facility is the contractual product (a loan account, a credit card, a revolver). For retail, default can be assigned at facility level; for non-retail it is always at obligor level. An exposure that has cured must complete a 12-month probation period (only 3 months for non-distressed restructuring) before being moved back to performing, and for LGD modelling an additional 9-month independence period is required before two consecutive defaults can be treated as separate events. Economic loss, the dependent variable in any LGD model, is the discounted shortfall between exposure at default and net recoveries net of direct and indirect costs. Resolved facilities are those for which a final outcome (cure, repayment, write-off, or maximum recovery period reached) is observed; everything else is unresolved and gets a conservative treatment.

Feynman test The definitions block answers four questions in sequence. Who owes the money? The obligor. What instrument do they owe it through? The facility. When are they considered to have stopped paying? At default, which is either 90 days late on a material amount, or judged unlikely to pay. When can they be considered to have recovered? At the end of a 12-month probation period during which no default trigger applies, with an additional 9-month buffer before a re-default can be treated as a new event. Everything else (collateral, ratings, netting, guarantees) is the apparatus for modifying the loss given that default has happened.
Step 2

Why this matters

Definitions sound dry. They aren't. The definition of default is the keystone of the whole capital model: PD is the probability of crossing it, LGD is the loss conditional on having crossed it, and EAD is the exposure at the moment of crossing. Move the boundary by 30 days in either direction and every parameter shifts. Banks that historically used a 180-DPD trigger had PDs that were structurally lower, LGDs that were structurally higher (because the worst recovery cases had already occurred by the time the default flag dropped), and capital that was not comparable across institutions. Basel II fixed the boundary at 90 days precisely so that capital numbers from different banks could be compared on a like-for-like basis.

The same logic applies inside a single bank. CRR Article 144 (the use test) requires that the definition of default used for capital is the same one used for credit-risk management. If origination scorecards, behavioural scorecards, collections triggers, IFRS 9 staging and regulatory capital all use slightly different defaults, the bank fails the use test and risks loss of IRB approval. So the definitions in this lecture are not "the regulatory definitions, as distinct from our internal definitions". They are the operating definitions of the whole credit risk function.

For the F107 exam, the definitions block is also where examiners check whether candidates understand the boundary between credit risk and adjacent disciplines: third-party fraud is operational risk and is written off rather than defaulted; deceased customers are credit risk because the contract cannot be honoured; debt sale is post-write-off and so happens after default rather than triggering it. Each of these distinctions is exam-testable.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture an exposure moving along a timeline. It opens in a performing state. At some point it may trip a default trigger (the upper rail of the diagram below). After the trigger it sits in a defaulted state during which workout, recoveries, write-off, debt sale, or eventually cure may occur. If it cures, it enters a 12-month probation window during which any re-trigger sends it straight back to default. If it completes probation, it is back in performing, with a further 9-month independence period before subsequent defaults are treated as wholly new events.

Three quantities anchor the picture. The maximum recovery period (MRP) is the upper bound on how long workout cash flows can keep accruing in the dataset (a 60-month window is typical for unsecured retail). The probation period is the buffer between exit from default and return to performing (12 months for cure, 3 months for non-distressed restructuring under CRR Article 178(5)(a)). The independence period is the SS4/24 §12.2 requirement that two defaults less than 9 months apart be collapsed into a single default event for LGD purposes. The numbers matter because they directly determine which observations land in the modelling dataset and how losses are accumulated.

Step 4

The detail

Obligor and facility

The obligor is the legal entity (individual, partnership, trust, corporation) that is liable for the credit obligation. CRR does not define the term explicitly but Article 178(1) requires default status to be recognised at obligor level for non-retail and allows facility-level assignment for retail. Banks must understand the structure of their obligor: simple in retail, often complex in corporate and specialised lending (organisational charts, legal shells, parent-subsidiary chains). KYC obligations sit on the obligor.

The facility is the contractual product: a term loan, a revolver, a credit card, an overdraft. Each facility has its own balance, limit, drawdown terms, and (often) its own LGD characteristics because the collateral and seniority differ. Retail capital is typically managed at facility level. The mapping from obligor to facility is one-to-many: a single obligor may hold multiple facilities, and the pulling rule requires that if a sufficient share of an obligor's exposures is in default, the rest are pulled in too.

Two retail-specific classifications matter operationally. A transactor is a revolving facility whose holder has repaid the balance in full at each scheduled repayment date for the previous 12 months (CRR Glossary, Basel CRE20.66); under standardised approach this earns a preferential 45% risk weight. An inactive account is one with no balance, purchases or payments for four consecutive months including the current month; inactive accounts are typically excluded from PD-model scoring populations but remain on the books.

Collateral and loan-to-value

Collateral is any asset pledged by the borrower to secure a loan. On default, where restructuring is not feasible, the bank takes possession of the collateral and offsets the loss. The protection is good only if the bank can take control of the asset without dispute, and only if the asset's value is stable. The best collateral is marketable in all economic conditions, low in price volatility, and denominated in the same currency as the loan.

Collateral does not change the PD (the obligor's likelihood of default is unchanged by what they have pledged); it reduces the LGD. The loan-to-value (LTV) ratio captures this: the loan amount divided by the collateral value, monitored over the life of the loan. A 70% LTV residential mortgage carries a 30% equity buffer before the bank's principal is at risk. Where LTV breaches a contractual threshold the bank may demand additional collateral ("margin call") or in extreme cases trigger acceleration.

Definition of default: the CRR Article 178 limbs

CRR Article 178(1) gives the legal definition. An exposure is in default when either or both of the following limbs apply:

Limb (a): the institution considers that the obligor is unlikely to pay
          its credit obligations in full without recourse by the
          institution to actions such as realising security (UTP).

Limb (b): the obligor is past due more than 90 days on any material
          credit obligation to the institution (90-DPD).

The two limbs are non-exclusive. An exposure may hit the 90-DPD limb without ever satisfying UTP (a temporarily distressed but ultimately solvent borrower), or hit UTP without ever passing 90 DPD (a bankrupt obligor flagged on day 1). The bank's default identification process must check both limbs continuously.

The 90-DPD limb

Days past due (DPD) is the number of calendar days between the due date of the oldest unpaid amount and the reference date. For credit cards, CRR Article 178(2)(c) anchors the count to the minimum-payment due date. An exposure becomes 90 DPD on the 91st day after the oldest unpaid material amount fell due.

Three subtleties matter. First, materiality: the past-due amount must exceed the materiality threshold defined under CRR Article 178(2)(d). The PRA has set the threshold for retail exposures in the UK at £0 absolute and 0% relative, which means any past-due amount counts. Second, technical past due: where the past-due status is caused by a data error, a system failure, or a payment-allocation timing lag, CRR allows the exposure to be exempted (SS3/24 §2.8 to 2.9). In practice banks operate an exception process to identify and reverse technical defaults. Third, disputes: under CRR Article 178(1D), the DPD counter is suspended for any disputed amount during a bona fide dispute between obligor and institution, and the disputed amount is removed from the minimum-payment calculation.

The UTP limb

CRR Article 178(3) lists six indicators of unlikeliness to pay. SS3/24 §3.27 to §3.32 require firms to identify any additional triggers relevant to their portfolio. The standard list comprises:

Several of these need unpacking. Forbearance covers any modification of contractual terms granted because the obligor is in financial difficulty: interest-rate reduction, hardship assistance, short-term payment holiday (up to three months), debt-management plan, settlement (agreed partial payment). All five types are typically treated as default. Distressed restructuring is the formal NPV-diminution test: if the present value of the restructured cash flows is more than 1% lower than the original, the exposure is in default. Charge-off is the accounting write-down (typically at 180 DPD for unsecured retail); it normally occurs after default but can sometimes trigger default if it pre-empts the DPD count.

Two boundary cases are exam-classic. First-party fraud (the customer is the fraudster) is treated as credit risk and so as a UTP indicator. Third-party fraud (the customer is the victim) is treated as operational risk and written off as soon as identified, with the loss excluded from the credit-risk PD/LGD development data. Deceased customers are UTP because the contract cannot be honoured.

Contagion (the pulling effect, SS3/24 §7.6 to §7.8) is the rule that if a significant share of an obligor's exposures is in default, the rest of the obligor's exposures must also be defaulted. The threshold "significant" is not fixed in regulation; banks commonly use 20% of total balance exposure (aligned with EBA/GL/2016/07). So a customer with two credit cards, where one card defaults and represents more than 20% of total exposure, has the second card defaulted automatically.

Return to non-default and cure

An exposure that has defaulted may eventually recover. CRR Article 178(5)(a) requires that no return to non-default status occurs until at least 3 months have passed since all default triggers ceased to apply. Article 178(5A) extends this to 12 months for distressed-restructuring defaults. In practice, UK retail banks operate a single 12-month probation period for all default types, which is the more conservative choice and avoids parallel workflows.

The probation logic is straightforward. On the day the last default trigger ceases (the obligor is no longer 90 DPD, the bankruptcy is resolved, the forbearance plan completes), a 12-month clock starts. If any default trigger reappears during that window, the clock resets and the exposure goes back to defaulted. If the 12 months elapse without a trigger, the exposure returns to performing.

For LGD-modelling purposes a further requirement applies. SS4/24 §12.2 requires that where the time between return to non-default and a subsequent re-default is less than 9 months, the two defaults must be treated as a single continuous default event for LGD estimation, with the default date set to the first occurrence. This is the independence period. The 12 + 9 framework therefore says: an exposure must spend 12 months out of default to be considered cured, and a further 9 months without re-defaulting before subsequent defaults count as new events.

Cure itself is not defined in CRR but is referenced in SS4/24. A cured account is one that has completed the 12-month probation period and the additional 9-month independence period without retriggering. The cure classification matters because it underpins the LGD risk-differentiation dependent variable (cure probability times loss-given-cure plus non-cure probability times loss-given-no-cure) and the choice of discount rate for post-default cash flows.

Economic loss

CRR Article 5(2) defines loss as "economic loss, including material discount effects, and material direct and indirect costs associated with collecting on the instrument". SS4/24 §13.2 gives the working formula:

Economic Loss_i = EAD_i
                 − NPV(Cash Recoveries)_i
                 − NPV(Debt Sales Proceeds)_i
                 + NPV(Cost of Recoveries)_i

All cash flows are discounted back to the moment of default. SS4/24 §13.16 to §13.18 set the discount rate at the relevant overnight risk-free rate plus 5 percentage points for long-run average and BEEL purposes (so SONIA + 5% for sterling exposures, SOFR + 5% for dollar). For downturn LGD and for LGD-in-default, the rate is floored at 9% (max(risk-free + 5%, 9%) per SS4/24 §13.17 and §13.18).

The economic-loss formula has three teeth worth noting. First, indirect costs (overheads, allocated time of collections staff) are included in the regulatory LGD calculation but excluded from the IFRS 9 LGD. Second, post-default fees and interest are not added to EAD (SS4/24 §13.9 and §13.10), so the bank cannot inflate economic loss by capitalising charges after the default date. Third, where realised recoveries exceed EAD (the bank ends up in profit on a defaulted exposure, typically because of penalty interest and fees received pre-default), realised LGD is floored at zero per SS4/24 §14.14.

A separate construction, economic loss given cure (ELGC), applies to exposures that return to non-default through cure rather than write-off or repayment. The formula adds an "artificial cash flow" representing the balance at the moment of cure (with adjustments for missed payments, post-default interest and fees, observed additional recoveries, and additional drawings), discounted back to the default date. The intuition: if a customer cures, the loss is not the full unpaid balance but rather the time value of delayed payments plus the bank's collection costs along the way.

ELGC_i = ( EAD_i − NPV(Observed payments)_i
                  − NPV(Artificial Cashflow)_i
                  + NPV(Costs)_i ) / EAD_i

Resolved facilities and the maximum recovery period

An LGD model cannot be calibrated on cases where the recovery story is still unfolding. SS4/24 §14.10 splits the default population into resolved and unresolved facilities. Resolved facilities are those for which one of four outcomes has been observed:

  1. The maximum recovery period (MRP) has been exceeded.
  2. The full obligation has been repaid and the facility closed.
  3. The facility has been written off (de-recognised from the IFRS accounts).
  4. A cure event has been recorded.

The maximum recovery period (SS4/24 §14.8) is the upper bound on how long workout cash flows can keep contributing to a single defaulted exposure's economic-loss calculation. For UK unsecured retail credit cards the MRP is typically 60 months: analysis shows that almost all recoveries arrive within five years of default, and the marginal cash flow beyond that point is too thin to materially change the LGD estimate. Exposures that exceed the MRP are forced to resolved status (non-cure) and their remaining balance is assigned a 100% LGD on the post-MRP residual.

Specialised lending default

For specialised lending (project finance, object finance, commodity finance, income-producing real estate), default is harder to pin down with a single 90-DPD rule. The triggers are typically covenant breaches: failures of the debt-service coverage ratio (DSCR, the ratio of net income to interest and principal payments), the loan life coverage ratio (LLCR, NPV of cash flows available for debt service divided by total debt), or the LTV. A breach can trigger penalty pricing, an early call (full repayment demanded), or formal default depending on the contract. The waterfall structure of senior versus subordinated lenders also matters: a covenant breach may trigger default on a junior tranche while the senior tranche is still performing.

Ratings

A rating system is, per the Basel Committee, "the conceptual methodology, management processes, and systems that play a role in the assignment of a rating". A rating is a "summary indicator of the risk inherent in an individual credit". Ratings have two dimensions: the borrower's PD (the obligor rating) and the transaction's LGD (the facility rating, capturing product, seniority, collateral).

For wholesale exposures, ratings are assigned obligor by obligor based on financial statements and qualitative assessment. For retail, the volume of customers makes this infeasible, so exposures are grouped into segments with homogeneous risk characteristics and a single segment-level rating applies. The choice between point-in-time (PIT, ratings track the current economic environment) and through-the-cycle (TTC, ratings stay stable across the cycle) is called the rating philosophy and is one of the first design choices in any PD-model project; we return to it in the PD-modelling lectures.

On-balance-sheet netting

A bank can offset client loans against client deposits for capital purposes provided three conditions hold: a well-founded legal right of set-off (LROS) in every relevant jurisdiction, the deposit maturity is at least as long as the loan, and the bank can identify the relevant assets and liabilities at any time. Netting is recognised for the standardised, F-IRB and A-IRB approaches but not for the Basel leverage ratio (which measures gross exposure deliberately).

Guarantees

Guarantees are issued by banks, corporations, sovereigns and individuals. Common forms include payment guarantees, advance-payment guarantees, performance bonds, bid bonds, warranty-obligation guarantees, credit-security bonds, sovereign guarantees, parent-company guarantees and director (personal) guarantees. Under the standardised approach, an exposure to a guaranteed obligor can borrow the guarantor's PD where the guarantor is higher-rated. Under A-IRB, where the guarantee includes collateral that the bank can legally take possession of on default, the guarantee also flows through to LGD. Basel treats insurance as a form of guarantee for these purposes.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The numbers in this lecture (90 days, 12 months, 9 months, 60 months, 20%, 1%) look arbitrary on first encounter. They aren't. Each one is the output of a deliberate trade-off, and understanding the trade-off is how an experienced practitioner reads new exposure types or new regulations.

Why 90 days? Basel II compromised between continental Europe (180 days, where bilateral relationship banking made longer arrears tolerable) and Anglo-Saxon supervision (already at 90 days under US practice). 90 was picked partly because it aligns with quarterly billing cycles, partly because empirical work showed that the cure rate of 60-DPD accounts was still high (often above 50%) while the cure rate of 90-DPD accounts had dropped sharply. The boundary is where the probability mass on cure starts dropping faster than the probability mass on charge-off.

Why a 12-month probation period? Internal cohort analyses across banks tell the same story: re-default rates fall sharply through the first 6 to 9 months of probation and then plateau by month 12. Too short a period (3 or 6 months) lets too many customers re-default into the performing book, contaminating the development data. Too long (24 months or more) suppresses the apparent cure rate and inflates the LGD because the bank is reluctant to let a customer return to performing. Twelve months is the empirical inflection point.

Why a 9-month independence period? A separate optimisation, set out in SS4/24 §12.2. If two defaults are too close together, treating them as separate events double-counts the loss; if they are too far apart, the second default is genuinely a new credit decision. Nine months is the standard separation that empirical studies find best reflects the difference. Some banks specify longer periods (12 months) if their exposure type justifies it.

Why a 20% contagion threshold? A pure interpretation of SS3/24 §7.6 to §7.8 would default all of an obligor's exposures whenever any one of them defaulted (the "obligor view"). At the other extreme, a pure facility view would ignore the obligor entirely. The 20% rule (carried over from EBA/GL/2016/07) is the compromise: small defaults stay isolated, but any material default propagates. The threshold matters most for credit cards, where the customer often holds multiple cards from the same issuer.

Why an MRP of 60 months for unsecured retail? Empirically, by month 60 the cumulative recovery curve has flattened: 90 to 95% of all recoveries ever observed have arrived. The marginal information from extending the window further does not justify the extra year of data lag that it costs the modelling team. For secured exposures, MRPs are longer (often 8 to 10 years) because property workouts can take years.

The shared logic across all these numbers is the same: each boundary is the point at which the marginal cost of one definition exceeds the marginal cost of the other. A practitioner reads new exposure types by asking the same question rather than memorising new numbers.

Test yourself

  1. What are the two limbs of CRR Article 178's definition of default? Is satisfaction of one sufficient?
  2. What is the materiality threshold for UK retail past-due amounts and which authority sets it?
  3. A credit-card customer is 95 days past due on a £3 minimum payment that the bank's system failed to debit because of a payment-processing outage. Is the exposure in default?
  4. A customer disputes £400 of a £600 minimum payment 100 days after the due date. The bank accepts the dispute is bona fide. How is the DPD counter affected?
  5. List four of the six CRR Article 178(3) UTP indicators.
  6. Why is first-party fraud treated as credit risk while third-party fraud is treated as operational risk? What does each treatment mean for the development data?
  7. Distinguish the 12-month probation period from the 9-month independence period. Which regulation sets each, and which purpose does each serve?
  8. A retail customer holds three credit cards with the same bank. Card A defaults and represents 25% of the customer's total balance. Card B is 30 DPD and Card C is current. What happens under the standard 20% contagion rule?
  9. State the economic-loss formula from SS4/24 §13.2 and explain why post-default fees are excluded.
  10. What is the maximum recovery period and what happens to exposures that exceed it without resolving?
