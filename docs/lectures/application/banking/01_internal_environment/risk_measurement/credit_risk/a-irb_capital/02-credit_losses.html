Credit losses: EL, UL, and the loss distribution

The shape that sits underneath every credit-risk number. The portfolio's one-year loss distribution decomposes into expected loss (covered by pricing and provisions), unexpected loss (covered by capital), and stress loss (uncovered, accepted residual). Every framework that follows, A-IRB, IFRS 9, ELBE, is a different reading of the same picture.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A credit portfolio's annual loss is a random variable. Its distribution has a mean (expected loss, EL), a spread (unexpected loss, UL, measured as a quantile distance), and a far tail (stress loss). The Basel framework draws two lines across that distribution: pricing and provisions cover everything to the left of EL, and capital covers the gap from EL to the 99.9% quantile. Anything beyond the 99.9% line is stress loss: not covered, not priced, accepted as residual.

Three loss estimates implement the same idea against different yardsticks. Regulatory EL uses through-the-cycle PD and downturn LGD to feed the Basel K formula. IFRS 9 ECL uses point-in-time PD, point-in-time LGD, scenario weights, and present-values cash flows at the effective interest rate, to set the accounting provision. ELBE is the loan-specific, post-default replacement for PD×LGD on a defaulted exposure, used only inside the regulatory machinery. The three answer different questions and almost never agree numerically. The mechanism that reconciles them is EL shortfall (Basel EL minus accounting provisions): if positive, it is deducted from CET1; if negative, the excess can be added to Tier 2 capital up to 0.6% of RWA.

Feynman test Imagine running the same bank for a thousand years. Most years it loses about the same modest amount on its loan book (the expected loss). Some years are unusually bad (the unexpected loss range). In one year out of those thousand, a Russia 1998 or a 2008 happens, and losses go off the chart (the stress loss). Pricing covers the average, capital covers up to the one-in-a-thousand, and the bank prays for the millennial event. The three loss estimates are three different ways of measuring the same picture: one for accounting (ECL), one for regulatory capital (Reg EL plus K), one for already-broken loans (ELBE). They look at the same loan and answer different questions.
Step 2

Why this matters

The EL versus UL decomposition is the single most consequential conceptual split in credit-risk regulation. Without it, the supervisor would have to size capital against the mean of the loss distribution, which is what naïve approaches do, and which collapses on first contact with a downturn. By separating "average losses" (priced into the spread, provisioned through the income statement) from "tail losses" (held against capital, available without an income statement entry), the framework lets the bank operate sustainably through cycles without forcing it to over-capitalise during expansions or under-capitalise during contractions.

The 2008 financial crisis revealed the cost of misjudging the tail. Mortgage-backed securities had been priced as if the underlying loss distribution had a thin tail; when correlations across regional housing markets jumped, the realised loss landed where the model had assigned almost zero probability. The Basel III response did not replace the loss-distribution framework; it tightened the inputs (Basel 3.1 input floors on PD and LGD, downturn calibration for LGD, output floor at 72.5% of standardised) so that the tail measurement could not be talked down by the bank's internal calibration to a benign sample.

The triple framework (Reg EL, ECL, ELBE) matters because each of the three has direct cash-flow consequences on a different line of the bank. ECL hits the P&L through impairment. Reg EL feeds the K formula and therefore the cost of capital on the loan. ELBE-versus-LGD-downturn determines the residual capital charge on defaulted assets. A model team that gets one of the three wrong, or fails to reconcile them coherently, finds out in the audit, in the SREP, or in the supervisory letter.

Step 4

The detail

Expected loss, bottom up

From a portfolio view, EL is the area under the loss density up to its mean. From a loan view, it is the product of three parameters defined in the notation lecture: PD over the chosen horizon, LGD on the exposure, and EAD at default. The Basel one-year EL for a single non-defaulted exposure is:

$$ \text{EL}^{\text{IRB}}_{i} \;=\; \text{PD}^{\text{reg}}_{g} \cdot \text{LGD}^{\text{DT}}_{g} \cdot \text{EAD}^{\text{reg}}_{i} $$

PD and LGD carry the grade subscript \(g\) because both are calibrated at pool level; only EAD is genuinely facility-specific. The product form is exact only if LGD and EAD are conditionally independent given default, which they are not: heavily utilised facilities tend to recover worse, and both deteriorate together in a downturn. Downturn LGD is, in part, a conservative loading standing in for that unmodelled dependence.

Aggregated across a portfolio it is the simple sum: \(\text{EL}_{\text{port}} = \sum_i \text{EL}^{\text{IRB}}_i\). The simple sum works because expected values are linear; correlations do not change the mean of the loss distribution, only its spread.

To make the three components concrete, consider a single facility with EAD = £100, LGD = 30%, PD = 15%:

  • The amount at risk, given default, is £65 (EAD on a 65% utilised line)
  • The amount expected to be lost, given default, is £19.50 (£65 × 30%)
  • The amount expected to be lost unconditionally is £2.93 (£19.50 × 15%)

EL is what pricing and provisions are meant to cover in the long run. A loan priced at a spread less than its EL is, in long-run-average terms, a loss-making loan before any cost of capital is added.

EL is not the K formula Regulatory EL is the through-the-cycle expected loss used in the EL shortfall reconciliation against accounting provisions. It is not the K formula. K = (conditional EL − EL) × MA scales the gap between WCDR and the regulatory PD; EL on its own only sets the lower bound. A common error is to think Reg EL is what drives capital. It is not; the stressed conditional loss drives capital, EL drives the provisions reconciliation, and the two share the same calibration inputs but answer different questions.

Unexpected loss and the role of correlation

Unlike EL, unexpected loss is a portfolio object and is not the sum of standalone facility ULs. Loss correlations between obligors matter: two loans in the same sector default together more often than two unrelated loans, so the portfolio UL exceeds what independence would give. It does decompose into facility contributions:

$$ \text{UL}_{\text{port}} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^N \underbrace{\sigma_i \cdot \rho_{i,\text{port}}}_{\text{contribution of facility } i} $$

where \(\sigma_i\) is the standalone standard deviation of credit losses on facility \(i\) and \(\rho_{i,\text{port}}\) is the correlation of that facility's losses with total portfolio losses. Note that the contribution is a share of a portfolio quantity, not a loss measure the facility possesses in isolation; a single facility's own 99.9% loss quantile is zero for any PD below ten basis points. The Basel IRB framework codifies the relationship between \(\rho\) and PD: lower PDs imply higher asset correlations (because low-PD borrowers are more likely to default together when a systematic shock arrives), with the corporate function ranging from 0.12 to 0.24 across PD levels and a flat 0.15 for residential mortgages.

Conditional expected loss = EL + UL

The Asymptotic Single Risk Factor (ASRF) model gives a clean algebraic identity:

$$ \text{Conditional Expected Loss} \;=\; \text{EL} \;+\; \text{UL} $$

The conditional expected loss is the expected loss in the bad state of the world (a 99.9% adverse realisation of the systematic factor). It equals the through-the-cycle EL plus the unexpected loss buffer needed to reach the 99.9% confidence level. This identity is why the K formula subtracts EL from a 99.9% conditional-PD-driven loss term: it isolates the unexpected portion, which is what capital is meant to cover.

The Vasicek derivation in compressed form

The IRB engine sits on Merton's structural model of default extended by Vasicek to a single-factor portfolio. A borrower defaults if its standardised latent asset value at the one-year horizon falls below a threshold \(c\), calibrated so that the unconditional default probability reproduces the through-the-cycle PD:

$$ \mathbb{P}\!\left[ A_i \leq c \right] \;=\; \text{PD}^{\text{TTC}}, \qquad c \;=\; N^{-1}\!\left( \text{PD}^{\text{TTC}} \right) $$

The latent value decomposes into a single systematic factor \(Z\) and an idiosyncratic shock \(\varepsilon_i\), both standard normal and mutually independent, with pairwise asset correlation \(R\). Following the sign convention fixed in the notation lecture, \(Z\) is the systematic stress factor, so positive \(Z\) means bad times and it enters the asset value with a minus:

$$ A_i \;=\; -\sqrt{R}\, Z \;+\; \sqrt{1 - R}\; \varepsilon_i, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(A_i) = R + (1 - R) = 1 $$

Conditional on \(Z = z\), the default probability for any obligor follows by rearranging the threshold condition:

$$ \mathbb{P}\!\left[ A_i \leq c \mid Z = z \right] \;=\; N\!\left( \frac{N^{-1}\!\left(\text{PD}^{\text{TTC}}\right) + \sqrt{R}\, z}{\sqrt{1 - R}} \right) $$

The plus sign in the numerator is a consequence of the minus sign in the latent model, not a free choice. Setting \(z = 0\) recovers the unconditional PD, which confirms the calibration. Setting \(z = N^{-1}(0.999) \approx 3.09\), the 99.9% stress quantile, gives the Basel worst-case default rate:

$$ \text{WCDR} \;=\; N\!\left( \frac{N^{-1}(\text{PD}^{\text{TTC}}) + \sqrt{R(\text{PD})} \cdot N^{-1}(0.999)}{\sqrt{1 - R(\text{PD})}} \right) $$

The Basel K formula plugs WCDR into the conditional-EL minus EL identity:

$$ K \;=\; \text{LGD}^{\text{DT}}_{g} \cdot \left[ \text{WCDR}_{g} - \text{PD}^{\text{reg}}_{g} \right] \cdot \text{MA}(\text{PD}, M) $$

The PD that enters production is \(\text{PD}^{\text{reg}}_{g}\), the calibrated through-the-cycle estimate plus its margin of conservatism and subject to the regulatory floor, not the raw \(\text{PD}^{\text{TTC}}\) of the derivation. It appears on both sides: inside WCDR and in the subtracted EL term.

Downturn LGD as the Basel patch for missing conditional LGD

The framework has a clean conditional PD (WCDR) but no equivalent conditional LGD function. Empirically, LGD rises in downturns: collateral values fall, recovery costs rise, workout periods lengthen. Rather than build a mapping function for conditional LGD analogous to the Vasicek mapping for PD, the BCBS asks A-IRB banks to report LGDs already calibrated to economic-downturn conditions. The downturn LGD enters the K formula twice: once multiplied by WCDR in the conditional-EL term, and once multiplied by TTC PD in the EL term that is subtracted off.

EL shortfall: reconciling regulatory and accounting losses

Because ECL and Reg EL are calibrated differently (PiT scenario-weighted versus TTC downturn), they almost never produce the same number. The CRR Article 159 reconciliation closes the gap inside capital:

OutcomeTreatment
ECL < Regulatory EL (shortfall)Deducted from CET1 capital
ECL > Regulatory EL (excess)Eligible as Tier 2 capital, capped at 0.6% of credit-risk RWA

The shortfall mechanism is the policy answer to a structural mismatch: if accounting provisions are less conservative than Basel EL, the residual gap is loss-absorbing capital the bank does not have on its balance sheet, so it is taken from CET1 instead. If accounting provisions are more conservative, the bank gets a partial credit at the lower-quality T2 tier (capped, because T2 is gone-concern not going-concern capital). The gap is a live capital-management lever: in a sharp downturn, IFRS 9 ECL spikes (PiT, scenario-weighted) while Reg EL barely moves (TTC), so EL excess opens up and the bank gets some T2 relief on the way down.

Defaulted assets

Once a loan defaults, PD is no longer informative (it is one by construction). The K formula collapses into a direct loss-coverage rule based on the gap between the downturn loss rate on defaulted exposures and the loan-specific best estimate of loss:

$$ K^{\text{def}}_{i} \;=\; \max\!\left( 0, \; \text{LGD}^{\text{Def}}_{i} - \text{ELBE}_{i} \right) $$

Both terms are loss rates, so \(K^{\text{def}}\) is per unit of EAD like the performing formula and feeds the same \(\text{RWA} = 12.5 \cdot K \cdot \text{EAD}\) conversion. The relevant EAD is the actual current balance (no CCF, no future-drawdown stress: the default has already happened), and \(\text{LGD}^{\text{Def}}\) is the downturn-calibrated loss rate on defaulted exposures, which is a different parameter from the \(\text{LGD}^{\text{DT}}\) estimated on the performing book. ELBE is the loan-specific point-in-time expected loss, defined as a probability-weighted blend of two recovery paths:

$$ \text{ELBE} \;=\; P(\text{cure}) \cdot \text{LGD}_{\text{cure}} \;+\; \big(1 - P(\text{cure})\big) \cdot \text{LGD}_{\text{no cure}} $$

Cure outcomes have low LGD (close to zero if the borrower rehabilitates); no-cure outcomes have LGD reflecting the expected workout or enforcement recovery. The reason ELBE replaces TTC LGD on defaulted loans is granular information: once a loan is in workout, the bank knows the collateral type, the recovery channel, the obligor's circumstances. A generic cycle-averaged LGD throws that information away.

Why not just use downturn LGD on defaulted exposures? Downturn LGD is a stressed estimate. Using it as the EL anchor on a defaulted loan would mean comparing a stressed loss rate against the bank's expected accounting provisions, systematically overstating EL shortfall and penalising banks for the asymmetry. ELBE is the unstressed, loan-specific point of comparison. The K formula then sizes capital against the gap from ELBE up to downturn LGD: that gap is the unexpected residual on already-defaulted exposures.

The four CRR Article 181 requirements on ELBE

The regulatory expectations on ELBE are tight, because it is the loan-specific number that sits inside both the EL shortfall calculation and the defaulted-asset K formula.

  1. It must reflect current economic circumstances, not a long-run average
  2. It must be grounded in the bank's own workout experience on comparable defaulted exposures
  3. It must account for all recovery costs (legal, administrative, time delays through discounting where appropriate)
  4. It must be independently validated and subject to supervisory review, and broadly consistent with the ECL provisioning process; material divergence requires justification
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The choice of 99.9% is policy, not physics

The 99.9% one-year confidence level was selected so that the resulting capital, on average across the system, would support a single-A standalone rating for the bank itself. A bank with capital sized to absorb its 99.9% loss should, in expectation, survive the one-in-a-thousand year. Move the dial to 99.99% and capital roughly doubles; move it to 99% and it halves. The supervisor's choice trades systemic resilience against the deadweight cost of capital sitting idle in expansions.

Stress loss is policy, not error

The tail beyond 99.9% is acknowledged but not capitalised. Tier 2 capital (subordinated debt) absorbs losses in resolution rather than on a going-concern basis. The deeper Pillar 2 buffers (countercyclical, systemic, conservation, G-SII surcharge) sit on top of Pillar 1 to lean against the residual. The 2008 Haldane critique was not that the framework failed to acknowledge the tail; it was that the calibration of the 99.9% line was too generous because banks had calibrated their inputs to a benign sample.

Pricing covers EL; capital covers UL; but capital does not stay still

A loan priced at PD × LGD recovers expected losses on average. A bank holding 8% of RWA in capital absorbs the unexpected portion in the one-in-a-thousand year. The two are independent in design and dependent in practice: a portfolio that has under-priced its EL also tends to have under-estimated its UL, because both calibrations draw on the same historical sample. The 2008 mortgage CDO market was a worked example of that joint failure mode.

Linearity of EL, non-linearity of UL

EL is linear: the portfolio EL is the simple sum of facility ELs. UL is sub-linear: the portfolio UL is less than the simple sum because correlation across facilities is less than perfect. That sub-linearity is the diversification benefit. The Basel ASRF formula implicitly captures a portion of it through the prescribed asset-correlation function \(R(\text{PD})\), but the formula assumes one and only one systematic factor across the whole book. A real bank's portfolio has many systematic factors (sector, geography, business cycle, FX), so the IRB formula systematically understates true diversification for well-diversified banks and overstates it for concentrated ones.

The TTC choice cuts both ways

TTC PD smooths the cycle and stabilises capital. The price is that K does not respond to deterioration in real time. In a sharp downturn, observed default rates spike, but the K-driving PD edges up modestly; the bank's regulatory capital ratio looks healthier than the underlying portfolio actually is. IFRS 9 ECL closes this gap from the accounting side: PiT, scenario-weighted ECL spikes immediately, raising the EL excess (or narrowing the shortfall), and the difference flows through P&L. In capital terms the bank does not necessarily move, but in accounting terms it does.

ELBE versus PiT IFRS 9 LGD: similar inputs, different governance

Both ELBE and IFRS 9 LGD on a defaulted exposure are PiT, loan-specific, current-information estimates of loss. They differ on three axes. ECL discounts at the original EIR; ELBE is undiscounted (or uses its own basis). ECL methodology varies by bank; ELBE meets CRR Article 181 standards, making it more comparable across institutions. And ECL is used inside the P&L; ELBE is used inside the regulatory capital calculation. Inside a single bank the two numbers can be quite close in absolute terms while serving different governance regimes.

The 0.6% Tier 2 cap on EL excess

The cap exists because Tier 2 is gone-concern capital, not going-concern. Recognising unlimited EL excess as T2 would let a bank funnel its accounting conservatism into regulatory capital relief. The 0.6% cap (of credit-risk RWA, not total RWA) limits the channel. In practice the cap rarely binds for a normal bank, but for one with conservatively calibrated provisions and a small IRB book it can.

Write-off resets both sides of the EL shortfall calculation

A full write-off eliminates the ECL provision on that loan, the ELBE-based regulatory EL on that loan, the EAD (so RWA and K go to zero), and any T2 eligibility from EL excess on that loan. The shortfall versus excess comparison resets simultaneously on both sides. The implication is that write-off timing is not a neutral accounting choice; it changes the capital arithmetic on the wider book through the shared denominator.

  2. Write down the Basel one-year EL formula for a single non-defaulted exposure and explain why portfolio EL is the simple sum across facilities but portfolio UL is not.

  6. Explain the EL shortfall mechanic under CRR Article 159. Under what cyclical conditions would you expect EL excess to open up and EL shortfall to widen?
  7. Write down the K formula for a defaulted exposure and the ELBE decomposition. Why is downturn LGD not used as the EL anchor for defaulted exposures?
  8. List the four CRR Article 181 requirements on ELBE. Which of these is the principal mechanism for ensuring cross-bank comparability?
