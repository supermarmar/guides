EAD target and data: EADF at the 12-month horizon

The data foundation of the EAD build. The EADF target at the 12-month forward horizon, the row-construction rules that produce one observation per defaulted facility, the three feature families the model is allowed to use (at-observation, pre-default trend, macro), and the data-history requirement that runs lighter than LGD because there is no MRP compounding.

A-IRB 24 of n — EAD 2 of 7 Reading time, 25 minutes Source: 04_feature_engineering/ead/02-data_requirements.md; supplemented from CRR Article 182, PRA SS4/24 §17, EBA/GL/2017/16.
Step 1

The 30-second version

The EAD target is the realised EADF for facilities that default within the 12-month forward window: EADF_i,t = EAD_at_default / Limit_at_observation_t. Each row of the development sample is a (facility, snapshot) pair where the facility was performing at the snapshot and defaulted within the next 12 months. The target value per row is the EADF realised at the default event. The continuous nature of the target (in [0, 1+], with most mass between current utilisation and 1) makes the rest of the pipeline a regression problem rather than a classification problem.

The independent variables split into three families. At-observation features describe the facility at the snapshot date: utilisation, balance, limit, age on book, product type, recent payment behaviour, days past due. Pre-default trend features describe the drawdown trajectory over the 12 months before observation: balance trend, utilisation trend, limit-change events, payment-pattern shifts. Macroeconomic features describe the economic environment that customers respond to: unemployment rate, credit-card industry usage, consumer-confidence index. Unlike PD where macro is typically excluded from the conditioning set, EAD typically does include macro because the recovery-market analogy from LGD applies: customer drawing behaviour in stress is fundamentally a macro phenomenon.

The data history requirement is 5 years for retail and 7 years for corporate / sovereign / bank exposures (CRR Article 182(3)). Importantly, there is no MRP compounding: EAD is captured at the moment of default, not at the end of a 60-month workout, so a 5-year history supports 5 cohorts of defaulted facilities directly. This makes EAD sampling lighter on data history than LGD sampling, though heavier than PD sampling because the population is still restricted to defaulted facilities.

Feynman test The PD target asked "did this facility default in the next 12 months?" (binary). The LGD target asked "given default, what fraction of EAD was lost over the next 60 months?" (continuous, requires full workout track). The EAD target sits between them: "given default, what fraction of the limit was drawn at the moment of default?" (continuous, captured at a single event). The single-event nature is what makes EAD data work easier than LGD data work: no MRP, no resolution wait, no 60-month tracking. The continuous nature is what makes it different from PD: GLM instead of logistic, MAE instead of Gini.
Step 2

Why this matters

The 12-month fixed-horizon construction is the structural difference between EAD sampling and PD sampling. PD uses overlapping cohorts (each obligor contributes one row per snapshot) and de-duplicates to one row per facility before fitting. EAD uses non-overlapping cohorts (each facility contributes one row per default event, not per snapshot), so de-duplication is automatic and the sample geometry is cleaner. This matters operationally: EAD development samples are typically 50,000 to 200,000 rows (one per defaulted facility plus some additional snapshots of the same facility at different observation dates if monthly cohorts are used) versus PD's millions. The smaller sample inherits LGD-style sample-size discipline (tighter VIF, no aggressive downsampling).

The target's continuous nature creates a data-leakage challenge unique to EAD. The strongest predictor of EADF at default is utilisation at observation (a customer at 90% utilisation will end up at high EADF almost regardless of what happens in the 12 months). But "utilisation at observation" is a snapshot-date variable, computed from balance and limit. If the snapshot-date balance is computed using forward-looking smoothing (a 3-month average centred on the snapshot, for example), the variable contains information from the outcome window and is a leakage. The discipline is to compute utilisation features using only data observed at or before the snapshot date, never centred or forward-windowed.

For F107 the exam-classic question is on the fixed-horizon construction: why is EAD sampling different from PD sampling, and what role does the absence of MRP play? Candidates who articulate both differences cleanly score well; candidates who treat all three IRB parameters as having identical data-engineering pipelines miss the structural distinctions.

Step 3

The mental model

The EAD row is a hybrid of the PD row and the LGD row. Like PD, it is indexed by (facility, snapshot) and looks 12 months forward to detect default. Like LGD, it only enters the development sample if a default occurs. Unlike LGD, the data captured at the default event is a single number (EAD), not a 60-month track. Three properties of the structure matter. First, the limit in the denominator is at observation, not at default. This is the standard convention from CRR Article 182(1)(b): EADF is expressed relative to the limit the bank had committed at the snapshot date, not the limit that may have changed between snapshot and default. A facility whose limit was reduced from £5,000 to £3,000 in the 12-month window can still have EADF > 1 if the EAD at default exceeds £5,000 (rare but operationally valid in cases with late post-observation drawdowns). Second, multiple snapshots of the same facility can contribute rows: a facility observed at January 2018, July 2018 and January 2019, defaulting in March 2019, contributes three rows (one from each snapshot), all sharing the same EAD-at-default but different limits at observation. Third, the row enters the sample only on default: the model fits on a sample of defaulted facilities, which is why the EAD development sample is smaller than the PD sample (only defaults contribute) but does not need the MRP waiting period of LGD.

Step 4

The detail

The EADF target

Per CRR Article 182(1)(f) and (g):

EADF_i,t  =  EAD_at_default_i  /  Limit_at_observation_i,t

       where  the snapshot date t precedes the default event by some s in (0, 12] months
              EAD_at_default = customer balance at the default event,
                                including all principal, interest and fees
              Limit_at_observation = the contractual credit limit at snapshot date t

The same outstanding-amount rules from lecture 17 apply to EAD-at-default: for defaulted facilities, customer balance with no further adjustment for SCRA, late fees not capitalised excluded, interest not capitalised excluded (SS4/24 §13.5, §13.9, §13.10). The result is that the EAD denominator in the LGD calculation and the EAD numerator in the EADF calculation are the same quantity, which the bank computes once and uses twice.

Row construction

Operational construction is a join between the MRD's monthly snapshots and the default-event table:

# Pseudo-SQL: build the EAD development sample
SELECT
    facility_id,
    snapshot_month,
    limit_at_snapshot,
    drawn_balance_at_snapshot,
    EAD_at_default,
    EAD_at_default / limit_at_snapshot AS target_eadf,
    ... -- features
FROM
    monthly_snapshots s
INNER JOIN
    default_events d
    ON s.facility_id = d.facility_id
WHERE
    d.default_date BETWEEN s.snapshot_month
                       AND DATE_ADD(s.snapshot_month, INTERVAL 12 MONTH)
    AND s.facility_status = 'PERFORMING'   -- not already in default at snapshot
    AND s.limit_at_snapshot > 0           -- EADF undefined for zero-limit facilities
    AND s.snapshot_month <= max_snapshot_date - INTERVAL 12 MONTH   -- avoid censoring

The four filters at the bottom are the standard EAD population-construction rules. The performing-at-snapshot filter mirrors the PD population rule (already-defaulted facilities have an EADF of 1 by construction and add no information). The zero-limit filter is EAD-specific (the target is undefined). The 12-month censoring filter mirrors the PD rule.

The three feature families

FamilyExamplesNotes
At-observation Utilisation_t, drawn balance_t, limit_t, days-past-due_t, transactor/revolver flag_t, age on book at t, segment / product type, channel of origination The strongest features; utilisation_t alone explains 40 to 60% of EADF variance in retail unsecured
Pre-observation trend Balance trend over t-12 to t, utilisation trend, count of limit changes in last 12m, payment regularity, recent draw / repay volatility Adds incremental signal on top of at-observation features; computed strictly with data prior to t (no centring, no forward look-ahead)
Macroeconomic Unemployment rate, credit-card industry utilisation, consumer-confidence index, regional house-price index (less relevant for unsecured) Unlike PD where macro is typically excluded from the conditioning set, EAD typically includes macro because drawdown behaviour in stress is a macro phenomenon. Banks that exclude macro absorb the cycle effect through LRA / downturn calibration; banks that include it capture the cycle in PiT EADF directly

The bureau feature family used in PD and LGD is less consequential for EAD: bureau scores predict default and recovery, not drawdown intensity. Some banks include bureau utilisation (the obligor's utilisation on other lenders' lines) as a proxy for general financial stress; this is the most useful bureau-derived EAD feature.

Look-back-only discipline for at-observation features

The standard data-leakage check from PD applies, with one EAD-specific subtlety. At-observation features must reflect data available at t, not at t plus any look-ahead. Three traps:

  • Balance smoothing. A 3-month centred moving-average balance includes one month before t and one month after; the after-t month is in the outcome window and is a leakage. Smoothing must be one-sided (trailing).
  • Limit changes. The "current limit" at snapshot t should be the limit on that calendar date; some systems backfill limit changes to the effective date weeks later, which creates a small look-ahead. The data engineering has to use as-of-t snapshots, not corrected versions.
  • Bureau pull dates. Bureau data is sometimes refreshed monthly with a 1 to 2 week lag. The "bureau score at t" used in the model must be the score available on the snapshot date in production, not the score available with the benefit of hindsight.

Data history

CRR Article 182(3) requires:

  • Retail: minimum 5 years; the window must include a downturn period or be supplemented by conservative assumptions.
  • Corporate, sovereign, bank: minimum 7 years.

Unlike LGD, there is no MRP compounding: each default event provides a single EAD observation captured at the default date. So a 5-year history supports 5 years of defaulted-facility rows directly. Most banks operate with 7 to 10 years of EAD history to ensure cycle coverage; the GFC vintage (2008 to 2009) is typically included to anchor downturn calibration.

Population construction nuances

  • Limit increases between observation and default: the limit in EADF is at observation, not at default, per CRR Article 182(1)(b). A facility whose limit was increased by the bank in the 12-month window can therefore have EADF > 1 if EAD at default exceeds the observation limit. The model accommodates this (the GLM in lecture 27 handles values above 1 if the link function permits).
  • Limit decreases: the bank's collections-action limit reduction shortly before default compresses available headroom and artificially lowers EADF. Standard practice is to flag limit decreases within the 12-month window and either exclude affected rows or include them with a "limit reduced" indicator.
  • Closed facilities at default: a facility closed by the bank (limit set to zero) with a non-zero balance at default is still in scope; the EAD is the closing balance, and the EADF is computed against the limit at observation (not at default, which is zero and would explode).
  • Related facilities (SS4/24 §13.8): if a facility is restructured to a new account number between observation and default, the data engineering links the two and treats them as a single facility for EAD purposes.

Data sources

Internal sources dominate; external sources play a smaller role than for PD.

  • Month-end snapshots. The MRD's monthly tables from lecture 06. Provides at-observation utilisation, balance, limit, and behavioural features.
  • Transaction records. Daily payment, drawing and fee transactions; aggregated to produce pre-observation trend features.
  • Limit-change records. Operational system event log; tracks proactive credit-line increases and decreases. Important for the limit-change diagnostics described above.
  • Default-event table. From the same MRD pipeline; provides the EAD at default and the default date.
  • Credit bureau. Bureau utilisation as a proxy for general financial stress; less central for EAD than for PD or LGD.
  • Macroeconomic data series. Same UR, CPI, GDP, HPI, CCI series used in the PD and LGD LRA / downturn work.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first lesson is that the choice of limit at observation as the denominator is the single most consequential design decision in EAD modelling. The alternative is limit at default. Using limit at observation freezes the denominator at a known quantity (and lets the model focus on predicting the numerator); using limit at default contaminates the denominator with future limit changes that the bank cannot forecast. CRR Article 182(1)(b) settles the question: use limit at observation. Modellers who try to use limit at default for theoretical purity end up with EADF distributions that move with limit-management actions rather than with customer behaviour.

The second lesson is on the trend-feature look-ahead discipline. Pre-observation trend features (balance trend over the previous 12 months, utilisation trend, etc.) are powerful predictors but require careful construction. The most common error is including the snapshot month itself in the trend window, which is fine in principle but creates a hidden dependency: the trend value at snapshot t includes data from t itself, and a feature engineering pipeline that subsequently does any data manipulation at t can leak forward. The safe convention is to compute trend features over t-12 to t-1 (exclusive of t) and to compute the at-observation feature as the value at t.

The third lesson is on including macro in the EAD conditioning set. PD's instinct is to exclude macro to avoid procyclicality; LGD's instinct is to include macro for secured exposures because collateral value is a macro phenomenon; EAD sits in between. The right answer for EAD is to include macro because customer drawing behaviour in stress is fundamentally a macro phenomenon (when unemployment rises, customers under stress draw down available credit at higher rates), and excluding macro produces a PiT EADF that systematically under-predicts in downturns. The cycle effect is then captured in PiT directly; the LRA / downturn calibration in lecture 28 picks up any residual.

The fourth lesson, operational: EAD data engineering is easier than LGD data engineering but harder than PD data engineering. Easier than LGD because there is no MRP waiting period and no 60-month workout track. Harder than PD because the target is continuous and the population is restricted to defaulted facilities. The right resource allocation is somewhere in the middle; teams that staff EAD data work at PD intensity over-deliver, teams that staff it at LGD intensity under-deliver.
