Portfolio description: knowing what you are modelling

Lifecycle stage 2. Before any model is built, the modelling team computes a standard package of descriptive statistics over the MRD: how big the portfolio is, how it splits across arrears stages, how the headline metrics move through time, and what the observed default rate has looked like. The same package is the supervisor's first read at SREP.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Portfolio description is the lecture-stage answer to the question "what does this book look like before we start modelling it". Eight headline metrics are computed and read together: total volume (count of facilities), total balance, average credit limit, average utilisation, transactor rate, inactive rate, 12-month observed default rate (ODR) and 12-month re-default rate. Each metric is reported as a current snapshot, a historical time series, and a decomposition by arrears stage. The same numbers travel with the model into the SREP package as exhibits 1 to 8.

The picture that emerges does two jobs. First, it characterises the central tendency of the portfolio: the typical balance, the typical utilisation, the typical default rate. This is the empirical starting point for PD and EAD calibration. Second, it surfaces structural breaks and mix shifts: when did the policy change, when did COVID hit, when did the new product land. These breaks are the input to the representativeness analysis that determines which slice of history feeds the long-run average (LRA) calibration. A modeller who skips portfolio description and goes straight to feature engineering will discover the breaks later, usually painfully.

Feynman test The portfolio-description package answers three questions the supervisor will ask in the first ten minutes of any review. How concentrated is the book, who is borrowing, how have the headline rates moved over time. If the answers are stable and the trends are explicable, the rest of the review proceeds. If the answers are unstable or the trends are unexplained, the rest of the review becomes a diagnostic exercise. Portfolio description is therefore the modelling team's chance to set the narrative: this is what we built our model on, this is the part of history we trust, this is the part we treat with conservatism.
Step 2

Why this matters

Two regulatory ideas pivot on portfolio description. The first is the long-run average (LRA) default rate that A-IRB PD calibration must achieve (CRR Article 180(1)(a) and SS3/24 §14). The LRA cannot be computed without the historical ODR series; portfolio description is where that series is produced and inspected. If the series shows a structural break, the LRA is computed on the segment of history that is representative of the foreseeable portfolio, and the broken segment is excluded with an MoC overlay (category A) to compensate.

The second is the representativeness requirement (CRR Article 174(c) and SS3/24 §11.11 to 11.13). The development sample used for modelling must reflect the current portfolio across every material risk driver. Portfolio description produces the diagnostic that establishes representativeness: distributions of balance, limit, utilisation, segment and arrears stage are compared between the development window and the current portfolio. Where they diverge materially, the modelling team either re-weights the development sample, excludes a non-representative period, or applies an MoC overlay.

For F107 the descriptive-statistics package is also where the candidate proves they understand what an A-IRB book looks like in practice. The exam-classic question is "describe the descriptive statistics you would compute over a retail portfolio before starting model development, and explain what each tells you". The eight metrics in this lecture form the spine of that answer.

Step 3

The mental model

The portfolio description package is best pictured as a cube. One axis is the metric (volume, balance, utilisation, ODR and so on). One axis is the arrears stage (performing, early arrears, late arrears, defaulted). The third axis is time (the historical snapshot months). Any cross-section of the cube is a view the modeller produces and the supervisor reads.

The three cross-sections are not interchangeable. The latest snapshot answers "what does the book look like today" and is the input to portfolio-mix discussions. The portfolio-level time series answers "how have the headline numbers moved", and is the input to LRA calibration and structural-break identification. The stage-decomposed time series answers "is the book getting healthier or sicker", and is the input to early-warning indicators and risk-appetite monitoring.

Step 4

The detail

The eight headline metrics

Each metric has a defined construction, a regulatory significance, and a typical reading. The names below use the canonical MRD columns from the previous lecture so the SQL is portable.

MetricConstructionWhat it tells you
Total volumeCOUNT(DISTINCT facility_id) at each snapshotScale of the book; growth or shrinkage trend; size of the modelling population
Total balanceSUM(outstanding_balance) at each snapshotNotional exposure; the denominator for any balance-weighted KPI
Average credit limitAVG(credit_limit) across active facilitiesOrigination policy posture; mix shifts toward larger or smaller exposures
Average utilisationAVG(outstanding_balance / credit_limit)How drawn the facilities are; the natural pre-default risk driver in revolvers
Transactor rateSUM(transactor_indicator) / COUNT(DISTINCT facility_id)Share of the book that pays in full each month; lower-risk segment by construction
Inactive rateSUM(inactive_indicator) / COUNT(DISTINCT facility_id)Share with no activity; signals quiet attrition or product-life-cycle drift

12m re-default rateSame ratio but restricted to cure_indicator = TRUE at the opening snapshotThe quality of cures; the empirical input to the probation-period analysis

Cross-section one: the latest snapshot

The first table the supervisor reads is the latest-month snapshot sliced by arrears stage. The structure looks like this (illustrative numbers, generalised; the absolute values will vary by portfolio):

arrears_stage    total_volume    total_balance    avg_credit_limit    avg_utilisation
1. performing    ~ majority      ~ majority       moderate            ~ 0.40
2. 1 to 30 DPD   small share     small share      higher              ~ 0.75
3. 31 to 90 DPD  smaller         smaller          higher still        ~ 0.80
4. 90+ DPD       smaller still   small but        higher              ~ 0.80
                                  material

Three readings to extract from this table. First, utilisation rises sharply with arrears stage: revolvers in late arrears are typically near or at limit. This is one of the strongest single PD drivers in retail and is the empirical justification for utilisation-band segmentation in PD models. Second, average credit limit rises with arrears stage: the bank originally lent more to customers who later got into trouble. This is not necessarily a bank error; it usually reflects the fact that larger limits are extended to customers with higher initial scores, who happen to be more sensitive to subsequent shocks. The pattern is exam-classic: rising limit by arrears stage looks counter-intuitive but is empirical reality. Third, balances are concentrated in the performing book, even if the default share looks large in volume terms: the late-arrears stages hold disproportionate balance per account but a smaller share of total book balance.

Cross-section two: the portfolio-level time series

The eight metrics computed each month and plotted over the available history. The supervisor's question is "is each line stable, trending, or structurally broken". Typical interpretations:

  • Total volume. Smooth growth reflects organic origination. A step change indicates a portfolio acquisition or divestment, which must be flagged in the limitations register and the representativeness analysis.
  • Total balance. Should track volume roughly proportionally unless average utilisation or limit is shifting; divergence between volume and balance is a mix-shift signal.
  • Average credit limit. Slow upward drift is normal (inflation, customer-management increases). Step changes signal policy moves: limit-up programmes, affordability-rule changes.
  • Average utilisation. Strongly cyclical: rises in downturns as customers draw down available credit. The shape of the utilisation curve through the cycle is what an IRRBB or stress-testing colleague will want to see.
  • Transactor and inactive rates. Slow-moving structural metrics. Step changes typically reflect product launches (more transactors) or policy tightening (more inactives as customers self-select out).
  • 12m ODR. The single most important line on the page. Rises in downturns, falls in expansions. The 1-in-25-year peak observable in the series is the empirical anchor for downturn scenarios. Long flat periods without a recession in scope are a flag that the LRA may not capture a full cycle.
  • 12m re-default rate. Quality-of-cure measure. A sustained drop indicates collections improvements; a sustained rise indicates either deterioration in workout strategy or upstream PD weakening.

Cross-section three: stage-decomposed time series

Each of the eight metrics broken down by arrears stage and plotted as a stacked-area or multi-line chart. The supervisor's question moves from "is the headline number stable" to "is the underlying mix stable". Two diagnostics dominate.

Volume and balance stack plots. A stacked-area chart of volume by arrears stage shows whether the share of late-arrears accounts is growing relative to the performing book. The same chart in balance terms is more sensitive (because late-arrears accounts have higher balances per account). A widening 90+ DPD slice in the balance stack with a stable volume stack is a classic early-warning signal of portfolio deterioration that the headline volume number masks.

Per-stage metric trends. Average utilisation by stage, average limit by stage, transactor rate by stage. Each line should be approximately monotonic across stages at any point in time (performing < early arrears < late arrears for utilisation) and broadly stable through time. Non-monotonicity (e.g. higher utilisation in 31-90 than 90+) is an artefact, usually of write-off practices: 90+ balances are written down faster, suppressing the apparent utilisation. The lecture team should be able to explain it.

Default-rate views

Three separate views of the default series are produced. The 12-month portfolio-level ODR measures the share of the opening non-defaulted population that defaults within 12 months. The 12-month 90+ DPD ODR isolates the DPD-driven limb of the default definition, useful for back-testing the time-based versus event-based default triggers separately. The 12-month re-default rate uses only the cured opening population to measure the probability of a second default within 12 months; this is what the 12-month probation period in CRR Article 178(5) is calibrated against.

Plotting all three on the same axis gives the canonical view of credit cycle. In an expansion, all three trend down; in a downturn, all three rise but the re-default rate rises faster (cured customers are more cycle-sensitive than the pristine book); in recovery, ODR falls first while re-default rates remain elevated as the workout queue clears.

Average versus balance-weighted

Every average has a balance-weighted analogue. The arithmetic average utilisation gives equal weight to a £50 facility and a £50,000 facility; the balance-weighted average treats the £50,000 facility as 1,000 times more important. For risk-management purposes the balance-weighted view is usually more informative because exposure scales with balance, not with count. The standard practice is to report both: the arithmetic average gives the account-level customer-experience picture, the balance-weighted average gives the book-level risk picture. The lecture team should not commit silently to one or the other; the supervisor will ask.

Vintage view as a complement

The cross-sections above are snapshot views: they pool customers regardless of when they were originated. A complementary view is the vintage view, which fixes the origination month and tracks the cohort forward. The same metrics (volume on book, balance on book, cumulative ODR, transactor rate) computed by vintage reveal patterns the snapshot view hides: a worsening underwriting policy shows up as higher cumulative ODR at month 12 of more recent vintages, even if the overall portfolio ODR is stable. Vintage analysis is the natural bridge from portfolio description to the feature-engineering and modelling stages, where origination-cohort risk drivers become explicit features.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The hardest skill in portfolio description is not computing the numbers; it is reading them in combination. The same headline ODR can be the residue of very different stories. A 2% ODR with a falling transactor rate, a rising average utilisation and a thickening 31-90 DPD slice is a portfolio that is sickening from the bottom; the next 12 months will see the 2% become 3% or 4%. A 2% ODR with a stable transactor rate, a stable utilisation and a thinning 31-90 DPD slice is a portfolio that has already absorbed the shock; the next 12 months will see the 2% normalise downwards. The headline number is the same; the prognosis is opposite. Reading the three together is what the supervisor will probe; reading them in isolation is what the junior analyst will do.

The second skill is spotting policy effects in the time series. Most apparent breaks in the ODR series are not credit-cycle breaks; they are policy breaks. Affordability rules tightened, credit-score cut-offs raised, collections team restructured, write-off threshold moved. Each leaves a fingerprint in the data: tightening affordability raises the average score of new originations and drives the front-book ODR down; restructured collections raises the apparent cure rate temporarily; a write-off-threshold change shifts balances from late-arrears into write-off and apparently improves the live-book LGD. The development team must maintain a policy timeline alongside the descriptive series and reconcile every break to either a policy change, an economic event or an exclusion.

The third skill is scepticism about monotonicity. Most metrics should be approximately monotonic across arrears stages (utilisation rises, transactor rate falls, ODR is undefined for already-defaulted, re-default rate rises with prior stage). When monotonicity breaks, the modelling team should be able to explain it. The most common explanations: write-off treatment, dormancy treatment, sample-period mix. When the explanation is "I do not know", the metric should not be used as a feature or a back-test until the explanation is found.
