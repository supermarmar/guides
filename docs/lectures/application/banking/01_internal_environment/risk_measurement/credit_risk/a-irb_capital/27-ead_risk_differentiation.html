EAD risk differentiation: segmentation and the GLM

Fit a Generalised Linear Model on the EADF target per segment. The model class is GLM (Gaussian distribution with Identity link in the standard retail build, alternatives Gamma + Log or Beta + Logit tested and rejected); segmentation is hierarchical and utilisation-driven; variable selection runs the five-criterion iterative refinement adapted for continuous targets. The output is a PiT EADF per facility that ranks exposure-intensity risk; calibration to LRA and downturn happens in the next lecture.

Step 1

The 30-second version

EAD risk differentiation is the rank-ordering stage of the EAD build. The development team segments the portfolio into homogeneous risk groups (typically 8 to 15, dominated by utilisation bands), fits a Generalised Linear Model in each segment with the EADF as the target, and tests every coefficient against five criteria adapted from PD (statistical significance, low collinearity, intuitive sign, meaningful incremental discrimination, coverage across data sources). The standard model specification is Gaussian distribution with Identity link: predicted EADF is a linear function of the features, with coefficients interpretable in raw EADF units.

Three architectural choices shape the lecture. First, segmentation cascades hierarchically: delinquency status (current vs delinquent), then seasoning (early MOB vs seasoned), then limit (under £1,000 vs higher), then activity (active vs inactive), then internal utilisation bands (decision-tree splits at typically 10%, 20%, 50%, 60%, 90%), then external bureau utilisation. Each split is driven by R-squared / Gini / Lift on the EADF target and decision-tree analysis on the data. Second, the GLM choice is empirical: Gaussian + Identity is the wiki's reference choice, but Gamma + Log and Beta + Logit are tested as alternatives; the choice depends on the empirical EADF distribution within the segment. Third, the limit-change feature is implicit, not explicit: limit increases and decreases are reflected in historical EADFs that the model is fitted on, but no "future limit change" feature enters the model directly (which would be a leakage).

This lecture is one half of the EAD modelling phase. Risk differentiation produces an EADF ranking; risk quantification (lecture 28) anchors the ranking to LRA and downturn levels. The two phases run sequentially; the GLM choice in this lecture feeds the calibration choices in the next.

Feynman test The GLM is the generalisation of OLS that handles non-normal targets via a link function. Pick Gaussian + Identity and you have OLS. Pick Gamma + Log and you have a regression that handles right-skewed targets. Pick Beta + Logit and you have a regression that respects the [0, 1] bound. The empirical EADF distribution decides which one to pick; in retail the answer is usually Gaussian + Identity because EADF is approximately symmetric within most segments after the triple-cap from lecture 26 has cleaned the tails.
Step 2

Why this matters

The choice of GLM specification matters because it determines how coefficients are interpreted, which feature transformations make sense, and what stability properties the model has. Gaussian + Identity gives the most interpretable coefficients (a one-unit change in feature x produces a beta-unit change in predicted EADF) at the cost of allowing predictions outside [0, 1] (handled by the triple cap from lecture 26). Gamma + Log handles right-skewed targets natively but requires log-space interpretation of coefficients. Beta + Logit respects the [0, 1] bound but is more complex to fit and validate. The empirical literature (Tong 2016 and others) and the wiki's reference build both land on Gaussian + Identity for retail card portfolios; modellers should be able to articulate why this is the empirically supported choice rather than treating it as a default.

Segmentation is even more consequential. EAD's dominant risk driver (utilisation) varies enormously across the portfolio: a transactor at 5% utilisation has a fundamentally different EADF distribution from a revolver at 90% utilisation. A single regression over the pooled portfolio would average these distributions and produce a model that fits no segment well. The utilisation-band-driven segmentation cascade is what lets each segment have its own GLM coefficients on a homogeneous-EADF population.

The implicit treatment of limit changes is the third decision worth understanding. A limit increase between observation and default raises the customer's available headroom and (sometimes) raises EADF; a limit decrease does the opposite. The temptation is to include "limit change in window" as a feature; the problem is that at the time of prediction (observation t) the model does not know what future limit changes will occur. Including future limit changes as a feature is a data-leakage violation. The standard practice is therefore to let limit-change effects flow through the historical training data implicitly: the LRA and downturn calibration in lecture 28 reflect averaged limit-management patterns over the historical window.

Step 4

The detail

The GLM specification

The Generalised Linear Model framework relaxes two restrictive assumptions of linear regression:

  • It allows non-normal distributions for the dependent variable (Gamma, Beta, Poisson, etc.).
  • It permits flexible link functions g(.) between the linear predictor and the expected response.

The general form:

g( E[y_i] )  =  beta_0  +  beta_1 * x_i1  +  ...  +  beta_k * x_ik

       where  y_i      = realised EADF for facility i in this segment
              g(.)     = link function (identity, log, logit)
              x_ij     = feature j for facility i (engineered per lecture 26)
              beta_j   = coefficients, estimated by MLE

Three GLM specifications are tested empirically:

GLM specificationLink function g(.)DistributionWhen chosen
Gaussian + Identity (standard)identityNormalEADF approximately symmetric within segment; the wiki's reference choice and the retail standard
Gamma + LoglogGamma (right-skewed, positive support)EADF right-skewed within segment; loss-rate framing more natural than recovery-rate framing
Beta + LogitlogitBeta (bounded in [0, 1])EADF strictly in (0, 1) within segment with bimodal tails; less common in retail

The standard retail-card answer is Gaussian + Identity after diagnostic testing. The wiki's documentation: "Following diagnostic testing, all final models were specified as Gaussian distribution with Identity link, as this combination provided the best fit and interpretability across portfolios." MLE estimation is performed in Python (statsmodels), R (glm or betareg) or equivalent; standard convergence diagnostics apply.

The hierarchical segmentation cascade

Segmentation precedes GLM fitting and produces homogeneous EADF populations within each leaf segment. The wiki's six-level cascade for retail card portfolios:

  1. Delinquency status at observation: current vs delinquent. A delinquent facility is closer to default by construction and has a different EADF dynamic.
  2. Seasoning: early month-on-book (EMOB) vs seasoned. EMOB facilities have limited behavioural data and rely more on application-form features; the cut-off is determined empirically via decision-tree analysis (typically MOB ≤ 3 to 6).
  3. Limit banding: low limit (under £1,000) vs higher. Low-limit facilities have a different drawdown-to-limit dynamic than typical accounts.
  4. Activity: active vs inactive accounts. Inactive accounts (no activity in last 4 months) rarely show large EADF moves and benefit from a dedicated segment.
  5. Internal utilisation bands: 10%, 20%, 50%, 60%, 90% cut-offs determined by decision-tree analysis with EADF as target. The dominant differentiator; produces 5 to 6 sub-segments.
  6. External (bureau) utilisation: a further split on the obligor's utilisation across other lenders' lines, where data is available.

The cascade produces 8 to 15 leaf segments for retail unsecured; each gets its own GLM. The split criteria (R-squared / Gini / Lift on EADF target) determine the cut-offs; decision trees identify the optimal split points within each level.

Within each segment, candidate features (from the engineered set in lecture 26) are tested against five criteria. The criteria mirror PD's iterative refinement but with continuous-target metrics:

Coefficients with signs opposite to the table are investigated. The remediation hierarchy is the same as PD: check for multicollinearity, re-examine transformation, consider segmentation, document the sign-flip with explicit justification if retained. The supervisor will probe any retained sign-flips at SREP.

Limit changes: implicit treatment

Limit increases and decreases between observation and default materially affect the realised EADF: a limit-up increases available headroom and (sometimes) increases EADF; a limit-down does the opposite. The temptation is to include "future limit change" as a feature. The problem is that at observation time t the model cannot know what future limit changes will occur; including them would be a leakage.

The standard treatment is implicit capture: the historical training data contains facilities that experienced limit changes between observation and default, so the realised EADFs reflect averaged limit-management patterns. The LRA EADF calibration in lecture 28 then provides the long-run average over the cycle, which absorbs the cycle effect on limit-management.

The alternative explicit treatment (including a "limit-management policy" indicator as a feature) is sometimes used by banks with stable limit-management strategies; the indicator captures whether the customer's segment is in a proactive-CLI programme. This is operationally defensible but adds policy dependency to the model that complicates monitoring.

Sub-population testing

Once the segment-level GLMs have converged, the composite EAD model is tested across sub-populations:

  • Utilisation bands at observation
  • Credit limit bands
  • Product / partner type
  • External score bands
  • Origination cohort (vintage)

The diagnostics are MAE / RMSE / MPE per sub-population. Sub-populations with materially weaker performance trigger either segment-specific re-modelling, a partner / product flag, or an MoC overlay (lecture 30).

Overall model performance

For a well-built retail card EAD model:

  • R-squared per segment: 0.2 to 0.5 (lower than for PD because utilisation alone explains so much that within-segment, after segmenting on utilisation, the residual signal is limited).
  • Overall portfolio MAE: 5 to 10 percentage points on the development sample, similar on OOS, up to 3 pp of decay on OOT.
  • Spearman rho on realised vs predicted EADF: 0.5 to 0.7 overall.

The metric levels are similar in spirit to LGD's discrimination metrics (both are continuous-target models); the supervisor's framing for EAD validation reads at the same scale.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first lesson is that the segmentation cascade does most of the discrimination work. A model with no segmentation (single GLM over the pooled portfolio) typically achieves R-squared of 0.35 on EADF; the same model with hierarchical segmentation achieves overall R-squared of 0.6 to 0.7 once segment-level fits are weighted appropriately. The within-segment GLM coefficients then add incremental signal but rarely dominate. Modellers who under-invest in segmentation and over-invest in within-segment features produce models that underperform OOT.

The second lesson is on Gaussian + Identity. The choice is mildly counter-intuitive at first (the EADF distribution does not look perfectly normal, especially with the spike near 1 from full-drawdown customers), but after the triple-cap from lecture 26 the within-segment distribution is close enough to symmetric that Gaussian + Identity gives the best fit. Gamma + Log is the natural-looking alternative for the right-skewed segments; Beta + Logit is the bounded-in-[0,1] alternative. Empirically neither beats Gaussian + Identity by enough to justify the loss of coefficient interpretability and the added validation complexity. The wiki's choice is supported by industry literature.

The third lesson is on the limit-change debate. The temptation to include limit-change features is real because they are strongly correlated with realised EADF. The supervisor's pushback is real because they create a leakage. The right answer is the implicit treatment: let limit-management patterns flow through the historical training data and rely on the LRA / downturn calibration to absorb cycle effects on limit-management. Banks that try clever explicit treatments (using lagged limit changes as features, for instance) sometimes find them works on the development sample but fail on OOS because the cycle changes the pattern.

The fourth lesson is structural: the EAD GLM is the simplest of the three modelling steps in this track. PD requires logistic regression plus mixture-stratification within retail, LGD requires three regressions plus closed-form aggregation, EAD requires one regression per segment. The discipline is the same but the moving parts are fewer. Modellers should not let the structural simplicity tempt them into over-feature work; the calibration in lecture 28 will catch over-feature artefacts on OOT.
