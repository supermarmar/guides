Risk differentiation: segmentation and the logistic regression

The PiT model build. Segment the portfolio into homogeneous risk groups, fit a binary logistic regression in each segment, and iterate variable selection against five regulatory criteria until the model rank-orders obligors well, generalises across sub-populations, and is defensible to a credit officer. Calibration to absolute PD levels happens in the next lecture.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Risk differentiation is the rank-ordering stage of the PD build. The development team segments the portfolio into roughly 8 to 12 homogeneous groups, fits a binary logistic regression in each segment using the features that survived feature engineering, and tests every coefficient against five criteria: statistical significance (p < 1%), low collinearity (VIF < 3), intuitive sign, meaningful incremental discrimination (incremental Gini ≥ 0.25%), and coverage across data sources (at least five predictors per segment drawn from both internal and external sources). The output is a PiT PD per obligor that ranks risk; it is not yet a calibrated probability in the absolute sense.

Three architectural choices shape the lecture. First, segmentation: rather than fit a single model over the whole book, the portfolio is split into segments that share underlying risk drivers (delinquency state, age on book, activity status, recent-history cleanliness, utilisation level). A decision-tree analysis over historical default rates produces the initial splits; the splits are then rounded and rationalised to avoid over-fitting. Second, the logistic regression itself: binary outcome, logit link, maximum-likelihood estimation via iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS). Alternatives (multinomial logistic, nested logistic, decision trees, random forests) are considered and rejected for reasons covered below. Third, the dual-model approach: a full-sample model and a post-COVID-only model are fit in parallel; the final variable set is the intersection of variables that score well in both, which produces a model robust to the structural break in 2020 to 2022.

This lecture is one half of the IRB modelling phase. Risk differentiation produces a ranking; risk quantification (lecture 14) converts the ranking into calibrated, regulator-grade PDs anchored to the LRA. Both are required for compliant capital. A team that bundles them produces a model with reasonable Gini and a misaligned absolute level, which is the most common technical failure mode at supervisory validation.

Feynman test The job in this lecture is to answer "who in the portfolio is more likely to default than whom". The job in the next lecture is to answer "what is the actual probability of default for each person". The two questions sound similar; they are not. The first is a discrimination question, solved by fitting a model that maximises a metric like Gini. The second is a calibration question, solved by anchoring the model's outputs to the long-run average of observed default rates. A team that confuses the two will get the discrimination right and the absolute level wrong by a factor of two or three.
Step 2

Why this matters

Segmentation is the first design choice that propagates everywhere. A portfolio modelled as a single homogeneous group with one regression has all its risk drivers averaged across segments: the same coefficient on "utilisation" applies to the customer who is in early arrears and the customer who is performing cleanly. Both are real customers, but the relationship between utilisation and default is very different in the two segments (steep in early arrears, modest in clean performing). Averaging is statistically valid but operationally weak; the resulting scorecard ranks obligors less effectively than a segmented model, and the supervisor will ask why segmentation was not attempted.

The choice of logistic regression also matters. The dominant alternative is a tree-based ML approach (random forest, gradient-boosted trees). On the same features, ML approaches typically deliver 1 to 3 Gini points of additional discrimination. The reasons banks still choose logistic regression are governance-related rather than statistical: every coefficient is interpretable, the regression is easy to explain to a credit committee, the validation methodology is mature and well-trodden, and the supervisor has decades of comfort with the approach. The Gini advantage of an ML model is not large enough to overcome the governance disadvantage; almost every retail A-IRB scorecard in production today is a logistic regression.

The dual-model approach (full sample plus post-COVID) is the modern modelling team's answer to the structural break problem. A model fit only on the full sample averages the pre-COVID and post-COVID relationships and may include variables that were predictive pre-COVID but are no longer informative post-COVID. A model fit only on post-COVID data has a small sample and weak cycle coverage. The intersection of variables that score well in both fits is the robust set that the production model uses. This pattern repeats whenever a major regime change has occurred in the development window.

Step 3

The mental model

Risk differentiation is the first phase of a two-phase build. The diagram below puts the whole modelling pipeline in one view: feature engineering on the left (lecture 12, done), risk differentiation in the middle (this lecture), risk quantification on the right (lecture 14, next). Each phase has a distinct output and a distinct regulatory anchor.

Three properties of the architecture to internalise. First, the two phases are separately validated: the validator runs discrimination tests on the Phase 1 output (Gini, AUC, KS) and calibration tests on the Phase 2 output (binomial, chi-square, MPE). Conflating them produces a model that passes one test and fails the other. Second, the segmentation in Phase 1 defines the perimeter for everything downstream: grades are built per segment, the LRA is calculated per segment, MoC is applied per segment. A late change to segmentation forces a rebuild of every later step. Third, the dual-model approach is not a hedge against being wrong; it is a tool for identifying which variables are robust. The final model is not the average of the two fits; it is a single model whose variable set is the intersection of variables that both fits found important.

Step 4

The detail

Logistic regression: the model class

The binary logistic regression maps a linear combination of features to a probability in [0, 1] via the logit link. With features x_1 ... x_n and coefficients beta_0 ... beta_n:

logit( p ) = log( p / (1 - p) ) = beta_0 + beta_1 * x_1 + ... + beta_n * x_n

p = exp(y) / (1 + exp(y))   where  y = beta_0 + beta_1 * x_1 + ...

An increase of one unit in feature x_j shifts the log-odds of default by beta_j units; equivalently, it multiplies the odds of default by exp(beta_j). The coefficients are estimated by Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE). There is no closed-form solution for the logistic likelihood maximiser; the standard numerical algorithm is Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares (IRLS), which converges in a small number of iterations for well-conditioned problems and produces coefficient estimates plus standard errors plus a covariance matrix (used for VIF and significance testing).

Alternative model classes considered and rejected

The wiki lists four alternatives that are considered and discarded in standard IRB builds:

AlternativeWhat it offersWhy rejected for IRB
Multinomial logistic regressionModels more than two outcome classes simultaneouslyImposes the Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives assumption, which is unrealistic when credit outcomes are correlated. Also unnecessary for a binary default / non-default target.
Nested (sequential) logistic regressionRelaxes the IIA assumption via sequential decision modellingMathematically sound but adds complexity without commensurate benefit on a binary target; the dual-model approach addresses the same concerns within standard logistic.
Decision treesHandle non-linearity natively, naturally interpretable as if-then rulesProne to overfitting; coefficients (or splits) less stable than logistic; harder to back-test under stress; less mature validation methodology.
Random forests / gradient boostingOften deliver 1 to 3 Gini points of additional discrimination on the same featuresBlack-box characterisation; validation team and supervisor comfort is weaker; coefficient interpretation is replaced by feature-importance ranking which is less precise; harder to defend at SREP.

The choice of binary logistic regression is therefore not a statement that logistic is the best discriminator; it is a statement that the trade-off between interpretability, governance maturity and discrimination favours logistic in the regulatory context.

Segmentation: the first design choice

Segmentation produces a small number of homogeneous risk pools within which a single set of regression coefficients applies. The aims are:

  1. Each segment shares underlying risk drivers with a similar relationship to default.
  2. Each segment has sufficient volume for statistically stable coefficient estimation.
  3. Each segment is stable over time, so the same segment definitions can be used across the development window.

The standard approach uses decision-tree analysis over historical default rates, taking multiple stacked snapshots from across the development window (typically four to six snapshots, spanning benign and stressed periods to mitigate seasonal and cyclical biases). The tree's splits are then rounded (cut-points moved to neat values like 40% and 80% rather than 41.1% and 81.8%) to avoid over-fitting to the development sample. The reference build in the wiki produces ten segments via the following hierarchical splits:

  1. Delinquency status at observation: in-order vs early delinquency (1 cycle past due) vs late delinquency (2 to 3 cycles past due).
  2. For in-order accounts: Seasoned vs Early Month on Book (EMOB); EMOB = MOB ≤ 3. EMOB obligors have limited behavioural data so their risk drivers are mostly application and bureau features.
  3. For seasoned accounts: active vs inactive. Inactive accounts often have proactive credit-limit decreases and a distinct risk pattern.
  4. For active accounts: clean vs dirty history based on whether any delinquency occurred in the past 12 months.
  5. For clean active accounts: internal utilisation bands (low / medium / high; rounded cut-offs at 40% and 80%).
  6. For the low-utilisation segment: further split by maximum bureau utilisation in the last 6 months (cut at 60%).
  7. For low-internal-and-bureau utilisation: further split by revolver vs non-revolver flag.

The result is a population breakdown roughly as follows (the volumes are dataset-specific but the structural pattern is generalisable):

The default rates span more than two orders of magnitude across segments. A single regression over all ten segments would average these differences out; the segmented approach lets each one have its own coefficient set.

The dual-model approach (full sample plus post-COVID)

Within each segment, two logistic regressions are fit in parallel:

  1. Full development sample: all snapshots in the development window, all transformed variables.
  2. Post-COVID subset: only snapshots from after the COVID exclusion window, all transformed variables.

From each model, the top 25 variables by marginal lift are identified. Variables that rank highly in both the full-sample and post-COVID fits are prioritised for the final model. This intersection step is the dual-model approach's value: it identifies variables whose predictive relationship survived the regime change, which is the property that matters for production use.

Iterative refinement: the five criteria

Once the candidate variable set is identified, each surviving variable is tested against five criteria. Variables that fail any criterion are dropped, the regression is re-fit, and the process iterates until all retained variables pass all criteria simultaneously.

CriterionThresholdRationale
Statistical significancep-value < 1%Confirms each variable contributes meaningfully; tighter than the conventional 5% to compensate for multiple testing in a large feature set
CollinearityVIF < 3Stricter than the conventional VIF < 10; reflects supervisory preference for highly stable coefficients
Intuitive sign and shapeConsistent with business logic in log-odds spaceThe coefficient direction must match the credit-officer's intuition (utilisation positive, payment-amount negative, etc.); otherwise the variable is dropped or transformed
Marginal discriminationIncremental Gini ≥ 0.25%Each retained variable must provide measurable lift after the others; below this threshold the variable is not contributing
Coverage across data sources≥ 5 predictors per segment, mix of internal and external, across multiple risk categoriesImproves model balance and resilience; prevents over-reliance on any single data feed

Sign conventions: the directionality table

The "intuitive sign" criterion is operationalised through a directionality table. Each variable category has an expected sign in log-odds space; coefficients with the wrong sign are flagged for investigation before the model is accepted. The wiki reference table for retail credit cards:

CategoryExpected signExamples
Balance+Outstanding balance, recent balance trend
Utilisation+Internal utilisation, bureau utilisation, max-utilisation in window
Cash advance+Cash-advance volume and frequency
Financial charge+Interest and fee burden
Revolver flag+Customer revolves rather than transacts
Delinquency+Days past due, months in arrears, prior delinquency count
Late fee+Late-fee occurrence and amount
Inquiry+Bureau enquiries in the recent past

Coefficients with a sign opposite to the expected one usually indicate a multicollinearity artefact (Step 5 of reduction missed something), an interaction with a segment-specific feature, or a transformation that needs adjusting. The standard remediation is to investigate, refit, and either retain the variable with a documented sign-flip justification or drop it.

Sub-population testing

Once a segment's logistic regression has converged and passed the five criteria, it is tested across key sub-populations to confirm performance is consistent. Standard sub-populations:

  • External score (e.g. FICO) bands: under 600, 600 to 699, 700 and above.
  • Credit-limit bands: low, medium, high.
  • Utilisation bands: under 30%, 30 to 70%, over 70%.
  • Strategic partners or product lines.

For each sub-population, the Gini, average ODR, average predicted PD and relative-prediction-error are computed. Sub-populations with materially weaker Gini or higher prediction error are investigated. Where the issue is a portfolio-specific risk pattern (a new product, a run-off book, a higher mortality-driven default rate), a partner flag or interaction variable is added to the model to capture the differential.

Overall model performance

The ten segment models are combined into a single portfolio-level PiT PD model. The combined model is evaluated on:

  • Development sample: typically 75 to 80% overall Gini for a well-built retail card model.
  • Out-of-time (OOT) sample: the most recent reserved snapshot. Gini should hold within a few percentage points of development.
  • Sub-populations: Gini, ODR, predicted PD and relative error reported per band per segment.

The deliverables are an accuracy curve (predicted PD vs realised ODR per decile), a Lorenz curve (cumulative bad-rate vs cumulative population, ordered by predicted PD; the area between this curve and the diagonal is the Gini), and a comparative Gini chart across segments and sub-populations.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The most important lesson in risk differentiation is that segmentation is the design choice with the highest leverage. A poorly segmented model with the best possible features will always under-perform a well-segmented model with average features. The diagnostic is the spread of default rates across segments: a portfolio that defaults at 0.3% in one segment and 75% in another has been segmented effectively, because a single regression cannot describe both populations with one coefficient set. A portfolio whose segments all default at 1 to 5% has either been segmented along the wrong dimensions or genuinely is homogeneous, and the modelling team should investigate which it is before proceeding.

The second lesson is on the trade-off between Gini and interpretability. An ML model on the same features will typically deliver 1 to 3 Gini points more. The temptation to use it is real, especially when modelling teams have ML expertise on hand. The right answer for IRB capital is to stick with logistic regression and capture the marginal lift in two other places: better segmentation (the trees-as-segmenter approach gets the ML's non-linear handling at the segment-definition stage) and better feature engineering (Tukey transformations, splines, interactions captured in the regression). Practitioners who push hardest for ML are often the ones who have under-invested in segmentation and transformation; both have higher governance return per unit of Gini lift.

The third lesson is on the dual-model approach. The temptation is to skip it ("we have enough post-COVID data now"); the discipline is to keep running both regressions every recalibration cycle until the variable sets converge stably. The intersection step is what makes the model robust to the next regime change, which we can be confident will happen and cannot predict the timing of. The cost is two fits per segment per recalibration, which is trivial on modern infrastructure.

The fourth lesson is operational: the iterative refinement process is where most junior modellers underestimate the time required. Each iteration is a fit, a coefficient inspection, a sign check, a Gini test, a sub-population test, a discussion with the model owner. Each iteration can take half a day to a day. A segment typically needs five to ten iterations to converge. A ten-segment portfolio with five to ten iterations per segment is fifty to a hundred iterations, which is two to three months of disciplined work. Modellers who try to compress this into a sprint produce models that fail at validation, which costs much more than the compressed schedule saved.
