EAD testing: discrimination, calibration and stability

The closing lecture of the EAD sequence. Back-tests of the composite PiT EADF and the calibrated regulatory EADF, run on OOS and OOT samples and re-run quarterly in production. The two-block battery from PD and LGD transfers with three EAD-specific adjustments: continuous-target metrics throughout, the max-of-paths binding check on the regulatory EADF, and the implicit-limit-change diagnostic.

Step 1

The 30-second version

EAD testing closes the validation cycle for everything the previous six EAD lectures produced. The same two-block architecture as PD and LGD applies. Block A differentiation tests validate the composite PiT EADF from lecture 27: accuracy via MAE / RMSE / MPE; discrimination via Spearman rho and R-squared; stability via PSI on the predicted-EADF distribution and CSI per feature; parameter sensitivity via GLM refit on OOS; robustness across utilisation bands, limit bands, MoB and partners; stress via input shocks; benchmarking where external EAD datasets are available. Block B calibration tests validate the regulatory EADF from lecture 28: LRA EADF accuracy against realised, downturn EADF adequacy against 2008 to 2009 realised, grade monotonicity (SS4/24 §11.31), concentration (HHI), grade migration, and the EAD-specific max-of-paths binding check that confirms which of the two regulatory-EADF paths (LRA + MoC_LRA or Downturn + MoC_DT) binds per grade and whether the binding is stable across cohorts.

Three structural adjustments versus PD testing matter. First, continuous-target metrics replace binary metrics throughout, exactly as in LGD testing: Spearman rho replaces Gini, MAE / RMSE / MPE replace classification accuracy, t-test replaces binomial z-test for grade-level heterogeneity. Second, the max-of-paths binding check is unique to EAD: per grade, the bank reports which path binds (typically the downturn + MoC path) and tracks the bound choice's stability over time. Third, the implicit-limit-change diagnostic tests whether the model's outputs reflect limit-management actions appropriately even though limit-change features were not included in the regression (lecture 27).

The legal anchor is CRR Article 185 (validation of internal estimates), the supervisory anchor is PRA SS4/24 §17. The test battery is also the production monitoring battery, run quarterly to detect drift and trigger material model change (lecture 32). Anchored in the small EAD development sample, the test battery has fewer rows but tighter discipline than the equivalent PD battery; the supervisor's standard is the same.

Feynman test PD testing asked whether the binary default flag could be predicted; LGD testing asked whether a continuous noisy loss rate matched realised outcomes; EAD testing asks whether a continuous bounded exposure factor matches realised drawdown behaviour. The continuous-target framing is the same as LGD's, so the metrics transfer. The max-of-paths construction is unique to EAD, so the calibration check transfers from neither PD nor LGD; it is the new diagnostic this lecture introduces.
Step 2

Why this matters

EAD validation has historically been the lightest of the three IRB parameters, partly because the wiki documentation is thinnest there and partly because the supervisor focuses on PD and LGD where the capital sensitivity is higher. The risk is that an EAD model that under-predicts drawdown can silently inflate the gap between modelled EAD and realised EAD over the cycle, with the realised side rising in stress while the modelled side stays anchored. The max-of-paths regulatory rule from lecture 28 is designed to prevent this; the testing in this lecture is what verifies the rule has worked.

The implicit-limit-change diagnostic matters because limit-management policy is the single most consequential operational lever for EAD outputs. A bank that switches from proactive credit-line increases to a more conservative limit-down strategy will see EADFs drift downward; if the model does not pick up the drift, the modelled EAD will overpredict on the new policy regime. The diagnostic checks that the model's predicted EADFs on the application portfolio remain close to the realised EADFs from comparable historical cohorts, after controlling for utilisation and limit composition. Where the divergence is material the bank either retrains on more recent data, adds a policy flag, or carries an MoC overlay.

For F107 candidates the EAD testing material is exam-classic on three counts: the continuous-target metric framing (Spearman rho, MAE versus PD's Gini), the max-of-paths binding check (a quantitative test that combines lecture 28's regulatory construction with lecture 29's diagnostics), and the absence of a SS4/24 §15.7-style reference value (EAD has nothing equivalent, which makes the max-of-paths conservatism do the floor work instead).

Step 3

The mental model

The two-block battery from PD lecture 15 and LGD lecture 22 maps onto EAD with the same structural adjustments as LGD (continuous-target metrics) plus the max-of-paths binding check that is unique to EAD.

Three properties to internalise. First, the continuous-target metrics are identical to LGD's: a model that scores Spearman rho 0.6 on EADF is doing the same job as one that scores Gini 80% on PD (a continuous-target Spearman is not directly comparable in level to a binary Gini, as discussed in lecture 22). Second, the max-of-paths binding check is the EAD-unique diagnostic: it confirms the bank's regulatory construction is doing the conservatism work the supervisor expects. Third, the implicit-limit-change diagnostic closes the loop on the design choice from lecture 27 (limit changes not included as features); the test verifies the model still picks up limit-management effects through the historical training data.

Step 4

The detail

Block A: differentiation testing

A1. Composite accuracy: MAE, RMSE, MPE

Same three metrics as LGD testing (lecture 22), applied to predicted EADF versus realised EADF on OOS and OOT samples:

MAE  = mean over facilities of  | predicted_EADF - realised_EADF |
RMSE = sqrt( mean over facilities of  ( predicted_EADF - realised_EADF )^2 )
MPE  = mean over facilities of  ( predicted_EADF - realised_EADF ) / realised_EADF

Typical retail card benchmarks: MAE 5 to 10 pp, RMSE 10 to 15 pp, MPE within ±5% at the portfolio level and ±10% at the segment level. EAD metrics typically come in tighter than LGD because the target is less noisy: realised EADF is largely determined by utilisation at observation (which is observable), whereas realised LGD depends on a 60-month workout track with many sources of variance.

A2. Composite discrimination: Spearman rho, R-squared

Same continuous-target machinery as LGD. Spearman rho is the rank correlation between predicted and realised EADF; typical retail benchmarks 0.5 to 0.7. R-squared is the coefficient of determination on the GLM fit; typical per-segment values 0.2 to 0.5 (lower than aggregate because within-segment heterogeneity is smaller).

A3 and A4. Stability (PSI and CSI)

Same machinery as PD and LGD. PSI on the predicted EADF distribution between development sample and OOT / application; CSI on each GLM feature (per segment, since the GLM is segmented). Thresholds: PSI / CSI < 0.10 stable; 0.10 to 0.25 watch; > 0.25 material drift.

A5. Parameter sensitivity

Each segment's GLM is re-fit on the OOS sample; coefficients compared to development. Same two checks as PD / LGD: sign stability (coefficients should not flip between development and OOS) and significance stability (p-values stay below 0.01 on the refit). For Gaussian + Identity GLMs the standard machinery applies; for Gamma + Log or Beta + Logit alternatives the link-specific diagnostics (dispersion parameter stability) are also reported.

A6. Robustness across sub-populations

Per-sub-population MAE and Spearman computed across:

  • Utilisation bands at observation (under 30%, 30-70%, over 70%)
  • Credit-limit bands
  • Months on book
  • External score bands
  • Strategic partner or product line

Persistent under-performance in any sub-population triggers segment-specific re-modelling, partner-flag interactions, or MoC overlay.

A7. Stress tests

EAD-specific stress shocks differ from PD or LGD because the inputs are different:

  • Utilisation shocks: shift at-observation utilisation up by 10 or 20 percentage points across the portfolio; re-derive predicted EADF.
  • Limit shocks: reduce limits by 10 or 20% (a hypothetical collections-action wave); re-derive.
  • Drawdown-rate shocks: increase the CCF assumption by 10 or 20%; re-derive.
  • Multi-factor combined shocks: utilisation up + limit down + drawdown up simultaneously.

Each shock produces an EADF response; the responses feed ICAAP stress-testing inputs and the bank's risk-appetite framework.

A8. External benchmarking

EAD external benchmarking is more limited than PD or LGD. Global Credit Data (GCD) maintains LGD pools but its EAD coverage is thinner; some industry surveys publish EADF distributions by product and region. Banks compare their per-segment regulatory EADF to industry pools where data is available. Material deviation triggers either documented justification (the bank's portfolio differs from the pool composition) or MoC adjustment.

A9. The implicit-limit-change diagnostic (EAD-specific)

The lecture-27 design excluded limit-change features from the GLM to avoid leakage; this test verifies the model still picks up limit-management effects through the historical training data. The diagnostic:

  1. Partition the OOS / application sample into facilities that experienced a limit increase in the 12 months before observation versus those that did not.
  2. Compare predicted EADF versus realised EADF separately for the two groups.
  3. The model should over-predict (slightly) for the limit-increase group (because the historical training data captured similar facilities at the moment of their limit increase, when realised drawdown was modest) and predict accurately for the no-limit-change group.
  4. If the model under-predicts for the limit-increase group materially, the implicit treatment has failed and the model needs a policy flag added.

The same diagnostic runs for limit-decrease facilities (collections-action lines). Both diagnostics together confirm the model is robust to the bank's limit-management policy.

Block B: calibration testing

B1. LRA EADF accuracy

For each grade, the calibrated LRA EADF is compared to the empirical realised EADF over the calibration window. The MPE:

MPE_LRA(grade)  =  ( LRA_EADF(grade) - realised_avg_EADF(grade) ) / realised_avg_EADF(grade)

By construction this should be close to zero. Non-zero MPE indicates the calibration sample's weighting or MoC application is inconsistent with the pure averaging definition.

B2. Downturn EADF adequacy

Per grade, the downturn EADF is compared to the realised EADF in cohorts within the identified downturn window (2008-2009). The downturn EADF should match or modestly exceed the realised; under-prediction is a red flag and triggers either downturn-window re-selection or an MoC top-up.

B3. Grade monotonicity (PRA SS4/24 §11.31)

The regulatory EADF must increase monotonically across grades. Two checks:

  • Plot regulatory EADF against grade. Monotonic by construction in the design but cross-overs can appear after MoC application; cross-overs are flagged for grade-boundary review.
  • Plot realised EADF against grade per cohort. Cross-overs at the same grade boundary across multiple cohorts indicate the boundary needs revisiting.

B4. Concentration (HHI)

Same construction as PD: HHI = sum of grade-volume-share squared. Threshold 0.25. Reported as a line chart over time. EAD's smaller grade count (10 to 15 versus PD's 26) makes HHI inherently higher than PD's; the 0.25 threshold still applies but interpretation is contextual.

B5. Grade migration

Per cohort transition, count the share of facilities that move between grades. Under a PiT philosophy some migration is expected; excessive migration creates RWA volatility. Standard report: migration matrices per cohort plus the share of facilities that stayed put / moved ±1 / moved ±2 or more bands. Most facilities should stay within ±1 band per cohort transition.

B6. Max-of-paths binding check (EAD-specific)

The unique EAD calibration diagnostic. Per grade, the bank reports which of the two regulatory paths binds:

Path 1 (LRA + MoC_LRA)         vs    Path 2 (Downturn + MoC_DT)

if Path 1 > Path 2: bind = LRA       (rare, indicates downturn period was mild for this grade)
else:               bind = downturn   (typical; downturn path is binding for most grades)

The diagnostic reports two metrics per grade:

  • Which path binds. Typical retail card outcome: downturn binds for 8 of 12 grades; LRA binds for grades at the very low end (where the downturn premium was small in absolute terms).
  • Stability of the binding. Over multiple cohort-on-cohort re-runs, does the binding stay the same? Material flipping between paths cohort-to-cohort indicates instability in the MoC overlays and triggers MoC review.

B7. Homogeneity and heterogeneity within and between grades

Adapted from PD lecture 15 for continuous targets. Homogeneity within grade: F-test on within-grade EADF variance across bootstrap sub-samples; null hypothesis is equal variances. Heterogeneity between adjacent grades: t-test on the difference in mean realised EADF between adjacent grades. Both tests report fail rates per grade per cohort; overall fail rate should sit below 5%.

The reporting pack

The standard EAD test report contains the same six artefacts as PD plus the two EAD-specific ones:

  1. Summary scoreboard with traffic-light status per test.
  2. Per-test detailed pages with tables and visualisations (predicted-vs-realised scatter per grade, MAE over time, PSI over time).
  3. Per-segment GLM result pages.
  4. The max-of-paths binding table per grade per cohort.
  5. The implicit-limit-change diagnostic chart (predicted vs realised by limit-change subgroup).
  6. External-benchmarking comparison (where available).
  7. Limitations register / MoC mapping for amber findings.
  8. Remediation register for red findings.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first lesson is that the max-of-paths binding check is the single most distinctive EAD calibration diagnostic. PD's analogous diagnostic is the LRA back-test; LGD's is the SS4/24 §15.7 reference value compliance. EAD's binding check tells the supervisor which conservatism mechanism (LRA + MoC or Downturn + MoC) is doing the work per grade. A well-built EAD model has the downturn path binding for most grades, with stable binding over cohorts. A model where the binding flips between cohorts has unstable MoC overlays and is amber-rated.

The second lesson is on the implicit-limit-change diagnostic. The supervisor's pushback on EAD models is often that they may not adequately capture limit-management effects. The diagnostic is the bank's response: show separately that the model predicts well on limit-increase facilities, limit-decrease facilities and no-limit-change facilities. Banks that include the diagnostic in their validation pack pre-empt the supervisor's question; banks that do not get asked at SREP.

The third lesson is on the absence of a reference-value test. LGD has SS4/24 §15.7 (the two-worst-years average) as a quantitative floor on downturn LGD. EAD has nothing equivalent. The max-of-paths construction plays the same conservatism role but is methodology-driven rather than data-driven. The supervisor's instinct on EAD is therefore to probe the MoC quantum more aggressively than for LGD, where the reference value gives them a hard external comparison point. Banks should expect more MoC dialogue on EAD than on LGD as a result.

The fourth lesson is on EAD validation in production. The same test battery runs quarterly in production. A green scoreboard at development that turns amber four quarters into production is a model drifting toward recalibration. For EAD specifically the most common drift signal is on the PSI of predicted EADF distribution: a shift in the application portfolio's utilisation mix (more revolvers, fewer transactors) moves the predicted EADF distribution rightward and shows up as PSI > 0.10 before any of the underlying realised metrics deteriorate. Modellers who treat PSI as an early warning rather than a lagging indicator catch issues 2 to 4 quarters earlier than those who wait for MAE to deteriorate.

The fifth and structurally most important: the EAD test battery closes the IRB-parameter modelling track. Lectures 9 through 29 have produced three calibrated regulatory parameters (PD, LGD, EAD) ready to feed the K formula in lecture 31. The MoC framework in lecture 30 wraps all three with the conservatism overlays that bridge known weaknesses to the regulatory output. The monitoring framework in lecture 32 runs the same test batteries quarterly in production. The IRB capital model is complete at that point; everything else (lecture 31 and beyond) is mechanical or governance.
