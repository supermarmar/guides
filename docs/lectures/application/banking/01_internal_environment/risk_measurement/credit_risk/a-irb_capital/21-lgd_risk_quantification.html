LGD risk quantification: LRA, downturn, ELBE and MoC

Turn the PiT composite LGD from lecture 20 into a regulator-grade LGD. Anchor each calibration segment to its long-run average loss rate, build the downturn LGD via the SS4/24 §15.7 reference value, construct ELBE for defaulted exposures, and add the MoC overlay. The result is the calibrated LGD per calibration segment that feeds the K formula in lecture 31.

Step 1

The 30-second version

LGD risk quantification is the calibration phase of the LGD build. The composite PiT LGD from lecture 20 is grouped into calibration segments (typically 5 to 15, much fewer than PD grades because the LGD population is smaller); each segment is calibrated to three regulator-relevant levels. The LRA LGD is the long-run average of realised LGDs in the segment over a cycle-covering window (CRR Article 181(1)(b) and (j)). The downturn LGD reflects loss severity in adverse economic conditions and is anchored at no less than the SS4/24 §15.7 reference value: the simple average of realised LGDs in the two years with the highest balance-weighted economic loss. ELBE (Expected Loss Best Estimate) is the analogous calibrated parameter for defaulted exposures (CRR Article 181(1)(h), SS4/24 §16).

The downstream consumption differs between LGD1 and LGD2. The downturn LGD from LGD1 enters the K formula for non-defaulted exposures as the LGD input. ELBE from LGD2 enters the K formula for defaulted exposures via K_defaulted = max(0, LGD_downturn − ELBE) × EAD: capital is held only against the stress portion that exceeds the bank's best expectation, since the bank should already be provisioning against the expected portion through IFRS 9. MoC overlays apply at the calibration-segment level under the same A / B / C categorisation as PD (data limitations, methodology weaknesses, general estimation error), feeding lecture 30.

Unlike PD, there is no regulatory floor on LGD equivalent to PD's 0.03%. The supervisor's conservatism on LGD instead comes through the discount-rate floor (max(SOFR + 5%, 9%) for downturn LGD per SS4/24 §13.17) and the reference-value constraint (SS4/24 §15.7). The principles-based regulatory framework allows more bank-specific design than PD and so generates more supervisory dialogue at SREP.

Feynman test The lecture-20 model ranks facilities by predicted loss severity but does not yet say what the absolute number is. The lecture-21 calibration says it. The same arithmetic logic as PD calibration applies (assign a single calibrated value per segment, anchored to a long-run average, uplifted for downturn and MoC), but the LGD regulatory texture is different: principles-based rather than prescriptive, with a specific two-worst-years reference value, and with two distinct downstream uses (downturn for performing, ELBE for defaulted). Holding both downstream uses in mind is what separates a clean LGD calibration from a confused one.
Step 2

Why this matters

Calibration is where the LGD model becomes consequential for capital. The PiT LGD from lecture 20 might rank facilities accurately but have an average level 10 to 20 percentage points below realised LGDs; the calibration step closes that gap and ensures the bank holds the right amount of capital. The supervisor's standard challenge is to compare the calibrated LGD per segment to the empirical realised LGDs in the same segment across multiple cohorts; persistent under-prediction triggers remediation. The LGD reference value (SS4/24 §15.7) gives the supervisor a hard floor: if the calibrated downturn LGD does not match or exceed the average of the two worst years' balance-weighted realised LGDs, the calibration is inadequate.

ELBE is the lesser-known but operationally important construct. CRR Article 181(1)(h) requires firms to compute a best estimate of expected loss for defaulted exposures, distinct from the downturn LGD. The construct sits at the boundary between the IRB capital regime and the IFRS 9 accounting regime: ELBE is conceptually similar to the IFRS 9 Stage 3 ECL but uses the IRB cost convention (indirect costs included). The K formula for defaulted exposures then says: hold capital against the stress portion (downturn LGD minus ELBE), because the bank's IFRS 9 provisions already cover the best-estimate portion. A bank whose ELBE is too low double-counts the loss in capital and accounting; one whose ELBE is too high under-capitalises.

For F107 candidates the lecture has three exam-classic constructs: the LGD reference value (a specific quantitative test the supervisor inspects), ELBE (the only place in the IRB framework where the K formula explicitly subtracts the bank's expectation from the stress estimate), and the principles-based versus prescriptive distinction between LGD and PD regulation. A candidate who can articulate all three understands the deeper architecture of the IRB framework.

Step 3

The mental model

LGD calibration has the same shape as PD calibration in lecture 14 but with three differences: fewer segments, principles-based downturn instead of LRA-anchored, and two distinct calibrated outputs (downturn LGD plus ELBE).

Three properties of the workflow matter. First, calibration is segment-level, not facility-level: the composite PiT LGD per facility is averaged across calibration segments to produce a single LGD per segment. This mirrors PD's grade-level calibration but with fewer segments. Second, the downturn LGD is constructed as a maximum across multiple candidate floors; the bank picks the most conservative of the four candidates, then justifies the choice. Third, ELBE is its own calibrated output: not a downturn LGD with a discount applied, but a separately constructed best estimate of remaining loss for defaulted exposures.

Step 4

The detail

Calibration segmentation

The composite PiT LGD per facility is grouped into a small number of calibration segments for which a single calibrated LGD is computed. Standard dimensions:

  • Collateral type: residential property, commercial property, vehicle, securities, unsecured. The dominant LGD differentiator and the first segmentation axis.
  • Cure / no-cure: cured facilities recover with low loss; no-cure facilities recover with high loss. The bimodality from the mixture model expresses itself most clearly along this axis.
  • Product type: credit card, personal loan, mortgage, overdraft, etc.
  • Strategic partner or origination channel, where material.

Typical retail card portfolios end up with 6 to 10 calibration segments. The same five CRR Article 170(3) design tests apply (volume between 1% and 30%, homogeneity within segment, heterogeneity between segments, granularity, stability), with the constraint that each segment have enough resolved defaults to support credible calibration; for thin segments the bank either pools with similar segments or carries an MoC overlay.

LRA LGD per segment

For each calibration segment, the LRA LGD is the average realised LGD over a cycle-covering window:

LRA_LGD(segment)  =  EAD-weighted mean over LRA window  of  realised_LGD_i
                     for resolved facilities i in this segment

Two weighting conventions exist:

  • EAD-weighted: each facility's realised LGD is weighted by its EAD at default. The standard for IRB. Reflects the loss-rate that the bank would have realised on a balance-weighted basis.
  • Default-count-weighted (the "observed average LGD" of SS4/24 §14.6): each facility's realised LGD is weighted equally. Used as a back-test, not as the calibration anchor.

The LRA window is the same one selected in lecture 18: typically 10+ years spanning the GFC downturn and at least one subsequent benign period. Two ways the LRA can be biased low:

  • The window excludes a downturn period (the LGD reference-value test will catch this).
  • Truncation at the MRP forces unresolved long-tail recoveries to 100% loss, but only late in the window where the MRP has elapsed; the early window contributes only fast-resolving cases.

The SS4/24 §15.7 reference value

PRA SS4/24 paragraph 15.7 requires the bank to calculate a reference value as a quantitative floor on the downturn LGD:

Reference value  =  simple average of realised LGDs in the two calendar years
                    with the highest total economic loss / total outstanding amount
                    (i.e. the two years with the highest balance-weighted LGD)

Computed at the calibration-segment level (and at the portfolio level). For most banks the two-worst-years correspond to 2009 and 2010, capturing the post-GFC workout-completion wave. The reference value is typically 10 to 20 percentage points above the LRA LGD for unsecured retail and 5 to 10 percentage points above for secured retail; the gap reflects how much stress the downturn period embedded compared to the cycle average.

Downturn LGD construction

Unlike PD's LRA where the calibration anchor is the LRA itself, LGD's downturn LGD is constructed as the maximum across multiple candidates:

LGD_downturn(segment)  =  max(
                              LRA_LGD(segment),
                              reference_value(segment),
                              downturn_period_average(segment),
                              add-on_methodology(segment)
                            )

Each candidate has its own role:

  • LRA LGD: the base. Downturn LGD cannot be lower than the LRA per CRR Article 181(1)(b).
  • Reference value: the SS4/24 §15.7 floor described above.
  • Downturn-period average: the realised LGD averaged over a pre-identified downturn window (typically 2008 to 2009, sometimes extended to 2010 to 2011 to capture workout-completion lag).
  • Add-on methodology: a bank-specific overlay (regression-based recovery-stress model, macro-driven add-on, expert-judgement uplift). The least standardised; most variation across banks; most supervisory dialogue.

The bank computes all four for each segment and picks the maximum. The choice is documented per segment with explicit justification. The supervisor will probe whether the bank tested all candidates honestly or selected a candidate that under-represented the downturn.

The 9% discount-rate floor

From SS4/24 §13.17 (already established in lecture 17): for downturn LGD calculations, the discount rate applied to recoveries is floored at max(SOFR + 5%, 9%). The 9% floor is the operational anchor that makes downturn LGD materially above LRA LGD for many banks: under the LRA convention (SOFR + 5% with no floor) the discount rate has been historically 5 to 7% in benign periods, so the move to a 9% floor adds 2 to 4 percentage points of discount drag, which translates into 3 to 6 percentage points of additional downturn LGD versus LRA.

ELBE construction

CRR Article 181(1)(h) requires the bank to compute a Best Estimate of Expected Loss (BEEL or ELBE) for each defaulted exposure: the bank's best estimate of the loss that will be realised, given that the obligor is already in default. SS4/24 §16 sets the operational expectations:

  • Per facility: ELBE is computed at the facility level, not at a calibration-segment level. Each defaulted facility has its own ELBE based on its workout-stage features.
  • Best estimate, not stressed: ELBE does not include a downturn add-on. It is the bank's expectation of the remaining loss, computed as the calibrated output of the LGD2 model from lecture 20.
  • IRB cost convention: indirect costs are included (unlike IFRS 9, which excludes them).
  • Discount rate: max(SOFR + 5%, 9%) per SS4/24 §13.18, the same as downturn LGD.
  • Update cadence: ELBE is updated regularly as the workout progresses; SS4/24 expects at least quarterly.

For the K formula for defaulted exposures:

K_defaulted_i  =  max( 0,  LGD_downturn(segment_i)  -  ELBE_i )  *  EAD_i

The intuition: the supervisor wants capital held against the stress portion of the loss on a defaulted exposure. The expected portion is already provisioned for through IFRS 9 / accounting expected loss. The K formula's LGD_downturn − ELBE isolates the stress portion; the max(0, ...) floor ensures K is non-negative even if ELBE happens to exceed LGD_downturn (which would indicate a model inconsistency the bank should investigate).

MoC overlay

MoC for LGD follows the same A / B / C categorisation as PD (full treatment in lecture 30).

  • Category A: data limitations. The dominant LGD MoC category. Thin segments, MRP truncation bias, cohort lag, structural breaks in workout strategy. The limitations register from lecture 07 feeds this directly.
  • Category B: methodology weaknesses. The mixture-model conditioning order, the GLM distributional choice, the debt-sale and CoR assumptions.
  • Category C: general estimation error. The residual statistical uncertainty in coefficient estimates and small-sample variance.

Total LGD MoC for a typical retail bank adds 5 to 15% to the calibrated LGD (relative; e.g. a calibrated 60% becomes 63 to 69%). Higher MoC reflects thinner data or known methodological gaps.

No regulatory floor on LGD

Unlike PD, which has a Basel 3.1 input floor at 0.03% (retail), LGD has no equivalent quantitative floor. The supervisor's conservatism instead comes through:

  • The 9% discount-rate floor on downturn / in-default cash flows (SS4/24 §13.17 to §13.18).
  • The reference value constraint (SS4/24 §15.7).
  • The Basel 3.1 LGD input floors that do exist: 25% for unsecured retail, 50% for secured corporate, 5% for residential mortgage where LTV is 50% or below (rising with LTV). These are Basel-3.1 introduced floors that bind only on very-low-LGD segments, much narrower than the PD floor.

The result: LGD calibration has more bank-specific design space than PD calibration, and more supervisor-specific challenge at SREP.

The deliverable: regulatory LGD per calibration segment

The output of risk quantification is a table similar in shape to the PD grade table from lecture 14 but with fewer rows. Each row contains:

segment    description           volume    realised_avg    LRA_LGD    ref_value    LGD_DT    ELBE    MoC    reg_LGD
1          unsecured no-cure      35%       72%             68%        78%          78%       65%     10%    86%
2          unsecured cure         25%       18%             16%        22%          22%       15%     10%    24%
3          secured residential    20%       28%             24%        32%          35%       22%     8%     38%
...

This table is the regulatory LGD model. It feeds the K formula in lecture 31 (downturn LGD column for non-defaulted, ELBE column for defaulted) and the IFRS 9 LGD pipeline (with indirect costs stripped out and the downturn add-on replaced by macro overlays).

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first lesson is that the principles-based nature of LGD calibration is both a feature and a hazard. It is a feature because the bank can incorporate its specific recovery operations, collateral practices and workout strategies into the calibration in ways PD's prescriptive framework does not allow. It is a hazard because the supervisor has more room to challenge the bank's design choices, and banks that have not thought carefully about every step (LRA window, reference-value periods, downturn-period definition, add-on methodology, MoC quantum) get caught at SREP. The right operating posture is to over-document every choice and stand ready to defend each one.

The second lesson is on the maximum-over-candidates construction. Building downturn LGD as max(LRA, reference, downturn period, add-on) is the deliberately conservative approach the regulator expects. Banks that try to construct downturn LGD by a single-method approach (only the add-on, only the downturn-period average) usually under-estimate; banks that take the max across all four candidates rarely do. The cost of being conservative on downturn LGD is real (RWA goes up); the cost of being non-conservative is much higher (supervisory remediation, capital add-on, reputational damage at SREP).

The third lesson is on ELBE. The construct is operationally subtle because it sits at the intersection of regulatory capital and accounting provisioning. The bank's IFRS 9 Stage 3 ECL covers the expected portion of the loss on a defaulted exposure; ELBE in the IRB framework covers the same thing but with the IRB cost convention. The difference between the two (a few percentage points in most banks) is whether indirect costs are included. A bank that conflates ELBE with Stage 3 ECL loses the IRB / IFRS 9 distinction; the supervisor will probe.

The fourth lesson is on the 9% discount-rate floor. It is the single most consequential mechanical lever in LGD calibration. Banks that pre-2018 used a low discount rate (some benign-funding-cost banks used 4 or 5% for everything) found their downturn LGDs jumping materially when SS4/24 introduced the 9% floor for downturn and in-default cash flows. Practitioners should be able to articulate why the floor exists (downturn raises bank funding costs) and why it makes downturn LGD materially above LRA LGD in mechanical terms (the higher discount rate is applied to the same cash flow profile, producing a lower PV of recoveries).

The fifth and structurally most important: LGD calibration is where the bank's specific operating model meets regulatory conservatism. The model from lecture 20 told you about the bank's recovery patterns; the calibration in this lecture tells you about the regulatory wrapper around them. Practitioners who hold both in mind simultaneously will produce calibrations that are tight (high model utilisation) and defensible (supervisory comfort); those who focus on one or the other produce calibrations that are either over-conservative (too high downturn LGD, RWA inflation) or unable to defend (under-conservative downturn LGD, remediation).

Test yourself

  1. State the SS4/24 §15.7 reference value formula. How is it computed per calibration segment, and what is its role in downturn LGD construction?
  2. The downturn LGD is constructed as a maximum across four candidates. Name each candidate and explain its role.
  3. Distinguish LRA LGD from downturn LGD. Why must the downturn LGD be no lower than the LRA LGD?
  4. Why is there no regulatory floor on LGD analogous to PD's 0.03%? What plays the role of conservatism instead?
  5. Write the K formula for defaulted exposures in terms of LGD_downturn, ELBE and EAD. Explain the role of the max(0, ...) operator.
  6. State three operational differences between ELBE and the IFRS 9 Stage 3 ECL.
  7. How does the 9% discount-rate floor affect the difference between downturn LGD and LRA LGD? Be specific about the mechanism.
  8. The LGD calibration table has fewer rows than the PD grade table. Why? What is the typical number of calibration segments for retail unsecured?
  9. A bank's LRA LGD for an unsecured-no-cure segment is 65%, its reference value is 78%, its downturn-period average is 72%, and its add-on methodology produces 80%. What is the downturn LGD?
  10. The MoC categories for LGD are the same as for PD (A, B, C). Name one LGD-specific example for each category.
