RWA assembly: the K formula and the output floor

The climax of the A-IRB sequence. Take the regulatory PD, LGD and EAD produced over the previous twenty-one lectures, plug them into the BCBS one-factor Vasicek formula to get the worst-case default rate at 99.9%, multiply by LGD and the maturity adjustment to get the capital requirement K per exposure, then multiply by 12.5 and EAD to get RWA. Apply Basel 3.1 input floors per parameter and the output floor at 72.5% of standardised RWA.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The K formula is the single equation that turns the bank's PD, LGD and EAD inputs into a capital requirement per exposure. It comes from the BCBS one-factor Vasicek model: each obligor's default depends on a systematic factor (the economy) and an idiosyncratic factor (the obligor); at the 99.9% one-year confidence level, the worst-case default rate (WCDR) for a portfolio is given in closed form. Multiplied by LGD and a maturity adjustment, the WCDR gives K, the capital ratio per unit of EAD. RWA = K × 12.5 × EAD then expresses the capital requirement in the standardised-approach denominator units.

Formally:

WCDR(PD, R)    = N( (N^-1(PD) + sqrt(R) * N^-1(0.999)) / sqrt(1 - R) )
MA(PD, M)      = (1 + (M - 2.5) * b(PD)) / (1 - 1.5 * b(PD))    where b(PD) = (0.11852 - 0.05478 * ln(PD))^2
K              = (LGD * WCDR - PD * LGD) * MA(PD, M)
RWA            = K * 12.5 * EAD

The asset correlation R is set by exposure class per BCBS d424: 0.12 to 0.24 for corporate / sovereign / bank (decreasing in PD), 0.15 fixed for residential mortgages, 0.04 fixed for qualifying revolving retail (QRRE), 0.03 to 0.16 for other retail. The maturity adjustment applies only to wholesale exposures; for retail, M = 1 by convention and MA equals 1. For defaulted exposures, K is the residual stress portion: K = max(0, LGD_downturn − ELBE) × EAD.

Basel 3.1 (BCBS d424) wraps the formula with three additional constraints. Input floors: PD floored at 0.05% (corporate) or 0.03% (retail); LGD floored at 25% (unsecured retail), 50% (unsecured wholesale), with LTV-dependent floors for secured exposures. A-IRB scope reductions: A-IRB removed for low-default-portfolios (large corporates, financial institutions, sovereigns); these must use F-IRB or standardised. Output floor: aggregate IRB RWA must be at least 72.5% of standardised RWA computed on the same portfolio; below this floor, the bank holds the standardised number. The output floor is phased in through 2030.

Feynman test The K formula is the mathematical embodiment of the IRB bargain. The bank estimates the four parameters internally; the supervisor specifies the asset correlation and the 99.9% confidence level; the formula combines them deterministically. Once you have PD, LGD, EAD and M, K is fixed. Every lecture from 9 through 30 has been producing inputs to this one equation. The capital number the bank reports is the residue.
Step 2

Why this matters

The K formula is the regulatory contract. The bank delivers PD, LGD, EAD, M; the supervisor delivers the asset correlation, the confidence level, the maturity-adjustment functional form, and the output floor. The combination produces a capital number that is comparable across banks within the same asset class. The supervisor's review of the K calculation itself is mechanical: given the inputs, the output is determined. The review effort instead concentrates on the inputs (PD, LGD, EAD calibration) and on the floors (have they been applied correctly).

The Vasicek one-factor model that sits underneath the formula has three crucial properties. First, it is portfolio-invariant: K per exposure does not depend on other exposures in the portfolio; the bank can compute K per facility and sum to get portfolio K. Second, it captures systematic risk only: the idiosyncratic component is assumed diversified away in a large portfolio. Third, it produces a closed-form worst-case default rate at 99.9% confidence: no Monte Carlo simulation required. These properties make the formula tractable, comparable across institutions, and inspectable by supervisors. The cost is that the model is necessarily a simplification: real portfolios have non-trivial granularity adjustments, sector concentrations, and correlations that the single-factor model does not capture; these get picked up under Pillar 2 (lecture 7 of risk_management) rather than Pillar 1.

Basel 3.1 changes the bargain. The introduction of input floors limits how aggressive a bank's parameter estimates can be; the output floor at 72.5% caps the aggregate capital relief from IRB. The supervisor's calculation is now: IRB RWA versus standardised RWA, take the larger of (IRB, 72.5% × standardised), apply that to the 8% Pillar 1 minimum. Banks for whom IRB delivers less than 27.5% of relief versus standardised find the output floor binding; banks with stronger IRB relief still benefit but capped.

Step 3

The mental model

The full data flow from the parameter outputs to the final RWA is best held as a single picture. Inputs come from the previous twenty-one lectures; the K formula is the middle of the pipeline; the output floor sits at the end.

Three properties to internalise. First, the asset correlation R is not estimated by the bank; it is set by the supervisor per asset class. The bank cannot move it. Second, the 99.9% confidence level corresponds to a 1-in-1000 year stress; this is the regulatory choice for the tail risk that capital must cover. Third, the 12.5 factor is the inverse of the 8% Pillar 1 capital ratio: RWA is denominated such that the 8% minimum applied to it gives the required capital in £, which equals K × EAD by construction.

Step 4

The detail

The Vasicek single-factor model

The mathematical anchor of the K formula is the Vasicek (2002) one-factor structural model. Each obligor i's standardised asset return at the one-year horizon decomposes as:

X_i  =  sqrt(R) * S  +  sqrt(1 - R) * Z_i

  where  S       ~ N(0, 1)       systematic (economy-wide) factor
         Z_i     ~ N(0, 1)       idiosyncratic factor (per obligor)
         R       in [0, 1]       asset correlation
         S, Z_i  independent

Default occurs when X_i falls below a threshold c such that P(X_i < c) = PD. Conditional on a realised systematic factor S = s, the conditional default probability is:

P(default | S = s)  =  P( sqrt(R) * s + sqrt(1 - R) * Z_i  <  N^-1(PD) )
                    =  P( Z_i  <  ( N^-1(PD) - sqrt(R) * s ) / sqrt(1 - R) )
                    =  N( ( N^-1(PD) - sqrt(R) * s ) / sqrt(1 - R) )

At the 99.9% one-year tail (a 1-in-1000 year systematic stress), s = N^-1(0.001) = -N^-1(0.999). Substituting:

WCDR(PD, R)  =  P(default | S = N^-1(0.001))
              =  N( ( N^-1(PD) + sqrt(R) * N^-1(0.999) ) / sqrt(1 - R) )

This is the closed-form worst-case default rate at 99.9% confidence. It is the conditional PD given a 1-in-1000 systematic stress event. For PD = 1%, R = 0.15: WCDR ≈ 8.4%. For PD = 5%, R = 0.04 (QRRE): WCDR ≈ 13.3%. The relationship is non-linear in PD and increasing in R.

Asset correlation by exposure class

BCBS d424 specifies the asset correlation R per exposure class. The pattern reflects the supervisor's view of how much systematic risk each class carries.

Exposure classAsset correlation R
Corporate, sovereign, bank0.12 · (1 − e−50·PD) / (1 − e−50) + 0.24 · (1 − (1 − e−50·PD) / (1 − e−50))
SME corporate adjustmentSubtract 0.04 · (1 − (S − 5)/45) where S = annual sales in €m, capped at 50
Residential mortgage0.15 (fixed)
Qualifying revolving retail (QRRE)0.04 (fixed)
Other retail0.03 · (1 − e−35·PD) / (1 − e−35) + 0.16 · (1 − (1 − e−35·PD) / (1 − e−35))

Three patterns to read from the table. First, corporate R decreases in PD (from 0.24 at very low PD to 0.12 at very high PD). The intuition: lower-PD obligors are larger and more sensitive to the macro cycle; higher-PD obligors are smaller and more idiosyncratic. Second, mortgages have a high fixed R of 0.15 reflecting collateral-value correlation across regions. Third, QRRE has a low fixed R of 0.04 reflecting that credit-card defaults are dominated by idiosyncratic life events more than macro stress.

The maturity adjustment

For wholesale exposures, the K formula includes a maturity adjustment that increases capital for longer-dated facilities (because of the cumulative probability of credit deterioration over time). For retail, the maturity adjustment is omitted (M = 1, so MA = 1).

b(PD)       =  ( 0.11852 − 0.05478 · ln(PD) )^2
MA(PD, M)   =  ( 1 + (M − 2.5) · b(PD) ) / ( 1 − 1.5 · b(PD) )

where  M = effective maturity in years
       M = 1 for retail (MA = 1)
       M is in [1, 5] for wholesale (capped per CRR Article 162)

The function b(PD) is decreasing in PD: longer-dated low-PD facilities attract a larger maturity-adjustment uplift than longer-dated high-PD ones. The 2.5-year anchor reflects the assumption that the average loan in the regulator's reference portfolio is 2.5 years; facilities longer than 2.5 years are uplifted, shorter than 2.5 years are scaled down.

The K formula

Combining WCDR, LGD and the maturity adjustment:

K  =  ( LGD · WCDR(PD, R)  −  PD · LGD )  ·  MA(PD, M)

The structure: LGD × WCDR is the conditional expected loss at the 1-in-1000 stress (capital should cover this); PD × LGD is the expected loss in normal conditions (already covered by IFRS 9 provisions and the bank's income); the difference is the unexpected loss at the 99.9% confidence level, which is what capital exists to absorb. The maturity adjustment scales this for facilities longer or shorter than the 2.5-year anchor.

The defaulted-exposure K formula

For facilities already in default, the K formula switches to:

K_defaulted  =  max( 0,  LGD_downturn  −  ELBE )  ×  EAD

Intuition: for a defaulted exposure, the bank's expectation (ELBE) is already provisioned for via IFRS 9 Stage 3 ECL; capital is held only against the stress portion (downturn LGD above ELBE). The max(0, ...) floor catches any data inconsistency where ELBE happens to exceed LGD_downturn.

RWA and the 12.5 factor

RWA per exposure  =  K × 12.5 × EAD

The 12.5 factor is the inverse of the 8% Pillar 1 minimum capital ratio (1 / 0.08 = 12.5). Multiplying K (capital required per unit of EAD) by 12.5 and EAD produces an RWA expressed in the same denominator units as the standardised approach, so the bank's 8% capital requirement applied to RWA produces the same £ capital as K applied to EAD. The standard arithmetic identity:

Capital_required  =  8%  ×  RWA  =  8%  ×  K × 12.5 × EAD  =  K × EAD

The 12.5 factor is therefore a unit conversion, not a regulatory choice. Portfolio RWA is the sum across exposures.

Basel 3.1 input floors

BCBS d424 introduced explicit minimum values for the IRB inputs to limit how aggressive the bank's parameter estimates can be:

ParameterExposure classFloor
PDCorporate, sovereign, bank (where A-IRB still applies)0.05%
PDRetail (all sub-classes)0.03%
LGDUnsecured corporate25%
LGDUnsecured retail25%
LGDSecured retail residential mortgage5% (LTV-dependent, rising with LTV)
LGDOther secured exposuresPer BCBS d424 collateral schedule

The floors apply after MoC is added: the bank's regulatory PD or LGD is the maximum of (calibrated + MoC, floor). Floors bind on a minority of grades for most banks (typically the lowest 2 to 3 PD grades and the highest-LTV secured segments); for typical retail card portfolios most grades sit well above their floors.

A-IRB scope reductions under Basel 3.1

Basel 3.1 removes A-IRB permission for certain exposure classes where the supervisor judged that the data is too thin and the estimation too unreliable to justify internal modelling:

  • Large corporates (consolidated revenue above €500m): A-IRB removed; banks use F-IRB (with supervisory LGD and EAD) or standardised.
  • Financial institutions: A-IRB removed; F-IRB or standardised.
  • Sovereigns: A-IRB removed; standardised or F-IRB at supervisory discretion.
  • Equity: IRB removed entirely; standardised only.

The retail A-IRB perimeter (the focus of this lecture sequence) is unchanged.

The output floor at 72.5%

The headline Basel 3.1 reform. The bank computes RWA two ways: under IRB and under standardised. The final RWA is the maximum of:

final_RWA  =  max( aggregate_IRB_RWA,  0.725 × aggregate_standardised_RWA )

The output floor caps the total capital relief from IRB at 27.5% versus standardised. Banks for whom IRB delivers more than 27.5% relief see the output floor binding; those for whom IRB delivers less continue to use the IRB number. The output floor is phased in: 50% from 2023, rising in steps to 72.5% by 2030.

The output floor is the supervisor's response to two perceived issues with IRB: post-GFC distrust of internal models, and the dispersion of IRB RWAs across banks holding similar portfolios. The floor caps the dispersion at the floor level and ensures banks cannot avoid all the standardised-approach RWA through aggressive modelling.

The SARB 1.06 scaling factor

The South African Reserve Bank applies an explicit 1.06 multiplier to the IRB RWA computed via the formulas above (a transposition of an earlier Basel calibration adjustment). For SARB-regulated banks, the effective formula is:

RWA_SARB  =  K × 12.5 × EAD × 1.06

This is a jurisdiction-specific scaling factor; UK / EU banks under PRA / EBA do not apply it.

A worked numerical example

To make the formulas concrete, take a retail credit-card facility (QRRE) with the following parameters from the previous lectures:

Regulatory PD            =  2.25%      (from lecture 14, after MoC and floor)
Regulatory LGD           =  78%        (from lecture 21, downturn + MoC, above 25% floor)
Regulatory EAD           =  £6,000     (= EADF 75% × £8,000 limit, from lecture 28)
Effective maturity M     =  1          (retail)
Asset correlation R      =  0.04       (QRRE fixed per BCBS d424)

Step by step:

1. WCDR(0.0225, 0.04)
   =  N( ( N^-1(0.0225) + sqrt(0.04) · N^-1(0.999) ) / sqrt(1 - 0.04) )
   =  N( ( -2.005 + 0.2 · 3.090 ) / sqrt(0.96) )
   =  N( ( -2.005 + 0.618 ) / 0.980 )
   =  N( -1.416 )
   =  0.0784       (i.e. 7.84%)

2. b(PD) = (0.11852 - 0.05478 · ln(0.0225))^2
        =  (0.11852 - 0.05478 · -3.794)^2
        =  (0.11852 + 0.20783)^2
        =  0.32635^2
        =  0.1065

3. MA(PD, M) = (1 + (1 - 2.5) · 0.1065) / (1 - 1.5 · 0.1065)
            =  (1 - 0.160) / (1 - 0.160)
            =  1.000 / 0.840
            =  1.190
   For retail M = 1, MA is set to 1 by regulation.

4. K  =  ( LGD · WCDR - PD · LGD ) · MA(PD, M)
      =  ( 0.78 · 0.0784 - 0.0225 · 0.78 ) · 1.000   (retail: MA = 1)
      =  ( 0.0611 - 0.0176 )
      =  0.0436        (i.e. 4.36%)

5. RWA  =  K × 12.5 × EAD
       =  0.0436 × 12.5 × £6,000
       =  £3,267

6. Capital required at 8%  =  8% × £3,267  =  £261

For comparison, under the standardised approach (assuming a 75% risk weight for the QRRE exposure):

Standardised RWA  =  £6,000 × 75%  =  £4,500
Standardised capital at 8%  =  £360

So under IRB the bank holds £261, under standardised £360. The output floor check: 72.5% × £4,500 = £3,263. The IRB RWA (£3,267) is just above the floor, so IRB applies as-is. For comparable portfolios with stronger IRB relief (say PD 1% rather than 2.25%), the IRB RWA would drop below £3,263 and the output floor would bind.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first lesson is that the K formula does the least work in the IRB capital pipeline. Lectures 9 through 30 produced PD, LGD, EAD, M. Lecture 31 plugs them into a deterministic equation. The supervisor's review effort follows the same allocation: 90% on the inputs, 10% on the K calculation. Modellers who think the formula is the substance of capital modelling have the priorities wrong; modellers who think it is mechanical and uninteresting are also wrong (the formula's mathematics is the regulator's policy statement on tail risk).

The second lesson is on the 99.9% confidence level. This is the regulator's choice of the tail event that capital must cover. The implied stress is roughly 1-in-1000-years; it is not a forecast of an actual stress likelihood but a regulatory convention. The dispersion across asset classes (R values from 0.04 for QRRE to 0.24 for low-PD corporate) reflects the regulator's view of how systematic each class's defaults are. Modellers who absorb the dispersion as fixed and stop asking why one number is 0.04 and another 0.15 miss the underlying policy logic.

The third lesson is on the maturity adjustment. The 2.5-year anchor reflects the regulator's reference portfolio average maturity; the function b(PD) reflects the empirically-fitted shape of credit migration over time. The omission of MA for retail is a recognition that retail facilities have effectively continuous reset (every monthly billing cycle is a re-pricing opportunity), so the term-structure-of-credit-quality concern that motivates MA for wholesale does not apply. F107 candidates who can articulate why retail has MA = 1 score well.

The fourth lesson is on the output floor. The 72.5% number is the headline Basel 3.1 reform and the most consequential policy change in IRB capital since Basel II. It limits the total capital relief from IRB to 27.5% versus standardised. Banks have responded in two ways: those for whom the floor binds rebalance toward standardised RWA-intensive segments (the floor is no longer a punishment); those for whom it does not bind invest in standardised-approach optimisations as a hedge against future tightening. The floor is the explicit acknowledgement that internal-model RWA can deviate from standardised RWA only by a bounded amount.

The fifth and structurally most important: this lecture closes the loop of A-IRB capital. Every lecture from 1 through 30 has been preparation for one equation. The equation produces one number per exposure (K); summed across the portfolio, it produces the bank's RWA. The 8% Pillar 1 minimum applied to that RWA produces the bank's regulatory capital requirement for credit risk. The journey from the lecture-1 notation through the lecture-22 LGD testing has been the long path to that one number; the lecture-31 K formula is where the path arrives.
