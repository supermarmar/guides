The use test: same model, every purpose

The use test is the principle that internal ratings and PD/LGD/EAD estimates must be embedded in day-to-day credit decisions, not built solely for regulatory capital. CRR Article 144(1)(b) codifies the rule; SS3/24 and the EBA guidelines turn it into specific evidence requirements; the supervisor's question at SREP is always "show me where the model is actually used".

Step 1

The 30-second version

CRR Article 144(1)(b) requires that the internal ratings, default and loss estimates used to calculate own-funds requirements "play an essential role in the risk management and decision-making process, and in the credit approval, internal capital allocation and corporate governance functions of the institution". The rule has one purpose: stop banks from running a "capital model" that nobody in the front office trusts, while keeping a parallel "real" model for pricing and limits. If the IRB model is good enough for capital, it has to be good enough for the business.

The use test sits beside seven other minimum IRB requirements: a defined risk-grading methodology with a stated rating philosophy, at least seven non-default grades plus one default grade, ratings assigned to all borrowers, annual rating and model reviews, stress-testing under adverse scenarios, full documentation that allows replication, and a three-year operating history with supervisory approval. The use test is the requirement that ties all the others to the bank's actual business: a model nobody uses cannot pass the test no matter how good its statistics.

Feynman test The use test exists because the regulator is trying to defeat a single bad incentive. A bank that built one model to minimise its capital number and a separate, more conservative model for actual lending decisions would game the system. The supervisor closes this loophole by requiring that the capital model is the same one used to decide who gets a loan, what they are charged, what provisions are held against them and how much capital is allocated to them. If the front-line credit officers and the capital team are not using the same numbers, the IRB approval is at risk.
Step 2

Why this matters

The use test is, in practice, the most common supervisory finding in IRB reviews. The model itself is usually within tolerance: the discrimination is acceptable, the calibration is defensible. What fails is the evidence chain that the model is actually informing decisions. A pricing committee that overrides the IRB-implied risk-adjusted price every quarter, a limit-setting framework that uses an external bureau score rather than the internal PD, a Stage 3 ECL calculation under IFRS 9 that uses a separate LGD: each of these is a use-test breach. A pattern of breaches will trigger a Pillar 2A capital add-on or, in serious cases, withdrawal of A-IRB approval for the affected portfolio.

The use test also drives the design of internal reporting. Risk-weighted return on equity (RoRWA), risk-adjusted return on tangible equity (ROTE), and economic value-add measures all sit on top of IRB outputs precisely so that the use test is met by construction. If the management committee tracks RoRWA monthly, the model is provably embedded; if it tracks an alternative profitability measure that bypasses RWA, the auditor will ask why.

For F107 the use test is the natural complement to the A-IRB context lecture. Where the context lecture answered "what is regulatory capital", the use test answers "what stops a bank from gaming the capital calculation". Examiners often pair these two: the regulator's permission to use internal models is conditioned on the bank's commitment to use them everywhere.

Step 4

The detail

The minimum IRB requirements

Use the diagram above as the centrepiece, but the wider regulatory check-list extends well beyond it. To run the A-IRB approach a bank must satisfy the following minimum requirements:

  • Define a rating philosophy on the point-in-time to through-the-cycle spectrum, and document the assessment horizon implied by the chosen position.
  • Maintain at least seven borrower grades for non-defaulted exposures and at least one grade for defaulted exposures. This is the regulatory floor on granularity.
  • Assign a rating to every borrower in the IRB perimeter; no exposure may be unrated.
  • Review individual ratings at least annually (more frequently for higher-risk grades).
  • Review the rating system itself, including the underlying model, at least annually.
  • Stress-test the rating system under adverse economic and market conditions, with results feeding the ICAAP.
  • Document the rating system fully, with clear definitions and criteria, so an independent party can replicate the ratings.
  • Provide a three-year operating history showing the model has been used as the basis for credit-risk management before applying for capital recognition.
  • Obtain explicit approval from the national supervisor, with periodic re-approval at each material model change.

National variations on the use test

The Basel framework sets the principle but leaves implementation to national supervisors. The differences are minor in spirit but specific in operational requirement.

The PRA (United Kingdom) has the most prescriptive set of use-test expectations in Europe. SS3/24 §22 requires firms to evidence the use of IRB outputs in credit approval, exposure limit setting, pricing, provisioning, capital allocation and management information. The PRA also expects that the credit risk control unit (the team that owns the IRB models) is independent of the front line, with internal outsourcing within the group permitted only under specific circumstances. The accuracy of the rating systems must be tested against specific guidelines, and the use test is expanded into formal evidence requirements.

The Prudential Authority (South Africa, under SARB) requires additional governance: the board of directors and senior management must approve the bank's rating and risk-measurement processes; the board must annually self-assess governance and regulatory compliance; and banks must complete self-assessment templates that qualitatively and quantitatively justify their models.

The FCA / PRA split in the UK and the ECB / EBA split in the EU mean a UK or EU bank operating across jurisdictions can face overlapping use-test expectations. Practical solution: maintain a single use-test evidence register that maps each application of the IRB outputs to the most prescriptive applicable supervisor's expectation.

What happens when the use test fails

A use-test breach is treated as a model-governance failure, not a model-accuracy failure. The remediation hierarchy is:

  1. Internal finding: the model-validation team or internal audit identifies the gap, opens a remediation item with a target date, and tracks it on the model-risk register.
  2. Self-disclosure: the bank notifies the supervisor of the finding and the planned remediation. Self-disclosure substantially reduces the supervisor's escalation response.
  3. Pillar 2A add-on: where the supervisor concludes the use test is not met across a material portfolio, an explicit capital add-on is imposed under the SREP until remediation is complete.
  4. Revocation of A-IRB approval: in serious cases (sustained pattern of breaches, lack of credible remediation plan, governance failure at board level), the supervisor revokes A-IRB permission for the affected portfolio and forces a reversion to standardised. This is rare but it has happened: each occurrence is a multi-hundred-basis-point hit to the bank's capital ratio.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The use test is the regulator's answer to a structural agency problem: the team that benefits from a lower PD (the capital team, whose RWA is the denominator of every ratio they report) is not the team that bears the cost of a low PD (the front-line credit officers, who get burned when actual defaults exceed expectations). Without the use test, those two teams would optimise different numbers. The use test forces them to optimise the same number, which means a model that is too aggressive at the capital level is also too aggressive at the credit-approval level, and front-line losses tell on it quickly.

The other thing the use test does is sharpen the design of the model. A model that is built only for capital can afford to be calibrated TTC, smoothed across the cycle, and updated rarely. A model that has to feed pricing and ECL also has to be sensitive to current conditions: it needs a credible PiT layer. The use test is therefore what drives the dual PiT/TTC framing that recurs throughout the lectures that follow. Without the use test, the PiT layer would be optional. With it, the PiT layer is the workhorse and the TTC layer is the regulatory floor on top.

For practitioners the practical implication is to maintain an evidence register from day one of model development. Every time an IRB output is referenced in a committee paper, a pricing template, an ECL calculation or a stress test, the reference is logged. Three years later, when the supervisor asks for the use-test evidence, the register is the answer. Banks that try to reconstruct the evidence retroactively almost always fail; the register exists because the alternative is provably worse.
