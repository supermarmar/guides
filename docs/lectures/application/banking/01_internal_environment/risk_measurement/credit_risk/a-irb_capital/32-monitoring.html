Monitoring: keeping the IRB approval current

The final lecture of the A-IRB capital track. IRB approval is not a one-time event; it is an ongoing supervisory relationship that the bank maintains through perpetual monitoring of its PD, LGD and EAD models. The same test batteries from lectures 15, 22 and 29 run quarterly in production, producing a traffic-light scoreboard that triggers remediation, recalibration or material model change as findings escalate.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Monitoring is the perpetual operation of the IRB model in production. CRR Article 175 requires firms to review their internal estimates at least annually; PRA SS3/24 §19 to §21 specifies quarterly back-testing of PD, LGD and EAD parameters against realised outcomes. The operational pattern: the test batteries built in lectures 15 (PD), 22 (LGD) and 29 (EAD) re-run automatically every quarter on the latest application cohort, the results land in a traffic-light scoreboard, and any amber or red findings trigger a defined escalation pathway. The aim is not to catch problems after they have happened but to detect drift before it materially affects RWA.

The escalation pathway has four levels. Green findings are recorded and no action is needed. Amber findings are flagged for watch, fed into the limitations register (lecture 7), and may trigger MoC top-ups at the next monitoring cycle. Red findings trigger recalibration: the modelling team rebuilds the affected parameter on the most recent cohort and re-validates. Material model change findings (sustained red across multiple parameters or methodological breakage) trigger formal model-change governance: notification to the supervisor, model-risk committee approval, and in serious cases the IRB-permission renewal process.

Beyond the quarterly drumbeat, three larger cadences sit on top. The annual review (CRR Article 175) consolidates the four quarterly packs and produces a single yearly model-validation report that the supervisor inspects. The 3-to-5-year redevelopment cycle rebuilds the model end-to-end on refreshed data, with full re-running of every lecture from 6 (data engineering) through 31 (RWA). The continuous use-test compliance (CRR Article 144) is the supervisor's check that the model's outputs are still driving credit decisions, pricing, ECL and capital allocation in the same direction as at approval.

Feynman test IRB approval is a relationship, not a transaction. The bank submits its model package at approval and receives permission to use internal estimates for capital. The continuation of that permission depends on the bank's ability to demonstrate, quarter after quarter, that the model still performs as expected. The monitoring framework is the evidence the bank builds for that demonstration. A bank that monitors well retains approval indefinitely; a bank that does not has its permission revisited at every SREP and, in serious cases, removed.
Step 2

Why this matters

The cost of poor monitoring is measured in capital. A model that drifts undetected for four quarters produces RWA numbers that diverge from what the model would now estimate if rebuilt. If the drift is in the bank's favour (modelled RWA below recalibrated), the supervisor at the next inspection imposes a Pillar 2A capital add-on to bridge the gap and may revoke A-IRB permission for the affected sub-portfolio. If the drift is against the bank (modelled RWA above recalibrated), the bank is over-capitalised, which is operationally tolerable but eroded competitive position. Either way, the cost of missing the drift is higher than the cost of detecting it.

The continuous-use-test angle matters too. CRR Article 144 requires that the IRB outputs are embedded in credit-risk management, pricing, ECL and capital allocation. Over time, business pressure can drive the front office to use alternative models for some decisions (a partner-specific scorecard, a new ML-based underwriting tool, a separate ECL methodology). The supervisor's monitoring inspection picks this up: divergence between the IRB outputs and the actual decision-making outputs is treated as a use-test breach with the same severity as a calibration breach. The monitoring framework therefore tracks both technical model performance and operational use, with separate evidence registers for each.

For F107 candidates the monitoring material covers the operational realities of IRB that pure modelling questions miss: the quarterly cadence, the traffic-light framework, the material-model-change governance, the redevelopment cycle. These details are exam-classic on the "describe how an IRB model is maintained in production" type of question.

Step 3

The mental model

Monitoring runs on three nested cadences (quarterly, annual, 3-to-5-year redevelopment) with escalation pathways feeding into the bank's model-risk governance. The diagram below shows the full architecture.

Three properties of the architecture to internalise. First, the quarterly cadence is automated: the test batteries built in lectures 15, 22 and 29 are scheduled jobs that re-run on the latest application cohort with no manual intervention. The traffic-light scoreboard is the output. Manual review happens only on amber and red findings. Second, the annual review consolidates rather than replaces the quarterly packs: it produces a single yearly validation report that goes to the supervisor and the board. Third, the redevelopment cycle is the only time the lectures 6 through 31 work is fully redone: the data engineering, the feature engineering, the segmentation, the GLM specification, the LRA window selection. Between redevelopments, partial recalibrations adjust parameters within the existing architecture.

Step 4

The detail

The quarterly cadence

Every quarter, the test batteries from lectures 15, 22 and 29 re-run automatically on the latest application cohort. Each test produces a metric value, compared to its threshold, with a green / amber / red status. The combined output is a single scoreboard with one row per test per parameter:

The scoreboard typically runs to 40 to 50 rows (14 PD tests, 16 LGD tests, 16 EAD tests). The model risk committee reviews it monthly; the full validation team reviews quarterly with detailed pages per amber finding.

The traffic-light framework

Three statuses, each with a defined response:

  • Green: metric within threshold; no action. Recorded in the quarterly pack and aggregated to the annual report.
  • Amber: metric in the watch band (typically between the threshold and 1.5x or 2x the threshold). Recorded as a watch item; investigated for root cause; added to the limitations register if structural; may trigger MoC top-up at the next monitoring cycle.
  • Red: metric beyond the watch band, or sustained amber across multiple quarters. Triggers recalibration of the affected parameter on the most recent cohort, with re-validation before the recalibrated model goes live.

A bank can typically operate with 2 to 4 amber findings per quarter without supervisory concern; persistent amber on the same finding across 3+ quarters typically gets upgraded to red regardless of metric movement.

Recalibration triggers

Red findings on any of the following typically trigger recalibration:

  • Discrimination decay: Gini (PD) or Spearman (LGD / EAD) drops by more than 5 percentage points versus development.
  • Calibration drift: MPE on regulatory parameter exceeds 15% at portfolio level.
  • Stability breach: PSI on key risk drivers exceeds 0.25.
  • Reference-value breach (LGD): downturn LGD falls below SS4/24 §15.7 reference value at any calibration segment.
  • Max-of-paths instability (EAD): binding path flips between cohorts on more than 2 grades.
  • Sub-population deterioration: any sub-population's metric falls outside threshold for 2+ consecutive quarters.

Recalibration is a defined process: refit the affected parameter on the most recent eligible cohort, re-validate against the same test battery, document the changes, and obtain MRC approval before deployment. A typical recalibration takes 6 to 12 weeks.

Material model change governance

The distinction between recalibration (a parameter update within the existing architecture) and material model change (a structural change requiring supervisor notification) is operationally critical. CRR Article 175 and PRA SS3/24 §21 define material model change broadly, but the operational guidance covers:

Change typeExamplesNotification required
Minor (recalibration only)Recomputing LRA on more recent data with same window methodology; updating MoC for newly identified limitationsNone; internal MRC sign-off only
ModerateChanging the LRA window selection; adding a new feature to the regression; revising the asset correlation per asset classSupervisor pre-notification; impact assessment on RWA
MaterialChanging the GLM specification; restructuring the segmentation cascade; switching from EADF to CCF target; changing the MoC frameworkFormal supervisor approval before deployment; IRB-permission impact review
FundamentalSwitching the modelling class (logistic to ML); changing the default definition; significant expansion of the IRB perimeter to new asset classesFull re-permission process; can take 12+ months

The distinction matters because the wrong classification has serious consequences. A bank that treats a material change as minor and deploys without supervisor approval can face IRB-permission suspension; a bank that treats a minor change as material adds months of unnecessary supervisory dialogue. The Model Risk Committee makes the classification call with documented justification.

The annual review (CRR Article 175)

Once per year, the four quarterly packs are consolidated into a single annual validation report. The standard contents:

  1. Executive summary: traffic-light status across parameters, key amber / red items, RWA impact.
  2. Performance back-tests for each of PD, LGD, EAD across the four quarterly cohorts.
  3. MoC review: each MoC item re-evaluated, with category, quantum, and rationale; revisions documented.
  4. Use-test evidence: list of decisioning, pricing, ECL and capital-allocation processes that consume the IRB outputs, with sample evidence for each.
  5. Independent validation sign-off: the validation team's challenge log with resolution status.
  6. Action plan: remediation items for the next year, with owners and target dates.

The annual report goes to the Model Risk Committee, the board's Risk Committee, and the supervisor. PRA SS3/24 §20 expects the supervisor to receive the report within 60 working days of the year-end.

The 3-to-5-year redevelopment cycle

The longest cadence in the framework. A full redevelopment rebuilds the model end-to-end on refreshed data:

  • Lectures 6 to 8 (data engineering, governance, portfolio description) are re-run on the latest 10+ years of data.
  • Lectures 9 to 15 (PD pipeline) produce a new PD model; same for LGD (16 to 22) and EAD (23 to 29).
  • Lecture 30 (MoC) is reconstructed from scratch with refreshed limitations register.
  • Lecture 31 (RWA) produces a new K-formula output for the same exposures, allowing a before / after RWA comparison.

Redevelopment is a 12-to-24-month project for a typical retail bank. It is triggered either by the calendar (most banks do a full rebuild every 3 to 5 years regardless of model performance) or by structural change in the portfolio (an acquisition that materially changes the composition; a regulatory reform like Basel 3.1 that changes the framework).

Continuous use-test compliance

Parallel to the technical monitoring runs a continuous use-test compliance check. CRR Article 144 requires that the IRB outputs are embedded in:

  • Credit decisioning (origination cut-offs, exposure limits)
  • Risk-based pricing (loan rate decomposition includes EL = PD × LGD × EAD)
  • IFRS 9 ECL (Stage 1 / 2 / 3 ECL computed from PiT versions of the IRB parameters)
  • Collections strategy (PiT LGD drives workout and debt-sale decisions)
  • Risk appetite and capital allocation (RoRWA and ROTE targets sit on top of RWA)

The use-test evidence register is updated continuously: each instance of an IRB output appearing in a committee paper, a pricing model, an ECL calculation, or a strategic decision is logged. The annual review samples the register and confirms the IRB outputs are still driving decisions; the supervisor inspects the register at SREP and probes any apparent divergence between IRB outputs and operational decisioning.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first lesson is that the quarterly cadence is the operational reality of IRB. Banks that treat IRB approval as a one-time event and let the monitoring framework atrophy find their permission revisited at every SREP. Banks that operate the monitoring framework rigorously have IRB permission renewed without dialogue. The cost of running the framework is real (a small team plus automated infrastructure); the cost of not running it is much larger.

The second lesson is on the material-model-change boundary. The line between recalibration and material change is the most consequential operational distinction in the framework, and it is not always clear. The temptation is to classify changes as minor to avoid supervisory dialogue; the cost is a regulatory finding if the classification is wrong. The discipline is to err on the side of supervisor pre-notification: a moderate change that the supervisor blesses costs little; an uncommunicated material change that the supervisor later discovers can cost permission.

The third lesson is on amber-to-red transitions. The classic operational pattern is that a single PSI breach in Q1 is amber; sustained PSI breach across Q2 and Q3 is upgraded to red; the recalibration that follows is meant to deploy in Q4. A bank that has the discipline to upgrade Q3 to red and start the recalibration immediately is operating well; a bank that lets sustained amber drift into Q4 or Q5 is operating poorly. The supervisor reads the upgrade pattern at SREP and treats it as a leading indicator of monitoring quality.

The fourth lesson is on the use-test evidence register. The register sounds bureaucratic but is the single most consequential piece of operational documentation in IRB. Banks with strong registers (every decision touching IRB outputs logged with date, decision and reference) breeze through SREP use-test review. Banks with weak registers face extended dialogue and sometimes Pillar 2 capital add-ons specifically for use-test inadequacy. The discipline is to build the register as decisions happen, not retroactively before SREP.

The fifth and structurally most important: the IRB capital framework is a perpetual operation. The 31 previous lectures produced the model; this lecture is the framework that keeps it alive. The capital number the bank reports next quarter is the residue of the model from the most recent recalibration; the model the bank operates today is the cumulative result of every quarterly amber finding addressed, every annual MoC revision, every redevelopment refresh. There is no "finished" IRB model; there is only "currently operating" with a documented history of how it got there.
