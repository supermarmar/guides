Margin of conservatism: categories A, B, C

The conservatism overlay that bridges the bank's known weaknesses to its regulatory IRB parameters. Three categories (data limitations, methodology weaknesses, general estimation error), applied per grade or per calibration segment, quantified explicitly and signed off by validation. The supervisor's first question at SREP is not "is the MoC large enough" but "is the MoC honest".

Step 1

The 30-second version

Margin of conservatism (MoC) is the uplift applied to calibrated PD, LGD and EAD parameters to bridge known weaknesses to the regulatory output. CRR Article 179(1)(f) requires it; EBA/GL/2017/16 specifies the framework; the bank operationalises it. Every deficiency identified anywhere in the modelling lifecycle (data limitations from lecture 07, sampling exclusions from lecture 11 / 18 / 25, methodology weaknesses from the modelling lectures) lands as an MoC item, gets quantified, and lifts the calibrated parameter upward.

The EBA framework splits MoC into three categories. Category A — data and methodological deficiencies: gaps in the data (thin history, sample exclusions, definitional inconsistencies) and acknowledged limitations of the chosen methodology. The dominant category for most banks; the limitations register from lecture 07 feeds this directly. Category B — relevant changes: shifts in underwriting standards, risk appetite, collections and recovery policies, or portfolio composition that are not yet fully captured in the calibration data. Category C — general estimation error: the residual statistical uncertainty in coefficient estimates and the calibration. Each category is quantified separately, and aggregate MoC per grade is the sum.

Operational mechanics: MoC is applied per grade for PD (lecture 14), per calibration segment for LGD (lecture 21) and per grade for EAD (lecture 28). Per parameter the typical aggregate uplift is 5 to 20% (PD), 5 to 15% (LGD) and 3 to 10 percentage points (EAD). Lower MoC is better only if the underlying limitations are genuinely absent; the supervisor's red flag is a low MoC with no supporting evidence of why the standard limitations do not apply.

Feynman test The instinct is to view MoC as a penalty for imperfect modelling. The right framing is the opposite: MoC is the mechanism by which the bank acknowledges and quantifies its weaknesses. A model with high MoC is not a worse model; it is a more honest one. The supervisor's preferred MoC package contains an explicit limitations register paired with explicit MoC quanta for each material limitation. A bank that submits with vague MoC and no register is signalling that the weaknesses have not been catalogued, which the supervisor catches at inspection.
Step 2

Why this matters

MoC is the regulatory bridge between the calibrated parameter (which reflects the bank's best estimate of the long-run average) and the regulatory parameter (which reflects the supervisor's expectation of conservatism). Without MoC, every limitation in the development data would have to either be excluded (losing useful information) or accommodated by re-doing the calibration on artificially clean data (which would not reflect reality). MoC lets the bank use real-world imperfect data and add a quantified uplift to compensate for what is known to be missing.

The supervisor reads MoC as a credibility signal. EBA/GL/2017/16 sets the framework; SS3/24 §18 and SS4/24 §19 set the UK supervisory expectations. The expected behaviours at SREP:

  • A bank that submits with a comprehensive limitations register (lecture 07) and explicit MoC quanta per category for each limitation is signalling honest acknowledgement of weakness. Supervisory review proceeds.
  • A bank that submits with low aggregate MoC but a thick limitations register is signalling that limitations have been catalogued but compensation has not been applied. The supervisor will probe the gap, typically resulting in MoC top-ups during review.
  • A bank that submits with low aggregate MoC and a thin limitations register is signalling either that weaknesses have not been catalogued or that the bank is over-confident in its data. The supervisor will probe both possibilities.

For F107 the MoC framework is exam-classic on three counts: the three-category EBA structure (memorisable and testable), the operational mechanics across PD / LGD / EAD (which differ in cadence and aggregation), and the connection to the limitations register from lecture 07 (the closing of the loop from "data engineering" to "regulatory output").

The mental model

The MoC framework feeds three sources of input through three categories of uplift, applied to three regulatory parameters. The diagram below shows the full mapping.

Three properties of the framework to internalise. First, the categories are not interchangeable: a data gap (category A) and a coefficient-uncertainty issue (category C) require different quantification methods and different remediation pathways. Conflating them is a common error and gets caught at validation. Second, the limitations register is the upstream feeder: every item on the register should appear as an MoC item, and every MoC item should trace back to a register entry. A register entry with no MoC, or an MoC with no register entry, is an audit anomaly. Third, aggregation is by parameter, not by category: per grade or calibration segment, the bank sums the category-A, category-B and category-C uplifts to produce a single MoC quantum per parameter.

Step 4

The detail

The regulatory anchor

CRR Article 179(1)(f) requires that estimates of PD, LGD and EAD "incorporate a margin of conservatism that is related to the expected range of estimation errors". The article does not specify the framework; EBA/GL/2017/16 fills the gap with the three-category structure that has become the European standard.

The EBA paragraphs on MoC (44 to 54 for the framework, 137 to 154 for the application to PD, 196 to 217 for LGD) set out:

  • The three-category structure (A, B, C).
  • The requirement to quantify each category separately at the grade or calibration-segment level.
  • The expectation that MoC is not used to compensate for fundamentally flawed methodology (which should be remediated, not papered over).
  • The requirement to track MoC over time and revise as data deficiencies are addressed.

PRA SS3/24 §18 and SS4/24 §19 transpose the EBA framework into UK supervisory expectations with two amplifications: MoC must be reviewed at least annually as part of model monitoring, and any reduction in MoC quantum from one cycle to the next must be explicitly justified by evidence that the underlying limitation has been resolved.

Category A: data and methodological deficiencies

The dominant category for most banks. Splits into two sub-categories:

A.1: Data deficiencies

  • Thin history: less than the regulatory minimum (5 years retail, 7 years non-retail) or, more commonly, history short of a full credit cycle. A bank with 6 years of data starting in 2018 has met the regulatory minimum but missed the GFC; the gap demands category-A MoC.
  • Sample exclusions: COVID exclusion (lectures 11, 18, 25), system-migration exclusions, distorted-period exclusions. Each exclusion narrows the development sample and demands compensating MoC for the loss of information.
  • Definitional inconsistencies: changes in the operational implementation of the default definition over time (write-off threshold changes, forbearance flag changes), changes in collateral valuation methodology, changes in product definitions. Each creates a discontinuity in the historical data.
  • Thin segments: segments where the resolved-default count is too low to support credible calibration. The bank either pools (with an MoC for the pooling) or carries explicit conservative assumptions.
  • External data limitations: bureau coverage gaps, external benchmark divergence from internal experience.

A.2: Methodology limitations

  • Modelling-class choices: logistic regression for PD where an ML approach might score higher; Gaussian + Identity GLM for EAD where the empirical distribution is not perfectly Gaussian; the three-component mixture for LGD with debatable conditioning order.
  • Segmentation cut-offs: decision-tree-derived cut-offs that are rounded for stability lose some discriminatory power.
  • Variable selection conventions: tighter thresholds (VIF < 2 for LGD / EAD) drop variables that might have added marginal signal.
  • Calibration-window selection: the chosen LRA window may not perfectly represent the full cycle.

Quantification for category A is typically based on sensitivity analysis: rebuild the calibration with the exclusion / methodology choice reversed where feasible, and observe the difference. Where reversal is not feasible (the data does not exist to remove a COVID exclusion, for example), expert judgement guided by industry pools or external benchmarks fills the gap.

Category B: relevant changes

Captures shifts in the bank's operating environment that are not yet fully reflected in the calibration data. The development sample is necessarily backward-looking; the application portfolio is forward-looking. Category B bridges the gap.

Standard examples:

  • Underwriting-standards changes: tightened credit-score cut-offs, new affordability rules, revised limit-grant strategy. The development data reflects the old policy; the application data reflects the new.
  • Risk-appetite shifts: a board decision to grow into a new geography or segment; the calibration data does not yet include the new exposure profile.
  • Collections and recovery policy changes: a switch from in-house collections to external agency, a change in debt-sale forward-flow contracts, a new collateral-valuation methodology. The historical realised LGDs reflect the old policy.
  • Portfolio composition shifts: an acquisition that materially changes the product mix, a run-off of a legacy product line.
  • Limit-management policy: a shift from proactive credit-line increases to a more conservative limit-management approach. Particularly relevant for EAD.

Category B quantification typically involves comparing the application portfolio against the development portfolio across the relevant dimension (PSI / CSI work from lectures 11 / 18 / 25), then sizing the MoC based on the observed shift and expert judgement on its direction. Category B uplifts are reviewed at every monitoring cycle and revised as the change becomes embedded in the calibration data.

Category C: general estimation error

The residual statistical uncertainty in coefficient estimates and the calibration. Even with perfect data and perfect methodology, finite samples produce coefficient estimates with standard errors; finite calibration windows produce LRA estimates with sampling variance; small grade populations produce per-grade calibrations with wider confidence intervals.

Quantification approaches for category C:

  • Bootstrap analysis: resample the development data with replacement, rebuild the model, observe the distribution of calibrated outputs. The category-C MoC at confidence level 95% is the difference between the calibrated output and the 95th percentile of the bootstrap distribution.
  • Standard-error propagation: compute the prediction interval analytically using the coefficient covariance matrix and the input feature vector. Less robust to model misspecification than bootstrap but computationally cheaper.
  • Cross-validation variance: variance of the calibration output across k-fold cross-validation splits.

Category C is typically the smallest of the three categories at well-populated grades; for thin grades (high MoC category A) the category C also rises because the standard errors are larger.

The MoC process

The end-to-end workflow:

  1. Identify: every limitation register entry, every modelling-team note, every validation amber finding, every credit-strategy memo is an MoC candidate.
  2. Categorise: assign each item to A.1, A.2, B, or C with documented rationale.
  3. Quantify: apply the appropriate quantification method per category (sensitivity analysis, PSI-based sizing, bootstrap, etc.).
  4. Aggregate: sum the per-category quanta per grade or calibration segment.
  5. Apply: add the aggregate MoC to the calibrated parameter to produce the regulatory parameter. For PD this is straightforward; for LGD and EAD, the MoC is applied separately to LRA and downturn paths.
  6. Document: every MoC item recorded with limitation, category, quantification method, quantum, supporting evidence, and the parameter / grade it applies to.
  7. Sign off: validation team challenges, model risk committee approves.
  8. Revisit: at each monitoring cycle and at each redevelopment, review all MoC items and revise as warranted.

Honest MoC versus over-conservative MoC

Two pathologies recur. Under-MoC: the bank submits with low quanta to minimise RWA, hoping the supervisor will not probe. The supervisor probes; the bank ends up with top-ups during review, sometimes severe ones. Over-MoC: the bank submits with very high quanta to pre-empt any supervisory challenge, accepting the RWA cost. The supervisor accepts the conservatism but flags the lack of rigour in the quantification.

The right answer is honest MoC: each item quantified using the documented method, with the magnitude proportionate to the underlying limitation. Honest MoC produces a RWA cost the bank can defend on its own terms; over- or under-MoC produces RWA outcomes the bank cannot fully justify.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first lesson is that MoC is the closing of the loop from data engineering to capital. The limitations register from lecture 07 was the first artefact in the IRB build; the MoC table in this lecture is the last. Every line of the register should map to a line of the MoC table; every line of the MoC table should trace back to the register. Banks that maintain both registers as a single integrated artefact (one row per limitation, with columns for description, category, quantification, MoC quantum, parameter / grade application) pass validation more smoothly than banks that maintain them separately and reconcile after.

The second lesson is on category B specifically. Categories A and C are largely about the past (data the bank has, statistical errors the bank can compute). Category B is about the future (changes the bank knows are coming but the calibration data does not yet reflect). It is therefore the most judgemental category and the most contested at validation. The standard discipline is to have category B items reviewed by both the modelling team and the policy team (credit, collections, partner-management) so the quantum reflects both modelling rigour and policy reality.

The third lesson is on the temptation to load everything into category A. Category A has the most reliable quantification methods (sensitivity analysis, documented exclusions); category B requires judgement; category C requires statistical sophistication. Modellers often default to category A for items that are properly category B or C because the quantification is easier. The supervisor will look at the category mix; an MoC budget that is 90% category A and almost nothing in B or C is a signal that the bank has not done the harder quantification work. The expected balance is roughly 60% A, 25% B, 15% C for a typical retail book.

The fourth lesson is on MoC reduction over time. PRA SS3/24 §18 expects that as data deficiencies are addressed (more years of history accumulate, sample exclusions become smaller in relative terms, policy changes embed into the calibration data), the MoC quantum should decline. A bank that submits the same MoC quantum at every monitoring cycle for years is signalling that the underlying limitations are not being addressed. The supervisor's expectation is a gradual MoC compression as the IRB model matures.

The fifth and structurally most important: the MoC table is the bridge between the limitations register and the K formula. Before MoC, the calibrated parameter is the bank's best estimate. After MoC, the regulatory parameter is what the K formula consumes. The supervisor reviews both: the calibration accuracy (is the best estimate right?) and the MoC adequacy (does the conservatism cover the gap?). A model can fail validation by being right on calibration but inadequate on MoC; the inverse failure is also possible. Both must be defensible simultaneously.

Step 6

Test yourself

  1. State the CRR Article that requires MoC and the EBA guideline that specifies the three-category framework.
  2. Name the three EBA MoC categories. For each, give one example specific to a retail credit-card portfolio.
  3. For each category, name a standard quantification approach.
  4. Per parameter (PD, LGD, EAD), state the MoC unit (per grade vs per calibration segment) and the typical aggregate magnitude.
  5. Why does LGD have separate MoC_LRA and MoC_DT? What different concern does each address?
  6. An EAD MoC budget is 90% category A and almost nothing in B or C. What signal does this send to the supervisor, and what is the typical correction?
  7. SS3/24 §18 expects MoC quanta to decline over time. Under what conditions? What signal does a flat MoC over multiple cycles send?
  8. Distinguish under-MoC from over-MoC pathologies. What is the supervisor's view of each, and what is the right answer?
  9. The limitations register has 15 entries; the MoC table has 8 entries. What is the audit anomaly, and what is the remediation?
  10. Compute a regulatory PD for a grade with LRA PD = 2.4%, category A MoC = 20% relative, category B MoC = 5% relative, category C MoC = 5% relative, and the 0.03% retail floor.
