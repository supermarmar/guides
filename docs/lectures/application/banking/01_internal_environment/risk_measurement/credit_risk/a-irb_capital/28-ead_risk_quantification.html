EAD risk quantification: grades, LRA, downturn and MoC

Turn the PiT EADF from lecture 27 into a regulator-grade EAD. Group the PiT scores into a small number of grades, calibrate each grade to its long-run average EADF, compute the downturn EADF over the 2008-09 stress period, apply MoC, and take the max of the LRA + MoC and downturn + MoC paths to produce the regulatory EADF that enters the K formula.

Step 1

The 30-second version

EAD risk quantification is the calibration phase of the EAD build. The continuous PiT EADF outputs from lecture 27 are binned into EAD risk grades (typically 10 to 15, fewer than PD's 26 because EADF is bounded in [0, 1+] and shows less rank-dispersion than PD). Each grade is calibrated to its long-run average EADF (LRA EADF) over a cycle-covering window selected via the same 4-step PCA + F-test process as PD. A downturn EADF is computed per grade by averaging realised EADFs over the identified downturn window (typically January 2008 to December 2008, the GFC peak). MoC overlays apply under the same A / B / C categorisation. The final regulatory EADF per grade is:

Regulatory_EADF(grade) = max( LRA_EADF(grade) + MoC_LRA(grade),
                              Downturn_EADF(grade) + MoC_DT(grade) )

This is the EAD analogue of PD's regulatory PD construction (lecture 14) and LGD's regulatory LGD construction (lecture 21). Three structural similarities matter: the same 4-step LRA period selection process via PCA over MEVs; the same overlap-bias check under PRA SS4/24 §11.10c; the same MoC three-category framework. Three structural differences: fewer grades (10 to 15 versus PD's 26), the max-of-paths regulatory rule (closer in spirit to LGD's max-over-candidates downturn construction), and no Basel 3.1 input floor analogous to PD's 0.03% (Basel 3.1 introduces certain EAD-input floors but they are exposure-class-specific rather than a portfolio-wide minimum).

The output is a table of around 12 rows where each row contains the EADF range, the calibrated LRA, the downturn, the MoC, and the final regulatory EADF. Applied to a facility at observation time, regulatory_EADF × limit_at_observation gives the EAD that enters the K formula in lecture 31.

Feynman test The lecture-27 GLM ranked facilities by exposure intensity. The lecture-28 calibration says what those rankings mean in absolute terms. The same logic as PD calibration applies (grade construction, LRA anchor, MoC), with one EAD-specific twist: the regulatory EADF takes the maximum of two MoC-adjusted paths (LRA-based and downturn-based) rather than constructing downturn as a separate add-on to LRA. The max-of-paths approach is operationally simpler than PD's because EAD does not have a single LRA-floor convention.
Step 2

Why this matters

EAD calibration is less material per percentage point than LGD or PD calibration, but for revolving books with large undrawn commitments the lever is still substantial. A retail credit-card book with 60% average utilisation at observation and a calibrated regulatory EADF of 75% versus 80% moves average EAD per facility by roughly 8% of the limit; for a £10 billion limit book that is £800 million of EAD difference, which translates to materially different RWA. The supervisor's calibration review on EAD focuses on the downturn-EADF construction (was the downturn period identified correctly?) and the MoC adequacy (does the bank acknowledge its data limitations?).

The max-of-paths regulatory rule is the structural feature most worth understanding. PD calibration takes the LRA, then adds an MoC on top to get the regulatory PD. EAD calibration computes two MoC-adjusted candidates (LRA + MoC_LRA and Downturn + MoC_DT) and takes the larger. The downturn EADF is typically higher than the LRA EADF (because customers draw down more in stress), so the downturn + MoC path is usually the binding constraint; banks where the downturn is unusually mild can find the LRA + MoC path binding instead. Either way the construction is deliberately conservative and the bank does not get to pick the lower of the two.

For F107 the exam-classic question on EAD calibration combines three elements: the 4-step LRA period selection (same as PD), the max-of-paths regulatory rule (EAD-specific), and the role of MoC. Candidates who articulate all three score well; candidates who treat EAD calibration as identical to PD calibration miss the max-of-paths distinction.

Step 3

The mental model

EAD calibration mirrors PD calibration's two-block architecture (grade construction then LRA / downturn calibration) with the max-of-paths rule at the end.

Three properties of the workflow matter. First, the grade count is smaller than PD (10 to 15 versus 26): the continuous EADF target naturally has less rank-dispersion than the bounded-but-rare-event PD distribution, so fewer grades are needed to achieve discriminatory power. Second, the LRA period selection is identical to PD: the 4-step PCA process from lecture 14 applies unchanged (the same MEVs, the same F-test on observed-rate variability, the same COVID exclusion and 2008-2009 GFC anchor). Third, the max-of-paths rule replaces PD's MoC-then-floor: where PD adds MoC on top of LRA then applies the 0.03% floor, EAD computes two MoC-adjusted paths and takes the larger.

Step 4

The detail

Grade construction

The continuous PiT EADF per facility is binned into a small number of risk grades. The construction follows the same four-step iterative process as PD's lecture 14:

  1. Back-score the PiT EADF model on every historical cohort in the development window to generate PiT EADF per (facility, snapshot).
  2. Create 100 initial granular bins, each containing 1% of the development sample, ranked by PiT EADF.
  3. Combine into final grades: merge adjacent bins to satisfy minimum-volume constraints (CRR 170(3)(b) of 1% to 30% per grade), monotonicity in realised EADF, and heterogeneity tests between adjacent grades. The typical retail-card outcome is 10 to 15 grades.
  4. Test on historical cohorts: apply the constructed grade boundaries to every historical cohort and compute the five CRR 170(3) test metrics (rank-order breaks, homogeneity, heterogeneity, migration, concentration). Iterate until all tests pass.

The five CRR 170(3) design tests applied to EAD grades:

ObjectiveReferenceTestThreshold
Minimum / maximum volume per gradeCRR 170(3)(b)Grade volume as % of portfolio≥ 1% and ≤ 30%
Homogeneity within gradeCRR 170(3)(c)F-test on within-grade EADF variance across cohortsp > 5%
Heterogeneity between adjacent gradesCRR 170(3)(c)t-test on the difference in mean EADF between adjacent gradesp < 5%

The substitution of F-test for chi-square (homogeneity) and t-test for binomial z-test (heterogeneity) reflects the continuous nature of the target.

LRA period selection (same 4-step process as PD)

The 4-step process from PD lecture 14 applies unchanged. Briefly:

  1. Derive economic factor via PCA over MEVs (unemployment rate, CPI, DTI, GDP, HPI, CCI, disposable income, industry charge-off rate; the same set used in PD calibration plus credit-card industry utilisation for EAD).
  2. Classify good and bad periods using PCA scores.
  3. Identify the economic cycle (peak-to-peak).
  4. F-test on observed-rate variability within the candidate LRA window versus the full cycle.

Standard outcomes for retail card portfolios:

  • COVID-19 exclusion (typically 2020 to 2022) because of distortions in customer drawing behaviour during stimulus / forbearance.
  • Downturn (DT) period: January 2008 to December 2008 (the wiki's specific anchor) or a slightly broader 2008 to 2009 window covering the GFC.
  • LRA period: typically 2008 Q1 to 2017 Q1 (mirroring PD), spanning the downturn through the next benign peak.
  • Selected MEV combination: UR + CPI + DTI from PCA option testing, with balanced good / bad quarter mix and F-test pass on EADF variability.

LRA EADF per grade

For each grade and each calibration cohort within the LRA window, the realised EADF for facilities in that grade is observed. The LRA EADF per grade is the average across cohorts:

LRA_EADF(grade)  =  average over cohorts in LRA window
                    of  (EAD-weighted mean realised EADF for facilities in this grade at this cohort)

Two weighting choices apply, analogous to LGD lecture 21:

  • EAD-weighted: each facility's realised EADF weighted by its limit at observation. The standard for IRB capital purposes.
  • Default-count-weighted: each facility weighted equally. Used as a back-test cross-check.

The overlap-bias check from PRA SS4/24 §11.10c also applies: where the cohort structure produces overlapping outcome windows (it does not for EAD because the fixed-horizon construction is non-overlapping; see lecture 25), the bank would recompute on non-overlapping windows and compare. For EAD this check usually returns negligible bias because the underlying sample is already non-overlapping by construction.

Downturn EADF per grade

For each grade, the downturn EADF is the realised EADF averaged over the identified downturn window (2008 to 2009):

Downturn_EADF(grade)  =  EAD-weighted mean realised EADF
                         for facilities in this grade
                         observed in cohorts within 2008-2009

Two operational checks accompany this calculation:

  • The downturn EADF per grade should be materially higher than the LRA EADF per grade (typically 5 to 10 percentage points higher for retail card portfolios). Where the difference is small or negative, the bank investigates: either the downturn period was insufficiently stressed or the grade construction has obscured the within-grade heterogeneity.
  • The downturn EADF must monotonically increase across grades (PRA SS4/24 §11.31 monotonicity requirement applied to the downturn calibration). Non-monotone downturn EADFs trigger grade-boundary adjustment.

MoC overlay

MoC for EAD follows the same A / B / C categorisation as PD and LGD:

  • Category A: data limitations. Limited cohort history (only one year of development cohorts is typical for EAD, see lecture 25), thin segment representation, structural breaks in limit-management policy. The dominant category for many banks.
  • Category B: methodology weaknesses. GLM specification choice (Gaussian + Identity is a simplification of the underlying Beta-like distribution), segmentation cut-off choices, implicit treatment of limit changes.
  • Category C: general estimation error. Residual statistical uncertainty in coefficient estimates and small-sample variance.

MoC is applied per grade. Typical aggregate MoC for a retail card EAD model adds 3 to 10 percentage points to the LRA EADF (smaller than PD's percentage-point MoC because EADF starts higher in level terms).

The max-of-paths regulatory rule

The wiki's specification:

Regulatory_EADF(grade)  =  max( LRA_EADF(grade)      + MoC_LRA(grade),
                                Downturn_EADF(grade) + MoC_DT(grade) )

This differs structurally from PD's LRA + MoC + floor framework. The max-of-paths rule is conservative by construction: if the downturn path produces a higher number than the LRA path, the bank holds the higher; if the LRA path is higher (rare, possible in segments where the downturn effect is mild), the LRA path wins.

For most retail card grades the downturn + MoC path is binding (Downturn_EADF + MoC_DT is typically 3 to 10 percentage points higher than LRA_EADF + MoC_LRA). Banks should be able to articulate per grade which path binds and why; the supervisor will sample-test the calculation.

No regulatory floor analogous to PD's 0.03%

Unlike PD, where Basel 3.1 introduces a 0.03% retail input floor, EAD has no portfolio-wide minimum input floor. Basel 3.1 does introduce certain EAD-related constraints:

  • For specific exposure classes (residential mortgages, qualifying revolving exposures), the EAD calculation incorporates supervisory haircuts and floor adjustments.
  • The output floor at 72.5% of standardised RWA effectively floors aggregate IRB RWA from below, which indirectly constrains the EAD modelling outputs at the aggregate level.

These are exposure-class-specific or aggregate-level mechanisms rather than per-grade input floors. The supervisor's conservatism on EAD instead comes through the max-of-paths rule, the SS4/24 §17 expectations on EAD methodology, and the EAD-specific MoC requirements.

The regulatory EADF table

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first lesson is that the max-of-paths rule does most of the conservatism work for EAD. Where PD's conservatism comes through a combination of LRA-anchored calibration and the 0.03% floor, EAD's comes through the parallel-paths construction and the MoC overlays applied to both. The downturn path is binding for nearly all grades on a typical retail card book; banks whose downturn EADF is below their LRA EADF are flagged for supervisory dialogue (it usually indicates a poorly-identified downturn period or a segmentation issue).

The second lesson is on the fewer-grades-than-PD outcome. EAD has 10 to 15 grades versus PD's 26 for two reasons. First, the EADF target is continuous and bounded in [0, 1+], so the rank-dispersion of model outputs is naturally smaller than PD's [0%, 100%] range. Second, the EAD development sample is smaller than PD's, so each grade needs more facilities to support credible calibration; this caps the practical grade count at around 15. Modellers who insist on 25 to 30 EAD grades find their per-grade volumes drop below the CRR 170(3)(b) 1% minimum or their per-grade homogeneity tests fail.

The third lesson is on MoC for EAD specifically. The wiki's note that "consistent with the PD methodology, MoC adjustments are applied to both LRA EADF and DT EADF" is operationally important: the two MoC overlays are typically different because they address different concerns. MoC_LRA addresses the LRA's representativeness of the cycle (data history gaps, sample-period exclusions); MoC_DT addresses the downturn period's representativeness of the worst stress the bank could face (the GFC may not be the worst stress; what if 2030 turns out to be worse?). The supervisor's MoC framework expects both to be quantified separately, not as a single aggregate uplift.

The fourth lesson is on the "no regulatory floor" framing. The supervisor's conservatism on EAD does not come through a portfolio-wide floor but through a combination of the max-of-paths rule, the SS4/24 §17 expectations, the Basel 3.1 exposure-class-specific haircuts, and the 72.5% output floor at the aggregate level. Banks should be able to articulate all four mechanisms; candidates at F107 who can list them score well.

The fifth and structurally most important lesson: EAD calibration is the lightest of the three IRB-parameter calibration phases. The grade count is smaller, the regulatory framework is principles-based rather than prescriptive, and the max-of-paths rule simplifies the choice between LRA-anchored and downturn-anchored conservatism. Modellers who treat EAD as the "easy parameter" sometimes under-invest in the calibration discipline; the validator and the supervisor still apply the full CRR 170(3) test battery and expect the same documentation rigour as for PD.

Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Write the regulatory EADF formula. Why is the construction called "max-of-paths" and how does it differ from PD's LRA + MoC + floor construction?
  2. Typical retail card EAD models use 10 to 15 grades versus PD's 26. Give two structural reasons for the smaller grade count.
  3. The CRR 170(3) tests apply to EAD grades. State the homogeneity and heterogeneity tests in EAD-specific form (continuous target).
  4. The LRA period selection uses the same 4-step PCA process as PD. State the four steps and identify what the F-test in step 4 tests for EAD specifically.
  5. Why is the downturn period for EAD typically January 2008 to December 2008 (or the broader 2008-2009 window)? What does this anchor produce empirically?
  6. Distinguish MoC_LRA from MoC_DT conceptually. What different concern does each address?
  7. Why does EAD not have a portfolio-wide regulatory input floor analogous to PD's 0.03%? Name three mechanisms that play the conservatism role instead.
  8. On a typical retail card portfolio, the downturn path is binding for most grades. What does it mean when the LRA path is binding instead, and what should the modelling team check?
  9. Write the EADF range, LRA EADF, MoC_LRA, downturn EADF, MoC_DT for a hypothetical grade where LRA = 30%, MoC_LRA = 3%, downturn = 38%, MoC_DT = 5%. Compute the regulatory EADF and identify which path binds.
  10. The regulatory EADF table feeds the K formula. Write the EAD formula in terms of regulatory EADF and limit at observation.
