The A-IRB process: nine stages, four parameters, one lifecycle

A roadmap of the rest of this track. How the model gets from raw transaction data through to a regulatory capital number, what each stage delivers, and how the lectures that follow map back onto the stages of the lifecycle.

Step 1

The 30-second version

An A-IRB model is built once and lives for many years. The discipline that keeps it valid is the development-and-maintenance lifecycle: nine stages that run from data sourcing through to ongoing monitoring, each with a defined output, a regulatory anchor, and an independent-validation tollgate. The same lifecycle applies to the PD, LGD and EAD components, with the dependent variable and statistical machinery varying per component.

The nine stages can be grouped into four blocks. Block one (stages 1 to 3) prepares the data and describes the portfolio. Block two (stages 4 to 6) is the modelling itself, run separately for PD, LGD and EAD, and within each component split into feature engineering, modelling, and testing. Block three (stages 7 and 8) wraps the model in conservatism (margin of conservatism, MoC) and assembles the regulatory capital number (RWA). Block four (stage 9) is the ongoing monitoring that keeps the model approved between full redevelopments. The remaining files in this track follow that order.

Feynman test The process exists because the regulator does not approve numbers, it approves processes. A point-estimate PD of 1.2% is meaningless on its own. What the supervisor approves is the evidence chain that produced it: which data, which quality checks, which variables, which calibration, which conservatism overlay, which back-testing. Each of the nine stages exists to add one verifiable link to that chain. The model output is the residue of the process; the process is the asset.
Step 2

Why this matters

Two of the most common reasons banks lose IRB approval are not modelling failures but process failures. The first is missing data lineage: the supervisor cannot trace a reported number back to source. The second is undocumented model change: a parameter has drifted between the original development and the current run without going through formal model-change governance. Neither of these has anything to do with statistics; both come down to whether the lifecycle has been operated as designed.

The lifecycle also forces honesty about uncertainty. Stage 7 (margin of conservatism) is where every weakness identified in earlier stages gets converted into an explicit uplift to the parameters. Category A MoC covers data limitations, category B covers methodology weaknesses, category C covers general estimation error. A model that reaches stage 7 without any MoC uplift is suspicious, not virtuous: it means the development team failed to surface the weaknesses that every model has.

For F107 the lifecycle is also the answer to the standard exam question "describe how an A-IRB model is developed". The expected answer is the nine stages, not a vague reference to "internal modelling". Each stage's purpose, regulatory anchor and headline output is testable in its own right.

Step 3

The mental model

The diagram below maps the nine stages onto the four blocks. The PD, LGD and EAD columns inside block two run in parallel: each parameter has its own feature engineering, its own modelling and its own testing, but they share data engineering upstream and MoC and RWA assembly downstream.

Three properties of the diagram are worth highlighting. First, data engineering is shared across the three components: PD, LGD and EAD all draw from the same model-ready dataset, which is why data quality has compounding influence. Second, MoC is applied after testing but before RWA assembly: conservatism is added to the parameters themselves, not to the headline capital number. Third, monitoring is a loop, not a terminus: a finding from quarterly monitoring (a drift in PiT-to-realised, a structural break in macro variables, a portfolio-mix shift) is what triggers the next recalibration or redevelopment.

Step 4

The detail

Each of the nine stages has a defined output, a regulatory anchor in the CRR and supervisory statements (SS3/24 for PD and DoD, SS4/24 for LGD and EAD), and an independent-validation tollgate where the model-validation function challenges the development team. Three transverse activities sit alongside the nine stages and apply throughout. Validation is the independent challenge function: model owners drive each stage to completion, and a separate validation team runs an adversarial review at each tollgate, maintaining a challenge log with issues tracked to resolution. Use-test compliance (covered in the next lecture) is the requirement that the same model used for capital is also used for credit decisioning, pricing and provisioning; the lifecycle is the evidence that this requirement is met in practice. Documentation is the deliverable that survives staff turnover and supervisory inspection; the development document and the validation document together form the evidence package the regulator audits.

Two failure modes recur across banks. The first is model drift without redevelopment: realised default rates pull away from PiT predictions year on year, but no recalibration is triggered because the in-house thresholds are too loose. The second is data refresh without revalidation: a new vintage of bureau or product data is plugged into the model without re-running representativeness checks. Both are caught at SREP if not caught internally. The monitoring stage exists to prevent the first; the change-control framework (covered later) exists to prevent the second.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The lifecycle reads like a linear pipeline but is operated as a layered system. The deepest layer (data engineering) is treated as a quasi-static infrastructure: it changes rarely, governed by IT change-control, and supports many models. The middle layer (the PD/LGD/EAD modelling components) is refreshed on a cycle, typically annually for recalibration and every three to five years for full redevelopment. The shallow layer (monitoring) runs continuously, looking for the signal that the middle layer needs work. The error a junior modeller makes is to treat the whole nine-stage process as a single redevelopment project. The experienced practitioner runs them at different cadences.

The other lesson worth internalising early is that conservatism flows in one direction. Once a model is built, every adjustment moves the parameters toward higher PD, higher LGD, higher EAD, never the reverse. A model whose recalibration tightens parameters (lowers PD, for instance) is treated by the regulator with substantially more suspicion than one that loosens them. This asymmetry is deliberate: the supervisor would rather see banks over-capitalised than under-capitalised, so the lifecycle is built with one-way valves at every stage where conservatism is added.

Finally, the lifecycle is the answer to the question "why are A-IRB models so much more expensive to operate than standardised?" Standardised has no lifecycle: the bank looks up an exposure in the risk-weight table and is done. A-IRB requires a team of quants, validators, data engineers and model owners, plus the surrounding infrastructure, to run the nine stages year in and year out. The capital relief A-IRB delivers must justify that operating cost, which is why A-IRB is concentrated in large books (mortgages, large-corporate, retail credit cards) where the unit economics work.
