LGD sampling and representativeness

Choose which resolved-facility rows from the workout-tracked MRD enter the development, calibration, OOS and OOT samples; verify that the chosen samples reflect the portfolio the LGD model will be applied to. The same principles as PD sampling apply, with three structural differences: the population is one to two orders of magnitude smaller, the time index is default vintage rather than calendar snapshot, and the resolved-versus-unresolved split is a hard filter.

Step 1

The 30-second version

LGD sampling carves the resolved-facility population from lecture 17 into four samples: a development sample for fitting the three-component mixture, a calibration sample for the LRA and downturn LGD, an out-of-sample (OOS) test set for cross-facility generalisation, and an out-of-time (OOT) hold-out for temporal stability. The protocol mirrors PD sampling from lecture 11 but with three structural adjustments forced by the LGD context: the population is smaller (tens of thousands of resolved facilities versus tens of millions of PD rows), the indexing is by default vintage (cohort year of the default event) rather than calendar snapshot, and only resolved facilities are eligible (SS4/24 §14.10).

The representativeness work asks the same question as the PD lecture: does the development sample reflect the portfolio the model will be applied to? The diagnostic battery (PSI, chi-square, CDI) applies unchanged, with two LGD-specific additions: resolved-vs-unresolved representativeness (the development sample contains only resolved workouts; do their characteristics match the wider defaulted population including those still in progress?) and collateral-type representativeness (LGD varies dramatically by collateral type, so the development sample must reflect the collateral mix of the application portfolio). Macroeconomic representativeness is checked only for the calibration sample, as with PD.

Because the LGD wiki has no dedicated sampling file, the protocol in this lecture is constructed from CRR Article 174(c) (general representativeness anchor), Article 181 (LGD-specific data requirements), PRA SS4/24 §8.2 to §8.15 (representativeness diagnostics) and §14.8 to §14.10 (resolved facilities and MRP), EBA/GL/2017/16 paragraphs 144 to 153 (LGD development sample design), and standard ML practice. The forward sampling design also feeds the LGD risk-quantification lecture (21), which uses the calibration sample to compute downturn LGD per SS4/24 §15.

Feynman test The PD model worried about whether the development sample of millions of rows reflected today's portfolio composition. The LGD model worries about something different: whether the few thousand resolved workouts in the development sample reflect what the next few thousand workouts will look like, including the ones currently in flight that have not yet resolved. A bank whose historical defaults are dominated by old, fully-collateralised mortgages, but whose current portfolio is dominated by new, partially-secured loans, has a representativeness gap that PSI on snapshot variables will not detect; LGD-specific diagnostics are required.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is sample size. A typical retail PD model has 10 to 50 million development rows. A typical retail LGD model has 10,000 to 100,000 resolved-workout rows. With three orders of magnitude less data, the standard ML protections (large-sample asymptotics, generous train/test splits, cross-validation noise) work less well, and the modelling team has to be more disciplined about leakage, multicollinearity and over-fitting. The downsampling rules from PD (which assumed surplus data) reverse: every resolved facility is precious; no segment is downsampled, and the question of how to up-weight under-represented segments becomes operationally important.

The second reason is the resolution constraint. Only resolved facilities can be used to label realised LGD; unresolved facilities are excluded (or treated with survival-analysis machinery, which is rare in practice). This creates a survivorship bias: the resolved population systematically over-represents fast-resolving workouts (small unsecured exposures that resolve via debt sale or charge-off in months) and under-represents slow-resolving workouts (large secured exposures where the workout genuinely takes years). The MRP truncation convention (force unresolved facilities to non-cure with 100% LGD on the remaining balance) is one defence against this, but it bakes in conservative bias that the supervisor will inspect at SREP.

The third reason is downturn coverage. PD's LRA window is constructed to include at least one downturn so the LRA reflects cyclical behaviour. The same requirement applies to LGD via CRR Article 181(1)(b), but with a complication: LGD downturn periods are often offset from PD downturn periods. PDs spike at the start of a recession; LGDs spike a year or two later as the workout backlog clears and recoveries underperform. A development window that captures the PD downturn cleanly may miss the LGD downturn; the LGD sampling design has to plan for this explicitly.

Step 3

The mental model

The LGD development population is indexed differently from the PD population. PD rows are (obligor, snapshot_month) tuples spread across all eligible snapshots. LGD rows are resolved-default events indexed by default vintage (the calendar quarter or year in which the default occurred). The diagram below puts the four LGD samples on a default-vintage timeline.

Three properties of the layout matter. First, the resolved cohort cutoff at v_C = today - MRP is a hard constraint: defaults occurring after this date have not yet been observable through a full workout and cannot be labelled with realised LGD. The cutoff varies by collateral type (60 months for unsecured retail; 96 to 120 months for secured property). Second, the calibration sample deliberately includes downturn vintages: unlike PD where the development and calibration windows often overlap heavily, LGD calibration needs the GFC vintage explicitly to capture downturn recovery experience. Third, the application sample includes unresolved facilities: the LGD2 model (covered in lecture 21) is the bank's tool for predicting remaining loss on these.

Five sampling decisions

The PD sampling lecture's five decisions all apply, with adjustments.

  1. Vintage window selection. Defaults from too early in the history are dropped if the bank's default-definition logic was not operational at the time (CRR Article 178 limbs were not consistently applied). Defaults from too late in the history are dropped because the workout has not had MRP months to complete. Distorted periods (COVID, system migrations) are excluded with documented dates. The eligible vintage list is recorded in the limitations register.
  2. De-duplication. One row per default event, not per facility. An obligor with two defaults in different vintages contributes two rows; an obligor with a single default that cures and re-defaults within the 9-month independence period is collapsed to one default event (SS4/24 §12.2; see lecture 03).
  3. Sample-splitting design. Random row-level splits within a vintage are acceptable for OOS (different facilities in the same vintage are reasonably independent). Temporal splits (OOT on the most recent vintage) are required for temporal stability. The PD rejection of random row-level splits (because of correlation across snapshots of the same facility) does not apply here: each facility contributes only one row, so row-level independence is restored automatically.
  4. Downsampling. Reversed from PD: every resolved facility is precious. No segment is downsampled. Where a segment is over-represented (a single product or a single partner accounting for most of the defaults), up-weighting of under-represented segments may be applied to recover proportionality to the application portfolio; the weights are documented per segment.
  5. Calibration window. Drawn separately from the development window. Must include downturn vintages to anchor downturn LGD. Typical retail calibration window: 10 to 15 years spanning the GFC and at least one subsequent benign period. The window is the same one used to compute the LGD reference value (SS4/24 §15.7).

Three LGD-specific sampling traps

The MRP truncation bias. Facilities whose workout exceeds the MRP are forced to non-cure with 100% LGD on the remaining balance. For unsecured retail with a 60-month MRP, this affects maybe 1 to 2% of resolved facilities and is operationally minor. For secured retail with a 60-month MRP applied to property workouts that genuinely take 7 to 10 years, it can affect 20 to 30% of resolved facilities and bakes in material conservative bias. The standard remediation is to lengthen the MRP for secured (96 or 120 months), at the cost of a longer data-history requirement.

The cohort lag problem. A default in vintage v_C - 5 years has had time to complete its workout and contribute a resolved row. A default in vintage v_C - 1 year has not. The development sample is therefore biased toward the older vintages, which had a different macroeconomic context, different policy environment and different operational practice. Modellers should verify that the vintage distribution of the development sample roughly matches the vintage distribution of all resolved facilities; up-weighting more recent resolved vintages compensates for the lag.

The cure / no-cure proportion. Cured facilities and no-cure facilities have very different realised LGDs; the proportion of each in the development sample directly affects the model's outputs. A development sample dominated by no-cure facilities will produce higher fitted LGDs; one dominated by cured facilities will produce lower. Representativeness on cure proportion (development sample vs application portfolio) is a specific diagnostic worth running explicitly.

Representativeness diagnostics

The same PSI / chi-square / CDI battery from lecture 11 applies. Three LGD-specific additions:

  • Resolved-vs-unresolved representativeness. The development sample contains only resolved facilities. The application portfolio contains both resolved and in-flight workouts. PSI is computed on key risk drivers (product, collateral type, exposure size, age on book at default) between the resolved development sample and the full defaulted population including in-flight workouts. Material divergence indicates the resolved facilities are systematically different from the unresolved ones, which is a survivorship-bias warning.
  • Collateral-type representativeness. For secured portfolios, the development sample's distribution across collateral types (residential property by region, commercial property, vehicles, securities) must reflect the application portfolio's distribution. Chi-square on the collateral mix is the standard diagnostic; material divergence triggers re-weighting or segmented modelling.
  • Vintage representativeness. The cohort distribution of the development sample is checked against the cohort distribution of all eligible defaults. PSI on default vintage detects whether old vintages are over-represented (the cohort lag problem above).

Macroeconomic representativeness for the calibration sample

Applied only to the LGD calibration sample (which feeds downturn LGD and the LGD reference value). The standard checks:

  • Quarterly realised LGDs are plotted against macroeconomic variables (GDP, unemployment, regional house-price index for secured, insolvency rate for unsecured) to confirm cyclical sensitivity.
  • The downturn vintage (GFC 2008 to 2009 in the reference build) is verified to produce materially higher realised LGDs than benign vintages.
  • The LGD reference value (SS4/24 §15.7) is computed as the simple average of the two years with the highest balance-weighted LGD; the calibration window must produce a reference value that the downturn LGD methodology can match or exceed.

Recovery-policy representativeness

The same qualitative check as in PD sampling, but with sharper stakes for LGD. A material change in collections strategy (a switch from in-house collections to external agency, or a change in debt-sale forward-flow contracts) shifts realised LGDs materially. The modelling team confirms with the collections team that no material policy change is anticipated in the deployment cycle; where one is anticipated, the model either accommodates it (via a policy flag) or excludes the affected vintages from development.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The first lesson is that the resolved-versus-unresolved diagnostic is the supervisor's first question. A development sample whose resolved facilities look systematically different from the unresolved facilities (older, smaller, less-secured) is one that has been silently biased by survivorship. The supervisor will probe with the PSI panel and look for material divergence in product mix, collateral mix or exposure size. The right answer is to acknowledge the bias, quantify it, and either lengthen the MRP (more data, less truncation), apply re-weighting, or carry an MoC overlay. The wrong answer is to claim no bias and let the supervisor demonstrate otherwise.

The second lesson is on cohort lag. Workouts that completed in time to contribute to the development sample are by definition older than today minus the MRP. They reflect a credit policy, collections strategy and macroeconomic environment that is at least 5 years out of date. A development sample dominated by 2008 to 2014 resolved workouts is being asked to predict 2026 LGDs; the supervisor's challenge is whether the model has been adjusted for the gap. Practitioners who treat the development sample as "the data" and apply it to today's portfolio without thinking about the lag get caught.

The third lesson is on vintage weighting. PD sampling worried about not having enough data; LGD sampling worries about not having representative data. The two responses are different. PD downsamples and weights to recover proportionality across the snapshot population. LGD up-weights under-represented segments and vintages to recover proportionality across the application portfolio. The weighting is more consequential for LGD because the sample is smaller and the heterogeneity within the sample is higher.

The fourth lesson is operational: the LGD sampling decisions, made once at development, propagate through every subsequent recalibration. Changing the MRP, the calibration window, the vintage selection or the resolved-cohort cutoff invalidates the back-test, the LRA calibration and the regulatory PD-LGD-EAD parameter set. Banks therefore standardise the sampling protocol once and revisit it only at full redevelopment. Modellers should document the protocol in enough detail that a successor team can reproduce it exactly.
