Regulatory capital for credit risk

The regulatory wrapper inside which A-IRB lives. What "regulatory capital" actually is, why three parallel approaches (standardised, F-IRB, A-IRB) coexist, how the Basel framework arrived at its current shape, and where A-IRB sits within that.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Regulatory capital is the supervisor's view of how much loss-absorbing equity a bank must hold against the risks it takes. For credit risk, the demand side of that calculation is risk-weighted assets (RWA), and the supply side is Common Equity Tier 1, Additional Tier 1, and Tier 2 capital instruments. Pillar 1 sets the minimum: total capital must equal at least 8% of RWA. Everything else in this lecture, and everything in the rest of the A-IRB track, is about how to compute the RWA on the demand side.

Three parallel approaches coexist. The standardised approach looks each exposure up against a regulatory risk-weight table, with external credit ratings doing most of the differentiation work. The foundation IRB approach (F-IRB) lets the bank estimate PD internally, but holds LGD, EAD, and maturity at supervisory values. The advanced IRB approach (A-IRB) lets the bank estimate all four parameters subject to regulatory approval. Behind A-IRB sits a single mathematical engine: the BCBS one-factor Vasicek model that turns PD, LGD, EAD, and effective maturity into a capital requirement K through a 99.9% one-year worst-case default rate. RWA is then K multiplied by 12.5 (the inverse of the 8% minimum). Basel 3.1, the final post-crisis reform package, places an output floor at 72.5% of standardised RWA underneath the IRB result, capping how much capital relief a bank's internal models can earn.

Feynman test Regulatory capital is the supervisor's rules-set answer to one question: how much shareholders' equity should this bank hold so that, when it loses money on its loans next year, the bank can absorb the loss without becoming insolvent? The standardised approach answers it with a lookup table. The IRB approach answers it with a model. A-IRB is the most permissive version of that model: the bank estimates all the parameters itself. In exchange the bank submits to a permanent regime of supervisory scrutiny, model validation, and a regulatory floor that bounds how much capital relief any model is allowed to deliver.
Step 2

Why this matters

Capital is the scarcest single resource on a bank's balance sheet. Equity costs roughly 10 to 12% per year to compensate shareholders for the risk they bear, against perhaps 3 to 4% for senior debt. Any rule that forces the bank to fund a higher share of its assets with equity raises its weighted average funding cost and depresses return on equity. The choice between standardised and A-IRB for a given portfolio can move RWA by a factor of four to six, which in turn moves required capital by the same factor. For a large lender, that is the difference between a competitive mortgage book and a structurally loss-making one.

For most large banks the IRB approach is therefore not optional. The competitive disadvantage of running standardised across a large book against IRB peers is severe enough that the multi-year approval programme is justifiable on commercial grounds alone. The supervisor accepts this; it is the explicit policy intent behind the IRB option that banks should be incentivised to invest in measurement infrastructure. The trade is permanent: once a portfolio is approved for IRB, reverting to standardised is operationally and reputationally costly, and exit requires supervisory sign-off.

The 2008 financial crisis stress-tested the bargain. Regulatory capital adequacy ratios before the crisis were comfortably above 8%; most failed institutions had headline ratios in the 11 to 14% range right up to the point of failure. The diagnosis was twofold. First, the numerator (Tier 1 capital) included instruments that did not absorb losses on a going-concern basis. Second, the denominator (RWA) had been understated by IRB models that calibrated to a benign sample and missed tail correlations. Basel III addressed the numerator (raising Common Equity Tier 1 minimums, introducing buffers, narrowing the definition of capital). Basel 3.1 addressed the denominator (input floors on PD and LGD, removal of A-IRB for low-default portfolios, and the output floor at 72.5% of standardised RWA). The A-IRB framework you encounter today is the post-2017 settled version of that response.

The mental model

The three approaches differ in one dimension: who supplies the parameters. The K formula is the same engine in all three; the disagreement is upstream of it. The second picture worth carrying is the timeline. Basel I (1988) introduced the 8% minimum and four broad risk-weight buckets. Basel II (2006) added the standardised approach with external ratings and opened the IRB option. Basel III (post-2010) tightened the supply side: more Common Equity Tier 1, conservation and countercyclical buffers, a leverage ratio, the LCR and NSFR. Basel 3.1 (BCBS d424, December 2017; implementation phased from 2023 to 2030) tightened the demand side: input floors on IRB parameters, removal of A-IRB for low-default portfolios, the output floor, and standardised-approach reforms.

Step 4

The detail

Pillar 1: the 8% rule and the capital adequacy ratio

The Basel Pillar 1 minimum capital requirement is mechanically simple. Total regulatory capital \(K\) must be at least 8% of total risk-weighted assets:

$$ K \;\geq\; 8\% \times \text{RWA} $$

Equivalently, the capital adequacy ratio (CAR) must be at least 8%:

$$ \text{CAR} \;=\; \frac{K}{\text{RWA}} \;\geq\; 8\% $$

The 8% number is the only constant across forty years of Basel evolution. Everything else, every approach, every refinement, is in service of computing the denominator. The original Basel I Accord considered only credit risk in the RWA. A 1996 Amendment added a market-risk component; Basel II added an operational-risk component; subsequent reforms refined each.

Basel I: four buckets and one rule of thumb

Under Basel I, a bank's assets were assigned to one of four broad risk categories with prescribed risk weights ranging from 0% (sovereign exposures in the bank's reporting currency) to 100% (corporate loans). Retail mortgages, viewed as safer, attracted a 50% risk weight. The RWA on any exposure was simply:

$$ \text{RWA} \;=\; \text{Exposure} \times w $$

The framework was deliberately blunt. A AAA-rated corporate and an unrated small business received the same 100% weight, which created two well-documented distortions: cherry-picking (a bank would prefer the higher-yielding unrated borrower because it consumed identical capital) and arbitrage (banks moved capital-heavy exposures off-balance-sheet through securitisation). Some emerging-markets jurisdictions still operate under Basel I, so multinational banking groups can find themselves running Basel I in one country, Basel II in another, and Basel III on consolidation.

Basel II standardised approach: external ratings and a lookup table

The standardised approach refined Basel I along the risk-sensitivity dimension. Risk weights are now driven by external credit ratings from approved external credit assessment institutions (ECAIs). Sovereign weights range from 0% to 150%; corporate weights from 20% to 150%; retail unsecured at 75%; residential mortgages at 35%; commercial real estate at 100%. Two governance requirements gate the use of external ratings: the supervisor must have approved the ECAI, and the bank must have performed independent due diligence (which can raise but not lower the risk weight).

Two methods exist for adjusting risk weights for collateral. The simple approach substitutes the collateral's risk weight for the counterparty's risk weight on the collateralised portion (with a 20% floor on the substituted weight). The comprehensive approach applies haircuts to the exposure and the collateral, then computes a net exposure to which the counterparty's risk weight is applied. The comprehensive approach is mandatory for the trading-book counterparty credit risk calculation; in the banking book the bank can choose.

The IRB approach: internal ratings, one Vasicek engine

The IRB approach replaces the lookup table with a parametric model. The capital requirement is the 99.9% one-year value at risk on the credit portfolio, less expected loss. The model behind the VaR is the BCBS one-factor Vasicek model (often described as a single-factor Gaussian copula on time to default). For a single exposure the model produces the worst-case default rate (WCDR), which is the probability of default conditional on a 99.9% adverse realisation of the systematic factor:

$$ \text{WCDR} \;=\; N\!\left( \frac{N^{-1}(\text{PD}) + \sqrt{R(\text{PD})} \cdot N^{-1}(0.999)}{\sqrt{1 - R(\text{PD})}} \right) $$

The capital requirement per unit of EAD is then:

$$ \text{UL} \;=\; \text{VaR}_{99.9\%} \;=\; \text{LGD}^{\text{DT}} \cdot \text{WCDR} $$

$$ \text{EL} \;=\; \text{LGD}^{\text{DT}} \cdot \text{PD} $$

$$ K \;=\; \big( \text{UL} - \text{EL} \big) \cdot \text{MA}(\text{PD}, M) \;=\; \text{LGD}^{\text{DT}} \cdot \big( \text{WCDR} - \text{PD} \big) \cdot \text{MA}(\text{PD}, M) $$

RWA is then K multiplied by 12.5 (the reciprocal of the 8% minimum) and by EAD:

$$ \text{RWA} \;=\; 12.5 \cdot K \cdot \text{EAD} $$

The 12.5 factor is a unit conversion. The Basel I tradition expressed capital demands as a ratio against asset values; the Basel II IRB engine produces capital demands directly. Multiplying K by 12.5 puts the IRB result back into the RWA units that the rest of the framework speaks.

Expected loss is priced, not capitalised The IRB capital formula subtracts EL from UL. The reasoning: expected loss is meant to be covered by the bank's pricing (the interest spread on a loan recovers expected losses on average). Capital is held against the unexpected portion: the gap between the worst-case 99.9% loss and the long-run average. This is the principle that lets the same model object (the loss distribution) feed two different mechanisms: pricing covers the body of the distribution, capital covers the tail.

F-IRB versus A-IRB: which parameters the bank supplies

The two IRB variants share the same engine but differ in the parameter allocation.

Under Basel 3.1, A-IRB is no longer available for exposures to large corporates (revenue above €500 million), financial institutions, or equities; those move to F-IRB or standardised. The rationale is that A-IRB rests on a calibration sample that, for low-default portfolios, is too thin to support reliable bank-specific estimates. F-IRB persists in those segments because the bank's PD remains informative even where the LGD calibration cannot be trusted.

Maturity adjustment

For corporate, sovereign, and bank exposures, the K formula scales by a maturity adjustment that captures the additional credit deterioration risk on longer-dated exposures. Even within a single year, a five-year loan to a borrower whose credit rating drops has lost economic value, even if the borrower has not defaulted. The maturity adjustment quantifies that migration risk.

$$ b(\text{PD}) \;=\; \left( 0.11852 - 0.05478 \cdot \ln(\text{PD}) \right)^2 $$

$$ \text{MA}(\text{PD}, M) \;=\; \frac{1 + (M - 2.5) \cdot b(\text{PD})}{1 - 1.5 \cdot b(\text{PD})} $$

At \(M = 2.5\) years the MA equals one. Below 2.5 it reduces capital; above 2.5 it raises capital, with the slope steeper at lower PD (because a low-PD borrower has more room to migrate downward than a high-PD borrower has). For retail exposures the maturity adjustment is set identically to one: the regulator views retail portfolios as sufficiently homogeneous in maturity that no within-portfolio adjustment is needed.

Defaulted assets

A defaulted exposure cannot have a PD (the borrower has already defaulted, so the probability is one). The K formula is replaced with a direct loss-coverage rule based on the best estimate of expected loss (BEEL, also called ELBE):

$$ K \;=\; \max\!\left( 0, \; \text{LGD}^{\text{DT}} - \text{BEEL} \right) \cdot \text{EAD} $$

The BEEL is the bank's best estimate of the loss that will ultimately be incurred on the defaulted facility, given current information; it is usually equal to the specific accounting provision. The capital requirement covers the residual difference between the downturn LGD estimate and the central expected outcome. If BEEL already exceeds downturn LGD (a defaulted facility expected to lose more than the downturn-LGD parameter would suggest), the capital charge is floored at zero; the excess flows through the income statement as a provision.

Worked example: corporate

Suppose a portfolio consists of £1 billion of loans to BBB-rated corporates. The bank estimates PD = 0.10%, LGD = 60%, average maturity 2.5 years (so MA = 1 by construction). Asset correlation at PD = 0.10% is approximately 0.24; WCDR works out to roughly 3.4%. RWA is:

$$ \text{RWA} \;=\; 12.5 \cdot 1000 \cdot 0.60 \cdot (0.034 - 0.001) \cdot 1.0 \;\approx\; \pounds 250\text{m} $$

Compare against Basel I (£1bn at 100%, giving £1bn of RWA) and the Basel II standardised approach (around £1bn at a 100% weight for an unrated corporate). A-IRB cuts RWA on this portfolio by roughly 75%. The output floor caps that benefit at 27.5% (1 minus 72.5%): once Basel 3.1 is fully phased in, the bank cannot drop below 72.5% of the standardised RWA, so on this portfolio the binding RWA would be £725m rather than £250m.

Worked example: retail mortgage

£500m of residential mortgages with PD = 0.5%, LGD = 20%. Asset correlation for residential mortgages is fixed at 0.15. WCDR works out to roughly 6.7%, and (with no maturity adjustment for retail) the K factor per unit of EAD is \( 0.20 \cdot (0.067 - 0.005) = 1.24\% \). RWA:

$$ \text{RWA} \;=\; 12.5 \cdot 500 \cdot 0.0124 \;\approx\; \pounds 78\text{m} $$

Against Basel I's £250m (50% weight) and standardised's £175m (35% weight). Mortgages are where the IRB benefit is largest, which is also where Basel 3.1's input floors and the output floor bite hardest.

The South African scaling factor

BCBS d424 reserves the right to apply a scaling factor (above or below 1.0) if the supervisor judges the aggregate capital requirement to be too high or too low. The South African Reserve Bank has historically applied a 1.06 multiplier to IRB RWA, consistent with the original Basel II calibration. Some jurisdictions apply additional add-ons; in South Africa, the Banks Act requires the greater of 10% of RWA (above the 8% BCBS minimum) or R250 million, with Tier 1 supplying at least 50% of the total. Jurisdictional adjustments like this are routine: the BCBS framework is a floor, not a ceiling.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The 99.9% confidence level and what it actually means

The IRB capital formula is calibrated so that the resulting capital, taken across the portfolio, is sufficient to absorb credit losses over a one-year horizon with 99.9% probability. The interpretation is "one bad year in a thousand". The horizon and the confidence level are both regulatory choices, not natural ones: a 99.9% one-year level was selected because it broadly corresponds to a single-A external rating for the bank itself, which the supervisor regards as the minimum acceptable standalone creditworthiness. A bank that holds exactly 8% of its IRB RWA in capital should, in expectation, be able to maintain a single-A credit rating through a one-in-a-thousand year. Move the confidence level to 99.99% and the capital requirement jumps by perhaps a third; move it to 99% and it falls by perhaps a quarter. The choice is a policy lever, not a derived constant.

The 12.5 factor is a unit conversion, not a buffer

The 12.5 multiplier between K and RWA exists because Basel I expressed capital in the ratio K/RWA. When the IRB approach produced K directly, the framework needed a way to put the new IRB output back into the old unit so the 8% minimum still made sense. Multiplying K by 12.5 (the inverse of 8%) is the conversion. It is not adding conservatism, it is preserving the convention.

Procyclicality and the TTC choice for K

The PD that enters the K formula is calibrated through-the-cycle. The point of TTC is to smooth out the cycle and produce a stable capital requirement: in a downturn, observed default rates rise sharply, but the TTC PD only edges up because it averages over expansion and downturn together. If point-in-time PDs were used instead, K would explode in a recession, force banks to deleverage, and amplify the downturn. The Basel countercyclical capital buffer (introduced under Basel III) is the policy patch on top: in good years, the supervisor accumulates an explicit buffer that can be released in bad years, neutralising the residual procyclicality that the TTC choice does not fully remove.

The output floor is a no-confidence vote in models

The Basel 3.1 output floor at 72.5% is the BCBS's formal admission that internal models cannot, in general, be trusted to produce capital numbers within 27.5% of the standardised approach. For credit risk specifically, the floor reflects an empirical observation: pre-2008 IRB models had calibrated to a 1990s-2000s sample that under-represented downturns. Bringing the IRB result within reach of the standardised approach is the regulator's way of capping how much the bank can lose if its model is wrong. The supervisory bargain that A-IRB once represented has narrowed accordingly.

F-IRB is increasingly a transitional category

Basel 3.1 removed A-IRB for several asset classes (large corporates, banks, equities), pushing them into F-IRB or standardised. F-IRB is the natural landing place where a bank's PD model is reliable but its LGD calibration cannot be supported by enough data. Over time, the asset classes that remain on A-IRB are the high-volume retail and mid-market corporate books where defaults are plentiful enough to support a defensible LGD calibration. The IRB framework is converging on the segments where it was always most defensible.

The use test as the supervisory anchor

CRR Article 144 requires that an IRB-approved model is used in the bank's actual decision-making, not only for the regulatory return. The IRB parameters must feed pricing, limit setting, capital allocation, and management information. The supervisor's logic is that a model the bank does not use is a model the bank has no incentive to maintain accurately. Most enforcement actions on IRB approvals trace back to a use-test gap: the model produces the regulatory number but a different number drives commercial decisions, and the divergence widens until the supervisor intervenes.

Scaling factors and add-ons

The 1.06 scaling factor the SARB applies, and the equivalent adjustments other jurisdictions apply, are reminders that BCBS d424 is a floor on the international rulebook, not a ceiling. National supervisors retain discretion to add capital where the local cycle, concentration profile, or supervisory judgement warrants it. The Pillar 2 capital add-on, which sits on top of Pillar 1, is the structured channel for this; jurisdictional scaling factors are a blunter instrument for risks the supervisor sees portfolio-wide.

Pricing versus capital: two readings of the same loss distribution

A single loss distribution feeds two different mechanisms. Pricing covers the mean of the distribution (expected loss). Capital covers the gap between the 99.9% quantile and the mean (unexpected loss). A loan priced at the regulatory PD and LGD recovers expected losses in average market conditions; a bank holding 8% of RWA absorbs unexpected losses in the one-in-a-thousand year. This separation is why a bank can be simultaneously well-capitalised and unprofitable: the capital pays for the tail of the loss distribution, the pricing has to pay for the body, and the two answers can move independently when the cycle turns.

  2. Name the three approaches to credit-risk RWA and identify, for each, which of PD, LGD, EAD, and M the bank supplies and which are supervisory.

  7. State the K formula for defaulted assets. Why is there no WCDR term?
