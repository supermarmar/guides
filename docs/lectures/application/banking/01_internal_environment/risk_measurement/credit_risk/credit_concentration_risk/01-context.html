Concentration risk: the assumption that makes Pillar 1 tractable

The IRB capital formula has a closed form because it assumes the portfolio is infinitely granular, so that idiosyncratic default risk cancels out and only the systematic factor remains. That assumption is worth quantifying rather than merely noting, because it is nearly exact for a retail book and wrong by a factor of three for a concentrated corporate one. The gap it leaves is the whole subject of this track.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Pillar 1 credit capital rests on the asymptotic single risk factor model: one systematic factor drives correlated defaults, the portfolio is infinitely granular, and therefore the law of large numbers removes idiosyncratic risk entirely, leaving a closed-form loss distribution. The capital charge is its 99.9th percentile less expected loss. Elegance is bought with the granularity assumption, and the assumption is not a rounding matter.

Simulated against the closed form on an otherwise identical portfolio, economic capital is 2.95 times the ASRF figure at five equal obligors, 1.80 times at twenty, 1.11 times at a hundred, and within two per cent of it by a thousand. So the assumption is close to exact for a retail portfolio with hundreds of thousands of accounts and catastrophically wrong for a corporate book with twenty names.

Economic capital, computed in the ICAAP, differs from Pillar 1 in three ways the source sets out: a higher confidence level, typically 99.95 to 99.97 per cent against 99.9; recognition of diversification, which Pillar 1 refuses; and a bank-defined formula rather than a prescribed one. The confidence level alone is a larger lever than most concentration add-ons: moving from 99.9 to 99.97 per cent raises capital by 27.0 per cent before any concentration adjustment at all.

Three kinds of concentration then sit in the gap between the two numbers: single-name, where a few obligors dominate; sector, where intra-industry correlation exceeds the fixed regulatory value; and geographic, where the systemic factor is one economy. The excess of internal economic capital over Pillar 1 becomes the credit concentration Pillar 2A add-on, which the supervisor examines in the SREP and converts into the Pillar 2 requirement.

Feynman test The capital formula pretends the bank has lent a tiny amount to infinitely many borrowers, so bad luck on any one of them cannot matter. That is a fair description of a mortgage book and a poor one of a book with twenty corporates in it. The difference is what this track is about.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this is where the modelling work in the rest of the credit risk track goes. The PDs, LGDs and EADs from the A-IRB and IFRS 9 chains are inputs to an economic capital model, whose output feeds the ICAAP, which the supervisor reviews in the SREP and converts into a Pillar 2 requirement. Concentration is the largest single reason those two capital numbers differ, so it is the largest single reason a bank has to explain itself.

The second reason is that the assumption being violated is not incidental to the formula, it is what makes the formula exist. Without infinite granularity there is no closed form and capital has to be simulated, which is exactly what an economic capital model does. So the concentration add-on is not a correction bolted onto a nearly-right answer; it is the difference between an analytic approximation and the quantity that approximation was standing in for.

The third reason is that the size of the violation varies by orders of magnitude across a single bank's portfolios, which has an organisational consequence. A retail mortgage book needs no granularity adjustment worth computing. A large corporate book needs one that can double the capital. A bank running one methodology across both is either wasting effort or understating risk, and which of the two depends on which portfolio dominates the calibration.

And a fourth reason that is about reading the framework honestly. The source says the IRB formula has conservatism baked in through PD floors, LGD floors and fixed correlations, so an internal model is a more honest reflection of current risk. That is true as far as it goes and it is only half the picture, because infinite granularity pushes regulatory capital down while the floors push it up. Step 5 shows that on a concentrated book the granularity effect dominates the floors by an order of magnitude, so Pillar 1 is not uniformly conservative. It is conservative on granular portfolios and anti-conservative on concentrated ones.

Step 4

The detail

Economic capital, and the three dimensions on which it differs

Economic capital is the bank's own estimate of the capital it needs, computed in the ICAAP without reference to regulatory prescription and anchored on its own risk appetite and strategy. Conceptually it uses the same construction as the IRB formula, a portfolio loss distribution with capital set at value-at-risk less expected loss, and differs in the inputs and the machinery rather than in the idea.

The confidence level is the bank's choice and reflects its target rating. A bank aiming at AA, with an implied one-year default probability around 0.03 per cent, uses a quantile of roughly 99.97 per cent, which is internally consistent since one minus 0.0003 is 0.9997. The formula is bank-defined, which is what permits point-in-time rather than through-the-cycle probabilities of default and multi-factor rather than single-factor systematic structures. And diversification is recognised, so portfolio economic capital is less than the sum of standalone contributions, with the difference being a benefit Pillar 1 declines to give.

The source also names the specific regulatory conservatism the internal model is free of: the 0.03 per cent probability of default floor for retail exposures, supervisory loss given default floors, and the fixed correlation formulae. Those are real constraints and step 5 puts them against the granularity effect, which runs the other way.

The three kinds of concentration, and why two of them are the same problem

The source names three. Single-name concentration is large exposures to individual obligors, which breaks the granularity assumption directly: idiosyncratic risk no longer cancels because there are too few independent draws. Sector concentration is correlated default within an industry, where the true intra-sector asset correlation exceeds the fixed value the regulatory formula uses. Geographic concentration is systemic risk tied to one economy.

The first is a violation of granularity and the second and third are violations of the single-factor structure, which makes them formally the same problem: the systematic risk is not one factor but several, and exposures cluster on subsets of them. That is why the standard treatment of geographic concentration is to add it as another dimension of the sector model rather than to build separate machinery, and it is why lecture 3 covers both under one heading.

The distinction matters for remediation as well as modelling. Single-name concentration is fixed by selling down or capping exposures, a portfolio management action on individual names. Sector and geographic concentration are fixed by changing the composition of new business, which is a strategy decision with a multi-year lead time. Two problems that appear on the same add-on line and have completely different response horizons.

The pipeline, and where the number becomes binding

The chain the source describes is worth stating explicitly because it is the answer to why any of this modelling matters. The A-IRB and IFRS 9 parameter models produce PDs, LGDs and EADs. Those feed the economic capital model, which simulates a portfolio loss distribution with the bank's own confidence level, point-in-time probabilities and multi-factor correlations. The excess of economic capital over Pillar 1 is decomposed by risk type in the ICAAP, with concentration usually the largest component on the credit side. The supervisor examines that decomposition in the SREP and sets the Pillar 2 requirement.

So the concentration add-on is one of the few places where an internal modelling judgement translates directly into a binding capital requirement without a prescribed formula standing in between. That is a considerable amount of weight resting on a correlation assumption, which is the subject of lecture 3.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Pillar 1 is not uniformly conservative, and the direction depends on the portfolio

The source's framing is that the IRB formula carries regulatory conservatism through probability of default floors, loss given default floors and fixed correlations, so an internal model gives a more honest picture of current risk. Each of those three is real. The framing is nonetheless incomplete, because it counts only the effects that push regulatory capital up.

Which reframes what an ICAAP is doing. On a retail book it is arguing that regulatory conservatism exceeds real risk, and the supervisor will resist. On a corporate book it is conceding that Pillar 1 understates, and the supervisor will agree. A single ICAAP does both at once, in different sections, and a bank that has not noticed the tension will be surprised in the SREP.

Basel dropped the granularity adjustment from Pillar 1, and the numbers say the decision was well aimed

The history the source records is worth having precisely. Vasicek's asymptotic result, developed through working papers from the late 1980s and formalised in 2002, gave the closed form. Gordy's 2003 paper in the Journal of Financial Intermediation provided the rigorous derivation of why that model justified the IRB formula and made the granularity assumption explicit as a limitation. Basel Committee Working Paper No. 8 in 2001 included a formal granularity adjustment, and the third consultative paper in 2003 proposed it as a mandatory Pillar 1 add-on. The final 2004 text removed it on grounds of complexity against benefit, and preserved it as a Pillar 2 expectation. Gordy and Lütkebohmert then gave the definitive treatment in a 2007 Bundesbank paper.

The source calls the decision pragmatic rather than theoretical, which is right, and it is worth adding that the simulation above says it was also well targeted rather than merely convenient.

Mandatory Pillar 1 adjustments are borne by every bank on every portfolio. The measured gap is under two per cent for portfolios above a thousand names, and the overwhelming majority of exposures in the overwhelming majority of banks sit in retail portfolios far above that threshold. So a mandatory adjustment would have imposed a computation on every bank that changed almost nothing for most of them, while the banks it mattered for are the minority with concentrated corporate books, are large enough to run a full portfolio model anyway, and are precisely the ones a supervisor examines individually in Pillar 2. Placing the requirement where the risk is, rather than where the rulebook is tidiest, is the right call and the numbers support it.

That is worth generalising, because the same test applies to any proposed refinement of a capital rule: compute what the refinement changes for the portfolios that dominate the system, not for the portfolio that motivated it. A correction that matters by a factor of three on five per cent of exposures and by two per cent on the rest belongs in Pillar 2.

The recurring shape of this track Every framework examined in the risk measurement series has an assumption that makes it tractable, and the assumption is always defensible on the portfolio it was calibrated against. ASRF assumes granularity. Value-at-risk assumed a distribution. The IRRBB outlier test assumes a parallel shock. In each case the failure is not that the assumption is wrong but that the framework is applied to portfolios the assumption was never intended to cover, and the bank rather than the rule is the thing that changed.
Where this goes wrong in practice A concentration add-on computed once, at a point in time, and then held as a fixed percentage uplift on Pillar 1 capital. Concentration is a property of the current portfolio and it moves when a single large exposure is written or repaid, whereas a fixed percentage moves only with total exposure. The two diverge immediately and in an uncontrolled direction: a bank that writes one large ticket and repays a hundred small ones has become more concentrated while its uplift has barely changed. The add-on has to be recomputed on the live portfolio, which is why the simpler diagnostic in lecture 2 exists alongside the rigorous one.
Exam tip If asked why Pillar 1 does not capture concentration risk, the marks are for naming the two assumptions separately. Infinite granularity means idiosyncratic risk is assumed to cancel, which is what single-name concentration violates. A single systematic factor means all correlation is captured by one number, which is what sector and geographic concentration violate. A third mark is available for noting that a granularity adjustment was proposed for Pillar 1 in the 2003 consultative paper and moved to Pillar 2 in the final 2004 text.
