Single-name concentration: two measures, one benchmark, and a proxy that answers a different question

The granularity adjustment and the Herfindahl-Hirschman index are usually presented as a rigorous method and a convenient proxy for the same quantity. They are not measuring the same thing. Simulating the source's own five-obligor portfolio gives a true add-on more than twenty times the one the index produces, and the reason is that the index compares the portfolio against equal weights while Pillar 1 compares it against infinity.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Two tools are in use. The Gordy-Lütkebohmert granularity adjustment derives the extra capital needed relative to the infinitely granular benchmark, and its structure is a sum of squared exposure weights scaled by conditional loss variance. The Herfindahl-Hirschman index, $\text{HHI} = \sum_i w_i^2$, is a one-line diagnostic borrowed from competition economics, with $1/\text{HHI}$ read as an effective number of obligors.

The source works a five-obligor portfolio with weights 0.30, 0.25, 0.20, 0.15 and 0.10, giving an HHI of 0.225, an effective count of 4.44, and, by scaling Pillar 1 capital by the ratio of the HHI to its equal-weight value, an add-on of R7.3m on a R58.7m base.

Simulating that exact portfolio gives an economic capital figure of R218.25m against the ASRF closed form of R58.76m, so the true add-on is R159.5m. The index-scaled figure understates it by a factor of 21.7. That is not a calibration weakness, it is a category error: $\text{HHI}/\text{HHI}_{\text{equal}}$ measures how uneven the portfolio is relative to an equally weighted portfolio of the same size, and is therefore blind to the size itself. Pillar 1 assumes infinite size, so that is the benchmark the add-on must be measured against.

The same defect shows up asymptotically. For one large name of weight $f$ among $N-1$ small ones, the HHI correctly converges to $f^2$ while the ratio $\text{HHI} \times N$ grows without bound, reaching 4,001 at a twenty per cent top name and a hundred thousand obligors. And the large exposures regime turns out to be doing the granularity adjustment's job with a blunt instrument: capping any single exposure at 25 per cent of Tier 1 forces the effective obligor count above about 50, which is exactly where the ASRF error falls from a multiple to a quarter.

Feynman test Count how lumpy the book is. But be careful what you compare it against: five borrowers of unequal size are only slightly worse than five equal ones, and vastly worse than the infinitely many the formula imagines. The second comparison is the one the capital number needs.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that both measures are in live use for different purposes and the source says so: the index for real-time portfolio monitoring and limit utilisation, the granularity adjustment or a full simulation for the ICAAP number submitted to the supervisor. That division of labour is sound. What is not sound is treating the index-scaled capital figure as an approximation to the ICAAP figure, because the two differ by an order of magnitude on a concentrated book.

The second reason is that the index is the measure a portfolio manager sees. Limit frameworks, dashboards and monthly packs carry it because it is one line of arithmetic on data the bank already has. If the number attached to it understates the capital consequence twenty-fold, the portfolio manager is receiving a signal that a large exposure is nearly free when it is not.

The third reason is that the history explains the present arrangement, and it is unusually well documented. The granularity adjustment was invented inside the Basel II process to fix a limitation its own authors had identified, was proposed as a mandatory Pillar 1 add-on in the 2003 consultative paper, and was removed from the final 2004 text on grounds of complexity against benefit. Everything about how banks treat concentration today follows from that removal.

And a fourth reason worth stating because it is where the lecture ends up. The large exposures regime is a hard limit expressed in capital terms, not a modelling tool, and it is usually taught as a separate topic about single counterparty risk. Translated into portfolio weights it turns out to bound the granularity problem directly, which means a bank complying with it has already been pushed into the region where the ASRF approximation is tolerable. That is a connection the source does not draw and it changes how the limit should be read.

Step 4

The detail

The granularity adjustment: what its structure tells you, and a caution about the algebra

The adjustment exists because the IRB formula implicitly sets idiosyncratic loss variance to zero. In a finite portfolio each obligor leaves residual idiosyncratic variance proportional to the square of its weight, and the adjustment estimates the additional capital needed to cover it. Three structural properties follow from that and the source states them correctly: the add-on is larger for obligors that are larger, through the squared weight; riskier, through their contribution to capital; and more correlated with the systematic factor, since correlated obligors offer less true diversification.

The rigorous derivation is a second-order asymptotic expansion of the quantile: the ASRF result is the leading term as the portfolio grows, and the granularity adjustment is the next term, involving the conditional variance of loss given the systematic factor and its derivative at the relevant quantile. That is the content of Gordy and Lütkebohmert's treatment, and it is why the adjustment is described as analytically rigorous rather than as a rule of thumb.

A caution about the formula as stated The source gives the adjustment as $\text{GA} = \frac{1}{2C}\sum_i w_i^2 \frac{\sigma_i^2(\text{UL}_i + \text{EL}_i)}{\text{EL}_i^2}$. That specific arrangement does not behave sensibly in one limit worth checking: as an obligor's default probability falls towards zero, $\sigma_i^2/\text{EL}_i^2$ behaves like $(1-p)/p$ and grows without bound, so the contribution of a nearly risk-free obligor tends to infinity rather than to zero. The dimensions also do not reconcile cleanly against a capital denominator. Treat the three structural properties above as the reliable content, and verify the algebra against Gordy and Lütkebohmert directly before implementing it. The simulation below sidesteps the issue by computing the quantity the adjustment approximates.

The index, the effective obligor count, and the source's example verified

The Herfindahl-Hirschman index is the sum of squared portfolio weights. Its reciprocal is read as an effective number of obligors: the count of equally weighted names that would produce the same index. The source's worked example checks out exactly.

The two lineages, and why they matter for how much weight each measure carries

The granularity adjustment was born inside the Basel II process. Vasicek's asymptotic single-factor result, developed in working papers from 1987 and formalised in 2002, supplied the closed form. Gordy's 2003 paper in the Journal of Financial Intermediation gave the rigorous foundation for the IRB formula and made the infinite granularity assumption explicit as a limitation. Basel Committee Working Paper No. 8 in 2001 contained a formal granularity adjustment, the third consultative paper in 2003 proposed it as a mandatory Pillar 1 add-on, the final 2004 text removed it as too complex for the benefit and preserved it as a Pillar 2 expectation, and Gordy and Lütkebohmert's 2007 Bundesbank paper gave the definitive derivation under a two-factor model.

The index has no such pedigree in finance. Orris Herfindahl developed it in a 1950 Columbia doctoral dissertation on concentration in the US steel industry, and Albert Hirschman had arrived at the same formulation in 1945 to measure export concentration. It became institutionally important when the US Department of Justice adopted it in merger guidelines as the test for anticompetitive concentration. Its arrival in credit risk was an analogy rather than a derivation: the same sensitivity to squared weights that made it useful for market concentration made it look natural for exposure concentration, and it was simple and already legitimate with regulators.

A small dating point on the thresholds. The source quotes 1,500 and 2,500 as the boundaries between unconcentrated, moderately concentrated and highly concentrated markets, attributed to the 1982 Merger Guidelines. Those figures are the ones used in the 2010 Horizontal Merger Guidelines; the 1982 guidelines used 1,000 and 1,800. The point is peripheral to banking but worth having right if the history is being told.

The consequence of the two lineages is the division of labour the source describes at the end, and it is the right one. The adjustment or a full simulation produces the ICAAP capital number, because it was derived to answer that question. The index supports real-time monitoring and limit utilisation, because it is one line of arithmetic on live data. Where the trouble starts is in using the index to produce a capital number, which is what step 5 examines.

The large exposures regime

Alongside the modelling sits a hard limit. Under the Basel large exposures framework of 2014, exposure to a single counterparty or group of connected counterparties must not exceed 25 per cent of Tier 1 capital, with a tighter 15 per cent limit between global systemically important banks reflecting the systemic consequence of their interconnection, and a reporting obligation at 10 per cent of Tier 1 even where the exposure is within the limit. In South Africa the regime is implemented by the Reserve Bank under the Banks Act.

The source flags a detail that is easy to skip and matters: the denominator moved from total capital, Tier 1 plus Tier 2, under the earlier framework to Tier 1 only in the 2014 standard. Since Tier 1 is the smaller and higher-quality component, that is a deliberate tightening of the limit rather than a presentational change.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The index-scaled add-on is measured against the wrong benchmark, by a factor of twenty-two

The source's five-obligor portfolio can be simulated directly rather than approximated, which settles what the index-scaled figure is worth.

THE SAME PORTFOLIO, THREE WAYS SIMULATION SETUP obligor i defaults if sqrt(rho) S + sqrt(1-rho) Z_i is below Phi-1(PD), loss is LGD times the summed weights of the defaulters, 600,000 draws, 99.9th percentile less expected loss RESULTS ON R1,000m ASRF closed form, infinitely granular R 58.76m HHI-scaled, the source's figure R 66.04m SIMULATED, actual weights R218.25m simulated, five EQUAL obligors R173.25m SO THE TRUE ADD-ON IS R159.5m AND THE INDEX-SCALED ADD-ON IS R7.3m. A FACTOR OF 21.7. NOW DECOMPOSE THE TRUE ADD-ON from infinite granularity to 5 equal names 58.76 -> 173.25, a factor of 2.95 from 5 equal names to these 5 unequal names 173.25 -> 218.25, a further factor of 1.26 So SMALLNESS accounts for the move from 1.00 to 2.95 and UNEVENNESS accounts for 2.95 to 3.71. Smallness is worth roughly four times what unevenness is worth here. AND THAT IS EXACTLY WHAT THE INDEX RATIO MISSES HHI / HHI_equal compares the portfolio against equal weights AT THE SAME N. It is a pure unevenness measure, so by construction it captures the smaller of the two effects and none of the larger one. Its value of 1.125 is not even a bad estimate of unevenness: the simulated unevenness factor is 1.26, so the ratio is in the right region for the thing it measures. It is measuring the wrong thing. A NOTE ON PRECISION AT SMALL N The 99.9th percentile of a five-obligor loss distribution is discrete: it can only fall on a value achievable by some subset of the weights. Here it lands on a subset summing to 0.50 of exposure, so the simulated figures are exact quantiles of a lumpy distribution rather than smooth estimates, and small changes in weights can move them in steps. That is a property of the portfolio, not of the simulation, and it is a further reason analytic approximations struggle at these sizes.

The asymptotic behaviour confirms it: the index is sound and the scaling is not

The same conclusion follows without any simulation, from what the two quantities do as a portfolio grows. Take one large name of weight $f$ alongside $N-1$ equal small names.

ONE MEASURE CONVERGES, THE OTHER DOES NOT N HHI at f=20% HHI x N 10 0.1111 1.1 100 0.0465 4.6 1,000 0.0406 40.6 10,000 0.0401 400.6 100,000 0.0400 4,000.6 THE INDEX CONVERGES TO f SQUARED = 0.0400, WHICH IS RIGHT. A portfolio of one twenty per cent name plus infinitely many crumbs carries exactly the concentration of that one name, and the index says so. THE RATIO DIVERGES WITHOUT BOUND. Because the equal-weight benchmark 1/N collapses to zero underneath it. At a hundred thousand obligors the scaling implies a capital multiplier of four thousand, which is not a large add-on, it is nonsense. SO THE DIAGNOSIS IS PRECISE Keep the index. It is a well-behaved, convergent measure of concentration and its reciprocal is a meaningful effective obligor count. Discard HHI / HHI_equal as a capital scalar. It has no theoretical basis, it understates by an order of magnitude on small portfolios, and it overstates without limit on large ones. THE CONSTRUCTIVE FIX, WHICH IS CHEAP Use the index to obtain the effective obligor count, then read the granularity ratio off a curve computed once by simulation for the portfolio's risk parameters. For the source's portfolio the effective count is 4.44, which on the equal-weight curve sits between the N = 4 ratio of 3.71 and the N = 5 ratio of 2.95. That lands in the region of the true answer rather than twenty-two times below it. One simulation, run annually per portfolio segment, plus one lookup per monthly monitoring cycle. The monitoring metric stays a single line of arithmetic and stops carrying a misleading capital number.

The large exposures limit is a granularity control in disguise

The limit is normally read as protection against the failure of one counterparty: cap the exposure so that no single default can exhaust capital. That reading is correct and it is not the only consequence. Expressed as a portfolio weight rather than a multiple of capital, the limit bounds the concentration of the whole book.

WHAT THE 25 PER CENT LIMIT IMPLIES FOR GRANULARITY IF TIER 1 IS A GIVEN PERCENTAGE OF TOTAL EXPOSURES, THEN 25% OF TIER 1 IS A MAXIMUM PORTFOLIO WEIGHT Tier 1 as % max single minimum effective N of the book weight obligors at the cap 4% 1.00% 100 100 6% 1.50% 67 67 8% 2.00% 50 50 10% 2.50% 40 40 12% 3.00% 33 33 AND FROM LECTURE 1'S GRANULARITY CURVE N = 5 ratio 2.95 the region the limit forbids N = 50 ratio 1.26 the region the limit permits N = 100 ratio 1.11 SO THE REGIME BOUNDS THE ASRF ERROR A bank in compliance cannot have a portfolio as concentrated as the source's five-name example, because each of those names is thirty per cent of the book and would be several multiples of Tier 1. The limit therefore does the granularity adjustment's job with a blunt instrument. It cannot compute the add-on and it does not try; it pushes the portfolio into the region where the add-on is a quarter rather than a multiple. WHICH REFRAMES WHY THE 2004 REMOVAL WAS TOLERABLE Lecture 1 argued the removal was well targeted because the adjustment is immaterial for granular portfolios. This adds the other half: for the portfolios where it would have mattered most, a separate hard limit already prevents the extreme cases. The framework has two instruments pointed at one problem, one of which is a limit and one a capital charge, and the limit is the one that binds first. THE GAP THAT REMAINS A portfolio of exactly fifty names each at the cap complies fully and still carries a granularity ratio of 1.26, so roughly a quarter of Pillar 1 capital again is genuinely missing. That is the residual the Pillar 2 add-on exists to collect, and it is a far more modest number than the five-name example suggests. Concentration add-ons in practice are tens of per cent, not multiples, and the reason is the limit rather than the model.
The pattern worth carrying A measure borrowed from another field arrives with its benchmark attached. The Herfindahl index was built to compare a market against an evenly divided one, because that is the counterfactual competition policy cares about. Credit capital cares about a different counterfactual, an infinitely granular portfolio, and the index cannot see it. The borrowing was sound and the benchmark came along unnoticed, which is the general hazard in taking a metric from a discipline whose question differs from yours.
Where this goes wrong in practice A limit framework calibrated on the index-scaled capital number. If a portfolio manager is told that a large ticket costs twelve per cent more capital when it costs three times as much, the ticket gets written. The fix is not to abandon the index in the limit framework, which would remove the only real-time measure available, but to attach the correct capital consequence to it, which the effective-count lookup provides at no extra data cost.
Exam tip If asked to compare the granularity adjustment with the Herfindahl index, the weak answer is that one is rigorous and one is simple. The strong answer names the benchmark: the adjustment measures the portfolio against infinite granularity, which is what Pillar 1 assumed, and the index ratio measures it against equal weights at the same size, which is a different and smaller question. A further mark is available for noting that the reciprocal of the index is an effective obligor count and that this, rather than the ratio, is the part worth feeding into a capital calculation.
