Sector concentration: one factor becomes several, and the shortcut that undoes it

Replacing the single systematic factor with a sector factor plus a market factor is the right correction, and the decomposition is internally consistent. The practical shortcut, re-running the closed form per sector at the higher correlation and adding up, then throws away the diversification the multi-factor model was built to capture. Simulated properly, the same portfolio needs an add-on two and a half times smaller.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Sector concentration violates the ASRF model's other assumption. Single-name concentration broke infinite granularity; here granularity is fine and the problem is that one systematic factor cannot represent both economy-wide co-movement and the additional co-movement inside an industry. The standard fix decomposes the latent variable:

$$X_i = \sqrt{\rho_s}\, S_{\text{sector}(i)} + \sqrt{\rho - \rho_s}\, S_{\text{market}} + \sqrt{1 - \rho}\, Z_i, \qquad \rho = \rho_s + \rho_m$$

That is internally consistent: the three loadings sum to unit variance, two obligors in the same sector correlate at $\rho$, and two in different sectors correlate at $\rho_m$, which is the regulatory value. So the single-factor model is recovered exactly when $\rho_s = 0$.

The source then computes the add-on by re-running the IRB formula per sector at the total correlation and summing. On its three-sector portfolio that gives R174.45m against a Pillar 1 figure of R117.90m, an add-on of R56.54m or 48.0 per cent, and every figure in its table reproduces to two decimal places.

But that shortcut assumes all sectors reach their 99.9th percentile at the same time. Simulating the multi-factor model properly gives economic capital of R140.43m, so the true add-on is R22.53m or 19.1 per cent. The shortcut overstates the capital by 24.2 per cent and the add-on by 151 per cent. A method introduced to capture sector concentration has discarded inter-sector diversification, which is the larger of the two effects it was supposed to model.

Feynman test Property, retail and mining do not all collapse in the same week. Charging capital as though each one separately had its worst year, and then adding the three bills together, prices a disaster that has never happened.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this add-on is usually the largest single line in the credit section of an ICAAP, and it rests on a method rather than on a rule. A 48 per cent uplift against a 19 per cent uplift is the difference between a capital plan that constrains lending and one that does not, and nothing in the framework arbitrates between them.

The second reason is that the error runs the opposite way from the one in the previous lecture, which is instructive. There the index-based proxy understated the true add-on twenty-two-fold by using the wrong benchmark. Here the closed-form shortcut overstates it two and a half times by ignoring diversification. Two simplifications of the same problem, errors in opposite directions, and in neither case does the sign follow from the simplification being crude. It follows from which term got dropped.

The third reason is that the correlation parameters are the most judgement-sensitive inputs in the whole credit capital framework, as the source says plainly. They are not observable, they are estimated from equity returns or rating transitions over full cycles, and the supervisor will challenge them precisely where the bank has large exposures. So the number that carries the most weight rests on the input with the least evidence.

And a fourth reason about what the exercise is really doing. Once the model is multi-factor and simulated, it is no longer the IRB formula with a correction; it is a different model that happens to reduce to the IRB formula in a special case. Recognising that is what makes the choice between the shortcut and the simulation a question of what you are computing rather than how accurately.

Step 3

The mental model

One latent variable decomposed three ways, and two routes from there to a capital number that differ by a factor of two and a half.

Step 4

The detail

The decomposition, and why it is the right correction

The question sector concentration asks is whether exposures are clustered in industries whose members move together more than the economy does. The single-factor model has no way to express that, because it has exactly one number, the asset correlation, and that number has to serve as both the economy-wide co-movement and the industry-specific co-movement at once.

Splitting the systematic part into a sector factor and a market factor resolves it, and the arithmetic of the split deserves a moment because it is what makes the model usable. With loadings $\sqrt{\rho_s}$, $\sqrt{\rho_m}$ and $\sqrt{1-\rho}$ on independent standard normals and $\rho = \rho_s + \rho_m$, the latent variable has unit variance as the Merton threshold construction requires. Two obligors in the same sector share both systematic factors, so they correlate at $\rho_s + \rho_m = \rho$. Two in different sectors share only the market factor, so they correlate at $\rho_m$.

That gives the model two properties worth naming. It nests the framework it corrects, since $\rho_s = 0$ returns the single-factor model exactly with $\rho_m$ as the regulatory correlation. And it interprets the regulatory correlation correctly as cross-sector co-movement, so the sector loading is genuinely an addition rather than a replacement. A bank that instead replaced the regulatory correlation with a higher within-sector estimate would be double counting the market component.

Is the tail effect convex? Yes, and less than the source implies

The source explains the large Real Estate add-on by two compounding factors: the largest exposure weight, and the largest correlation excess, arguing that moving $\rho$ from 15 to 25 per cent is "not a linear change" and substantially fattens the tail. The direction is right. The size of the non-linearity is worth measuring, because it is smaller than the phrasing suggests and the real explanation is simpler.

WHY THE PRECISION MATTERS If the add-on were dominated by convexity, small errors in rho_s would be amplified and the calibration would be dangerous. It is not: the response is close to linear over the plausible range, so a calibration error of one percentage point in rho_s costs roughly 0.45pp of capital density, predictably. That is reassuring about the calibration and it removes the excuse for not stating a sensitivity. A one-point band on rho_s translates into a stateable capital range, and the near-linearity is what makes the translation reliable.

Calibrating the sector correlations

The source is direct that $\rho_s$ is the model's most judgement-sensitive input and gives four calibration routes. Pairwise asset correlations estimated from equity return series in the Merton tradition, for obligors within each sector across full credit cycles, then decomposed into global and sector components. Rating agency transition data, giving sector-level cohort default rates over time. Regulatory guidance, since the Basel Committee's empirical studies underpinning the IRB correlation formula contain sector-level estimates and some national regulators publish indicative ranges for ICAAP use. And stress-implied correlations, backed out from the worst observed sector event, with South African property in the early 1990s and mining in 2015 and 2016 as the examples given.

Each has a characteristic weakness worth knowing. Equity-implied correlations measure the co-movement of listed equity, which is not the co-movement of default and is contaminated by leverage differences. Rating transitions have few sector-level defaults per cohort, so the estimates are noisy exactly where the sector is small. Regulatory ranges are calibrated on international portfolios that may not resemble a local book. And stress-implied correlations are estimated from a single realisation, so they have no standard error at all.

Which is why the source's closing warning is the right one: this is a Pillar 2 judgement, and the supervisor will challenge it hardest where the bank has chosen low values for sectors in which it has large concentrated exposures. The defence is not a better estimate but a documented range and a stated sensitivity, which step 4 shows is straightforward because the response is nearly linear.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Summing per-sector capital assumes every sector fails at once

The shortcut is analytically convenient and it contains an assumption that nobody would accept if stated aloud. Re-running the closed form for a sector at its total correlation produces the capital that sector would need if it were the whole portfolio, which is to say the 99.9th percentile of that sector's own loss distribution. Adding three such figures charges the bank for the simultaneous 99.9th percentile of all three sectors.

The probability that all three are simultaneously at their own 0.1% tail is therefore vanishingly small, far below the 0.1% the capital standard asks for. The portfolio's 99.9th percentile is driven by a bad market factor plus one or two bad sector draws, not three. THE IRONY IS WORTH STATING The multi-factor model exists to capture sector concentration, which is a diversification failure WITHIN sectors. The shortcut for using it discards diversification BETWEEN sectors, which on this portfolio is the larger effect by a factor of two and a half. So the shortcut does not merely approximate the multi-factor answer badly. It reintroduces a worse version of the problem it was built to solve, in a different dimension. AND THE SHORTCUT IS NOT USELESS It is a legitimate UPPER BOUND, and a bank with three sectors and no simulation capability can defend it as conservative. What it cannot be called is an estimate, and it should not be used to compare sectors' capital consumption, because the overstatement is not uniform across sectors.

Two qualifications keep the result honest. The simulation assumes each sector is granular internally, so it isolates the sector effect and excludes the single-name effect from lecture 2; a real portfolio carries both and they compound. And the overstatement depends on how many sectors there are and how unequal they are: with one sector the two methods agree exactly, and the gap widens as sectors multiply, because there is more inter-sector diversification to discard.

Two lectures, two simplifications, errors in opposite directions

Set the two shortcuts side by side, because the pairing is the most useful thing in this track.

Both follow from a specific term being dropped, and the sign of the error is the sign of the dropped term. The HHI proxy dropped the effect of portfolio SIZE, which increases capital, so it understates. The summation proxy dropped inter-sector DIVERSIFICATION, which decreases capital, so it overstates. THE DIAGNOSTIC THIS GIVES YOU For any concentration proxy, ask what the proxy would say about a portfolio where the answer is known. The HHI ratio says a fully equal portfolio of five names needs no add-on, which is false: five equal names need 2.95 times Pillar 1. The summation method says a one-sector portfolio needs its sector capital, which is true, and that is why the error is invisible until a second sector appears. Both tests are one line of arithmetic and both would have been run by anyone who asked what the proxy claims in a case with a known answer.

What this closes, across the credit risk track

This completes the concentration track, and with it the two heavyweight frameworks and the two adjacent risk types. It is worth naming what recurred, because it was the same thing every time.

The IFRS 9 chain found seven errors, all of which lived at a boundary between two models and all of which reduced the provision. The counterparty chain found that the netting benefit, the regulatory alpha and the market-implied default probability are each statements about joint behaviour that look like statements about an instrument. This chain found that Pillar 1's two tractability assumptions fail in opposite directions and that both standard proxies for the failures are wrong in ways a single sanity check would expose.

The common structure is that every one of these is a question about how quantities move together, and every framework that has to produce a number in closed form has to assume something about that. Assuming independence gives the granularity result. Assuming perfect dependence gives the summation result. Assuming a single common factor gives the sector result. And a bank's actual exposure to being wrong depends on which assumption its portfolio violates, which is a property of the book rather than of the framework, and therefore changes without anyone editing a model.

If you keep one sentence from the credit risk track Every closed-form risk number is a statement about correlation wearing the costume of a statement about an exposure, and the errors that matter are found by asking what the formula assumes about things happening together rather than by checking any single input.
Where this goes wrong in practice A sector taxonomy inherited from a general ledger or a regulatory reporting requirement rather than built for correlation. Sectors defined for reporting group activities that share a supervisor or a statistical code, not activities that fail together. A property developer, a construction firm, a building materials supplier and a mortgage book are four reporting sectors and one economic exposure to the same collateral values. A bank running a correctly specified multi-factor model on a taxonomy that splits its true concentration across four buckets will report modest sector concentration and hold the wrong capital, and no amount of correlation calibration fixes a segmentation error.
Exam tip If asked how to compute a sector concentration add-on, describe the decomposition first, then say explicitly that the add-on is the difference between the 99.9th percentile of the portfolio loss distribution under the multi-factor model and the Pillar 1 figure. A mark is available for noting that summing per-sector capital charges is an upper bound rather than an estimate, because it assumes all sector factors are simultaneously at their tail. A further mark is available for noting that the sector correlations are the least observable input and the one the supervisor will challenge hardest.
