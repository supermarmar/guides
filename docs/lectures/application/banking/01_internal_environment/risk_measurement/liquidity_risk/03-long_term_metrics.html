Long-term liquidity metrics

The bank-internal structural funding metrics that sit alongside the NSFR. The LRF measures the maturity transformation; the concentration report tells you who can pull the rug; the contingency funding plan tells you what to do when they pull.

Liquidity risk, lecture 3 of 5 Reading time, 22 minutes Source: docs/wiki/.../liquidity_risk/03-long_term_metrics.md
Step 1

The 30-second version

Three structural metrics make up the bank-internal long-term liquidity toolkit. The liquidity risk factor (LRF) measures the maturity transformation the bank is running. The concentration report identifies the single counterparties, sectors, or countries whose withdrawal would crystallise that maturity gap. The inter-entity lending report tracks intra-group funding and regulatory limits for consolidated banking groups.

All three predate, complement, and are largely superseded by, the regulatory NSFR. They survive in bank-internal MI because they expose information the NSFR ratio does not: the LRF as a trend signal, concentration as a single-name exposure, and inter-entity lending as a legal-entity self-sufficiency check. Sitting on top of the three metrics is the operational document that translates structural insight into action when something starts to fail, the contingency funding plan (CFP).

Feynman test The LRF tells you the slope of the maturity transformation. The concentration report tells you who can pull the rug. The CFP tells you what to do when they actually pull. A bank that can answer all three questions has a functional structural-liquidity discipline. A bank that can answer the first two but not the third is a bank that, in a crisis, will improvise.
Step 2

Why this matters

The LCR closes the 30-day window. The NSFR closes the one-year window. Neither metric is informative about what is going on inside the bank's structural funding profile day to day, week to week, between the regulatory reporting dates. The metrics in this lecture are the bank-internal management tools that fill that gap.

Structural funding risk has a slow tempo. A deteriorating maturity transformation profile takes quarters to develop and quarters to fix. By the time the NSFR breaches, the underlying structural drift has been visible for a year or more in the bank-internal metrics. The LRF, concentration, and inter-entity reports are the gauges that show the drift while it is still cheap to correct.

The contingency funding plan addresses a different need. The LRF, concentration, and inter-entity reports are diagnostic. The CFP is operational: a pre-agreed playbook that defines the trigger events, the escalation matrix, and the menu of actions available to treasury when a structural funding problem becomes acute. Banks discover during a crisis that improvising a funding response is slow, expensive, and often wrong. The CFP is the document that means improvisation is not necessary.

Step 3

The mental model

Banks create value through maturity transformation: borrowing short, often on demand, and lending long, typically over many years. The spread between long lending rates and short deposit rates is the bulk of the net interest margin. The risk associated with maturity transformation is the structural-liquidity risk these metrics measure.

Step 4

Liquidity risk factor (LRF)

The liquidity risk factor (LRF), also referred to as the maturity transformation ratio, is a static snapshot comparing the average tenor of assets to the average tenor of liabilities:

$$\text{LRF} = \frac{\text{Weighted average tenor of assets (days)}}{\text{Weighted average tenor of liabilities (days)}}$$

Tenors are calculated as weighted averages of either contractual tenors, behavioural tenors, or both. The weights are typically nominal amounts or actual drawn balances. A higher LRF indicates a larger maturity gap and so greater structural liquidity risk being run by the bank.

What the LRF actually tells you

As a one-off number, the LRF is of limited value. A "high" LRF for one bank is normal for another with a different business model: a building society with retail mortgages and retail deposits will run a much higher LRF than a corporate transaction bank with short-dated trade finance assets and operational deposits. The utility of the LRF is in trend monitoring over time, comparison against long-run averages, and the early warning it provides of the build-up of a potentially unsustainable funding structure.

The LRF limit is set judgementally by the ALCO and varies according to the risk profile and risk tolerance of the individual bank. A typical structure is to compute the LRF on both contractual and behavioural bases and to monitor the differential. A widening contractual-to-behavioural LRF differential, where the contractual LRF is rising faster than the behavioural LRF, is a useful signal that the bank is increasingly relying on the behavioural assumptions rather than on contractual stickiness, which is a precarious position to be in heading into a stress.

Why both, not just NSFR The NSFR has largely superseded the LRF as the primary regulatory measure of structural funding risk. The NSFR is more granular (uses weighted ASF and RSF factors, not just average tenors), more comparable across banks (uses a prescribed common metric), and has a binding regulatory minimum. The LRF survives in bank-internal MI because it is faster to compute, easier to explain to non-specialists, and produces a single time-series number that lends itself to trend charts. It is also the natural place to combine the contractual and behavioural views, where the NSFR is mostly a contractual / behavioural mixture set by the standard.
Step 5

Concentration and funding source report

The concentration report shows the extent of reliance on a single source of funds. The metric is computed across three dimensions: by depositor name, by sector, and by country. Excessive concentration to any one lender, sector, or country is an early-warning sign of potential funding stress, because the withdrawal of a concentrated source would leave the bank with an acute structural shortfall.

How concentration is monitored

Banks set a single-customer concentration limit, typically expressed as a percentage of the aggregate funding base (10% is a common threshold for the largest single depositor) and monitor the largest depositors against it. The tenor of the concentrated deposits is considered alongside the concentration level: a concentrated long-dated deposit carries materially less risk than a concentrated short-dated deposit, because the time to refinance is much longer in the long-dated case.

Where a limit is breached, the bank has three options. It can reduce the concentration by paying the customer to leave or accept tighter pricing; it can increase the aggregate deposit base so that the concentrated deposit becomes a smaller percentage; or it can maintain a sufficiently large liquid asset buffer to cover the potential outflow if the concentrated deposit walks. In practice the three options run in parallel and the choice depends on cost.

Step 6

Inter-entity lending report

The inter-entity lending report is relevant for group and consolidated banking entities. Intra-group lending is common in banking groups, and in some jurisdictions it is subject to cross-border and cross-legal-entity regulatory limits. The report measures the reliance of a specific subsidiary or branch on intra-group funding, and tracks proximity to those regulatory limits.

The use of the report is to provide visibility on the self-sufficiency or otherwise of each legal entity within the group. The metric a regulator cares about is not the consolidated group LCR (which can look perfectly comfortable thanks to internal netting) but the LCR of each individual regulated legal entity. A subsidiary that funds itself almost entirely from the parent has consolidated-group HQLA over consolidated-group outflows that look fine, but standalone HQLA over standalone outflows that look terrible.

Three patterns commonly trigger supervisor concern. The first is a UK subsidiary of an EU or non-EU parent that depends materially on parent funding. The PRA's ring-fencing regime for the UK and the EU's "subsidiarisation" pressure both work to limit this. The second is an emerging-market subsidiary of a developed-market parent, where capital and exchange controls can prevent intra-group funding moving when needed. The third is a wholesale subsidiary that depends on retail funding from a sister entity, with the implicit assumption that the retail entity will always lend at par.

Step 7

F107 extension, contingency funding plan

Beyond the wiki The wiki does not yet cover the contingency funding plan as a distinct topic. The remainder of this section is the missing material, flagged in the wiki with a <!-- GAP --> tag for later backfill. CFPs are required under BCBS 144 (Principles for Sound Liquidity Risk Management and Supervision, 2008), are an explicit ILAAP component under PRA and EBA expectations, and are an examinable F107 topic.

The contingency funding plan (CFP) is the formal operational document that tells a bank what to do when a liquidity stress crystallises. It is not a metric and it is not a forecast. It is a pre-agreed playbook signed off by the board, structured around three elements: triggers, escalation, and actions.

The three elements of a CFP

ElementWhat it specifies
TriggersThe pre-defined indicators that move the bank from business as usual into the CFP. Triggers are typically tiered: early-warning indicators that move the bank to amber status (heightened monitoring), and acute indicators that move it to red status (full CFP activation). Trigger types include LCR thresholds (e.g. amber at LCR 110%, red at LCR 100%), deposit run rates, rating-agency actions, CDS spreads, share price moves, withdrawal of credit lines, and supervisory communications.
Escalation matrixThe named individuals and committees who become decision-makers at each trigger level. A typical escalation runs: treasury daily MI → treasury head and CRO daily call → ALCO emergency meeting → CEO and board liquidity sub-committee → full board. Each level has a defined set of decisions it can take and a defined set it must escalate.
Action menuThe pre-agreed actions available at each trigger level, with the order of priority, the estimated capacity of each action (how much liquidity it can generate), and the time required to execute. Actions are grouped by lever: liquidate HQLA, draw on committed facilities, raise wholesale, raise retail, slow asset growth, sell assets, access central bank facilities.

The action menu in more detail

The CFP action menu is the part most useful for F107 because it forces a candidate to think concretely about what actually generates liquidity in a stress. A typical menu, in approximate order of priority and speed, is:

ActionSpeedConstraints and considerations
Use HQLA (Level 1 first)Hours to 1 dayThe bank's first line of defence. Repo rather than sell where possible, to preserve the optionality of recovering the asset.
Draw on committed credit lines1 dayPre-agreed but typically priced punitively, and may be cancellable by the lender on material adverse change.
Raise short-dated wholesaleDaysAvailable in normal markets but typically unavailable in market-wide stress. Useful for idiosyncratic stress.
Sell HQLA outright1 to 5 daysCrystallises any mark-to-market loss; signals weakness if the sale is visible.
Run a retail deposit campaignWeeks to monthsHeadline rate-led, with NIM cost. Useful for structural stress not acute stress.
Slow asset growth or sell non-HQLA assetsMonthsStrategic lever rather than tactical. Sells loans, securitises portfolios, runs off facilities.
Access central bank facilities (LOLR)1 dayThe last resort. Reputationally costly because supervisor approval often becomes public. Reserved for systemic stress where the central bank is intervening in any case.

Link to recovery and resolution

The CFP is the immediate operational document. Above it sits the bank's recovery plan, which sets out the broader range of actions the bank can take to recover from severe stress (including capital actions, business disposals, restructuring), and the resolution plan, which is the framework the resolution authority would use if the recovery plan failed. The CFP, recovery plan, and resolution plan together form the three-tier framework that BCBS 144, PRA SS3/24, and the EU BRRD all require. The CFP is the most operational and most testing-focused of the three; the resolution plan is the most institutional.

For F107 purposes, the CFP is the part to know in detail. Expect questions on the three elements (triggers, escalation, actions), the priority ordering of the action menu, and the link to the LCR scenario (the LCR's 30-day window is the natural CFP planning horizon, with the CFP actions being the levers that keep HQLA above zero through those 30 days).

Step 8

Synthesis and exam tips

The structural-liquidity discipline reads as a four-step loop: measure (LRF, concentration, inter-entity), monitor (against ALCO limits and trend signals), plan (the CFP playbook), test (regular CFP fire drills with the actions costed and timed). In a well-run bank the loop runs continuously, with the CFP rehearsed at least annually under realistic scenarios drawn from the stress testing programme.

Exam tip The most common F107 question in this area conflates the structural metrics with the regulatory NSFR. The distinction to make is that the NSFR is a single supervisor-calibrated point estimate, while the LRF and concentration report are bank-internal trend signals at higher frequency. The CFP is the operational document; it is not a metric. If you can explain the three elements of a CFP (triggers, escalation, actions) and show how the action menu is structured by speed and reputational cost, the question is mostly answered. The link from BCBS 144 to PRA SS3/24 to the ILAAP and recovery and resolution framework is worth the extra mark.
