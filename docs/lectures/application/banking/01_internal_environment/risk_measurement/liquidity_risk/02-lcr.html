Liquidity coverage ratio

The Basel III answer to Northern Rock and Lehman. HQLA over 30-day net stressed outflows, with prescribed run-off factors for every category of liability. The F107 heavyweight in the liquidity track.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The liquidity coverage ratio (LCR) is the Basel III short-term liquidity metric. It requires a bank to hold enough high-quality liquid assets (HQLA) to survive a 30-day combined idiosyncratic and market-wide stress scenario, in which a prescribed proportion of every liability category is assumed to run off.

$$\text{LCR} = \frac{\text{Stock of HQLA}}{\text{Total net cash outflows over the next 30 calendar days}} \geq 100\%$$

Total net cash outflows is total expected cash outflows minus total expected cash inflows under the stress scenario, with a cap that inflows may not exceed 75% of outflows. Banks may not double-count: an asset included in HQLA cannot also generate a cash inflow.

Feynman test The LCR is a survival-time question reframed as a ratio. The underlying question is "if a sudden bank run started today, would the bank have enough easily-saleable assets to meet 30 days of withdrawals before running out". The ratio form (HQLA divided by net stressed outflows) is mathematically equivalent: an LCR of 100% means the HQLA exactly covers the 30 days; an LCR of 150% means it covers 45 days; an LCR of 80% means the bank runs out at day 24.
Step 2

Why this matters

The LCR is the most important regulatory liquidity number a bank reports and, for F107, the single most likely topic to appear in a question. It exists because the pre-2008 Basel framework did not have a binding short-term liquidity requirement at all. Northern Rock collapsed in September 2007 with adequate capital but no functioning wholesale funding source. Lehman Brothers failed in September 2008 over a weekend, when its overnight repo book could not be rolled. By the time both banks reached the central bank, the option of orderly funding had passed.

BCBS 188 (December 2010) introduced the LCR as part of the Basel III package, with implementation phased from 60% in 2015 to the full 100% in 2019. Three design choices in the standard are worth understanding because they all show up in exam questions:

  • The 30-day horizon. Chosen as the period a supervisor or the bank itself realistically needs to organise a structural funding response (asset sales, deposit-raising campaigns, regulatory dispensations, recovery or resolution action). Shorter than 30 days assumes a magical bridge; longer than 30 days starts overlapping with the NSFR.
  • The combined stress. The LCR scenario assumes idiosyncratic and market-wide stresses happen at the same time. Each on its own would be survivable; the joint event is the calibration target.
  • The 100% minimum on an ongoing basis. The LCR must be at or above 100% in normal conditions. During an actual stress, the bank is expected to draw down HQLA and fall below 100%; the supervisor will not penalise this, because that is what the buffer is for.

The LCR is calibrated to be binding for many real banks, not merely a regulatory floor. This is deliberate: the standard wanted to force structural changes in liability composition, particularly a shift away from short-term unsecured wholesale funding and towards retail and term deposits.

Step 3

The mental model

Think of the LCR as a single equation with two sides, each side built up from a long list of prescribed weights applied to balance-sheet line items. The numerator is the HQLA stock. The denominator is the algebra of 30 days of stressed cash flows.

The combined stress scenario

The LCR assumes a single 30-day scenario in which idiosyncratic and market-wide stresses coincide. The standard lists seven specific stresses that drive the prescribed run-off factors:

Stress driverWhat it does to outflows
Run-off of retail depositsA proportion of stable, less-stable, and term deposits withdraws inside the 30 days.
Partial loss of unsecured wholesale funding capacityMaturing wholesale funding does not roll; new funding cannot be raised at any price.
Partial loss of secured short-term financingRepo funding against certain collateral or with certain counterparties is no longer available.
Three-notch rating downgradeContractual collateral-posting requirements and other downgrade triggers fire, accelerating cash outflows.
Increase in market volatilityLarger collateral haircuts and additional collateral on derivative positions become payable.
Draws on committed credit and liquidity facilitiesCustomers draw down unused facilities as their own funding sources dry up.
Buy-backs of debt and non-contractual obligationsReputational risk drives the bank to honour informal commitments and buy back its own securities.

Every prescribed run-off factor in the LCR schedule is a calibration to one or more of these seven drivers. The 3% factor on stable retail deposits, for example, is calibrated to driver one (retail run-off) at the lower end. The 100% factor on non-operational financial-corporate deposits is driver two (unsecured wholesale evaporation) at the upper end.

No double-counting A specific asset cannot simultaneously be HQLA in the numerator and generate a 30-day cash inflow in the denominator. The classic trap is a maturing sovereign bond: if the bond is included in HQLA, the redemption cash flow cannot also be netted against outflows. The bank chooses one treatment, not both.
Step 4

HQLA characteristics and tiering

For LCR purposes, HQLA must be unencumbered and must possess four fundamental characteristics. Each of these is a backwards-looking calibration: they describe what the empirically liquid asset classes of 2008-2010 had in common.

CharacteristicWhat it means
Low riskHigh credit standing of the issuer, low sensitivity to interest rate and market risk, low legal and inflation risk, denomination in a convertible currency with low FX risk.
Ease and certainty of valuationPricing must be straightforward, not dependent on strong assumptions, and based on publicly available inputs. Structured and exotic products are effectively excluded.
Low correlation with risky assetsThe stock of HQLA must not be subject to wrong-way risk, the systematic correlation between the bank's own credit deterioration and the value of its liquid asset stock.
Listed on a developed and recognised exchangeListing supports transparency and underpins market liquidity under stress.

The three tiers

HQLA is split into three tiers, with progressively larger haircuts and progressively tighter concentration caps. The tiering is the standard's mechanism for differentiating "really liquid in a crisis" from "liquid in normal conditions but uncertain in a crisis".

TierHaircutCapTypical contents
Level 10%NoneCoins and banknotes, central bank reserves (excess over the reserve requirement), marketable securities backed by sovereigns, central banks, BIS, IMF, ECB, qualifying multilateral development banks.
Level 2A15%Level 2 in aggregate ≤ 40% of total HQLA after haircutsMarketable securities backed by sovereigns and PSEs with a 20% risk weight; high-rated corporate debt and covered bonds (AA- or better, not own-issued, not subordinated).
Level 2B25% to 50%Level 2B ≤ 15% of total HQLA after haircuts (sub-cap inside the 40% Level 2 cap)Residential mortgage-backed securities (AA or better, with specific underwriting criteria), corporate debt rated BBB- to A+, and common equity (G10 listed, not issued by financial institutions).

The 40% and 15% caps are concentration limits expressed as proportions of total HQLA after haircuts. The Level 2B cap is a sub-cap inside the Level 2 cap. In practice this means that for a bank to maximise HQLA recognition, it should hold predominantly Level 1, with smaller stocks of Level 2A and Level 2B at or near the caps.

Why central bank eligibility is not the test Central bank eligibility is a necessary but not sufficient condition for HQLA. The standard explicitly excludes "central bank credit operations" from the criteria for HQLA classification, because central bank funding should not be considered a business-as-usual source of liquidity. An asset that is eligible at the central bank but illiquid in private markets relies entirely on the central bank stepping in, which defeats the purpose of an LCR-recognised liquid asset buffer.
Step 5

Outflow weights by counterparty and product

The LCR denominator is built up by applying prescribed run-off factors to each category of liability. The factors are calibrated by counterparty type (retail, SME, corporate, financial institution), product type (current account, term deposit, wholesale), and relationship type (operational, non-operational). The full schedule is long; the version below is the version a F107 candidate should be able to reproduce under exam conditions.

Operational versus non-operational deposits

The single most important distinction in the wholesale-deposit schedule is between operational and non-operational deposits.

TypeDefinition
OperationalThe customer has a substantive dependency on the bank for clearing, custody, or cash management. The relationship is governed by a legally binding agreement. The customer cannot terminate services with fewer than 30 days notice without significant switching cost. The customer must rely on the bank to perform these services in the next 30 days. Assigned lower run-off factors because the dependency makes withdrawal slow and expensive for the customer.
Non-operationalAll deposits that do not meet the operational criteria. Assigned higher run-off factors because they are pure funding deposits with no operational stickiness.

Stable versus less-stable retail deposits

TypeFactorDefinition
Stable, lower rate3%Fully covered by a deposit insurance scheme funded by periodic levies on banks with ready access to adequate funds; depositors have established relationships and hold transactional accounts.
Stable, standard rate5%Fully covered by a deposit insurance scheme or equivalent public guarantee; depositors have established relationships and hold transactional accounts.
Less stable10%All retail and small business deposits not covered by an effective deposit insurance scheme.

An effective deposit insurance scheme must guarantee prompt pay-outs with clearly defined and publicly known coverage, have the formal legal powers to fulfil its mandate, and operate with operational independence, transparency, and accountability. The 3% factor is the lowest run-off rate anywhere in the LCR schedule.

An alternative to the granular schedule is to apply a single conservative blanket estimate. This avoids the over-engineering of distinguishing between 2%, 4%, 10%, 15%, and 20% across every product and counterparty combination, at the cost of an artificially worse LCR. Small banks with simple liability books sometimes adopt this approach; large banks with material wholesale books almost never do, because the LCR penalty would be punitive.

Step 6

The four drivers of deposit liquidity value

Behind the prescribed run-off schedule sit four economic drivers. Understanding them is more useful than memorising the table, because they explain why the factors take the values they do and where the cliff edges sit.

(a) Counterparty type

Retail customers are stickier because they are less financially sophisticated, hold smaller balances spread across a large number of accounts, and are less likely to have an alternative bank account ready to receive a transfer. Corporates are more financially astute, with treasury functions actively managing deposits. Financial institutions and public-sector entities are considered the least sticky because they have sophisticated treasury management and concentrate funds in larger, more easily moved deposits.

(b) Deposit type

Current accounts (money transmission accounts, or MTAs) are stickier than term deposits because the lead time to set up a new current account makes rapid transfer difficult inside the 30-day stress window. A retail customer can in principle move a current account, but the practical friction (direct debits, salary mandates, beneficiary lists) means most do not, even during a confidence shock.

(c) Counterparty relationship

Customers with long-standing, multi-product relationships (mortgage plus current account plus savings) are stickier than standalone rate-chasing depositors. A customer who has a mortgage with the bank cannot easily walk away because their funding decision is entangled with their borrowing decision.

(d) Maturity and cliff risk

For corporate deposits with residual maturity outside the 30-day stress window, a breakage rate is assumed rather than a full outflow weight. The customer would incur a financial penalty for early withdrawal, so the assumed run-off is the proportion that breaks rather than the proportion that simply matures. This creates cliff risk: when the residual maturity falls inside the 30-day window, the outflow weight steps up sharply, potentially producing a large refinancing requirement concentrated in a short period.

Cliff risk in practice A treasury that funds the bank with a portfolio of 31-day wholesale deposits has a perfect LCR position today. In 24 hours, the same portfolio is at the cliff: every deposit has fallen inside the 30-day window and the LCR denominator jumps by the full outflow weight on each. Banks manage this by laddering wholesale maturities to spread the cliff over many days, by maintaining a buffer above the 100% LCR minimum, and by avoiding concentrations of maturities at exactly the cliff edge.
Step 7

Improving the LCR

A bank can address the LCR from the numerator, the denominator, or both. The two sides are interconnected because HQLA must itself be funded, and the funding source has implications for the denominator.

Numerator: increase HQLA

Conceptually straightforward, operationally costly. The bank buys more HQLA assets and either funds the purchase with longer-term liabilities (raising NSFR-friendly funding alongside) or accepts a higher cash-at-central-bank balance with the associated P&L cost. In South Africa, where Level 1 HQLA is structurally scarce, the SARB provides a committed liquidity facility (discussed below).

Denominator: reduce net stressed outflows

The denominator is where the more interesting management decisions live. A bank can:

  • Target longer-term fixed deposits with residual maturity above 30 days, which fall outside the LCR denominator.
  • Increase the share of retail, SME, operational, and stable deposit customers in the liability mix.
  • Introduce unbreakable fixed-term deposits that remove the early-withdrawal risk.
  • Reduce undrawn committed facility balances, either by closing facilities or pricing them to discourage commitment.
  • Price for liquidity risk: charge for undrawn headroom, price longer-dated products to encourage term extension.
  • Target online and instant-access accounts at the 10% outflow assumption rather than 100% non-operational financial-corporate funding.
  • Introduce 91-day or 95-day notice accounts (LCR-friendly because notice periods extend the effective maturity) and phase out 7-day or 30-day notice accounts (LCR-unfriendly).
  • Add breakage clauses to fixed-rate fixed-term deposits (for example, no access for 95 days on early breakage).

Each of these measures has product-design and customer-economics implications that go well beyond the LCR ratio itself. The job of treasury is to find the liability composition that meets the LCR at the lowest cost in NIM and customer experience.

Step 8

South Africa specifics

The LCR is a single standard implemented globally, but national specifics produce material differences in how it bites. South Africa is the case the F107 candidate must know in detail.

No deposit insurance scheme until recently

For most of the LCR's history, South Africa had no deposit insurance scheme. The consequence under the standard is mechanical: all retail and small business deposits are classified as less-stable and attract a 10% run-off factor, with no access to the 3% or 5% stable factors. This structurally raises the LCR denominator for South African banks compared to peers in deposit-insured jurisdictions. The South African deposit insurance regime, the Corporation for Deposit Insurance, was established in 2024 under the Financial Sector Laws Amendment Act, opening the path to lower factors over time once the operational criteria are demonstrably met.

Level 1 HQLA scarcity and the committed liquidity facility

In South Africa, government paper (Treasury bills and government bonds) is the only Level 1 HQLA available. Given limited issuance volumes by National Treasury, a structural HQLA shortage emerged for South African banks as the LCR phased in. The SARB responded by providing a committed liquidity facility (CLF), available for a fee against pledged collateral, that South African banks could include in their HQLA stock as a substitute for Level 2 assets. The CLF is a regulatory dispensation specific to the South African context; it does not exist in deeper sovereign-paper markets such as the UK, Germany, or the US.

The combination of higher denominator (10% retail factor) and constrained numerator (Level 1 scarcity, mitigated by the CLF) makes the South African LCR environment structurally more demanding than the international norm. Expect F107 questions on the rationale and mechanics of the CLF and on the implications of the deposit insurance position for retail outflow rates.

Step 9

F107 extension, liquidity stress testing scenarios

Beyond the wiki The wiki covers the LCR's own stress scenario in detail but does not separately develop the broader liquidity stress testing framework that sits alongside the LCR. The remainder of this section is the missing material, flagged in the wiki with a <!-- GAP --> tag for later backfill.

The LCR scenario is one specific calibration. The supervisor expects every bank to run a broader liquidity stress testing programme that triangulates around the LCR with bank-specific assumptions and alternative scenario shapes. The PRA's SS24/15 (the UK supervisory expectation on the ILAAP) and SS3/24 (the IRB and IFRS 9 use test, applied here by analogy to liquidity) require a stress testing framework with at least three named scenarios.

The three scenario types

ScenarioCalibration
Idiosyncratic onlyThe bank itself is the source of stress: a rating downgrade, a fraud event, a reputation loss, a regulatory enforcement action. Markets remain normal; counterparties are functional; the bank is the bad actor in an otherwise healthy system. Calibration emphasises rapid retail withdrawal, loss of unsecured wholesale, collateral calls from CSA counterparties, and behavioural drawdown of committed facilities.
Market-wide onlyThe bank is fine; the system is in stress. A pandemic, a geopolitical shock, a sudden re-pricing of credit risk. Markets seize, wholesale funding becomes unavailable to all banks, asset prices fall in correlation, central bank facilities are activated. The bank's idiosyncratic stickiness (deposit insurance coverage, operational relationships) is preserved, but wholesale and securitisation channels close.
CombinedBoth above, simultaneously. This is the LCR scenario, calibrated by the supervisor and applied uniformly. The standard 30-day combined stress is one calibration; banks typically run additional combined scenarios at longer horizons (90 days, 180 days) with their own assumptions.

Reverse stress testing

Reverse stress testing inverts the question. Rather than asking "what is the impact of a defined scenario", it asks "what scenario, of any shape, would cause the bank to fail". The bank works backwards from a failure outcome (LCR breach below 50%, inability to meet a major obligation, regulatory intervention) to identify the combination of stresses that produces it. Reverse stress testing is particularly useful for liquidity, because the failure modes are well-defined and the time to failure is short.

For an F107 answer, the structure to remember is: idiosyncratic, market-wide, combined, plus reverse. Each scenario is run over multiple horizons (typically 30, 90, 180 days), is calibrated with bank-specific behavioural assumptions, and is governed by the ILAAP framework. The LCR is the regulatory minimum survival test; the broader stress testing programme is what tells the bank's own board whether the LCR buffer is enough.

The ILAAP linkage

The internal liquidity adequacy assessment process (ILAAP) is the liquidity-side analogue of the ICAAP. Every regulated bank in the UK and EU produces an annual ILAAP that documents its liquidity risk appetite, identifies all material liquidity risks, presents the stress testing programme and its results, and concludes on the adequacy of the liquidity buffer. The ILAAP is reviewed by the supervisor as part of the SREP process and a supervisor-set liquidity buffer (commonly described as a "P2L" overlay) can be imposed where the ILAAP analysis is judged insufficient. The detail of the ILAAP framework sits in the broader ICAAP and SREP lecture; for liquidity purposes the link to remember is that the stress testing programme above is the analytical core of the ILAAP.

Step 10

Deposits analysis template

Operating the LCR in practice requires a regular deposits analysis report, typically weekly or fortnightly, that translates the LCR position into management decisions. The recurring contents of a good deposits report are:

SectionContent
Year-end and forecast depositsCurrent month-end balance, year-end target, and forecast trajectory by product. Aligns the daily LCR view with the annual plan.
High-level deposits strategyStated strategy for the next 12 to 24 months by customer segment and product. Calls out major campaigns and product launches.
Treasury market viewTreasury's view on market rates and the implications for funds transfer pricing. Anchors product pricing decisions.
Product emphasisWhere the bank is leaning into and out of (MTAs, instant access, notice accounts, term). Reflects the optimal LCR-friendly mix.
Competitor rate analysisComparison of headline rates against named competitors. Identifies whether the bank is under-pricing (losing deposits) or over-paying (NIM compression).
Current vs planTracking of actual funding levels against the plan. Highlights where action is needed.

For South Africa specifically, competitor information is sourced from the monthly BA900 Economic Returns published by the SARB and from competitors' quarterly and annual Basel Pillar 3 reports. Banks typically aim to offer deposit rates in the middle of the competitive range, avoiding both under-pricing (losing deposits) and over-paying (NIM compression).

Step 11

Synthesis and exam tips

The LCR is the largest single F107 topic inside the liquidity track. Four things to have memorised by exam day:

  • The formula, the 100% minimum, the 75% inflow cap, and the no-double-counting rule.
  • The three HQLA tiers with their haircuts (0%, 15%, 25-50%) and concentration caps (Level 2 ≤ 40%, Level 2B ≤ 15%).
  • The retail run-off factors (3%, 5%, 10%) and the operational-versus-non-operational split for wholesale (25%-40% versus 100%).
  • The South African specifics: the 10% retail factor in the absence of historical deposit insurance, and the CLF as a Level 2 substitute for the structural Level 1 shortage.
Exam tip The most common trap in LCR questions is treating the seven stress drivers and the prescribed run-off factors as independent. They are not. Every factor in the schedule is a calibration to one or more of the seven drivers. If a question asks why the financial-corporate non-operational deposit factor is 100%, the answer is driver two (loss of unsecured wholesale funding capacity), not "because the rules say so". Showing the connection between scenario and factor is worth materially more marks than reciting the factor.
