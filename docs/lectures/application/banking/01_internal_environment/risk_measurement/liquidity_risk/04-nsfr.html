Net stable funding ratio

The one-year structural liquidity metric. ASF over RSF must be at least 100%. Where the LCR asks whether the bank survives 30 days, the NSFR asks whether the bank's funding structure is sustainable for a year.

Liquidity risk, lecture 4 of 5 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: docs/wiki/.../liquidity_risk/04-nsfr.md
Step 1

The 30-second version

The net stable funding ratio (NSFR) is the Basel III long-term structural liquidity metric. Its purpose is to promote resilience over a one-year horizon by requiring banks to fund their activities with stable sources of funding on an ongoing basis. The NSFR aims to reduce dependency on short-term wholesale markets, promote diversification of funding sources, and limit rollover and maturity mismatch risks.

$$\text{NSFR} = \frac{\text{Available amount of stable funding (ASF)}}{\text{Required amount of stable funding (RSF)}} \geq 100\%$$

Basel III requires the NSFR to be maintained at or above 100% on an ongoing basis. A low NSFR indicates a concentration of funding in shorter maturities (less than one year), which creates structural rollover and mismatch risk. The NSFR complements the LCR (30-day horizon) by addressing the longer-term structural funding position.

Feynman test The LCR asks "what about next month". The NSFR asks "what about next year". Together they pin the funding curve at both ends. A bank can be perfectly LCR-compliant but NSFR-deficient if it has plenty of HQLA today but is funding long-dated assets with 6-month wholesale that will need to be refinanced repeatedly through the year. The NSFR is the metric that says: that refinancing risk is too large.
Step 2

Why this matters

The LCR's blind spot is everything outside 30 days. A bank can comfortably meet the LCR while running a fundamentally unstable funding structure, as long as the instability does not bite inside the 30-day window. The classic pattern is a bank funding a long-dated mortgage book or corporate loan book with rolling 90-day wholesale paper. At any moment the 30-day LCR window is fine, because the wholesale is not yet maturing. Across a year, the same bank refinances its entire wholesale book four times, and each refinance is an exposure to a closed funding market.

The NSFR was the Basel III response. It extends the regulatory liquidity horizon to one year and asks whether the bank's stock of stable funding is sufficient to cover its stock of funding-needing assets, with both weighted by tenor and risk.

For F107 purposes, the NSFR is the second-largest liquidity topic after the LCR. The candidate is expected to know the formula, the ASF and RSF factor tables, the worked example mechanics, and the South African dispensation that softens the standard for ZAR-denominated short-dated financial-corporate funding.

Step 3

The mental model

Read the NSFR as a supply-and-demand identity for stable funding over a one-year horizon. ASF measures the supply: how much of the bank's liabilities and equity count as stable funding, weighted by their stickiness and tenor. RSF measures the demand: how much stable funding the bank's assets and off-balance-sheet exposures require, weighted by their tenor and risk.

Step 4

Available stable funding factors

The ASF for each liability category is its book amount multiplied by a prescribed ASF factor. The factors run from 0% (no stable funding value) to 100% (full stable funding value). The factor depends on the counterparty type, the residual maturity, and any structural stickiness features.

FactorAvailable stable funding components
100%Total regulatory capital (Tier 1 and Tier 2); other capital instruments and liabilities with residual maturity greater than one year.
95%Retail and SME "stable" demand and term deposits with residual maturity less than one year.
90%Retail and SME "less stable" demand and term deposits with residual maturity less than one year.
50%Non-financial corporate funding with residual maturity less than one year; operational deposits; sovereigns and PSEs funding with residual maturity less than one year; other funding with residual maturity greater than six months but less than one year.
35%ZAR financial-corporate funding (excluding banks) with residual maturity less than six months. South Africa specific (see section 8).
0%All other liabilities and equity not included above (including liabilities without a stated maturity); derivatives payable net of receivable.

Reading the ASF schedule

Three principles drive the factor calibration. Tenor matters: anything with residual maturity above one year gets 100%, because by definition it cannot run off inside the NSFR's horizon. Counterparty type matters: retail and SME at the top of the schedule, non-financial corporate in the middle, financial corporate at the bottom, mirroring the same stickiness gradient as the LCR. Structural features matter: operational deposits get 50% even though their tenor and counterparty would otherwise place them at 0%, because the operational dependency creates stickiness.

The most important number to know is that retail and SME stable deposits at 95% are not very different from regulatory capital at 100%. The standard treats a stable retail deposit as almost-equity from a structural funding perspective, which is the calibration that makes a deposit-heavy retail bank structurally NSFR-friendly.

Step 5

Required stable funding factors

The RSF for each asset category is its book amount multiplied by a prescribed RSF factor. The factors run from 0% (asset is itself liquid, requires no stable funding) to 100% (asset is long-dated and risky, requires full stable funding cover).

FactorRequired stable funding components
0%Coins and banknotes; all central bank reserves.
5%Unencumbered Level 1 HQLA, excluding coins, banknotes, and central bank reserves.
15%Unencumbered Level 2A assets.
50%Unencumbered Level 2B assets; deposits held at other financial institutions for operational purposes; loans to non-financial corporates, sovereigns, central banks, and PSEs with maturity less than one year.
65%Unencumbered residential mortgages with residual maturity greater than one year and risk weight at most 35%; other unencumbered loans with residual maturity greater than one year and risk weight at most 35%.
85%Unencumbered non-financial loans (risk weight greater than 35%) with residual maturity greater than one year; unencumbered listed securities not qualifying as HQLA; physical traded commodities including gold.
100%All other assets; derivatives receivable net of derivatives payable where receivables exceed payables (plus 10% of derivatives payable).

Reading the RSF schedule

The RSF mirrors the HQLA tiering. Cash and central bank reserves at 0% are perfectly liquid and need no funding. Level 1 HQLA at 5% reflects the small haircut to recognise some funding need even for the most liquid assets. Level 2A and 2B step up to 15% and 50% as liquidity weakens. Then the schedule jumps to long-dated lending, with the 65% factor for retail-collateralised low-risk-weight loans (residential mortgages, the bank's bread and butter) and the 85% factor for higher-risk-weight unsecured corporate lending.

Liabilities and equity are valued before regulatory deductions, filters, or other adjustments. Assets are valued at accounting value (net of specific provisions).

Step 6

Worked example

The wiki worked example is small enough to do mentally. The balance sheet has £650,000 of assets and £650,000 of liabilities, with the composition below.

AssetsLiabilities and equity
ItemAmount (£)RSF factorItemAmount (£)ASF factor
Loans (maturity > 1yr, RW ≤ 35%)500,00065%Stable retail deposits400,00095%
Central bank deposits25,0000%Less-stable SME deposits150,00090%
Cash80,0000%Non-financial corporate funding60,00050%
Other assets45,000100%Equity40,000100%
Total650,000Total650,000

Compute ASF

ASF is the sum of each liability multiplied by its factor:

$$\text{ASF} = (400{,}000 \times 0.95) + (150{,}000 \times 0.90) + (60{,}000 \times 0.50) + (40{,}000 \times 1.00)$$ $$\text{ASF} = 380{,}000 + 135{,}000 + 30{,}000 + 40{,}000 = 585{,}000$$

Compute RSF

RSF is the sum of each asset multiplied by its factor:

$$\text{RSF} = (500{,}000 \times 0.65) + (25{,}000 \times 0) + (80{,}000 \times 0) + (45{,}000 \times 1.00)$$ $$\text{RSF} = 325{,}000 + 0 + 0 + 45{,}000 = 370{,}000$$

The ratio

$$\text{NSFR} = \frac{585{,}000}{370{,}000} = 158\%$$

The bank passes comfortably. The ASF is dominated by retail and SME deposits (which together contribute £515,000 of the £585,000 ASF). The RSF is dominated by the long-dated mortgage book (£325,000 of the £370,000 RSF). The 158% reading reflects a fundamentally retail-funded, mortgage-asset bank, which is exactly the bank that the NSFR was designed to reward.

What changes the answer Try the same balance sheet with the £400,000 stable retail deposits replaced by £400,000 of 6-month financial-corporate funding (ASF factor 0% outside the SA dispensation). ASF falls to £585,000 minus £380,000, equal to £205,000. The NSFR drops from 158% to 55%, well below the 100% minimum, requiring an immediate funding restructuring. The example demonstrates that retail funding is the single biggest NSFR-improvement lever a bank has.
Step 7

Improving NSFR compliance

Adjusting the NSFR requires balance-sheet restructuring, which is necessarily slow. Strategic decisions should always be assessed for their NSFR implications before implementation, because by the time the NSFR is breaching, the corrective actions take quarters to bite.

The two levers

LeverActions
Increase ASF (liability side)Lengthen liability maturities (term wholesale issuance, longer-dated retail products); shift towards liabilities with higher ASF factors (retail deposits instead of short-term wholesale); raise longer-term capital instruments (subordinated debt, AT1).
Decrease RSF (asset side)Shorten asset maturities (refinance long loans into shorter ones); shift towards assets with lower RSF factors (reduce long-term loans, increase central bank reserves and HQLA); reduce off-balance-sheet exposures (cancel committed facilities, price for utilisation).

The extreme case

In extreme cases, major NSFR shortfalls may require a fundamental business model realignment. Banks that have run aggressive NSFR positions, particularly in the trading book and derivatives businesses, have responded by compressing net derivatives positions, cutting credit lines, focusing on advisory rather than balance-sheet-intensive activities, or adopting an "originate and distribute" model in which loans are sold rather than held. Each of these is a fundamental change in the bank's strategy, not a treasury tactic.

Step 8

South African structural challenges and dispensations

South Africa faces structural constraints that produce a more challenging NSFR environment than in many international jurisdictions. The constraints all flow from a single underlying fact: most South African savings sit in pension, insurance, and asset management vehicles rather than in bank deposits.

The structural picture

  • A low domestic savings rate, combined with well-developed insurance, pension, and asset management (unit trust) markets, channels savings away from bank deposits.
  • South African banks consequently source a large portion of funding through money market funds and institutional investors, both classified as financial corporates and deemed less stable by Basel III.
  • Under the standard Basel III framework, ZAR-denominated funding from financial corporates with residual maturity under six months attracts an ASF factor of 0%, implying it provides no stable funding value.

The SARB dispensation

The SARB concluded that the standard treatment was overly punitive given South Africa's structural features. There are regulatory and economic barriers, including exchange controls and prudential requirements on financial corporates, that prevent capital flowing out of the domestic economy. The rand operates effectively as a "closed rand system": ZAR deposits cannot easily be drained by offshore currency withdrawal, because exchange controls and the prudential rules on local financial corporates prevent it.

Accordingly, the SARB granted a South Africa-specific dispensation: secured and unsecured ZAR funding from financial-corporate customers (excluding banks) with residual maturity less than six months is assigned an ASF factor of 35% rather than 0%. The dispensation is available only to banks conducting business in South Africa, applies only to ZAR-denominated funding, and applies only to non-bank financial corporates. The 35% factor is in the section 4 schedule above as the "35% ZAR fin-corp <6m" row.

Common exam confusion The dispensation is sometimes incorrectly described as "South Africa's NSFR works differently". It does not. The South African NSFR uses the standard Basel III formula and the standard schedule, with one specific factor altered: 35% instead of 0% for short-dated ZAR funding from non-bank financial corporates. Everything else is identical to the international standard. F107 questions on this topic typically reward a precise statement of which factor is altered and why.
Step 9

The LCR-NSFR interaction

The LCR and NSFR look like two independent metrics but in practice they interact strongly. A bank optimises one at the cost of the other unless management is careful.

Where they pull in the same direction

Both metrics reward stable retail deposits. A stable retail deposit attracts a low LCR run-off factor (3% to 5%) and a high NSFR ASF factor (95%). Both metrics also reward Level 1 HQLA. A Level 1 sovereign bond contributes fully to LCR HQLA and attracts a low 5% RSF factor. A retail-funded balance sheet of Level 1 HQLA and stable retail deposits is the textbook structure that both LCR and NSFR were calibrated to reward.

Where they pull in opposite directions

The interesting cases are where the two metrics conflict.

ActionLCR impactNSFR impact
Issue 6-month wholesale paperImproves (outside 30-day window, does not affect denominator)Worsens (counts only 50% ASF or 0% if financial corporate)
Buy a 30-year sovereign bond and hold as HQLAImproves (adds Level 1 HQLA)Worsens (adds 5% RSF on a long-dated asset)
Replace a 90-day retail deposit with a 13-month retail depositImproves (outside 30-day window)Improves more (counts 100% ASF as >1yr liability)
Refinance a 10-year mortgage into a 6-month bullet loanNo direct impactImproves (RSF drops from 65% to 50%)

The cases where the two metrics pull together (retail deposits, retail mortgages, sovereign HQLA) define the bank that both standards were designed to reward. The cases where they conflict (wholesale paper inside the LCR window but outside it, long-dated HQLA accumulation) are the management decisions where treasury earns its budget. The right answer is almost always the joint optimisation, with the binding constraint depending on which metric is closest to its minimum.

The bigger picture The LCR and NSFR were deliberately designed to complement rather than substitute. A bank that satisfies both at minimum (100% each) is in a fundamentally different structural-funding position than a bank that satisfies one comfortably and the other at minimum. The metrics together approximately pin the funding cost curve: short rates by the LCR (because high LCR-friendliness commands a price), long rates by the NSFR (because high NSFR-friendliness commands a different price). The funds transfer pricing system is the internal mechanism that translates both binding constraints into product-level pricing.
Step 10

Synthesis and exam tips

Three things to have memorised for the NSFR by exam day:

  • The formula, the 100% minimum, the one-year horizon, and the basic ASF / RSF distinction.
  • The top three rows of each table: 100% / 95% / 90% on the ASF side (capital, stable retail, less-stable retail); 0% / 5% / 65% on the RSF side (cash, Level 1 HQLA, long residential mortgages with low risk weight).
  • The South African dispensation: 35% ASF factor for short-dated ZAR financial-corporate funding, justified by the closed-rand-system argument.
Exam tip The most common NSFR question structure is the worked-example calculation, exactly like section 6. The trap is forgetting that the ASF and RSF factors apply to the book amount, not to the maturity-bucket cash flows. NSFR is a stock measure, not a flow measure. If the question gives you a balance sheet, treat each line as a stock and apply the factor. If the question gives you a cash flow schedule, you are looking at an LCR or CLM problem, not NSFR. The NSFR-LCR interaction analysis in section 9 is the more advanced material; expect it on a higher-marks question rather than as a standalone item.
