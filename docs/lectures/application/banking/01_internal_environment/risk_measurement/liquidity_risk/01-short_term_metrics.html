Short-term liquidity metrics

Four metrics, four questions. Can we fund the book today, what survives the next week, what is the next year going to demand, and what is the buffer that absorbs the shock when something we did not forecast actually happens.

Liquidity risk, lecture 1 of 5 Reading time, 25 minutes Source: docs/wiki/.../liquidity_risk/01-short_term_metrics.md
Step 1

The 30-second version

Banks measure short-term liquidity through a stack of four metrics. Each answers a different question and each fails in a different way. Together they form the bank-internal early-warning system that sits underneath the regulatory LCR.

The loan-to-deposit ratio (LDR) is a stock ratio that asks whether customer deposits are sufficient to fund the loan book as it actually stands on the balance sheet. The 1-week and 1-month liquidity ratios are gap ratios that ask whether projected inflows plus liquid assets cover projected outflows over the named bucket. The cumulative liquidity model (CLM) extends the gap view forward to a 12-month horizon on a cumulative cash-flow basis, identifying when and where stress will emerge if current trends continue. The liquid asset buffer (LAB) is the stock of unencumbered, high-quality assets the bank holds to absorb whatever the first three metrics did not see coming.

Feynman test Think of a household. The LDR asks whether your salary covers your mortgage at all. The 1-week and 1-month ratios ask whether next week's salary and savings cover next week's bills and next month's bills. The CLM asks whether the same calculation, run forward through the year, ever turns negative. The LAB is the emergency savings account you keep precisely because none of the first three forecasts is ever quite right.
Step 2

Why this matters

The regulatory short-term metric is the LCR, covered in the next lecture. The metrics in this lecture predate the LCR, were largely the source material from which the LCR was constructed, and continue to operate alongside it for three reasons.

First, the LCR is a single point estimate at a single horizon (30 days). Banks need a sequence of point estimates across horizons (overnight, 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 12 months) to see structural trends developing before they reach the LCR window. Second, the LCR scenario is a fixed regulatory specification. Banks need their own internally calibrated stress views to triangulate around it. Third, the LCR is a ratio of two stocks under a hypothetical stress. The CLM is a cash projection under business as usual. A bank can be perfectly LCR-compliant and still have a structural CLM deficit that is invisible inside the 30-day window.

The four metrics together form a layered early-warning system. A worsening LDR usually shows up months before the 1-month liquidity ratio breaches; the 1-month ratio shows up weeks before the CLM forecast turns; the CLM shows up days or weeks before the LAB is actually called on. By the time the LAB is being mobilised, the first three metrics should already have triggered a strategic response.

Step 3

The mental model

Map the four metrics onto a single diagram by horizon and by type. Horizon runs from "right now" on the left to "twelve months" on the right. Type splits into stock measures (snapshots of balances), flow measures (gap analysis over a maturity bucket), and forward cumulative views.

Read the diagram as a layered defence. The LDR is the trend gauge: it changes slowly, but a deteriorating LDR over a year is a structural signal. The 1-week and 1-month ratios are the gap gauges: they pick up acute mismatches in the close-in maturity buckets where stress crystallises. The CLM is the forward gauge: it shows the shape of the funding problem rather than the size at one point. The LAB is the shock absorber.

Step 4

Loan-to-deposit ratio

The loan-to-deposit ratio (LDR) measures the relationship between net lending and customer deposits over the same period, reported monthly. It is a measure of the self-sustainability of the bank, specifically the degree to which lending is funded by stable customer deposits rather than wholesale markets:

$$\text{LDR} = \frac{\text{Net carrying amount for loans}}{\text{Retail deposits}}$$

Why net, not gross

The LDR is a funding sustainability metric. It asks whether deposits are sufficient to fund the loan book as it actually stands on the balance sheet. Gross loans would overstate the asset being funded, because the provision represents a portion of the asset that is expected to be unrecoverable and has already been expensed through the P&L.

The ECL paradox A bank with a rapidly deteriorating loan book will see its ECL provisions increase, which reduces net loans and so mechanically improves the LDR, even though credit quality is worsening. This is the single most important reason the LDR must always be read alongside credit quality metrics (Stage 2 ratio, NPL ratio, cost of credit), not in isolation.

Interpretation thresholds

The conventional reading of LDR levels uses three thresholds, all of which are heuristics rather than hard rules. The right range for any individual bank depends on its business model and jurisdiction.

LevelReadingWhy
Above 100%Funding stress signalThe bank is funding loans from sources other than customer deposits, typically wholesale markets. Where wholesale is short-dated, the risk is acute.
85% to 95%Business best practiceThe loan book is materially funded by stable deposits, with some headroom for growth and a small contribution from wholesale.
Below 70%Returns dragExcess deposits over loans must be deployed somewhere. If they sit in low-yielding liquid assets, NIM compresses and returns suffer.

An LDR above 100% is not automatically a problem if the bank holds a sufficiently large liquid asset buffer. The number that matters is the joint position of LDR and LAB together: a high LDR with a thin LAB is the classic structural funding squeeze, while a high LDR with a deep LAB is a deliberate business model choice.

What the LDR does not tell you

The LDR is useful but not predictive. It is a snapshot that ignores the tenor of the loans (a 30-year mortgage and a 1-year personal loan count equally), the tenor of the deposits (a sticky current account and a 1-week wholesale deposit count equally), the concentration of funds (a single £500m corporate deposit and 5,000 retail deposits of £100k each look identical), and the volatility of those funds under stress. Each of those blind spots is the reason another metric exists.

Step 5

1-week and 1-month liquidity ratios

The liquidity ratio at a specified horizon measures gap risk, the net cash flow position over a defined maturity bucket as a proportion of total liabilities. The standard reporting cuts are at 1 week and 1 month, though the same form generalises to any bucket:

$$\text{Liquidity ratio}_{[t]} = \frac{\text{Cash inflows}_{[t]} - \text{Cash outflows}_{[t]} + \text{Liquid assets}_{[t]}}{\text{Total liabilities}}$$

The numerator combines three elements. Cash inflows are scheduled receipts inside the bucket (interest, scheduled loan repayments, maturing investments). Cash outflows are scheduled payments inside the bucket (deposit maturities, scheduled drawdowns, interest payable). The liquid assets term captures the cash a bank could raise inside the bucket by liquidating securities from the LAB. Dividing by total liabilities makes the metric comparable across banks of different sizes.

These ratios are typically measured against a regulatory or internal limit, and a worsening ratio approaching that limit should trigger a funding-strategy review. Common managerial responses include lengthening the liability maturity profile, increasing the LAB, slowing asset growth, or pricing more aggressively in the at-risk maturity buckets.

Why these still exist alongside the LCR

The 1-week and 1-month liquidity ratios have been largely superseded by the LCR as the primary regulatory measure of short-term liquidity. They survive in bank-internal MI for two reasons. First, the LCR runs at a single 30-day horizon under a fixed scenario; the older ratios at multiple horizons under business-as-usual cashflows provide complementary information the LCR does not replicate. Second, the older ratios are simpler and faster to compute, making them suitable for daily or intra-day MI where the full LCR machinery is impractical.

Step 6

Cumulative liquidity model

The cumulative liquidity model (CLM) extends the liquidity ratio concept into a forward-looking projection over a 12-month horizon. It models cumulative cash inflows, outflows, and available liquidity across all maturity buckets, identifying forward-looking liquidity stress points on a cash basis. The CLM is typically prepared daily at legal-entity and group level and is the primary tool for identifying when and where funding stress will emerge.

The mechanics are straightforward. For each forward time bucket (overnight, 2 to 7 days, 8 to 30 days, 1 to 3 months, 3 to 6 months, 6 to 12 months) the model accumulates expected cash inflows and outflows, adjusts for the projected available liquid asset stock, and reports the cumulative net cash position at the end of each bucket. The horizon at which the cumulative position first turns negative is the bank's structural funding cliff.

The strategic response to a CLM deficit depends on where the cliff sits. A near-term cliff (inside 30 days) demands acute action: liquidate LAB, raise short-dated wholesale, slow new lending. A medium-term cliff (3 to 6 months) demands a structural funding plan: longer-dated deposit campaigns, term wholesale issuance, balance-sheet composition changes. A 12-month cliff signals a strategic mismatch that needs to be addressed in the next planning cycle.

Step 7

Liquid asset buffer and HQLA

All banks are required to hold a buffer of unencumbered liquid assets, the liquid asset buffer (LAB), to generate liquidity when a stress event occurs. The principle predates Basel III but was codified by the LCR through the concept of the stock of high-quality liquid assets (HQLA). For a buffer to qualify as a LAB, three criteria apply:

CriterionWhat it means in practice
Good credit qualityIssuer credit standing is sufficient that the asset retains value under stress. Sovereign and supranational paper qualifies most readily; structured credit and unrated paper do not.
Funded out of stable or term fundingThe LAB itself must not be subject to maturity rollover risk. Funding a LAB with overnight wholesale is self-defeating: the wholesale rolls off in the stress the LAB exists to absorb.
Readily convertible to cash at 1-day noticeMarket depth and repo eligibility matter. The asset must monetise in 24 hours without a fire-sale discount.

The stock of assets in the LAB is more commonly known, in the context of the Basel III LCR, as the stock of high-quality liquid assets (HQLA). Regulators expect HQLA to retain both value and market liquidity in a stressed environment, meaning they can be monetised without large discounts in sale or repo markets even under fire-sale conditions. The detailed HQLA classification (Level 1, 2A, 2B) and the associated haircuts and concentration caps are covered in the LCR lecture.

Central bank eligibility is not enough Central bank repo eligibility is a necessary but not sufficient condition for LAB classification. Central bank funding should not be considered a business-as-usual source of liquidity. An asset that is eligible at the central bank but illiquid in private markets relies entirely on the central bank stepping in, which is precisely what the LAB is supposed to make unnecessary. The South African Reserve Bank's committed liquidity facility, discussed in the LCR lecture, is an exception that proves the rule: it exists because South African Level 2 HQLA is structurally scarce.
Step 8

F107 extension, intraday liquidity

Beyond the wiki The wiki currently treats intraday liquidity as a sub-topic of liquidity management and does not present the BCBS 248 framework directly. The remainder of this section is the missing material, flagged in the wiki with a <!-- GAP --> tag for later backfill.

The metrics above all run at daily granularity or longer. The LCR specifies a 30-day stress; the CLM runs out to 12 months; the 1-week ratio averages over its bucket. None of them addresses the fact that a bank's intraday liquidity position can move by tens of billions of pounds inside a single trading day as payments clear through the central bank's real-time gross settlement (RTGS) system.

BCBS 248, Monitoring tools for intraday liquidity management (April 2013), introduced a standardised set of supervisory monitoring tools to address this gap. The tools are not capital or liquidity requirements (they do not have a binding minimum analogous to the LCR's 100%). They are reporting metrics that supervisors use to assess whether a bank can meet payment and settlement obligations on time, in both normal and stressed conditions.

The seven monitoring tools

BCBS 248 prescribes seven tools, with three categories applicable to all banks, two further tools for banks providing correspondent banking services, and two more for direct participants in payment systems. The standard set (all banks) is:

ToolWhat it measures
1. Daily maximum intraday liquidity usageThe largest net cumulative position (positive or negative) reached during a business day, summed across the bank's settlement accounts at the central bank and correspondents. Indicates the peak intraday liquidity need.
2. Available intraday liquidity at the start of the business dayTotal funds available at the open: balances at the central bank, unencumbered eligible collateral that can be pledged for intraday credit, committed and uncommitted credit lines, balances at correspondent banks.
3. Total paymentsGross value of payments made and received during the day across the bank's settlement accounts. Sizes the throughput against the available intraday liquidity stock.
4. Time-specific obligationsPayments that must be settled by a specific time of day (margin calls, CLS-eligible FX settlements, securities settlements with deadline cut-offs). Failure carries direct financial or reputational cost.
5. Value of customer payments made on behalf of financial-institution customersFor banks that provide correspondent services, the volume of third-party payments routed through the bank's settlement account. Concentration here amplifies intraday funding needs.
6. Intraday credit lines extended to customersFor correspondent banks, the gross value of intraday credit extended to financial-institution customers. Captures the bank's role as an intraday liquidity provider to others.
7. Intraday throughputFor direct payment-system participants, the cumulative value of outgoing payments settled at each hour of the day, expressed as a percentage of total daily outgoing payments. Identifies whether the bank is back-loading payments to the end of the day, a behaviour that propagates intraday stress through the system.

Why this matters for F107

Intraday liquidity stress is the failure mode that the LCR cannot see. The classic example is a bank that holds adequate HQLA for the 30-day LCR scenario but cannot meet a 14:00 margin call because its intraday credit line has been drawn and the LAB is in the form of bonds that take 2 hours to repo. The intraday metrics force the bank to demonstrate that it can meet time-specific obligations, not merely that it has enough HQLA in total. For supervisors, the metrics also expose the operational concentration risk that arises when a bank acts as a major correspondent: the bank's intraday liquidity profile is dominated by other banks' payment flows, which can pull in either direction unpredictably.

The BCBS 248 framework was endorsed by the EBA and PRA for European and UK banks, and a parallel set of monitoring tools is implemented by the SARB for South African banks through the BA325 return. Expect F107 to test the existence and purpose of intraday liquidity monitoring rather than the detailed mechanics of each tool.

Step 9

Synthesis

The five short-term liquidity instruments (four metrics plus the intraday monitoring set) form a hierarchy of resolution. The LDR runs monthly at the bank-wide level. The 1-week and 1-month ratios run daily at legal-entity level. The CLM runs daily at legal-entity level over a longer horizon. The LAB is a daily stock measure. The BCBS 248 tools run intraday at settlement-account level. Each instrument trades resolution for granularity: the higher the resolution in time, the narrower the slice of the balance sheet it can usefully look at.

The right way to read a bank's short-term liquidity position is bottom-up, starting at the BCBS 248 monitoring and working out through the daily ratios to the monthly LDR. Each tier should be consistent with the tier above. If the LDR is fine but the CLM is forecasting a 4-month cliff, the LDR is stale. If the CLM is fine but the daily 1-week ratio is breaching, the CLM cashflow forecasts are wrong. If the daily ratios are fine but the bank cannot meet a 14:00 margin call, the intraday monitoring is exposing an operational gap the daily numbers cannot see.

Exam tip Examiners commonly test the relationship between the LCR and the metrics that predate it, particularly the question of why a bank still bothers with the 1-week and 1-month liquidity ratios when the LCR is the binding regulatory measure. The answer is the layered defence argument: the LCR is a single point estimate at a single horizon under a single scenario; bank-internal management needs the time-series and multi-horizon view that the older metrics provide. Memorise the LDR thresholds (70 / 85-95 / 100%), the LAB criteria (good credit quality, term-funded, 1-day liquidatable), and at least the existence and purpose of the seven BCBS 248 tools.
