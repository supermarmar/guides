Risk identification

Before a bank can measure or manage a risk, it has to recognise that the risk exists and decide what to call it. Risk identification is the taxonomy step. It is unglamorous and it is the place most failures start.

Lecture 9 of Phase 1 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: docs/wiki/application/banking/01_internal_environment/risk_management/03-risk_identification.md
Step 1

The 30-second version

Risk identification is the step where a bank writes down the full list of risks it faces, groups them into a taxonomy, and assigns each to a function. The taxonomy is the foundation everything else stands on. A risk that has no name has no owner, no measurement, no limit, and no capital.

Banks face five primary risk types: liquidity, capital, credit, market, and operational. Around those sit a wider ring of risks that either cut across the primary five or are large enough to warrant their own attention: climate, systemic, margin, cyber, conduct, reputation, business / strategic, model, and pension risk. The taxonomy is not theoretical. It dictates which committee sees an exposure, which limit applies, which capital model runs, and which regulator asks about it.

Feynman test Identifying risks is like cataloguing a museum. Every object has to go on the inventory. The collection has thematic rooms (paintings, sculpture, manuscripts), but some objects do not fit neatly anywhere (a sculpted manuscript, an illuminated panel) and the curator has to choose where to place them. New donations arrive (cyber, climate) and the curator has to decide whether to open a new room or fit them into an existing one. The thing the curator dreads most is the object that arrives, gets lost in a back corridor, and surfaces years later as someone's missing masterpiece. Bank risks behave the same way.
Step 2

Why this matters

Silicon Valley Bank in March 2023 is the textbook case of risk identification failure. SVB's balance sheet was wildly exposed to interest rate risk in the banking book: a portfolio of long-duration Treasuries and agency MBS funded by uninsured tech-startup deposits. IRRBB was on every bank's standard taxonomy, but inside SVB it had been treated as a secondary risk: the chief risk officer position was vacant for most of 2022, the IRRBB committee was meeting infrequently, and the hedging programme had been allowed to lapse to boost reported margins. When rates rose, the bond portfolio took a $15 billion mark-to-market hit, the depositors saw the hole, and the bank ran in a single afternoon. The risk was named in every supervisory manual on earth. It was simply not actively identified as material inside SVB.

The same pattern appears across the modern catalogue of banking failures. Northern Rock in 2007 had identified credit risk on its mortgage book but had not identified funding liquidity risk on its wholesale-funded model. Wells Fargo in 2016 had identified market and credit risk but had not put conduct risk at board level. Crypto-adjacent banks in 2022 had not identified the concentration risk of a single sector with correlated funding flows. In each case the underlying risk was discoverable, and someone at the bank knew about it. It was not promoted into the live taxonomy, and so no committee owned it, no metric tracked it, and no capital backed it.

Identification matters because it is the gatekeeper for every step that follows. The risk appetite statement can only constrain risks that have been identified. The measurement function can only quantify risks that have been identified. The mitigation toolkit can only hedge risks that have been identified. Miss a risk at the identification step and the entire downstream apparatus is blind to it.

Step 3

The mental model

The taxonomy is a hierarchy, not a flat list. Liquidity risk stands alone at the top because it is about meeting obligations rather than absorbing losses. Capital risk is the umbrella for everything that can erode the bank's loss-absorbing capacity, and it has five children: credit, market, operational, climate, and a basket of further "other" risks. Each of those branches has its own sub-risks.

Step 4

The detail

The top-level split: liquidity and capital

Two risks sit at the top of the hierarchy. Liquidity risk is about whether the bank can settle its obligations as they come due. Capital risk is about whether it has enough loss-absorbing capacity to stay solvent through whatever its other risks throw at it. The two are connected (a liquidity event will eventually become a capital event, and vice versa), but they are kept structurally separate because they call on different defences: liquidity is solved by buffers and lines of funding, capital is solved by retained earnings and issuance.

Liquidity risk

Liquidity risk is the uncertainty around meeting all obligations as they fall due. It arises structurally from the maturity transformation business model covered in Lecture 1: long-dated assets are funded by short-dated liabilities, and the mismatch must be actively managed. The risk has two flavours. Funding liquidity risk is the inability to raise cash to meet outflows on either side of the balance sheet. Trading (or market) liquidity risk is the inability to convert held assets into cash without an unacceptable price discount. The two are linked, because in a stress event funding outflows force assets to be sold into a thin market and the discount widens.

Capital risk

The risk that the bank does not hold enough capital to absorb losses or to meet regulatory capital requirements. The standard measure is the capital adequacy ratio. A bank that has adequate capital relative to risk-weighted assets is more likely to absorb unexpected losses and remain solvent. Capital risk is a meta-risk: a realised loss from any of the other risk types becomes a capital risk event if the loss is large enough.

Credit risk

Credit risk is the risk that a contracted payment is not made. It is typically the largest risk on a commercial bank's balance sheet and is a Pillar 1 risk under Basel. Most credit risk sits in the lending book, but credit risk also arises in the trading book through counterparty exposures, settlement, and unsettled derivatives. It is both on- and off-balance-sheet (undrawn commitments are credit exposures). Markets price credit risk through the credit spread, the part of the yield on a risky bond that compensates the lender for expected loss and the risk of unexpected loss. A credit risk model takes economy-wide and firm-specific inputs and produces a credit spread, or equivalently a probability of default, loss given default, and exposure at default. Concentration risk is a sub-type that BIS studies consistently identify as the single biggest driver of bank failures. Modern concentration risk is less about over-exposure to a single name and more about correlated exposures across a sector or geography.

Market risk

Market risk is the risk of loss from movements in market prices. It can arise from any on- or off-balance-sheet position in cash, securities, derivatives, lending, market-making, or trading. Sub-types include interest rate risk, foreign exchange risk, commodity risk, and equity risk. Within interest rate exposure, several sub-risks are tracked separately:

Interest rate sub-risks

Sub-risk Mechanism Example or measurement
Interest rate risk in the banking book (IRRBB) Rate moves change the bank's profit or capital through banking-book exposures (i.e. not the trading book). Delta-NII (sensitivity of net interest income) and delta-EVE (sensitivity of economic value of equity), measured against the BCBS 368 scenarios.
Yield curve risk Rates do not move in parallel. Short rates rising faster than long rates compresses margins when long assets are funded by short liabilities. Steepening, flattening and twist shocks applied to the curve.
Basis risk The bank's loan rate and its deposit rate reference different benchmarks. When the spread between the two benchmarks moves, the net interest margin moves with it. SONIA-linked loans funded by base-rate-linked deposits; SOFR vs Fed Funds.
Option risk Embedded optionality means the cash-flow profile depends on the rate path. When rates fall, callables get called and mortgages prepay; when rates rise, the opposite happens. Callable bonds, prepayable mortgages, deposit redemption options.
Mismatch / gap risk Maturity (and sometimes currency) mismatch between assets and liabilities. A move in the relevant short rate or in the cross-currency basis hits the bank directly. Long-dated fixed-rate mortgages in one currency funded by short-dated variable-rate deposits in another.

Equity risk deserves a separate note. The market-risk version covers equity positions held to trade. The non-trading version (which is what most banks have most of) covers strategic equity holdings: minority stakes in fintech acquisitions, sponsor positions in investment funds, joint ventures. These are held for ownership rather than profit, but their valuations still hit the balance sheet and income statement. The rise of bank-led fintech investment in the last decade has materially increased the non-trading equity exposure of large universal banks.

Operational risk

The Basel II definition is "the risk of direct or indirect loss resulting from inadequate or failed internal processes, people and systems or from external events". For regulatory capital purposes it includes legal risk but explicitly excludes reputational and strategic risk, which sit elsewhere. Operational risk is the most heterogeneous of the children: it covers fraud, process failure, system outages, legal disputes, regulatory breaches, natural disasters, and outsourcing failures. It is also the risk type most likely to surprise.

Climate risk

The TCFD splits climate risk into two primary sub-types. Physical risk is the direct impact of climate events: acute physical risk from event-driven shocks (floods, wildfires, storms) and chronic physical risk from long-term shifts (sea-level rise, sustained temperature change). Transition risk is the second-order risk from the move to a lower-emission economy, broken into market (repricing of climate-sensitive assets), technology (existing tech becoming obsolete), policy and legal (new emissions rules, climate litigation), and reputational (being seen as a driver of climate damage). Banks have a small direct carbon footprint but a very large indirect one through funded emissions, the emissions of the borrowers and counterparties they finance. Transition risk for a bank is therefore primarily a credit risk and concentration risk on its high-carbon portfolio.

The "other risks" bucket beneath capital

The wiki groups eight further categories under a single "other risks" heading, sitting as a peer of credit, market, operational, and climate under capital risk. Most are technically sub-types of operational risk, but each is managed as a separate process because of its scale or distinct character. Two patterns to note: several "other risks" interact with one another (a conduct event drives a reputational event drives a business risk event), and several are cross-cutting in the sense that they can show up inside more than one primary branch.

Systemic risk

The risk that the failure of one bank propagates through the system via inter-bank deposits, derivative exposures, payment systems, and confidence channels. Lehman in 2008 is the canonical example. The post-crisis regulatory response (G-SIBs, D-SIBs, systemic risk buffer, TLAC / MREL) was designed to internalise the cost of systemic risk into the banks that create it. From a single bank's perspective, systemic risk is both a risk to it (contagion from peers) and a risk it imposes on others (its own potential failure).

Margin risk

The risk of a significant fall in net interest margin, the bank's primary income source. Three drivers dominate. Competitive pressure in benign periods can push loan rates down without a matching cut in deposit rates. Mismatched repricing on the asset and liability sides (covered above under interest rate sub-risks). And sustained low rates, where the floor on deposit pricing (politically and behaviourally, zero) compresses the spread, as the eurozone bank sector experienced through the 2014 to 2022 negative-rate era.

Cyber risk

The risk of loss from digital incidents: ransomware, denial of service, data breach, third-party software compromise. Technically a sub-type of operational risk, but managed separately at almost every major bank because of its growth rate and its potential to interact with reputation, conduct, and counterparty risk simultaneously. Cyber events have become the single largest source of operational loss across the global banking system over the last five years.

Conduct risk

The risk of negative or unfair outcomes for customers. Sits within the operational risk taxonomy in principle but is almost always managed separately, partly because the dollar amounts are so large (PPI mis-selling in the UK cost the industry over £50 billion in remediation), partly because the front-line incentive problem is so distinctive. The salespeople interacting with clients are not risk practitioners, and they are incentivised to sell as much as possible. The defence is primarily qualitative: strong product governance, suitability standards, mystery-shopping, and a culture that rewards saying no. Conduct risk cuts across retail and wholesale, and across product lifecycle from design to sale to post-sale servicing.

Reputational risk

The risk of damage to a bank's good standing, again technically within operational risk but usually separately managed because of its impact on franchise value. A common measurement approach is to estimate the bank's brand-driven revenue contribution and then assess factors that may degrade it. Conduct risk events, AML failures, and high-profile data breaches are the most common reputational risk events.

Business / strategic risk

The risk to future profitability from factors not explicitly covered by other risk types. A catch-all that captures things like cost overruns, strategic mis-steps, planning errors, and skill gaps in management. Often labelled strategic risk when the cause is upstream planning rather than downstream execution. Its primary mitigation is hiring effective managers and a competent board.

Model risk

The risk of negative outcomes from misuse or inaccuracy of models. Two distinct flavours: inappropriate use (a model fit for purpose A being repurposed for B without recalibration), and inherent inaccuracy (a model that is wrong by design, or whose parameters are stale). Mitigated through a strong model risk function with independent validation, an inventory of all material models, regular performance testing, and a defined process for re-parameterising, recalibrating, or rebuilding when thresholds are breached. The Federal Reserve's SR 11-7 and the PRA's SS1/23 are the supervisory anchors.

Pension risk

The risk that the bank has to cover an underperforming pension scheme for its own employees. Material for banks with large defined-benefit schemes (less so in South Africa, where defined contribution dominates). Banks with material DB exposure see pension risk modelled and capitalised separately under Pillar 2A.

Identification techniques

Risk identification is not a one-off exercise. The standard toolkit blends top-down and bottom-up methods. Top-down: management workshops, scenario exercises, peer-bank benchmarking, regulatory review feedback. Bottom-up: control self-assessments (RCSAs) carried out by first-line teams, loss event databases, audit findings, customer complaints. The two have to be reconciled, because the bottom-up view often misses emerging risks (no one fills in an RCSA for a risk type they have not yet recognised) and the top-down view often misses operational granularity (the board does not know which payment process is fragile). A mature risk function runs both annually, plus continuous review of incoming loss data and external events.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The hardest part of identification is naming the risk you do not have a name for

Every major banking failure in living memory was driven by a risk that existed in some taxonomy somewhere but was not promoted to material status inside the failing bank. SVB had IRRBB on its risk register but treated as immaterial. Lehman had funding liquidity on its register but treated as managed by trading desks. The hardest part of identification is recognising that an existing entry on a long list needs to move to the top. The discipline that helps is forcing the identification process to ask "what would have to be true for this risk to bring the bank down?", and then assessing whether that "what" is becoming more likely.

The taxonomy overlap problem

Many risks belong to more than one box. Cyber risk is an operational risk by Basel definition but it can trigger reputational, conduct, and counterparty losses. Climate is a credit risk through funded emissions, a market risk through asset repricing, an operational risk through physical damage to branches, and a reputational risk through customer expectations. The disciplined response is to assign a primary owner for each risk (the function with most of the exposure and the deepest expertise) and a set of secondary stakeholders who must be consulted. The undisciplined response is to leave the boundary fuzzy, with the predictable outcome that nobody owns it.

Goodhart, again, but earlier

Risk identification can be gamed by classification choice. A loss that lands in operational risk eats operational risk capital. A loss that lands in business risk does not, because business risk is not in Pillar 1. Banks have, in the past, been creative about which category a particular event gets booked into, with material implications for capital. Modern frameworks try to constrain this through standardised loss event taxonomies (Basel's seven operational loss event types) and through external loss data benchmarks. The lesson is that taxonomy boundaries are not innocent, they are part of the bank's regulatory and economic surface.

Emerging risks and the "what is new this year" exercise

The most useful annual exercise for any risk identification team is a structured "what is new this year" review. Possible inputs: regulator speeches, peer-bank disclosures, FSB and BCBS thematic papers, board strategic horizon scans, supervisory dear-CEO letters. The output is a short list of risks not currently material that may become material within the planning horizon. Typical candidates over the last five years have included third-party / supplier risk, AI model risk, geopolitical fragmentation risk, and crypto-asset exposure risk. Most do not graduate to primary status; the discipline is in catching the ones that do early.

The relationship to risk appetite

The output of identification feeds directly into the risk appetite statement. Every primary risk gets a quantitative metric and a threshold. Every other risk gets either a metric or a qualitative commitment. The cleanest test of an identification framework is to take the appetite statement and ask: is every line item in the statement traceable to a risk type that the identification process explicitly named, and is every named risk reflected in the statement? If the answer to either is no, identification and appetite have drifted out of alignment.
