Risk appetite

Risk appetite is the bank's written answer to one question: how much can we lose, in which ways, and still keep going? It is set by the board, expressed in numbers and words, and tested every day against the live position of the balance sheet.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A bank's risk appetite is the maximum aggregate risk it is willing to assume in pursuit of its strategy, set by the board, written down in a risk appetite statement, and monitored through a small set of key risk indicators against pre-defined breach levels.

Appetite is not the same as capacity. Capacity is what the balance sheet could survive in a tail event; appetite is the line the board chooses to draw well inside that. The statement has two halves. The quantitative half is a table of metrics (capital ratios, liquidity ratios, RAROC hurdles, concentration limits) each with a green (business as usual), amber (warning), and red (breach) zone. The qualitative half is a set of plain-English commitments: zero tolerance for internal fraud, treating customers fairly, climate constraints. The whole thing is approved by the board, cascaded down to business units, and tested in the supervisory review.

Feynman test Think about driving a car on a motorway with a maximum legal speed of 70 mph. Capacity is what the car can do, perhaps 150 mph. Appetite is the speed you choose, say 75 mph. The dashboard is your KRI panel: speedometer, fuel gauge, oil temperature. Green is normal driving, amber is the low fuel warning, red is the engine warning light. Your written rule, "I will not exceed 75 mph and I will refuel before the tank hits empty", is the risk appetite statement. The driver who only ever looks at the dashboard when something flashes red is the bank that only manages risk by remediation. The driver who scans the dashboard every minute is the bank that runs a real risk function.
Step 2

Why this matters

Credit Suisse is the cleanest recent example. By 2021 it had a risk appetite statement that, on paper, set tight concentration limits and a low tolerance for reputational events. Through that same year it lost USD 5.5 billion on the Archegos family office (a single counterparty concentration) and USD 10 billion of client money in the Greensill supply-chain funds (a conduct event). The numbers blew through every red line in the statement. Senior management changed, but the underlying behaviour did not, because the appetite was not effectively cascaded into business-unit decisions or wired into the front-line incentive structure. By March 2023 the liquidity drained out of the bank in days and it was sold to UBS for a fraction of its book value. The risk appetite statement had been a document, not a decision rule.

For the supervisor, the risk appetite statement is the spine of the SREP review. The PRA, ECB, and the Prudential Authority all explicitly assess whether the bank's stated appetite is consistent with its actual exposures, capital plan, and ICAAP. A bank with a tight written appetite and a hot trading book gets capital add-ons. A bank with a vague appetite gets a governance finding. Internally, the risk appetite is what ties the abstract idea of "risk culture" to actual numbers a credit committee can vote against. Without it, every transaction becomes a one-off negotiation between the first and second lines.

Step 3

The mental model

A layered diagram of capital ratios from the bottom up. The regulatory minimum at the base, the combined buffer requirement on top, the management buffer above that, the BAU operating zone in the middle, and the risk appetite ceiling at the top. The same shape works for liquidity, concentration, or any other KRI. Appetite sits comfortably inside capacity, capacity sits comfortably above the regulatory minimum, and the gap between the BAU level and the breach levels is where the early-warning thresholds live.

Step 4

The detail

What drives appetite: capital, funding, stakeholders

Appetite is fundamentally a function of capital and funding. The bank's free or available capital sets the size of the bet it can take; its ability to raise stable long-term liquidity sets how long it can hold a position when markets close. The amount of capital the bank itself assesses as needed to remain solvent at a given confidence level and horizon (say, the loss the bank could absorb at the 99.9% level over one year) is its economic capital, sometimes called risk capital. Economic capital is the internal twin of regulatory capital, and risk appetite is calibrated to both.

Other stakeholders narrow the choice further. Shareholders want returns and want their dividend not to be suspended. Regulators want resilience. Rating agencies want consistency, because a credit rating downgrade widens the bank's wholesale funding spread and can become self-fulfilling. Customers want a bank that will not lose their deposits or stop their payments. The risk appetite statement is the place where management writes down which constraints bind and by how much.

The risk appetite statement

The risk appetite statement (RAS) is a written document, coordinated by the risk function, signed off by senior management, and formally approved by the board. It runs typically to between twenty and fifty pages depending on the size of the bank. It is split into a quantitative section and a qualitative section, and it cascades down into business-unit-level statements that have to be consistent with the top-level one.

Quantitative metrics

The quantitative section is a table of metrics with thresholds, grouped by risk family. Each metric is calibrated against the bank's strategy and risk capacity. The standard set is below.

Metric What it controls Typical calibration
Capital and solvency
Capital adequacy Loss-absorbing capacity. Works both ways: too low is undercapitalised, too high is unproductively cautious. CET1 ratio above the regulatory floor + combined buffer + Pillar 2A + a management buffer. Parallel constraints on Tier 1, total capital and the leverage ratio.
External rating The agency view of solvency, which feeds directly into wholesale funding cost. A target external credit rating, typically a single-A floor for an investment-grade bank.
Profitability
Earnings volatility How much the bank is willing to see its quarterly results swing. Maximum earnings volatility, often expressed as a one-year value-at-risk on net income.
Risk-adjusted return Underwriting discipline at origination: a loan that does not clear the hurdle does not get written. Minimum RAROC hurdle for new business, typically calibrated around the cost of equity (e.g. ~14%).
Credit risk
Credit quality The credit book's vital signs. Minimum weighted-average portfolio rating, maximum credit loss ratio, minimum provisions coverage.
Concentration Caps single points of failure across obligor, sector, country and asset class. e.g. single-name capped at ~10% of CET1; oil-and-gas sector capped at ~15% of the total credit book.
Liquidity and ALM
Liquidity Capacity to meet outflows under normal and stressed conditions. Minimum LCR and NSFR set 10 to 20 percentage points above the 100% regulatory floor; maximum loan-to-deposit and encumbrance ratios; intraday liquidity buffers.
ALM mismatch Sensitivity of earnings and economic value to rate moves and FX. Maximum IRRBB sensitivity (delta-EVE and delta-NII against the BCBS 368 scenarios) and a maximum FX gap.
Operational risk
Operational capital The internal cap on operational risk-taking, set as a slice of total risk. Maximum internal allocation to operational risk, often expressed as a share of total economic capital.

Qualitative metrics

The qualitative section captures the things that do not reduce to a single number but matter for the bank's identity. It includes the link to business strategy, explicit zero-tolerance statements for internal fraud or regulatory breach, customer-conduct commitments (treating customers fairly, suitability standards), and increasingly an environmental and social commitment (limits on financing thermal coal, alignment with a net-zero transition plan, climate disclosure standards). These are the items most likely to be breached first, because they are not monitored in real time.

The four-step risk appetite framework

Formulating the framework follows a sequence. First, identify the key risks facing the bank, drawing on the risk taxonomy from the identification process. Second, evaluate liquidity needs against the funding model. Third, assess capital adequacy under regulatory requirements and economic capital. Fourth, set funding requirements, including the wholesale and retail mix. The output is the calibrated set of thresholds that goes into the RAS.

Alignment with ICAAP, ILAAP, and SREP

The risk appetite statement sits at the top of a four-document Pillar 2 cycle. Three of the documents are written by the bank, one by the supervisor, and the outputs of the supervisor's document feed back into the bank's documents for the next cycle.

The RAS is the bank's stated tolerance for risk. It is drafted by the chief risk officer with input from the executive committee, approved by the board, and shared with the supervisor. Its job is to give every downstream decision a benchmark: any number quoted in the ICAAP, the ILAAP, or a divisional capital plan has to be reconcilable to a line in the RAS.

The ICAAP (Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process) and the ILAAP (Internal Liquidity Adequacy Assessment Process) are the bank's evidence that the appetite is fundable. The ICAAP is drafted by the CRO and CFO with the risk and finance teams, models the bank's capital position under base case and stress, and shows that capital stays above the RAS thresholds and above the regulatory minimum throughout. The ILAAP is its mirror on the liquidity side, drafted by the treasurer with risk, modelling LCR, NSFR and survival horizons under base case and stress. Both are approved by the board and submitted annually to the supervisor. Their primary audience is the supervisor; the secondary audience is the board itself, which uses them as the formal capital and liquidity sign-off.

The SREP (Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process) is the supervisor's document. The PRA, ECB, or SARB Prudential Authority reads the RAS, ICAAP and ILAAP together, layers in its own business-model and governance assessment, and produces a letter to the bank. That letter sets the Pillar 2A add-on for risks Pillar 1 underprices (concentration, IRRBB, pension, model risk), in some jurisdictions a Pillar 2B buffer informed by the stress test, and any qualitative supervisory measures (governance findings, capital distribution restrictions, dividend caps). Its audience is the bank's board and senior management — and, indirectly, the next ICAAP and ILAAP, because Pillar 2A becomes the new minimum the next cycle has to clear. The RAS is the spine the regulator measures all three against.

Key risk indicators and the breach plan

The metrics in the RAS are operationalised as key risk indicators (KRIs). A KRI is a metric that is measured, reported, and assessed against pre-defined thresholds on a regular cycle (typically monthly at the group level, daily for liquidity, real time for market risk on the trading floor). The standard breach-level convention is a three-zone scheme: green (BAU), amber (warning, requiring management attention), red (breach, requiring escalation and remediation).

Crucially, each KRI has a defined trigger plan: when amber, who is informed, who acts, on what timetable. When red, the same questions plus a defined escalation up to the group risk committee or the board. The KRI without the trigger plan is just a number on a dashboard. The KRI with the trigger plan is a control. Effective KRI monitoring requires investment in the data pipeline: source systems, aggregation, validation, and reporting. The BCBS 239 standard on risk data aggregation is what supervisors use to assess whether the KRI machine is actually working.

A common trap Banks tend to overproduce KRIs. A real risk appetite has perhaps ten to fifteen group-level KRIs that the board reviews, with the rest pushed down to the relevant business unit dashboards. The sign of a healthy framework is that the board's KRI report fits on one page. The sign of an unhealthy one is a 200-row spreadsheet that nobody reads.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Appetite vs capacity, and the gap that matters

Risk capacity is the maximum risk the bank could absorb without becoming insolvent or illiquid. Risk appetite is the level the board chooses to operate at, well inside that capacity. The gap is deliberate and is the buffer that absorbs surprise. A bank that runs at 100% of its capacity has no margin for error; a bank that runs at 30% is leaving shareholder return on the table. Setting appetite is fundamentally an exercise in choosing where in that gap to sit, and the answer changes with the cycle. Most banks raise the implicit gap in good times by buying back stock or paying out earnings, and discover late in the cycle that they have very little headroom left when shocks arrive.

The cascade problem

The group RAS is approved by the board. To be operational it has to cascade into divisional appetites, then into business-unit appetites, then into individual mandate letters for desks and credit officers. Each layer of cascade can introduce drift, optimism, or outright reinterpretation. By the time it reaches the trader at the desk, the original constraint has become a soft hurdle. Strong risk functions catch this with reverse cascade checks: aggregate up the divisional appetites and confirm they fit inside the group appetite, with a buffer for correlation. Weak risk functions discover the gap during the first real stress.

Qualitative limits fail first

The quantitative metrics get monitored because they fit into a spreadsheet. The qualitative limits, the ones that say "treat customers fairly" or "zero tolerance for misconduct", get monitored by exception and that exception is normally a regulator finding it before the bank does. Conduct losses (PPI, LIBOR, FX manipulation, Wells Fargo cross-selling) and AML failures (HSBC, Standard Chartered, Danske Bank Estonia) have routinely been an order of magnitude larger than any market or credit loss for the same period. The qualitative section of the appetite is where the next billion-pound fine is already living.

Goodhart's law again

When a hurdle becomes a target, the business will optimise to it. Set a 14% RAROC hurdle and you will get a portfolio of loans priced to clear 14.1%, with the marginal price-makers (assumptions on LGD, capital allocation, funding cost) tweaked until they do. The defence is the same as in Lecture 7: refresh the metrics, monitor for gaming, and benchmark periodically against external comparators. The RAROC hurdle that does not move for five years is a hurdle the business has fully optimised around.

What "risk appetite for the next risk" looks like

Climate, AI model risk, third-party (cyber and supplier), and geopolitical risk are the four areas where appetite statements are evolving fastest. The honest answer for most banks today is that they have a paragraph rather than a calibrated set of thresholds. Climate appetite, in particular, is moving from a soft commitment ("aligned with the Paris Agreement") towards numerical scope-3 financed-emissions limits and sector-by-sector pathway thresholds, partly driven by the supervisory CBES exercises in the UK and similar exercises in the euro area. Expect this to be a substantial part of the next two cycles of appetite refresh at any major bank.
