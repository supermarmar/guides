Economic capital

Regulatory capital tells the bank what it has to hold. Economic capital tells the bank what it actually needs. The two answers do not coincide, and the gap is the most interesting line on the capital plan.

Lecture 12 of Phase 1 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: docs/wiki/application/banking/01_internal_environment/risk_management/06-economic_capital.md
Step 1

The 30-second version

Economic capital is the amount of capital a bank estimates it needs to remain solvent over a one-year horizon at a confidence level tied to its target credit rating, covering all material risks, using its own models and current inputs, and recognising portfolio diversification. It is the bank's own answer to "how much capital do we really need?", as distinct from "how much does the regulator say we must hold?".

Four ideas do most of the work. Target credit rating sets the confidence level: a bank aiming at an A rating runs at 99.9%, one aiming at AA runs at 99.97%. All material risks means everything that matters to the bank, not just the Pillar 1 trio of credit, market, and operational. Point-in-time inputs mean a current view of risk, not a through-cycle average. Diversification means recognising that credit, market, and operational losses do not all peak at the same time. The combination of these four choices produces a number that is usually larger than Pillar 1 (because it captures more risks at a higher confidence level) but smaller than a naive sum of those parts (because of the diversification benefit). The number then feeds the ICAAP, capital allocation across business units, RAROC, pricing, and the bank's strategic decisions.

Feynman test Think of two parallel weighing scales for the same bag of risk. The regulatory scale weighs the bag using prescribed rules and a fixed set of weights, designed to be conservative and comparable across banks. The economic capital scale weighs the same bag using the bank's own instruments, with its own view of what each item weighs and how much the items in the bag cancel each other out. The two scales rarely agree exactly. The interesting work is understanding why they disagree, which scale is right in which conditions, and how to manage the bank against both at once.
Step 2

Why this matters

Economic capital exists because regulatory capital is, by design, an imperfect proxy for the risk the bank actually carries. Basel I in 1988 introduced a credit-risk framework with only five risk-weight buckets: 0%, 10%, 20%, 50%, and 100%. A loan to a AAA corporate sat in the same 100% bucket as a loan to a near-default obligor. Banks responded by booking the high-risk loans (which earned more) and selling the low-risk loans (which were equally expensive in capital), a process the literature labelled regulatory capital arbitrage. The result was the opposite of what the framework had intended: portfolios became riskier under a framework designed to make them safer. The introduction of internal-models-based capital under Basel II in 2004 was a direct response, and economic capital frameworks at the larger banks were the technical foundation on which the move was built.

The 2008 financial crisis carried the lesson in the opposite direction. Many banks held large off-balance-sheet exposures (ABCP conduits, structured investment vehicles, liquidity backstops to securitisations) that consumed almost no regulatory capital but generated substantial economic risk. The internal economic capital frameworks at the banks running these vehicles often showed the exposure clearly. The regulatory capital ratios did not. Northern Rock failed in 2007 with what looked like a comfortable Tier 1 ratio. Bear Stearns failed in 2008 with one of the highest regulatory capital ratios in its peer group. The supervisor's response, embodied in Basel III's capital buffers, the leverage ratio, and the formalisation of Pillar 2A and Pillar 2B, was to bring economic capital and regulatory capital closer together while leaving the bank's own internal view in place as the bridge.

For the supervisor today, the economic capital framework is the technical gateway to a productive ICAAP and SREP. A bank with a credible economic capital framework can argue for lower Pillar 2A add-ons, defend its diversification benefit, and explain its capital plan with reference to its own risk profile. A bank without one is reduced to debating supervisory benchmarks, and the dialogue is usually less favourable.

Step 3

The mental model

The single most useful visual for economic capital is the capital stack: the layered diagram showing the regulatory minimum at the bottom, the buffers above it, and the bank's available capital on top. Economic capital sits parallel to this stack, as the bank's own internal answer to the same solvency question.

Step 4

The detail

The four defining characteristics

Economic capital is defined by four interlocking choices, and the same four words come back in every supervisor's challenge of the framework.

Characteristic What it means in practice
Target credit rating The confidence level is a strategic choice tied to the rating the bank wants to retain. An A target with implied one-year PD around 0.1% maps to 99.9%; AA at around 0.03% maps to 99.97%. The choice is documented, approved by the board, and revisited when the target rating or business mix changes.
All material risks Not just credit, market, and operational. The framework captures IRRBB, credit concentration, pension, business and strategic risk, model risk, and anything else risk identification has surfaced as material. The materiality threshold itself is a documented choice.
Point-in-time inputs Current PD, current LGD, current correlation, current volatility. EC answers "how much capital do we need today?", not "how much do we need on average across the cycle?". This is in deliberate contrast to IRB regulatory capital, which uses through-the-cycle PDs to dampen procyclicality.
Portfolio diversification Credit, market, and operational losses are imperfectly correlated. Economic capital recognises that they do not all peak at the same time and applies a documented diversification benefit. The benefit is typically 10 to 25% of the gross sum, and its size is the most heavily challenged line in the ICAAP.

Risk by risk: economic capital versus the regulatory floor

Each risk type is treated differently under the two frameworks, and understanding the deltas is what makes the economic capital number defensible.

Risk Regulatory capital basis Economic capital basis (illustrative 99.95%)
Credit 99.9% ASRF formula, through-the-cycle PD, downturn LGD. Portfolio-invariant by design. 99.95% Monte Carlo over a multi-factor structural or factor model, point-in-time PD, full correlation structure. Captures name and sector concentration explicitly.
Market FRTB: 97.5% expected shortfall, liquidity-adjusted horizons of 10 to 250 days. 99.95% VaR / ES, one-year horizon. Materially larger than the regulatory number for illiquid positions.
Operational SMA formula based on business indicator and historic losses. No explicit confidence level. Loss-distribution approach: Poisson frequency, lognormal or GPD severity, convolved to give the aggregate loss. 99.95th percentile read off directly.
IRRBB Not in Pillar 1. Captured through supervisory outlier tests and Pillar 2A. EVE sensitivity at the chosen confidence level, with a yield-curve shock calibrated to the bank's balance sheet.
Concentration Implicit in IRB only; the formula assumes infinite granularity. Explicit single-name and sector add-on, computed from the gap between the IRB formula and a Monte Carlo run on the actual portfolio.
Pension, business, model Not in Pillar 1. Explicit management overlays, often built from scenario analysis and expert judgement.
Diversification None. Risk-type capital is summed. Variance-covariance aggregation with a judgement-led inter-risk correlation matrix.

Cross-risk diversification

The single most consequential difference between economic and regulatory capital is the treatment of cross-risk correlation. Pillar 1 adds the three risk-type capitals; economic capital recognises that the three loss distributions are imperfectly correlated and applies a benefit. The standard formulation is the variance-covariance aggregation:

$$\mathrm{EC}_{\text{total}} \;=\; \sqrt{\mathbf{EC}^{\top} \, \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \, \mathbf{EC}}$$

where \(\mathbf{EC}\) is the vector of risk-type economic capital and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) is the inter-risk correlation matrix. The matrix is symmetric, the diagonal is one by construction, and the off-diagonals typically sit in the 0.3 to 0.5 range from expert judgement. There is no recent data set rich enough to estimate, say, the credit-operational correlation at the firm level with statistical precision; the matrix is a documented judgement that the supervisor will probe directly. The diversification benefit at firm level usually lands in the 10 to 25% range, and the line is one of the most heavily debated entries in the ICAAP.

A common trap Plugging a correlation matrix lifted from a peer bank or a vendor study into the bank's own EC framework is the fast way to fail a supervisory challenge. The matrix must be defensible on the basis of the bank's own business mix, not on what others have done. The standard test the validator should run is: vary each off-diagonal by plus and minus 0.1; how much does total EC move? If the answer is "a lot", the documentation around the chosen value needs to be commensurately thorough.

The capital stack from the regulatory floor up

Economic capital sits in parallel to a regulatory stack of seven layers. The order matters because the layers are met by different capital instruments and breach has different consequences.

Layer Composition Function and consequence of breach
Pillar 1 minimum 4.5% CET1 + 1.5% AT1 + 2.0% T2 = 8% of RWA Hard regulatory floor across credit, market, and operational risk. Breach triggers prompt corrective action.
Pillar 2A Bank-specific, met mostly with CET1 Binding going-concern add-on for risks not captured in Pillar 1. Set by the supervisor through SREP.
Capital conservation buffer 2.5% CET1 Always-on buffer. Breach triggers a Maximum Distributable Amount calculation that restricts dividends, AT1 coupons, and variable remuneration.
Countercyclical buffer 0 to 2.5% CET1, time-varying Set by the macroprudential authority; rises in boom, falls in bust. Same MDA consequences if breached.
Systemic risk buffers G-SII / O-SII / SyRB, CET1 Structural add-on for systemically important or sector-concentrated banks.
Pillar 2B Bank-specific, CET1; sits inside the combined buffer requirement Stress-test add-on. Covers the gap that materialises under a severe but plausible scenario.
Management buffer Bank choice, mostly CET1 Cushion above all of the above to avoid breaching in normal volatility. Internal target only, no regulatory consequence.

Economic capital, in this picture, is the bank's parallel internal answer to the same solvency question. The regulatory stack is binding; the economic capital framework is informative and feeds the dialogue with the supervisor through the ICAAP. A well-diversified universal bank can occasionally find that its economic capital number sits below the sum of Pillar 1 and Pillar 2A, even at a higher confidence level, because the diversification benefit at firm level exceeds the conservatism in the regulatory formulae. The supervisor will then often raise the binding floor through Pillar 2A so the bank's reported number is the regulatory one.

What the bank uses economic capital for

Use How it works Why it matters
Capital allocation Total EC is allocated to business units in proportion to their contribution to total risk. A unit running a high-tail-risk book gets a larger allocation than one running a fee book of the same revenue. The allocation is the denominator each unit must earn a return on. It anchors growth and investment decisions to the actual capital each unit consumes.
Risk-adjusted performance (RAROC) Net revenue minus expected loss minus cost, divided by allocated economic capital. The standard internal measure of whether a unit is creating shareholder value. A unit with RAROC below the bank's cost of equity is destroying value, however large its top line. RAROC is what links unit-level performance to the bank's return on equity.
Pricing The capital allocated to a single loan or trade, multiplied by the required return on capital, gives the capital component of the price. A loan that does not at least cover its capital charge does not earn its keep, even if it covers expected loss and operating cost.
Limit setting Concentration, sector, and counterparty limits are calibrated against the economic capital impact of a maximum exposure rather than against notional. Makes the limit framework risk-sensitive without manual recalibration for every product type or counterparty class.
Strategic decisions A proposed acquisition or new business is evaluated against its EC consumption, its standalone RAROC, and its impact on the bank's diversification benefit. A loss-making business can be capital-accretive at the group level if it diversifies the risk mix, and a profitable business can be capital-destructive if it concentrates it.

The standard RAROC formula:

$$\mathrm{RAROC} \;=\; \frac{\text{Net revenue} - \text{Expected loss} - \text{Cost}}{\text{Allocated EC}}$$

The denominator is the lever. A unit whose risk has fallen because hedges have been put on, or because correlation with the rest of the book has dropped, sees its allocated EC fall and its RAROC rise without any change in revenue. The same logic in reverse means a unit that takes on more risk, or whose risk becomes more correlated with the bank, sees allocated EC rise and RAROC fall. The framework is what aligns business-unit incentives with the bank's overall solvency position.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The correlation matrix carries the framework

Move any off-diagonal of the inter-risk correlation matrix by 0.1 and the firm-level economic capital number moves by single-digit percentages. Stack three such moves together and the bank's reported capital adequacy can move by tens of basis points. The matrix is mostly judgement, the judgement is mostly documented after the fact, and the supervisor knows this. The strongest banks treat the matrix as the central exhibit in their ICAAP: a single page that defends every off-diagonal with a specific business-mix argument and a sensitivity table that shows how much each value can be perturbed before the framework's conclusion changes. The weakest banks copy peer numbers and hope nobody asks.

Two parallel ratios, and which one binds

The bank runs two parallel solvency views: regulatory capital and economic capital. Most of the time the regulatory ratio binds, because Pillar 1 plus Pillar 2A plus the combined buffer is usually higher than the bank's own internal number. But there are conditions in which economic capital binds: when the bank takes on a new risk type not yet in Pillar 1 (climate, model, conduct), when concentration risk is large enough to dominate the formula benefit, or when the supervisor sets a higher confidence level than the bank's internal target. A mature capital function tracks both ratios continuously, understands which is binding now, and forecasts when the binding ratio is expected to change.

RAROC can be gamed

Every business unit knows that lower allocated economic capital means higher RAROC. The defensive moves include lobbying for a lower confidence level, arguing for a higher diversification credit against the rest of the bank, pushing for off-balance-sheet structures that reduce notional, and selecting reference portfolios for correlation estimation that flatter the unit. The risk function's job is to resist all four and to keep the methodology consistent across the bank, because inconsistency in capital allocation methodology produces inconsistency in business-unit decisions, which produces a bank optimised for RAROC rather than for actual return on equity. The discipline is the same as for any internal performance measure: the methodology owner has to outrank the methodology user.

The confidence-level choice is strategic

Moving from 99.9% to 99.95% adds roughly 10% to the total economic capital number for a typical bank. The choice is therefore not a technical detail but a strategic decision about how much resilience the bank wants to buy. It is anchored to the target credit rating, but the rating itself is a choice: a higher rating means lower funding cost and stronger counterparty standing, paid for with more capital. The board owns this choice. The CRO defends it. The CFO costs it. The supervisor watches it. The interaction of all four is what produces a capital plan that holds together for three years rather than for the next quarter.

Pillar 2A is the supervisor's verdict on the bank's EC framework

An ICAAP is judged less on the elegance of its methodology and more on the credibility of its outputs against the bank's own risk profile and the supervisor's benchmarks. Pillar 2A is the formal output of that judgement: where the supervisor agrees with the bank's analysis, Pillar 2A captures the gap to Pillar 1; where the supervisor disagrees, Pillar 2A is set higher and the bank's economic capital number is overridden in the binding stack. The art of the ICAAP submission is to make the supervisor's life easier: clear materiality assessment, defensible methodology, transparent sensitivities, and explicit identification of the bank's own areas of conservatism. A bank that does this routinely sees a relatively stable Pillar 2A; a bank that does not sees the number move around with the supervisor's mood.

Capital allocation is politics with arithmetic

Allocating the bank's capital across business units is not a purely technical exercise. The allocation drives compensation, drives investment, drives the relative status of business heads. A unit that gets less capital gets less ability to grow. The risk function's job is to ensure the allocation is defensible on the methodology, but the conversation that follows is unavoidably political. The strongest banks separate the methodology decision (owned by risk) from the allocation decision (owned by the executive), and they resist any attempt to renegotiate the methodology mid-cycle to land on a different allocation.
