Risk management: the process and the lines of defence

Banks make money by taking risk. The whole organisation has to be built so that the risk takers, the risk controllers, and the risk auditors are different people, reporting through different chains, with different incentives. This lecture is about that architecture.

Lecture 7 of Phase 1 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: docs/wiki/application/banking/01_internal_environment/risk_management/01-risk_management.md
Step 1

The 30-second version

A bank is a risk-taking machine. The job of risk management is not to stop the machine, but to make sure the people pulling the levers are not the same people checking that the levers work, and that someone independent watches both.

The industry-standard architecture is the three lines of defence, codified by the Institute of Internal Auditors in 2013. The first line owns the risk and the controls inside the business unit. The second line is an independent risk and compliance function that sets policy, reviews exposures, and challenges the first line. The third line is internal audit, providing independent assurance to the board. In the original IIA model, external auditors and regulators sit outside these three lines as "external controls". A 2015 BIS Financial Stability Institute paper (Arndorfer and Minto, FSI Occasional Paper No 11) argued that for regulated financial institutions this is not enough, and proposed a fourth line of defence that explicitly brings external auditors and supervisors inside the assurance architecture. Most regulators have since adopted this framing for banks. The board owns the whole edifice. The point of the structure is to separate the person who books the trade from the person who values it, from the person who audits the valuation, and from the person who tells the board whether any of them are telling the truth.

Feynman test Think of a casino. The dealers take the bets (first line). The pit boss watches the dealers and the players, and resets a table that has drifted (second line). Once a quarter, the casino brings in an outside firm to count the chips, walk the floor, and check that the cameras worked (third line). The state gaming commission can drop in any time (fourth line). The owner is responsible for all of it. If a dealer cheats, the owner cannot blame "the system", because the system is the owner's job. A bank is the same idea, but with twenty desks, fifty regulators, and trillion-rand balance sheets.
Step 2

Why this matters

Almost every famous bank scandal of the last thirty years has been a lines-of-defence failure, not a model failure. In 1995 Nick Leeson at Barings Bank ran both the trading desk and the back office in Singapore, so when his bets went sour he could hide them in the settlement system. The first line owned the risk, the first line owned the controls, the second line did not exist locally, and a 233-year-old merchant bank collapsed in three months. In 2012 JPMorgan's London chief investment office (the "London Whale") accumulated a six-billion-dollar loss on a synthetic credit book because the second-line risk model was being recalibrated by the same desk it was supposed to challenge. In 2016 Wells Fargo opened 3.5 million fake customer accounts because the reward system told first-line branch staff to hit cross-sell targets, and the second line did not push back hard enough until the regulator did.

The point is not that these banks had no risk function. They all had risk committees, risk policies, and risk-adjusted return metrics. The point is that the structure failed. Independence was nominal rather than real. Reporting lines went through the people being challenged. The reward system told staff to take risk, and the risk function was too quiet, too late, or too captured to stop them. The three-lines model is the regulator's attempt to make those failure modes harder by writing the separation into the org chart.

Step 3

The mental model

Four concentric layers around the risk-taking business, with the board on top. The closer to the business, the more granular the control. The further out, the more independent the assurance.

The FSI paper's distinctive contribution is to argue that internal audit, external audit and the supervisor form a regulatory triangle with bilateral information flows on every side, and that the four-lines model only works if those flows are real, formal, and protected by the right confidentiality and safe-harbour rules. Step 4

The detail

Framework and culture

A risk management framework is the written architecture of how the bank takes and controls risk. The five components are well established. First, the business model is defined and grounded in competitive strengths. A retail mortgage lender and a fixed-income trading house have entirely different risk profiles, and the framework has to fit. Second, the bank identifies and quantifies the risks the model produces, and reviews them continuously as markets change. Third, monitoring capabilities are built on top, supported by IT systems that can actually deliver timely risk numbers. Fourth, mitigation techniques are designed for each risk, from collateral and netting on the credit side to hedging on the market side. Fifth, and most important, a culture is instilled so that every employee, not just the risk team, knows what the bank's risk appetite is and behaves accordingly.

The split of duties at the top of the bank matters. Risk oversight is the responsibility of the board. Risk management, the day-to-day execution, is the responsibility of management. The board is nevertheless ultimately accountable for every aspect of risk at every level. The board defines which risk issues must always be escalated directly to it (typically anything that breaches risk appetite, anything with reputational impact, anything regulatory). Each board member signs up to a formal risk appetite and guidance statement that they then own personally.

The reward system test The most reliable diagnostic for whether a bank's risk culture is real or theatre: read the bonus plan. If a trading desk's compensation is paid out before its book matures, the bank is paying for revenue today out of risk it has not yet realised. If a branch's bonus depends on cross-sell, the second line had better be tight on misselling controls. Risk culture is set by the cash. Everything else is decoration.

First line of defence: risk ownership

The first line is the revenue-generating business. Lending officers in a corporate banking team, traders on a fixed-income desk, branch staff in retail, RM-facing sales in private banking. They take the risk because they are the ones interacting with customers and counterparties. They also own the controls. The controls in the first line are granular and transaction-level. A credit officer running a covenants check on a loan, a trader respecting a single-name limit, a teller validating an FX transaction against KYC: these are first-line controls.

The model assumes that, because first-line staff are involved in processes every day, they spot weaknesses faster than anyone else and they own the remediation. This is true, but it is also a source of capture. People do not enjoy adding friction to processes that generate their bonus. The second line exists for exactly that reason.

Second line of defence: risk control

The second line is the independent risk and compliance function. Critically, it must be independent of the first line. That means a separate reporting chain (the head of risk reports to the CEO or the board, not to the head of trading), a separate budget (the first line cannot cut the second line's headcount), and ideally a separate physical location. The second line writes policy, sets and monitors limits, validates models, runs the stress tests, and challenges the first line's view of its own risk. Compliance, finance, model validation, and back-office settlement all sit here.

Within the second line a set of standing committees do the heavy lifting. The exact names vary by bank but the functions are universal.

Why three lines were not enough for banks

The IIA's 2013 model was designed for organisations in general. The Arndorfer and Minto FSI paper sets out four ways in which it tends to fail in regulated financial institutions, each rooted in real incidents.

1. Misaligned 1L incentives
Most risk-takers in the first line are paid for revenue, not for control. UBS accumulated US residential-mortgage-backed-securities positions in 2006 to 2007 whose true size did not reach the top of the bank in usable form, because the desks were rewarded for growing the book and the reporting was watered down on the way up. The fix is to put a control-objective gate in front of any bonus and to demand a properly comprehensive view at board level.
2. Lack of 2L organisational independence
Most risk functions report formally to the board but in practice run on day-to-day reporting lines to senior management, the same senior management whose business they are challenging. Risk officers embedded next to traders eventually start sounding like traders. Remuneration is the canary: if the 2L pay scale is benchmarked to the 1L it is supposed to challenge, independence is decorative.
3. Lack of 2L skills and expertise
Independence is necessary but not sufficient. The 2L also has to be technically able to challenge complex IRB / IRRBB models, value illiquid instruments, and unpick structured trades. At Société Générale in 2008, Jérôme Kerviel ran unauthorised speculative positions for more than a year. The back office and risk control teams opened multiple inquiries into the irregularities, but accepted Kerviel's untruthful explanations because they lacked the seniority and expertise to push back hard enough. A 4.9 billion EUR loss followed.
4. Inadequate 3L risk assessment
Internal audit's annual plan is only as good as its risk assessment. At UBS, internal audit reviewed the critical mortgage-derivatives desk and detected control weaknesses, but the report took months to validate and finalise. By the time it landed, the exposure had grown into the loss that nearly took the bank down. The third line has to be both right and timely.

The FSI paper's response is the four-lines extension: bring external auditors and supervisors formally inside the assurance architecture, and define the information flows between them and internal audit. The argument is not that banks should have more controllers, but that the controllers they already have should talk to each other on a defined footing.

Third line of defence: independent assurance

The third line is internal audit. The clue is in the name: internal, so it is part of the bank; audit, so it does not own the risks or the controls. It assesses whether the first two lines work in practice. Its standing is structural: it reports to the audit committee of the board, not to the executive, and the head of internal audit is one of the few executives whose dismissal typically requires board approval.

The board audit committee is its main client. Under the Companies Act in South Africa, every public company and state-owned company must have one. Under King IV (the South African corporate governance code) and equivalent codes in other jurisdictions, board members must enquire into and then confirm in the annual financial statements that internal controls are adequate. The audit committee is how they discharge that duty. Internal audit teams also protect against fraud and validate the integrity of the financial reports the bank publishes. The reporting line up to the board, not down through management, is what makes the third line independent rather than just well-meaning.

Fourth line of defence: external scrutiny

External controls complement the internal three. The most common are external auditors (typically one of the Big Four firms), who provide a statutory opinion on the annual financial statements, and regulators, who supervise the bank under the relevant prudential and conduct regimes. In the UK that means the PRA and the FCA. In the euro area it is the ECB, supported by national competent authorities. In South Africa it is the Prudential Authority (within SARB) and the FSCA. The regulator's tools (on-site inspections, thematic reviews, SREP-style supervisory reviews, capital add-ons) are covered in Lecture 14. For now the point is that the fourth line is structurally outside the bank, can compel disclosure, and can impose sanctions.

The reason the FSI paper insists on treating these two parties as a distinct line of defence, rather than as ad-hoc external controls, is that for a bank they cannot be treated as ad-hoc. Supervisors set the standards the external auditor's opinion has to satisfy, they can request specific audit scope items, and in many jurisdictions external auditors have a duty to report matters of "material significance" to the supervisor. BCBS Core Principle 16 (in The internal audit function in banks, 2012) requires supervisors to communicate regularly with the bank's internal audit function on risks identified, mitigation measures, and follow-up.

For this to work in practice, three relationships have to be functional: supervisor with external auditor, supervisor with internal auditor, and internal auditor with external auditor. The FSI paper distinguishes two modes for the supervisor-to-internal-audit channel:

Vertical mode
Hierarchical. The supervisor assesses the quality of internal audit as part of its mandate to confirm that the bank has an effective independent assurance function. The supervisor sits above; internal audit is being inspected.
Horizontal mode
Collaborative. The supervisor acts as a peer, sharing its own view of risks and inspection findings with internal audit, in exchange for internal audit sharing its work programme and material issues. The FSI paper argues that the two modes should be run by different teams within the supervisor, so that the act of being assessed does not chill the act of sharing.

The horizontal channel only works if internal auditors are legally protected when they disclose confidential information to supervisors in good faith. This protection (often called safe harbour) exists for external auditors in most jurisdictions but is unevenly extended to internal auditors. Where it is missing, the four-lines model collapses back to a one-way reporting flow from the bank to the regulator.

Putting it together

The architecture survives because each layer can fail without the whole thing collapsing. A loose first-line control will be caught by a second-line review. A weak second-line review will be flagged in the third-line audit. A captured third line will eventually surface in the fourth-line external audit or the regulator's inspection. The board owns the design and is liable when it fails. The whole point is redundancy. The lines do not need to be perfect, they need to be different.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Independence on paper vs independence in fact

The most common failure of the three-lines model is not that a bank does not have the structure, it is that the structure is nominal. The head of credit risk reports to the chief risk officer, who reports to the CEO. So far, so independent. But the head of credit risk's bonus is set by a committee chaired by the CEO. The risk team's budget is allocated by finance, which reports to the CFO, who reports to the CEO. The risk team's office sits on the same floor as the trading floor. The second line is independent only as long as it does not test that independence. The regulator's job, particularly under SREP-style reviews, is to probe exactly this.

The "constructive challenge" problem

Banks like to describe the second line as providing "constructive challenge" to the first. In practice, challenge is uncomfortable. A risk manager who blocks a deal is a risk manager who is unpopular at the next bonus round, and over time the unpopular get filtered out. The defence against this is structural: written policies that the first line cannot override without an explicit escalation, model thresholds that trigger automatic intervention, and risk officers whose performance is measured on the quality of their challenges (and the avoided losses) rather than the volume of approvals.

Goodhart's law and risk metrics

"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." The moment a bank tells its first line that the risk metric for the year is, say, a particular RAROC threshold, the first line will optimise to that metric. Loans that just clear the threshold will be written. Loans that almost clear will be tweaked until they do. Loans that comfortably clear will not be priced as aggressively as they could be. The metric becomes the strategy. The second line's job is partly to keep refreshing the metrics, partly to monitor for gaming behaviour, and partly to escalate the cases where the metric and the underlying economics have diverged.

The cost of risk

A common boardroom debate: "How much should the risk function cost?" The answer in cost-of-capital terms is well understood. The risk function is an investment that pays back through avoided losses, lower regulatory capital, and a lower funding spread (because investors trust a well-controlled bank). Banks that have underinvested in risk pay for it in regulatory add-ons (the PRA's Pillar 2A model risk loading, for example) and in the eye-watering cost of remediation programmes when something breaks. The Wells Fargo cross-selling scandal cost the bank roughly $3 billion in fines and a multi-year asset cap that arguably cost more in lost growth than the original fines. A bigger risk function does not always solve the problem, but a smaller one almost never does.

The IIA never adopted "four lines"

It is worth being precise about where the 4LoD comes from. The IIA's 2013 paper sets out three lines plus "external controls" as a residual category. The IIA never promoted a four-line model and in 2020 it actually moved the other way, dropping the word "defence" and rebranding the standard as the Three Lines Model, with the governing body and external assurance providers reframed rather than added. The four-lines framing in this lecture comes from the BIS Financial Stability Institute (Arndorfer and Minto, 2015) and has been adopted by bank supervisors as a useful framing for financial institutions specifically. If you sit an audit-profession exam you will see the IIA's Three Lines Model. If you sit a banking exam you will see four lines. Both are alive.

Where the model is evolving: data-driven assurance

The traditional three-lines model assumes humans reading policy documents and sampling transactions. The modern equivalent is data-driven: continuous monitoring pipelines, machine-learning anomaly detection on transaction flows, and automated control testing across every booking. Internal audit functions at major banks now run quasi-continuous control-monitoring tools rather than annual point-in-time tests. The lines have not changed, but the granularity of what each line can see in real time has. Expect this trend to continue as risk data aggregation (BCBS 239) matures across the industry.

Test yourself

  1. What is the difference between risk oversight and risk management, and who is accountable for each?
  2. List the four lines of defence and give one example of a control that sits in each.
  3. Why does the second line need a separate reporting line from the first, and what fails when it does not?
  4. Name the four core second-line committees and state the primary remit of each.
  5. Internal audit reports to the audit committee, not to the CFO. Explain why this matters.
  6. Give one historical example where a lines-of-defence failure (rather than a model failure) caused a major loss, and identify which line failed.
  7. What is the regulator's role within the four-lines architecture, and how does it differ from internal audit?

  9. Distinguish the vertical and horizontal modes of the supervisor's relationship with internal audit. Why does the FSI paper suggest they should be run by different teams?
