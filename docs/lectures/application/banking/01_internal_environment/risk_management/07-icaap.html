ICAAP

The bank writes the ICAAP to make its own case for how much capital it needs. The supervisor reads it and decides whether to agree. The dialogue between the two is what determines the binding capital requirement.

Lecture 13 of Phase 1 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: docs/wiki/application/banking/01_internal_environment/risk_management/07-icaap.md
Step 1

The 30-second version

The Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process is the bank's annual self-assessment of whether its capital is adequate for its risk profile, now and over a three- to five-year planning horizon. It is owned by the board, produced annually, and submitted to the supervisor as the primary input to the SREP.

The standard ICAAP has eight chapters: business model (what the bank does and where it is going), risk appetite (what risk it is willing to take), governance (who owns the document and how it is challenged), risk identification (the MIRA: which risks are material and how each is captured), capital assessment (Pillar 1 plus Pillar 2A for the current view, Pillar 2B for the forward-looking view), capital planning (a multi-year projection and the contingency plan), linkage to ILAAP and recovery (consistency with the liquidity and resolution frameworks), and an executive summary that explicitly states the board's conclusion on capital adequacy. The eight chapters answer eight different questions, and a chapter written generically rather than to a specific question fails the test.

Feynman test Think of the ICAAP as the bank making its case in a planning application. The bank says: this is what we want to do, this is the land and the building, this is what could go wrong, and this is the insurance and structural reinforcement we have in place. The planning officer reads the application, asks questions, benchmarks the proposal against other recent submissions, and ultimately grants or denies permission, often with conditions attached. The ICAAP is the application, the SREP is the planning officer's response, and the Total Capital Requirement is the permission with its conditions.
Step 2

Why this matters

Before Basel II, most banks did not run a structured forward-looking capital adequacy process. Capital was assessed against the Basel I floor, business plans were built off revenue projections, and the link between risk profile and capital was implicit at best. The 2007 to 2008 financial crisis exposed how thin this was. Northern Rock, when it failed in 2007, had a Tier 1 ratio above its regulatory floor and a profitable business model on paper. What it did not have was a stress-tested capital plan: a forward-looking assessment of what would happen to its funding profile, its capital, and its liquidity under a plausible market dislocation. The ICAAP, embedded in Basel II Pillar 2 and supplemented by the post-crisis SREP, was the supervisory response.

The 2014 EBA SREP guidelines made the ICAAP the centrepiece of the supervisory dialogue with each bank. The guidelines set out a four-element supervisory assessment (business model, governance and controls, risks to capital, risks to liquidity), each of which draws directly from the ICAAP. A bank with a strong ICAAP runs a faster, lower-cost SREP and is given more credit for its own internal capital view. A bank with a weak ICAAP receives larger Pillar 2A add-ons, more frequent supervisory visits, and a thinner benefit of the doubt on its own models. The cost of the ICAAP, measured in effort and management time, is materially less than the cost of running into an unprepared SREP, and the strongest banks treat the document as a continuously maintained working file rather than an annual sprint.

For the bank itself, the ICAAP has a second function that is sometimes underweighted: it is the document the board uses to discuss capital strategy. A board challenge over the methodology, the scenarios, or the diversification benefit is captured in the document and becomes part of the institutional memory. A board that signs the ICAAP without challenge has signed away its own discipline. A board that pushes back, demands revisions, and records its concerns in minutes is doing its job, and supervisors read those minutes carefully.

Step 3

The mental model

The single most useful visual for ICAAP is the flow that links the bank's internal inputs through the ICAAP document, into the SREP, and out as the binding Total Capital Requirement. The whole picture is a loop, run annually, with the supervisor's decision feeding back into the following year's planning.

Step 4

The detail

The regulatory anchor

The ICAAP is anchored in Article 73 of CRD V in the EU and the equivalent rule in the PRA rulebook in the UK, supplemented by the EBA Guidelines on ICAAP and ILAAP information (2016) and the wider Basel Pillar 2 framework. The PRA's SS31/15 sets the UK supervisory expectations. The US equivalent for large banks is the Federal Reserve's Capital Plan Rule (12 CFR 225.8), and the South African equivalent sits under SARB's Regulations relating to Banks. The depth and the language vary; the eight-chapter structure does not.

The eight chapters

Chapter What it covers What the supervisor looks for
1. Business model and strategy Product lines, customer segments, geographic and legal-entity footprint, revenue and cost composition, strategic initiatives, three- to five-year plan. Whether the model is profitable on its own terms and whether the risk profile that follows is consistent with everything that follows.
2. Risk appetite The board-approved appetite statement, the cascade of limits, and the live exposure metrics that monitor adherence. A clean chain from appetite to limit to live metric, with no broken links.
3. Governance and three lines of defence How the ICAAP is produced, challenged, and approved. Named roles for the board, CRO, CFO, CEO, and the validation function. Evidence of genuine board challenge: minutes that record debate, not just approval.
4. Risk identification (MIRA) The material identification of risks across the full risk universe, not only Pillar 1. Each risk has an owner, a methodology, and a capital approach. The omission test: what risks are not flagged as material, and would a peer agree? Climate, model, conduct, and concentration are the usual challenges.
5. Capital assessment Current view (Pillar 1 + Pillar 2A) and forward-looking view (Pillar 2B). Both views derive from the economic capital framework with prescribed adjustments. The reconciliation between the regulatory and internal views, and the documentation behind the diversification benefit.
6. Capital planning Three- to five-year projection of capital requirements and available capital, with planned capital actions and the capital contingency plan. Realistic management actions, unambiguous triggers, and consistency with the strategic plan.
7. Linkage to ILAAP and recovery Cross-references to the liquidity assessment and the recovery plan. Consistency of macro assumptions and management actions. That stress in one document produces the consequences expected in the other.
8. Executive summary The board's conclusion on capital adequacy, the binding numbers, and the principal areas of management focus. That the summary actually summarises and does not paper over weaknesses identified in the underlying chapters.

The MIRA: material identification of risks

The MIRA is the cornerstone of the ICAAP. The bank identifies all material risks it faces, classifies each as Pillar 1, Pillar 2A, or captured through scenario analysis, and provides an owner and methodology for each. A risk register that omits an obviously material risk is the most common ground for a formal supervisory finding. The standard universe to cover:

Category Risks included Where captured
Pillar 1 risks Credit (retail, SME, corporate, sovereign), market (trading book), operational (including conduct, cyber, IT). Pillar 1 RWA under standardised or internal-models-based approaches.
Pillar 2A risks IRRBB, credit concentration (name, sector, geographic, product), pension (where a defined benefit scheme exists), residual risk on credit risk mitigation, model risk, business and strategic risk. Pillar 2A add-on derived from the economic capital framework.
Cross-referenced to ILAAP Liquidity and funding risk. Quantified in the ILAAP; consistency with ICAAP assumptions tested.
Emerging or increasingly material Climate-related financial risk, cyber resilience, third-party and outsourcing risk. Typically a Pillar 2A overlay or scenario-based capital allocation, depending on maturity.
Declared immaterial Risks the bank does not face or considers de minimis. Explicitly listed and justified in the MIRA. "Not material because we don't do that activity" is acceptable; "not material" with no explanation is not.

Capital assessment: current view

The current view has two layers. Pillar 1 is the prescribed regulatory floor across credit, market, and operational risk, with the bank choosing between standardised and internal-models-based approaches. Pillar 2A captures risks not adequately covered by Pillar 1: IRRBB, credit concentration, pension risk, operational risk top-up, and model risk overlay. Pillar 2A is calculated from the bank's economic capital framework applied to those specific risk types, typically using through-the-cycle PDs and downturn LGDs to keep the binding capital requirement countercyclical.

The ICAAP presents both the regulatory and internal views with a reconciliation table. The regulatory view (Pillar 1 plus Pillar 2A) is what the supervisor acts on. The internal view, with point-in-time inputs and the bank's full diversification benefit, is the management tool that drives pricing, allocation, and RAROC. The two numbers will differ; the direction and size of the difference is what the supervisor probes.

Capital assessment: forward-looking view

The forward-looking view, the Pillar 2B capital planning buffer, answers a different question: under a severe but plausible stress, how much extra capital does the bank need to stay above its Pillar 1 plus Pillar 2A floor? The bank designs a suite of scenarios and runs each one over the planning horizon.

Scenario type What it represents What it tests
Baseline Management's central economic forecast. That the strategic plan is internally consistent in normal conditions.
Adverse A plausible but severe downside: sharp recession, property correction, rate spike, sovereign downgrade. Whether the bank stays above Pillar 1 plus Pillar 2A through a recession-like event.
Severe An extreme tail event, typically calibrated to a one-in-twenty-five or one-in-fifty year severity. The size of the Pillar 2B buffer needed to absorb the maximum projected shortfall.
Reverse stress test The combination of events that would render the bank non-viable, worked backwards from the cliff edge. Identification of the specific vulnerabilities that would break the business model.

The Pillar 2B buffer is sized as the maximum projected capital shortfall across scenarios. It sits inside the combined buffer requirement, is met with CET1 only, and breach forces a Maximum Distributable Amount calculation. The qualitative side of the assessment is equally important: the bank documents the management actions available pre-stress (de-risking, hedging, slowing growth) and assigns each an expected capital impact. See economic capital for the capital-stack context.

Capital planning and the contingency plan

The capital planning chapter projects capital requirements and available capital over the three- to five-year horizon under baseline and stress conditions. The projection covers retained earnings, dividend policy, planned issuance, and share buy-backs. Critically, it includes the capital contingency plan: a documented menu of management actions invoked if capital falls toward defined triggers.

A common trap A capital contingency plan that claims the bank can raise R5bn of equity in a stressed market when no such issuance has happened in years is not realistic. The supervisor will challenge the assumed effectiveness of each action and reduce or remove credit for ones that lack execution evidence. The strongest plans pre-position fundable structures (committed lines, contingent capital, RWA-light origination capacity) rather than relying on hypothetical capital raises.

Linkage to ILAAP and recovery

The ICAAP cross-references the Internal Liquidity Adequacy Assessment Process and the recovery plan. A stress that depletes capital usually creates liquidity pressure, and the two cannot be managed in isolation. The standard test is that the adverse scenario in the ICAAP capital projection produces a liquidity profile in the ILAAP that is internally consistent: the same macro assumptions, the same balance-sheet response, the same management actions.

The recovery plan goes further. It covers governance under severe stress, communications with stakeholders, the role of the resolution authority, and the bank's path back to a credible going-concern position. The capital contingency plan in the ICAAP operates inside the recovery plan's framework but at an earlier, less severe trigger.

Proportionality

ICAAP requirements scale with the bank's size and complexity. The EBA SREP guidelines categorise banks into four supervisory categories. Category 1 banks (large, complex, internationally active) face the deepest methodology, the most severe scenarios, and continuous supervisory dialogue. Category 4 banks (small, domestic, simple) produce shorter ICAAPs reviewed on a multi-year cycle. The structure of the framework is the same; the depth is not.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The ICAAP is a dialogue, not a document

The biggest mistake banks make is treating the ICAAP as an annual document-production exercise. It is a continuous dialogue with the supervisor that happens to produce a document once a year. The supervisor has been reading prior ICAAPs, watching the bank's actual capital position, attending board meetings, and reviewing the wider risk reports throughout the year. The annual submission is the formal moment in a year-round conversation. A bank that engages the supervisor on emerging issues mid-year, flags methodology changes as they happen, and pre-discusses contentious assumptions, runs a faster and lower-cost SREP. A bank that goes dark for eleven months and then submits a three-hundred-page document expecting a clean acceptance is in for a hard year.

The omission test is the most damaging finding

"You haven't told us about climate risk" is a much harder finding to remediate than "your IRRBB methodology is conservative". A risk identification chapter that lists everything the bank does consider material, with explicit justification for each item declared immaterial, removes most of the surface area for this finding. The supervisor will still challenge specific materiality assessments, but the bank is debating the methodology rather than defending its omission. The strongest banks maintain a standing risk universe document at the second-line level, updated quarterly, that the ICAAP draws from directly.

Board challenge is the most evidentially valuable artifact

Supervisors read board minutes. A minute that records "the ICAAP was reviewed and approved" is worth almost nothing. A minute that records "the board challenged the diversification benefit on grounds of stress correlation behaviour, requested a sensitivity analysis at zero correlation, and approved the revised number after seeing the impact" is worth several pages of methodology documentation. The challenge does not need to be theatrical to be valuable. It needs to be evidence that the people who own the bank's capital plan understand it deeply enough to push back where the methodology is judgemental.

The reconciliation table tells the supervisor what to probe

The internal-versus-regulatory reconciliation table is the page the supervisor reads most carefully. A bank whose internal view is much lower than its regulatory view is claiming either a generous diversification benefit, point-in-time inputs that flatter a benign cyclical position, or a confidence level lower than peer practice. Each of those is challengeable, and the bank should anticipate the challenge in the table itself. A bank whose internal view is materially higher is signalling residual concerns above the regulatory floor; the supervisor will ask why the Pillar 2A submission does not reflect them. Either direction tells the supervisor what to ask about, so the most defensible position is a clean reconciliation that walks line by line from one view to the other.

Stress scenarios should not be designed by the people they constrain

An adverse scenario that is plausible enough to be credible but mild enough not to embarrass the business plan is the most common form of stress-test failure. The scenario calibration should be owned by the second line, signed off by the CRO, and benchmarked against supervisory anchor scenarios published by the central bank or the EBA. The strongest banks deliberately calibrate scenarios that produce uncomfortable conclusions, because the discomfort itself is the value of the exercise: a scenario that everyone passes comfortably is not actually a stress test.

The contingency plan is a credibility test

The capital contingency plan is the chapter where the bank claims it can take management action under stress. A contingency plan that lists "issue additional Tier 1" as a recovery action without evidence that the bank has done so recently, without a committed counterparty, and without a credible market window, gets reduced or removed by the supervisor. The strongest plans pre-position the funding sources (committed contingent capital lines, mature AT1 issuance programmes, ready-to-execute RWA reductions) and document the trigger conditions clearly enough that invocation in stress is operational rather than novel.

The ICAAP and the ILAAP must be the same scenario

Inconsistency between ICAAP and ILAAP is the supervisor's gift. If the ICAAP adverse scenario assumes unemployment rises to 12% and the ILAAP assumes 9%, the bank has presented two contradictory views of the same future and the supervisor can challenge both. The strongest banks share a single macro scenario library across capital, liquidity, recovery, and resolution, and reuse it across all four exercises. The labour saving is incidental; the credibility benefit is not.

Step 6

Test yourself

  1. State the eight standard chapters of an ICAAP and the question each one answers.
  2. What is the MIRA, and why is the "omission test" the most damaging finding a supervisor can make?
  3. Explain the difference between the regulatory view (P1 + P2A) and the internal view in the ICAAP. Why does the bank present both?
  4. Why do most banks use through-the-cycle PDs and downturn LGDs for the Pillar 2A calculation rather than point-in-time inputs?
  5. Name the four scenario types in a standard ICAAP stress test and explain what each one tests.
  6. How is the Pillar 2B capital planning buffer sized, and what consequence does a breach trigger?
  7. Explain the relationship between the ICAAP, the ILAAP, and the recovery plan. What is the standard consistency test across the three?
  8. What is the capital contingency plan, and what evidence does the supervisor look for to credit each proposed action?
  9. Describe the four-element supervisory assessment performed under SREP, and how each element draws from the ICAAP.
  10. What is proportionality in the EBA SREP framework, and how does it affect the ICAAP requirements for a small domestic bank versus a G-SIB?
  11. Why does the supervisor read board minutes, and what does a useful board challenge look like?
  12. Explain why an ICAAP that produces a confortable conclusion under every stress scenario is not actually a stress test.
