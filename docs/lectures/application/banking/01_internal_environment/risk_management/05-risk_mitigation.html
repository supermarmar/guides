Risk mitigation

Once a risk has been identified and measured, the bank has four choices: avoid it, accept it, transfer it, or reduce it. This lecture is the catalogue of how each gets done in practice, and the situations in which each one fails.

Lecture 11 of Phase 1 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: docs/wiki/application/banking/01_internal_environment/risk_management/05-risk_mitigation.md
Step 1

The 30-second version

Risk mitigation is the action step. After identification and measurement, the bank has to do something. The four canonical responses are avoid (do not take the risk in the first place), accept (live with it, monitored), transfer (hedge, insure, sell, or securitise), and reduce (lower probability or impact through controls and underwriting).

Before reaching for the technical toolkit, management should always be able to answer four questions: what are our largest individual, sector, product, and regional exposures; in what circumstances should they be reduced or grown; what are the means and options to do so; and how should we grow target exposures without taking on excessive risk. Mitigation works only when those questions have been answered, and the available tools have been matched honestly to the situation. The most common mitigation failure is reaching for a complex instrument when the right answer was simply better underwriting at origination.

Feynman test Think of a house in a flood-prone area. You can avoid the risk by not buying the house. You can accept it if the chance of flooding is small and the damage tolerable. You can transfer it by buying insurance. You can reduce it by building defences, raising the floor, and rerouting drainage. A serious flood plan uses all four in combination. A bad plan reaches straight for insurance and never asks whether the house should have been built there in the first place. Banks face the same decision tree every day, on a scale measured in billions.
Step 2

Why this matters

The 2008 financial crisis was, at its root, a mitigation failure dressed up as innovation. The "originate to distribute" model promised that a bank could write subprime mortgages and transfer the credit risk away through securitisation. Each link in the chain (mortgage broker, originator, structurer, rating agency, distributor) earned a fee, none owned the residual economic risk, and so none had a strong incentive to underwrite carefully. When defaults rose, the transfer turned out to be incomplete (banks held large parts of their own securitisations, often in off-balance-sheet vehicles), the rating agency credit enhancement turned out to be model-driven and wrong, and the entire transfer machine reversed direction. The mitigation tool became the risk amplifier.

The lesson is that mitigation tools have failure modes. Hedging fails when the basis moves. Insurance fails when the insurer goes down (AIG in 2008). Securitisation fails when the originator did not underwrite carefully because they planned to sell the risk. Diversification fails in a systemic event when correlations rise to one. The standard "originate to hold" credit culture in commercial banking is a deliberate choice to keep the originator's skin in the game so that the underwriting discipline never lapses, exactly because every transfer tool has a moment when it stops working. A robust mitigation plan uses transfer instruments inside a frame of strong underwriting, not instead of it.

For the supervisor, the mitigation toolkit also has direct regulatory consequences. Hedge effectiveness, collateral eligibility, recognised guarantees, significant risk transfer in securitisations: each is governed by detailed rules in the Basel framework (CRR Articles 192 to 241 in the EU; PRA equivalents in the UK). A "hedge" that does not meet the recognition criteria delivers no capital relief, however effective it is economically. The first line that books a derivative without checking the regulatory recognition criteria has done the mitigation work without earning the capital benefit.

Step 3

The mental model

The canonical risk management decision matrix: probability on one axis, impact on the other, with each cell suggesting the appropriate primary mitigation. Low-likelihood, low-impact risks are usually accepted. High-likelihood, high-impact risks are usually avoided outright or wound down. The interesting cells are the off-diagonals: high-likelihood, low-impact (reduce through controls) and low-likelihood, high-impact (transfer through insurance or capital markets).

Step 4

The detail

Risk avoidance

The cleanest mitigation is not to take the risk at all. Avoidance means changing the plan so the risk does not arise: deciding not to launch a product in a volatile market, withdrawing from a jurisdiction whose conduct standards do not align with the bank, declining to lend to a sector whose collateral cannot be valued. HSBC's wind-down of large parts of its correspondent banking business after the 2012 AML failures is an avoidance decision: the risk-adjusted return on certain client relationships was negative once realistic compliance cost was loaded, so the bank exited. Avoidance is undervalued because it shows up as foregone revenue rather than as visible risk action, but it is often the cheapest mitigation by a wide margin.

Risk acceptance

Acknowledging the risk exists and choosing not to act, because the cost of mitigation exceeds the expected loss. Used for low-impact risks that are easy to monitor. The discipline is that acceptance still requires monitoring: an accepted risk is not an ignored risk. The classic mistake is to accept a risk implicitly, by failing to identify it, which is identification failure, not acceptance. Explicit acceptance is documented, reviewed, and re-assessed when the underlying exposure or environment changes.

Risk transfer

Shifting the consequence of a risk to a third party. The main vehicles in a bank context are hedging through derivatives (often routed through a central counterparty), debt sale, securitisation, and insurance and guarantees. Each carries its own residual risks: the third party must be willing and able to bear the risk, the documentation must be enforceable, and the basis between the original exposure and the transferred form must be small enough to leave the residual risk acceptable.

Hedging through derivatives

The standard bank toolkit covers interest rate, FX, market, and credit risk in both the trading and banking books. Before walking through the instruments, the most important warning is about the basis challenge: there is no guarantee that the rate on the bank's exposure moves in tandem with the rate referenced by the hedge. If a bank earns prime on its loans and pays JIBAR 3-months on its liabilities, entering a pay-fixed-receive-JIBAR swap eliminates the JIBAR exposure but leaves the prime-JIBAR basis as a residual risk. Risk reports must identify hedges separately so that aggregate long and short positions do not net to apparent zero while basis risk sits unhedged behind them.

Instrument How it works Use case and notes
Forward rate agreements (FRAs) Bilateral OTC contracts to exchange a future cashflow tied to a specified rate, amount, and maturity. Settlement at maturity reflects the realised versus agreed rate movement. Tailored to the bank's specific exposure. Counterparty credit risk applies and is managed through ISDA documentation and bilateral collateral arrangements. Prices off the yield curve.
Futures Exchange-traded with standard terms (the canonical example is the 90-day Eurodollar future with a $1 million notional, settling quarterly). The exchange's initial margin covers potential losses and the daily variation margin mitigates counterparty credit risk. Banks lay down "strips" of consecutive futures to create long-dated hedges. Standardisation and margining make futures liquid. Prices off the yield curve.
Swaps Interest rate swaps are the main bank tool for hedging floating-rate exposure. A pay-fixed-receive-floating swap referencing JIBAR 3-months offsets a JIBAR 3-months liability; the residual exposure is just the fixed leg. Nominal amounts are not exchanged, only the net cashflow. Overnight index swaps (OIS) are a subset where the floating leg is the average of an overnight benchmark; OIS lets the treasury separate interest rate risk from liquidity risk cleanly.
Options Optional rather than forward commitments. Buying an option is buying insurance: the holder pays a premium up front and exercises only when the strike is favourable. Preserves upside that forward instruments do not, at the cost of the premium. Swaptions are options on swaps; a payer swaption gives the right to enter a pay-fixed-receive-floating swap at a future date (useful for a bank with floating-rate funding worried about rising rates). FX options give the right to fix a future exchange rate.
Credit default swaps (CDS) A bilateral contract under which the protection seller pays the buyer if a specified credit event happens to a reference entity. Credit events include payment default, certain rating downgrades, and bankruptcy. CDS are unfunded: no nominal is transferred at inception. ISDA documentation governs margining and collateral. CDS spreads on traded names are watched closely as a real-time market view on credit quality. The 2012 Greek restructuring took place without triggering a credit event for some time because the haircut was deemed "voluntary", illustrating the documentation risk in CDS.

Credit risk is the hardest to hedge cleanly. The pre-CDS approach was to short the borrower's bonds (or a similar borrower's), but borrowing the bonds in sufficient size was often impossible and the repo cost was prohibitive. Most banks, for most of their credit book, rely on diversification and origination discipline rather than active credit hedging. CDS is used at the margins for large single names and on indices for portfolio-level hedges, but it does not replace good underwriting.

One last point on hedging: the accounting treatment of a hedge is a separate test from its economic effectiveness or its capital recognition. Hedge accounting under IFRS 9 requires documented hedge designation, prospective and retrospective effectiveness assessment, and falls into three categories (fair value hedge, cash flow hedge, net investment hedge). A trade that hedges economically but does not qualify for hedge accounting will still introduce P&L volatility through the income statement, which is why the front office, risk function, and finance function must agree on hedge design before the trade is booked.

Central clearing through CCPs

Following the 2009 G20 Pittsburgh agreement, standardised over-the-counter derivatives must be cleared through a central counterparty (CCP). The CCP novates each bilateral contract into two contracts with itself, becoming the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer. Counterparty credit risk is transferred from the original bilateral counterparty (whose default in 2008 was the systemic threat) to the CCP, which is mutualised, regulated, and in practice implicitly backstopped by the central bank. The CCP also performs multilateral netting across all members, compressing gross exposures to a single net exposure per member.

CCPs require members to post initial margin (covering potential future exposure under stressed market moves), variation margin (settled daily to reflect mark-to-market moves), and default fund contributions (a mutualised cushion that absorbs losses if a defaulting member's margin proves insufficient). The default waterfall consumes the defaulter's margin first, then the defaulter's default-fund contribution, then a "skin in the game" tranche from the CCP, then non-defaulting members' default-fund contributions, then assessment rights on surviving members. The trade-off is concentration: the CCP becomes too important to fail in its own right, and recovery and resolution planning for CCPs is now an active supervisory topic in its own right (CPMI-IOSCO has issued specific guidance).

Debt sale

The most direct transfer: sell the loan or bond. Avoids basis risk entirely because the exposure leaves the balance sheet. Suffers from a different problem: liquidity in the secondary market varies with the standardisation of the instrument, the borrower's credit rating, and current market conditions. In stress, the market for distressed paper widens its bid-ask spread dramatically, exactly when the bank most wants to sell. The same goes for the secondary market in syndicated leveraged loans (a major asset class for US and European banks): the market is deep in benign conditions and very thin in stress.

Securitisation

Transforming assets into securities. The bank sells assets to a bankruptcy-remote special purpose vehicle (SPV), which issues securities backed by the cashflows. The investor in the securities looks to the assets, not to the originating bank, for repayment. The mechanics deliver four benefits: enhanced liquidity (illiquid loans become tradeable securities), balance sheet management (assets and related risk-weighted assets move off-balance-sheet, subject to significant risk transfer rules), diversification (banks can swap idiosyncratic exposures with other banks through securitisation pools), and risk management (the originator can hold the senior tranches and sell the equity).

The asset universe is wide. Government bonds, trade receivables, auto loans and leases, credit card receivables, mortgages, and corporate loans dominate. Exotic deals have included rock star royalties (the Bowie bonds in 1997), football gate receipts, pharmaceutical patents, and funeral home contracts. The common structural elements are:

Structure Underlying asset pool Notes
Asset-backed securities (ABS) Any asset pool other than mortgages (auto loans, credit cards, trade receivables, leases). The generic label for non-mortgage securitisation.
Mortgage-backed securities (MBS) A pool of residential or commercial mortgages. Subdivided into RMBS (residential) and CMBS (commercial).
Collateralised debt obligations (CDOs) A pool of bonds, often with embedded synthetic exposure through credit default swaps. CDO-squared instruments are CDOs of CDOs and were heavily implicated in 2008.
Collateralised loan obligations (CLOs) A pool of corporate loans, typically leveraged loans. The leveraged loan market today is dominated by CLO demand.
Asset-backed commercial paper (ABCP) Short-dated paper issued by a conduit holding longer-dated assets, with bank liquidity support. A major source of the 2007 to 2008 funding stress when the support lines were drawn.

Credit enhancement is needed where the underlying asset pool's credit quality on its own would not support investment-grade ratings. The standard techniques are overcollateralisation (the asset pool exceeds the issued securities), reserve accounts (cash set aside upfront or built through retained spread), guarantees from third parties, letters of credit, monoline insurance (now largely defunct), and embedded credit default swaps. Tranching sub-divides the issued securities into senior, mezzanine, and junior (or equity) classes with different loss-absorption priority. The equity tranche absorbs first loss, the mezzanine absorbs the next layer, and the senior is paid first and impaired last.

The post-2008 reform agenda for securitisation is substantial. EU and UK rules now require risk retention: the originator must hold at least 5% of the deal on its own balance sheet (in one of several specified forms) to keep skin in the game. Investor due diligence is required both before and during the holding period. Transparency requirements standardise the disclosure to investors and supervisors. Capital treatment for the bank's securitisation exposures uses one of three approaches: the internal ratings-based approach (SEC-IRBA), the external ratings-based approach (SEC-ERBA), or the standardised approach (SEC-SA), each with its own input requirements and capital floors.

The 2017 EU regulation introduced a new category, STS (simple, transparent, standardised) securitisation in the EU and STC (comparable) under the BIS standard, with preferential capital treatment for deals that meet a tight list of criteria. Underlying exposures must be of restricted types and risk-weighting, with no single exposure above 1% of the total pool, and no exposures in default or to credit-impaired borrowers at origination. STS / STC was designed to revive the European securitisation market on a safer footing after the post-crisis collapse.

Insurance and guarantees

Beyond financial derivatives, the most established transfer mechanism is traditional insurance. The bank pays a premium to an insurer that agrees to indemnify a defined loss. The varieties most relevant to a bank are operational-risk policies (bankers blanket bond covering employee dishonesty, robbery, and forgery; professional indemnity for advisory negligence; directors and officers; cyber; crime), credit insurance (covering trade credit, single-name corporate exposure, or sovereign-political risk, common at export credit agencies and trade finance desks), title and surety bonds, and reinsurance for banks with insurance subsidiaries.

A guarantee is a contractual undertaking by a third party to pay a defined obligation if the primary obligor fails to do so. In capital terms, an eligible guarantee is unfunded credit protection under the CRR and qualifies for credit risk mitigation by substituting the guarantor's risk weight for the borrower's. Parent-company guarantees, government export-credit guarantees, and standby letters of credit are common forms. The same recognition tests apply (legal certainty, no escape clauses, no material residual basis); a "comfort letter" without legal force delivers economic comfort but no capital relief, however reassuring it sounds.

The recurring failure mode of insurance-based transfer is insurer concentration and credit. AIG in 2008 was the canonical example: a single counterparty had written so much protection that, when called on it, it could not pay, and the public sector absorbed the loss. The grandmaster's heuristic applies again: an insured exposure is at least three risks (the original, the basis, and the insurer's credit).

Risk reduction

Taking action to lower the probability or impact of a risk that the bank is taking. In a bank context, the most important reduction tools are the limit framework that operationalises the appetite, underwriting discipline at origination, the covenants that travel with the loan, collateral that protects loss given default, netting that compresses gross exposure to net, and bilateral margining for non-cleared derivatives. The shape of the toolkit is wider than it first appears, and most mature banks use all of these in combination.

Diversification and concentration limits

The oldest banking-risk mitigation is "don't put all your eggs in one basket". Diversification reduces the impact of any single default, sector downturn, or country shock by spreading exposure widely. The framework that operationalises diversification is the limit structure, set by the board through the risk appetite statement and enforced by the credit committee and the front-office limit system: single-name limits capping exposure to any one borrower or borrower group, sector limits reflecting the bank's view on correlated stress, country and sovereign limits, counterparty and trading limits combined with potential-future-exposure (PFE) measures, and large-exposure limits under CRR Article 395 (no exposure above 25% of Tier 1 capital to any single counterparty, with tighter limits for G-SIB-to-G-SIB exposures).

Limit breach is a hard control: a transaction that would exceed an applicable limit cannot be booked without escalation. Diversification has a known failure mode, namely that in a systemic event correlations rise toward one and apparently diversified portfolios concentrate. This is precisely what credit concentration stress testing exists to surface.

Loan underwriting at origination

Best-practice credit risk discipline: prevention is better than cure. New loans must be sanctioned only within the risk appetite statement and the risk-weighted capital criteria. The process differs by segment.

In corporate and wholesale banking, a credit request is submitted by the relationship banker to a separate body for sanction: a credit committee, or in smaller banks the executive management plus ALCO. The request package contains all the information the committee needs to evaluate the risk-return trade-off, the proposed terms, the customer's standing, and the strategic fit. Separation of relationship and credit decision is the equivalent of the lines-of-defence separation at deal level.

In retail banking, the volume of applications makes individual committee review impossible. The standard model uses automated decisioning via application scorecards (covered in detail in the credit risk lectures), with a referral process to a loan origination officer for borderline cases. The scorecard is the credit committee, codified.

Beyond the borrower's credit standing and the rate charged, the bank must evaluate the loan specifics: the legal framework and jurisdiction (governing law, enforceability), acceptable collateral and its enforceability, disclosure requirements, ability to terminate (material adverse change clauses, covenants), transferability (can the loan be sold or syndicated), currency, fixed or floating rate, and prepayment options. Most of these align directly with credit risk policies set in the appetite framework; some require legal input and committee escalation.

Know your customer (KYC) is integral to origination. For corporate and wholesale, relationship bankers maintain extensive dialogue with the customer to understand the business, the strategy, and the risks. For retail, the bank maintains behavioural scorecards and delinquency monitoring to spot trouble early and act before default. KYC sits at the intersection of credit, operational, conduct, and AML risk.

Loan covenants

Covenants are contractual undertakings by the borrower that travel with the loan. They give the bank a right to act before a default occurs, which is precisely why they sit just behind underwriting itself as a mitigation lever.

Family What it does Typical examples
Financial covenants Require the borrower to maintain specified ratios. Tested periodically, usually quarterly. Debt service coverage (DSCR), interest coverage (ICR), leverage (net debt to EBITDA), loan-to-value (LTV), minimum net worth.
Affirmative covenants Require the borrower to do specified things. Deliver audited accounts on time, maintain insurance, pay taxes, comply with laws, allow inspection.
Negative covenants Prohibit specified actions. Granting security to other creditors (the negative pledge), incurring further debt above a cap, paying dividends above a cap, disposing of material assets, changing the business.
Information covenants Require periodic disclosure. Management accounts, compliance certificates, notification of any material adverse event.
Material adverse change (MAC) clauses Give the bank the right to call default on a qualitative basis. Used rarely because the legal threshold is high and exercising the clause can itself crystallise the loss.

A covenant breach triggers a right to act, not an automatic default. The bank can demand cure, renegotiate terms, charge a waiver fee, increase pricing, demand additional collateral, or, in the most serious cases, accelerate the loan. The discipline lies in using covenant breaches as early warning signals of credit deterioration rather than waiting for missed payments. The mid-2010s saw a strong trend toward covenant-lite structures in leveraged finance, eliminating maintenance covenants in favour of incurrence-only tests; this is widely viewed as a weakening of mitigation in exchange for issuer flexibility.

Collateral and credit risk mitigation (CRM)

Beyond the underwriting decision, a bank's main risk-reduction lever is taking collateral. Collateral does not reduce the probability of default; it reduces the loss given default by giving the bank a claim on a specific asset.

The CRR (Articles 192 to 241, mirrored in the UK PRA rulebook) distinguishes two forms of recognised credit risk mitigation (CRM). Funded credit protection means the bank has direct control of a tangible asset; the eligible forms include cash deposits with the bank, government and corporate debt securities, gold, equities in main indices, certain UCITS funds, residential and commercial real estate, and trade receivables. Each carries its own eligibility tests and supervisory haircuts. Unfunded credit protection means a third party stands behind the obligation; the eligible forms are guarantees (covered above) and credit derivatives. The protection provider must itself be a "good" counterparty (sovereign, public-sector entity, regulated institution, or rated corporate above a threshold).

Two methods translate eligible CRM into capital relief. The financial collateral simple method substitutes the collateral's risk weight for the unsecured exposure's risk weight (subject to a 20% floor for most collateral). The financial collateral comprehensive method adjusts the exposure for the collateral's market value after applying supervisory or own-estimate haircuts, recognising volatility of both exposure and collateral and any currency or maturity mismatch.

CRM is recognised only if the legal and operational conditions hold. The collateral arrangement must be legally enforceable in all relevant jurisdictions, the bank must have the right to liquidate or retain the collateral on default, and (for financial collateral under the comprehensive method) the bank must mark to market the collateral at least every six months. A pledged asset whose enforcement is uncertain (a foreign-jurisdiction asset under unclear local law, an equity stake subject to political restriction) delivers economic comfort but no capital relief.

Netting

Netting compresses gross exposures into a smaller net exposure by offsetting amounts owed in both directions between the same parties. It does not transfer risk; it eliminates the gross exposure that should never have counted in the first place. Payment netting combines multiple same-day same-currency payments between two parties into a single net payment, reducing settlement risk. Close-out netting, on a counterparty default, terminates all outstanding transactions under the master agreement and replaces them with a single net obligation in one direction; this is the central function of the ISDA Master Agreement for derivatives, the GMRA for repo, and the GMSLA for securities lending. Cross-product netting extends close-out netting across product types where supported by legal opinion. On-balance-sheet netting gets limited recognition under CRR for the netting of loans and deposits with the same counterparty under a legally enforceable netting agreement.

The economic effect is large. A bank with $10 billion gross derivative exposure to a counterparty might have $200 million net after enforceable close-out netting. Basel rules permit netting in exposure measurement (and therefore capital) only where the netting agreement is supported by a clean legal opinion in every relevant jurisdiction. The single largest legal-risk exposure for a derivatives desk is a netting opinion that turns out to be wrong in a particular jurisdiction during a default.

Margining for non-cleared derivatives

For OTC derivatives that are not cleared through a CCP, the post-2008 reforms introduced bilateral margining as a parallel mitigation layer. The contractual home is the ISDA Credit Support Annex (CSA), which sits under the ISDA Master Agreement. Under the CSA, parties post variation margin (VM) daily, reflecting mark-to-market moves, so that the exposure between margin calls is the move over a single day. Since 2017, initial margin (IM) has also been required between in-scope counterparties under the global uncleared margin rules (UMR), reflecting potential future exposure under a stressed move. IM must be segregated at a third-party custodian; VM transfers title to the receiving party.

In-scope counterparties are determined by aggregate average notional amount of non-cleared derivatives. The thresholds were phased in (Phase 1 captured firms above $3 trillion in 2016, down to Phase 6 capturing firms above $8 billion in 2022), now covering all major financial counterparties and many corporate hedgers. UMR is one of the largest operational changes the derivatives industry has absorbed in the past decade. The mitigation effect is to constrain the residual exposure on a non-cleared trade to a few days of mark-to-market move, comparable to the position with a CCP. Margining complements but does not replace the basis-risk discipline of the hedging desk: an effective hedge that drifts in basis still loses value, regardless of who is posting collateral.

Management actions on the existing portfolio

The book is not static. As loans mature, the bank can adjust new business policy in response to portfolio composition: freeze certain sectors, reduce others, tighten standards (higher spread, higher fees, more collateral, tighter covenants). Counter-cyclical positioning is the more sophisticated version: deliberately growing exposure to sectors expected to outperform in the next phase of the cycle (consumer staples in a downturn, capital goods in a recovery). This is portfolio management as mitigation, and is one of the most important levers the credit committee has between the origination cycle and the regulatory capital cycle.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Every transfer tool has a counterparty

The recurring failure mode of risk transfer is that the third party stops paying just when the bank most needs them to. AIG sold credit protection on $441 billion of structured credit by mid-2008. When the structured credit fell, AIG could not post the collateral on its own CDS contracts, and the US Treasury stepped in to prevent a cascade. The same dynamic applies to monoline insurers (Ambac, MBIA), to interbank funding lines drawn at the wrong moment, and to derivative counterparties under stress. CVA (credit valuation adjustment), now a separate Pillar 1 charge in many jurisdictions, exists precisely to put a price on this risk. The grandmaster's heuristic: an unhedged exposure is one risk; a hedged exposure is at least two (the original plus the basis plus the counterparty).

The systemic backstop when transfer chains fail at scale is the resolution framework: TLAC at the G-SIB level and MREL for resolution entities more broadly, set aside specifically to be bailed in before public money is called on. Mitigation that relies on a counterparty assumes that counterparty's loss-absorbing capacity is in place; a bank that holds CDS protection bought from a globally systemic dealer is, in effect, relying on TLAC and the orderly-resolution regime as the last layer of mitigation behind its hedge.

Basis risk is the silent killer

Most "we are hedged" claims in banks are hedges of the gross position with residual basis risk that is rarely reported separately. The JIBAR-prime example above is the simple case. The harder cases include LIBOR transition residuals (banks that hedged USD LIBOR exposure with SOFR-referenced swaps carry the LIBOR-SOFR basis), cross-currency basis (the cost of swapping currencies has its own term structure and moves with funding stress), and floor risk (interest rate floors in retail products that activate when policy rates fall, asymmetrically with respect to most hedges). The disciplined risk report identifies each basis explicitly and gives it its own risk limit.

The originate-to-distribute lesson

The 2008 securitisation collapse showed what happens when transfer instruments are used to dilute origination discipline. The mortgage broker who knows the loan will be sold within 90 days has no incentive to verify income. The originator who knows the loan will be securitised within 6 months has weaker incentive to underwrite. The structurer who packages 5,000 loans into an ABS has no view on any individual loan. Each link is rational, each is paid its fee, the chain delivers a security that nobody understands fully. The regulatory response (5% risk retention, transparency, due diligence) addresses the dilution mechanically; the cultural lesson is that transfer must complement, not replace, underwriting.

Significant risk transfer and regulatory arbitrage

Modern synthetic securitisations are often structured specifically to achieve significant risk transfer (SRT) under the CRR. The bank sells the mezzanine tranche of credit risk on a portfolio (often to specialist funds), retains the senior and equity, and qualifies for capital relief on the bulk of the portfolio. Done well, SRT is genuine risk transfer that frees capital for new lending. Done badly, it is regulatory arbitrage that books the capital relief without meaningfully shifting the risk. Supervisors now scrutinise SRT structures closely; the test is whether the bank's economic exposure has materially fallen, not whether the legal documents say it has.

Underwriting is the cheapest mitigation

The most uncomfortable truth in this lecture: the cheapest tool in the toolkit is the one used first. Loans that should not be written are zero cost to mitigate, infinite cost to remediate. Loans that are written carefully require less hedging, less collateral, less monitoring, and less capital. Every mature credit-led bank carries on the wall, in some form, a version of "the time to fix a bad loan is before it is written". The hedging desk does not save a portfolio that the credit desk should not have built.

Securitisation done well

Covered bonds (a European tradition predating modern securitisation) are the counter-example to the originate-to-distribute critique. The originator stays on the hook for the underlying loans (they remain on balance sheet); the covered bond is over-collateralised by a ring-fenced pool; investors have dual recourse to the issuer and the pool. The product has performed extremely well through every crisis since the 1900s. The lesson is that secure transfer plus retained skin in the game is the durable structure. Plain securitisation that fully removes the originator's economic interest is the structure that breaks under stress.

  • The market risk lectures cover FRTB and the SBM method, which determine capital for the trading book where most hedging activity sits.
  • TLAC and MREL: loss-absorbing capacity is the systemic backstop behind every transfer chain when the counterparties to bilateral protection fail at scale.
  • IFRS 9: hedge accounting and ECL provisioning sit alongside mitigation in the bank's financial reporting.

Further reading:

  • BCBS / IOSCO, "Criteria for identifying simple, transparent and comparable securitisations" (2015) and the equivalent EU regulation (Regulation (EU) 2017/2402). The reference documents for STS / STC.
  • ISDA Master Agreement (1992 and 2002 versions). The legal backbone of every bilateral derivative contract a bank trades.
  • Hull, "Options, Futures and Other Derivatives" (any recent edition). The standard reference for instrument mechanics.
  • FCIC, "The Financial Crisis Inquiry Report" (2011). The official US post-mortem on how the transfer machine broke in 2007 to 2008; chapters on AIG and on the rating agencies are particularly relevant.
  • EBA, "Report on Significant Risk Transfer in Securitisations" (annual updates). The supervisor's current view on how SRT is being tested and where it is being structured around.
