SREP

The bank's ICAAP makes the case. The supervisor's SREP decides what to do about it. The output is a single binding decision that sets the bank's capital floor, its liquidity floor, and the supervisory intensity for the year ahead.

Lecture 14 of Phase 1 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: docs/wiki/application/banking/01_internal_environment/risk_management/08-srep.md
Step 1

The 30-second version

The Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process is the supervisor's structured annual assessment of whether a bank's capital and liquidity are adequate for its risk profile, whether its business model is viable, and whether its governance and controls are sound. The output is a binding supervisory decision that sets capital and liquidity requirements, prescribes management actions, and determines the supervisory intensity that follows.

SREP has four elements: business model analysis (is the bank making money sustainably?), governance and internal control (do the people running it have the framework to do so safely?), risks to capital (is capital enough for the risks taken?), and risks to liquidity (can the bank fund itself through stress?). Each element is scored on a one-to-four scale, the scores combine into an overall SREP score, and the capital arm of the decision produces two outputs: the Pillar 2 Requirement (P2R, binding) and the Pillar 2 Guidance (P2G, expected). The two are mechanically different: P2R breach is regulatory non-compliance, P2G breach is a soft trigger for heightened supervision.

Feynman test Think of SREP as the bank's annual MOT inspection, but for a vehicle worth a hundred billion that carries the public's deposits. The mechanic (the supervisor) tests four systems: the engine (business model), the steering and brakes (governance), the load capacity (capital), and the fuel system (liquidity). Each system gets a score. If any of them is below standard, the mechanic can require repairs (capital add-ons, management changes, new processes), restrict the load (limits on growth, distributions), or refuse to certify the vehicle for road use (early intervention). The bank cannot opt out of the inspection. It can only prepare for it.
Step 2

Why this matters

Pillar 2 was Basel II's explicit recognition that Pillar 1 alone was insufficient. The prescribed regulatory floor covers a defined list of risks (credit, market, operational) under prescribed methodologies; it cannot, by design, capture every risk a bank carries or every weakness in how the bank manages those risks. Pillar 2 gave supervisors the discretion to require more, and SREP is the process by which that discretion is exercised in a structured, comparable, and challengeable way.

The 2007 to 2008 financial crisis demonstrated that meeting Pillar 1 was not the same as being safe. Northern Rock had a healthy Tier 1 ratio when it failed in 2007; its funding profile, its governance, and its business model were the problem, none of which Pillar 1 measured. Bear Stearns had one of the highest regulatory capital ratios in its peer group in 2008; its dependence on overnight repo funding was the problem. Lehman Brothers reported regulatory capital ratios above the minimum days before its bankruptcy. The post-crisis response was to harmonise and intensify the supervisory review, and the EBA's SREP guidelines (originally 2014, revised substantially in 2022) are the structural output of that lesson.

For the bank, the practical importance of SREP is that it converts the supervisor's view into binding consequences. A bank that scores well receives lower P2R, lighter supervisory engagement, and more credit for its own internal models. A bank that scores poorly receives higher P2R, more frequent on-site inspections, mandatory management actions, and in serious cases the activation of early intervention measures. The cost difference between a strong SREP and a weak one, measured across capital, funding cost, supervisory resource demand, and management time, runs into hundreds of basis points of return on equity for a typical bank. The SREP is the supervisory decision the bank most needs to get right.

Step 3

The mental model

The single most useful visual for SREP is the flow that links the four assessment elements through the SREP score to the binding capital outputs. The four elements are graded separately, combine into an overall view, and produce the binding (TSCR), expected (P2G), and management-action consequences that follow.

Step 4

The detail

The regulatory anchor

SREP is anchored in Articles 97 to 101 of CRD V in the EU and the equivalent rules in the PRA rulebook in the UK. The standard methodology is set out in the EBA Guidelines on common procedures and methodologies for SREP, revised substantially in 2022. The SARB in South Africa applies the SREP framework via Banks Act Directives, producing an Individual Capital Requirement equivalent to the EU's P2R. The Federal Reserve's analogue for large US banks is the Comprehensive Capital Analysis and Review (CCAR) and the Dodd-Frank Act Stress Test (DFAST), with a more prescriptive scenario set than the EBA template. The convergence is real but not complete; the depth and the language differ across jurisdictions while the underlying logic does not.

The four elements in detail

Element What the supervisor assesses ICAAP / ILAAP chapter that supports it
Business model analysis Viability and sustainability of the business model: profit trajectory, strategic plan, competitive position, cost-to-income ratio, ability to earn the cost of capital through a cycle. ICAAP chapter 1 (business model and strategy).
Governance and internal controls Board effectiveness, three-lines-of-defence operation, risk and compliance frameworks, BCBS 239 data quality, risk culture, remuneration alignment, ability to identify and manage risks. ICAAP chapter 3 (governance) and the board challenge evidence.
Risks to capital Credit, market, operational, IRRBB, concentration, model risk, business risk, pension risk. Each scored individually; the composite feeds the capital decision. ICAAP chapters 4 (MIRA) and 5 (capital assessment).
Risks to liquidity and funding LCR, NSFR, intraday liquidity, funding diversification, structural funding profile, ability to withstand stress. ILAAP, cross-referenced from ICAAP chapter 7.

Each element is scored on the same four-point scale (1 = low risk to viability, 4 = high risk). Individual risks inside an element are scored on the same scale. The overall SREP score is not a simple average; it is a holistic supervisory judgement that weights toward the weakest element, because a bank with strong capital but weak governance is materially different from one with strong capital and strong governance.

The capital outputs: P2R, P2G, TSCR, OCR

The capital arm of the SREP decision produces two numbers. Pillar 2 Requirement (P2R) is a binding additional capital requirement set on top of Pillar 1 to cover risks not adequately captured by Pillar 1. Pillar 2 Guidance (P2G) is a non-binding supervisory expectation set above the combined buffer requirement to ensure the bank can withstand a severe but plausible stress. The mechanical difference between the two is the most commonly confused point in the framework, and the consequences of confusion are material.

Composite Definition Function and consequence of breach
TSCR Total SREP Capital Requirement = Pillar 1 + P2R Binding minimum. Breach triggers prompt corrective action; bank is in regulatory non-compliance.
OCR Overall Capital Requirement = TSCR + Combined Buffer Requirement The MDA threshold. Breach triggers automatic restrictions on dividends, AT1 coupons, and variable remuneration.
OCR + P2G The full supervisory expectation, with P2G sitting on top of OCR Non-binding. Breach signals to the supervisor that the bank is operating below the expected stress buffer; heightened supervision follows, and informal distribution restrictions may be applied.
Available CET1 What the bank actually holds Must sit comfortably above OCR + P2G in normal conditions; the cushion is the management buffer.
A common trap The MDA trigger is the OCR, not OCR + P2G. A bank whose CET1 falls into the P2G band is not in MDA; it is in a soft-trigger zone where the supervisor expects remediation and may apply informal restrictions, but automatic distribution restrictions only kick in when CET1 falls into the combined buffer requirement. Confusing the two leads to misallocation of management buffer and to communications missteps in stress.

The supervisory toolbox beyond capital

The SREP decision can reach further than capital. The EBA SREP guidelines list four categories of supervisory measure, and the supervisor combines them as the assessment requires.

Tool What it is When the supervisor uses it
Capital measures P2R, P2G, capital-quality requirements (e.g. more CET1), informal distribution restrictions, macroprudential overlays. Standard tools for any material capital-related weakness. The first lever the supervisor pulls.
Liquidity measures Bank-specific LCR or NSFR add-ons, HQLA composition requirements, intraday limits, funding mix restrictions. Where the ILAAP indicates the bank's liquidity profile is inadequate for its risk profile.
Other supervisory measures Governance changes, board composition, senior management replacement, remuneration adjustments, data quality remediation, model fixes, business-line divestment, growth limits. For weaknesses that capital alone cannot remediate. The most discretionary category.
Early intervention measures Mandatory activation of recovery plan actions, appointment of a temporary administrator, preparation for resolution. Where the bank is approaching the conditions for resolution. Used rarely; their existence is the credible threat that backs the rest of the decision.

Frequency and proportionality

SREP frequency scales with the EBA's four supervisory categories. Category 1 banks (G-SIIs, O-SIIs, large internationally active institutions) receive an annual SREP with continuous engagement and regular on-site inspections. Category 4 banks (small, domestic, simple) receive a SREP on a three-year cycle with a lighter methodology. The methodology is the same; the depth and frequency are not. The category itself is reviewed annually, and movement between categories follows growth, complexity changes, or systemic designation.

Joint decisions for cross-border banks

For cross-border banking groups, the consolidating supervisor coordinates a joint decision with the host supervisors of material subsidiaries. The joint decision covers capital and liquidity requirements at both consolidated and subsidiary level, and it has to be reached within four months of the consolidated SREP decision. Where supervisors cannot agree, the EBA mediates and can impose a binding decision. The standard friction points are the allocation of capital between consolidated and subsidiary levels, the treatment of intragroup exposures, and the host supervisor's right to ring-fence local capital and liquidity. The framework sits under Article 113 of CRD V.

Dialogue, evidence, and the SREP cycle

The SREP is not a single event. It is a year-round dialogue that culminates in the formal decision. The supervisor reads the ICAAP, attends board meetings, conducts on-site inspections, reviews regulatory returns (FINREP, COREP), benchmarks against peers, and tracks actual capital and risk metrics throughout the year. The annual SREP decision is the formal output of this continuous engagement, not a standalone review. The strongest banks make the evidence base coherent: same numbers in the same places, same scenarios driving ICAAP and ILAAP, same management actions in the recovery plan and the capital contingency plan.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

P2R is sticky once set

The P2R number is hard to move down once the supervisor has anchored on it. The reasons are political as much as methodological: a supervisor reducing a bank's P2R has to explain why the previous number was too high, which is rarely a comfortable conversation internally. The strongest banks therefore put their effort into the first P2R determination, not into subsequent challenges. The same effort, applied two years earlier, would have saved several quarters of capital cost. The discipline is to over-invest in the SREP cycle in which the P2R is being set or reviewed, and to do the work that supports a lower starting number rather than relying on a later renegotiation.

Peer benchmarking is the silent driver

Supervisors benchmark every SREP output against peer institutions in the same category. A bank whose P2R sits well below the peer median is signalling either an exceptionally strong risk profile or a methodology that has been calibrated to flatter the bank. The supervisor will probe the second possibility before accepting the first. The implication is that knowing where the peer median sits, and being able to explain the bank's position relative to it, is one of the most valuable pieces of intelligence the CFO and CRO can have going into the SREP cycle. This intelligence is not formally published; it is gleaned from supervisory dialogue, from public disclosures, and from informed peer conversations.

The scoring optics matter

An overall SREP score of 3 attracts a materially different supervisory programme from a score of 2. The difference can be a single weakness in a single element that the supervisor decides is severe enough to drive the composite. The strongest banks pay attention to which element is closest to a score-boundary and invest in remediation specifically targeted at moving that element's score down. A pure capital response to a governance finding is the most common form of misallocation; the score does not improve because the underlying weakness has not been addressed.

The four elements are not independent

A business-model finding often produces a capital outcome (the supervisor adds capital because the business model is uncertain). A governance finding often produces a liquidity outcome (the supervisor restricts funding mix because it does not trust the bank's stress management). Treating each element as a separate cost centre misses the cross-pollination. The strongest banks understand that an investment in governance can reduce P2R, an investment in business-model coherence can reduce P2G, and an investment in data quality can reduce both. The CFO who treats the SREP as a capital problem is solving the wrong optimisation.

Remediation is evidence

The most valuable thing a bank can demonstrate in a SREP cycle is timely, complete remediation of findings from the previous cycle. A supervisor who sees prior findings closed, with documented evidence, is in a different conversation from one who has to re-raise the same finding. The strongest banks treat the SREP findings register as a Tier 1 management priority, with named owners, board-level visibility, and integration into the bank's broader operational risk programme. The cost of remediation is almost always less than the cost of a re-issued finding with an escalated severity.

The joint decision creates information asymmetry

For cross-border groups, the joint decision between home and host supervisors can produce subsidiary-level requirements that differ materially from the consolidated view. A bank that engages early with host supervisors, before the consolidated SREP cycle concludes, can shape this conversation. A bank that waits for the joint decision to surface inconsistencies has to remediate after the fact. The implication is that the SREP is not really a single cycle; for cross-border banks it is several cycles run in parallel, each requiring its own management and its own supervisory relationship.

Communicating the SREP outcome is its own discipline

The SREP decision is confidential, but its consequences appear in the bank's capital ratios, in the requirements published in supervisory disclosures, and in the bank's strategic constraints. The market reads these signals carefully. A bank whose P2R rises materially without explanation invites speculation about underlying weaknesses; a bank that explains the move in advance, references the supervisor's specific concerns, and articulates a credible remediation plan retains market confidence even when the headline number moves against it. The communications strategy around the SREP outcome is a treasury and investor-relations exercise, not a risk function exercise, and the strongest banks plan it well before the decision letter arrives.

Step 6

Test yourself

  1. State the four SREP elements and explain what each one assesses.
  2. Describe the SREP scoring scale and explain why the overall score is not a simple average of the element scores.
  3. Define P2R and P2G. What is the mechanical difference between a breach of each one?
  4. Write down the definitions of TSCR and OCR. At which level does an MDA trigger fire?
  5. Name the four categories of supervisory measure in the EBA SREP toolbox and give one example from each.
  6. How does SREP frequency vary across the four supervisory categories, and what determines a bank's category?
  7. What is a joint decision for a cross-border banking group? Within what time frame must it be reached, and what happens if supervisors cannot agree?
  8. Explain why peer benchmarking is described as the "silent driver" of the SREP outcome.
  9. Why is the first P2R determination more important than subsequent reviews of the same number?
  10. Describe the difference between a capital response and a governance response to a SREP finding. Why does the distinction matter?
  11. Compare the EBA SREP, the Federal Reserve's CCAR, and the PRA's approach in terms of methodology prescriptiveness and supervisory discretion.
  12. What does the supervisor look for in the bank's remediation of prior-cycle findings, and why does timely closure matter?
