Lectures / Banking / Internal environment

Products

The catalogue of what a bank sells, organised the way an actuary in a central function should hold it in their head. Every product is a variant of one of three things, sold into one of three segments.

Lecture 5 of Phase 1 Reading time, 55 minutes Source: docs/wiki/application/banking/01_internal_environment/05-products.md
Step 1

The 30-second version

A bank sells three services: saving (we hold your money safely), borrowing (we lend you money), and transacting (we move money around). It sells those three services to three segments: retail (individuals and small businesses), corporate (mid-to-large companies), and investment (other financial institutions and the largest corporates). That gives a three-by-three grid. Every product in this lecture sits in one cell of the grid, with industry-specific adaptations.

You will not become an expert in any single product from this lecture. The goal is breadth: enough familiarity with each product that you understand the opportunities and risks when it shows up on a balance sheet, in a regulatory return, or in a stress-test scenario. For central-function actuaries (risk, finance, strategy, IT) this breadth is the job.

Feynman test Walk down your high street. The retail bank serves the cafe owner (small business loan, current account, card terminal) and the cafe's customers (current account, mortgage, credit card, savings). Behind the cafe, the bank funds the building it sits in (commercial real estate loan) and the supplier that delivers coffee (trade finance). The supplier's parent company is listed on the JSE; its IPO was arranged by an investment bank, its currency hedges sit on that same bank's derivatives book, and its bonds appear in pension portfolios run by other clients of the same bank group. The same three primitive services, repeated and reshaped at every layer.
Step 2

Why this matters

The product mix is the bank. A retail-and-corporate bank like Lloyds looks fundamentally different from a universal bank like JPMorgan, which looks fundamentally different from a pure investment bank like Morgan Stanley. Each business line carries different credit risk, market risk, operational risk, funding profile, and regulatory capital treatment. If you cannot quickly classify what a product is and what risks it brings, you cannot read any bank's annual report properly.

Most spectacular bank failures trace back to a specific product line. Northern Rock died on its wholesale-funded mortgage book. RBS died on its leveraged-loan book and the ABN Amro acquisition. Bear Stearns died on its mortgage-backed securities. AIG died on its credit default swap portfolio. Credit Suisse died on Archegos total return swaps and Greensill supply-chain financing. Knowing the products is knowing the failure modes.

Step 3

The mental model

The three-by-three product grid. Three customer segments across the top, three services down the side. Each cell is populated by a different family of products, but the underlying activity is the same.

Two extras sit outside the grid. Bancassurance products (life insurance, credit protection) where the bank cross-sells insurance to its retail customers. Advisory services (M&A, IPO, bond issuance) which generate fees but do not put anything on the bank's balance sheet.

Step 4

The detail

Retail banking products

Retail banking serves individuals and small businesses. Basel sets four tests for "regulatory retail" classification, which matters because retail exposures attract lower capital weights than corporate ones.

Orientation
Customer is an individual or SME, not a mid or large corporate.
Product type
Revolving credit, personal term loans / leases, small-business facilities, residential mortgages.
Granularity
The portfolio is large and diversified enough that one borrower's risk does not dominate. No single loan can be substantially riskier than the rest.
Maximum individual exposure
Total exposure to any single client cannot exceed 1 million EUR.

Retail products have become commoditised. Competition focuses on price, rewards programmes, and digital channels. Product innovation is rapidly copied, so it rarely creates sustainable advantage. The real moat is the deposit franchise.

Current accounts

The anchor product. Bundles payments, short-term saving, and overdraft borrowing in one account. The current-account holder is the source of cross-sell for everything else: mortgages, credit cards, insurance, savings products. UK banks have historically run "free if in credit" current accounts, earning income from overdraft interest and fees rather than monthly fees. SA and US banks more commonly charge monthly fees.

Three payment mechanisms live inside the current account: cash (branches, ATMs, supermarkets), cards (chip and PIN, contactless, online), direct payments (debit orders / direct debits initiated by the recipient's bank; standing orders initiated by the customer's bank). Cheques persist but are in long-term decline.

An overdraft is a credit facility attached to a current account that lets the balance go negative up to a pre-agreed limit. It is revolving credit: as the customer pays money in, the available headroom refreshes, and interest is charged daily on whatever balance is in the red.

Overdrafts come in two flavours:

Authorised overdraft
Pre-agreed limit. Customer can spend up to the limit. Bank charges interest plus possibly arrangement / maintenance fees. Creates both credit risk and liquidity risk for the bank, since customers can draw the unused portion at any time.
Unauthorised overdraft
No pre-agreed limit. Bank holds a "shadow limit" internally. Historically generated large fee income, often from vulnerable customers. UK regulators capped unauthorised overdraft charges in 2020 after years of regulatory pressure.

Current accounts create operational risks beyond credit and liquidity. KYC / AML failures can produce huge fines (HSBC paid 1.9bn USD in 2012; NatWest 265m GBP in 2021). Legacy IT systems create operational risk when banks add new digital services (TSB's 2018 IT migration disaster). Treating Customers Fairly rules can lead to retroactive consumer redress (PPI, overdraft fees). And three types of fraud: first-party (customer opens an account intending not to repay), second-party (customer lets someone else use their account, often for money laundering), and third-party (account takeover, increasingly defended via two-factor authentication).

The competitive dynamic is shifting. Open Banking (UK, since 2018) and the EU's PSD2 directive let customers grant third parties access to their account data via Open APIs. This reduces the barrier to entry: a new fintech does not need to offer a current account itself, it just needs access to customers' existing ones.

Credit cards

A credit card is a payment instrument plus short-term borrowing facility plus rewards programme bundled together. The borrowing is revolving credit: the line of credit refreshes as old debt is repaid. The economics are driven by which behavioural type the customer falls into.

Contrast this with the three-party scheme used by American Express, Diners and Discover. Here there is no separate issuer and acquirer to talk about: one single entity plays both roles. That entity is itself a regulated bank (Amex is a US bank holding company; Discover, which owns Diners, is too). So the "are issuer and acquirer banks" question doesn't quite apply: the only financial institution in the picture is the scheme operator, and it is a bank.

The retailer pays an interchange fee on every transaction. Strictly, "interchange" is the inter-bank fee paid by the acquirer to the issuer in the 4-party scheme; the merchant-facing fee that bundles it all together is the Merchant Service Charge (MSC). The MSC is split between the issuer (via interchange), the acquirer (markup) and the scheme itself (scheme fee). EU and UK regulators have capped consumer-credit interchange at 0.30% under the Interchange Fee Regulation (IFR), complaining that merchants paid too much. In a 3-party scheme there is no interchange to cap, which is part of why Amex MSCs tend to run higher and why some retailers refuse Amex.

The three customer types and their economics:

TypeBehaviourBank earnsRisk
TransactorPays full balance every monthInterchange fees onlyLow credit risk; may not cover costs for low spenders
RevolverCarries balance month to month, often pays minimumInterest (high rate) plus interchangeHighest credit risk, especially in recessions
Spend revolverSpends and carries some balanceInterest plus full interchangeThe profit centre. Active spender, makes payments

Savings accounts

Four product types arranged by depositor flexibility and bank value.

Instant access savings
Deposit and withdraw at any time. Most flexible for customer; least valuable for bank because they look like 30-day stress outflows under LCR. Often "front-book" priced (new money gets a higher rate for an introductory period).
Notice accounts
Customer must give 30, 60 or 90 days' notice to withdraw. Pays higher interest because outside the LCR's 30-day stress window. Bank does not need to hold HQLA against the deposit (or holds less).
Term deposits
Fixed-period commitment (1, 3, 5 years). Fixed rate. Most valuable to bank because contractually locked in. Usually upward sloping with the yield curve.
Money market mutual funds
Not a bank product but a competing alternative. Unit-linked fund investing in short-term debt, units priced at par (usually 1 USD). The Reserve Primary Fund "broke the buck" in 2008 after its Lehman holdings collapsed, triggering a US-Treasury-backed industry rescue.

Personal loans

Unsecured fixed-term lending. Typical maturity 1 to 7 years; fixed interest rate; possible early repayment penalty. Banks use risk-based pricing: a customer too risky for a 7% loan might be profitable at 15%. Common use: consolidating expensive credit-card debt into a structured repayment plan.

Regulation pushes back on the headline-rate problem. In the UK, banks can only advertise a "representative APR" if at least 51% of approved borrowers actually receive that rate or lower. SA's National Credit Act caps the maximum rate that can be charged to retail customers.

Vehicle finance

Loans secured against the vehicle. Three main structures.

Standard vehicle finance
Monthly repayments over 12 to 84 months. Title transfers when the loan completes. Sometimes structured with a balloon payment at the end to lower monthlies.
PCP / GFV (Personal Contract Purchase / Guaranteed Future Value)
The customer leases the car for the period. At the end, three options: pay the agreed "guaranteed future value" to keep the car; hand the car back; or refinance. Common for new cars. The bank carries residual-value risk: if used-car values fall below the GFV, the bank loses on returned vehicles. Mitigated by mileage limits (high-mileage contracts have higher monthlies because expected residual is lower).
Hire purchase
Customer uses the car but does not own it until the final payment. Cannot sell without lender consent. Early termination returns the car to the lender, with possible additional payment.

Mortgages

Loans secured against the property. The single biggest exposure on most retail-bank balance sheets. Three flavours by purpose: residential (owner-occupied), buy-to-let (rented out), commercial real estate (offices, shops, warehouses, covered later under corporate banking). Two flavours by rate: fixed (rate locked for a period, often 2 / 5 / 10 years) or variable (tracks the bank's standard variable rate or a benchmark).

Three risk dimensions matter most:

  • Probability of default: based on the borrower's income, employment, debt-to-income ratio, and stress-tested affordability if rates rise.
  • Loss given default: driven by loan-to-value (LTV). At 70% LTV, the property price needs to fall by 30% before the bank takes a loss on disposal. UK and SA mortgage lending typically caps LTV at 90 to 95%; lower-LTV loans get better rates.
  • Correlation: in a downturn, rising unemployment and falling house prices reinforce each other. PD rises just as LGD does. This is why mortgage portfolios stress so non-linearly.

The US mortgage market works differently. Government-sponsored entities (Freddie Mac, Fannie Mae) buy mortgages from originating banks, package them into mortgage-backed securities, and sell them to investors. This "originate to distribute" model fuelled the sub-prime CDO bubble that triggered the 2008 crisis. Japan's "lost decade" started with a similar property bubble in the late 1980s.

Credit insurance (PPI)

Insurance that continues to repay a loan if the borrower cannot, due to illness, accident, redundancy or death. In SA, credit insurance must cover at least 12 months of unemployment. In the UK, the product was historically known as Payment Protection Insurance (PPI) and was widely bundled with loans, often without proper consumer consent. The cleanup cost UK banks over 50 billion GBP in customer refunds, making it the largest consumer financial-services scandal in UK history.

Corporate banking products

Corporate banking serves mid-sized and large companies. The same three services (transact, save, borrow) reappear but with adaptations for scale, complexity, and the corporate treasury function on the other side. Customers usually deal with a named relationship manager based in a business centre rather than a high-street branch.

Corporate payments and FX

Large corporates need direct terminal access to payment systems (SWIFT for cross-border, domestic real-time payment networks). Banks may discount or even waive payment fees as part of the broader relationship economics. Corporate cards let employees pay for business expenses and separate them from personal spending.

International trade creates multi-currency needs. Corporate FX desks offer spot conversion (typically much tighter bid-offer than retail), forward contracts to lock in future exchange rates, and FX swaps. Corporate accounts in multiple currencies simplify the operational cash management.

Corporate deposits and loans

Corporate deposits are less attractive to banks than retail deposits because they are less sticky. Corporate treasurers are price-sensitive and multi-banked. Under stress, corporate deposits flee quickly. This is why retail deposit franchises (Lloyds, Barclays UK, Standard Bank SA) trade at higher multiples than wholesale-funded banks.

Corporate loans are mostly variable rate, priced as a spread over a benchmark. The benchmarks themselves have changed:

  • Before 2021: LIBOR was the dominant benchmark. Calculated daily from banks' self-reported borrowing costs. After the manipulation scandal in 2012, regulators decided to retire it.
  • After 2021: risk-free overnight rates have replaced LIBOR. SONIA in the UK (Bank of England), SOFR in the US (NY Fed), ESTR in the EU (ECB), ZARONIA in SA (SARB). These are transaction-based, not survey-based, so much harder to manipulate.

Banks reduce credit risk on corporate loans through covenants:

Negative covenants
Things the borrower must not do. Examples: not exceed a stated debt-to-income ratio, not take on additional debt without consent, not pay dividends above a threshold.
Positive covenants
Things the borrower must do. Examples: provide audited accounts quarterly, maintain insurance on pledged assets, comply with environmental regulations.

Large loan applications go to a credit committee; loans above a threshold need board approval.

Project finance

A specialised structure for large, long-dated, asset-heavy projects (power plants, toll roads, mines, airports). The lender's only recourse is to the project's own cash flows, not the parent company's balance sheet. This is achieved by lending into a special purpose vehicle (SPV).

The SPV is a separate legal entity whose sole purpose is to undertake the project. It contracts with builders, operators and offtakers. Lenders take security over the SPV's assets and cash flows. Contractors often provide performance and completion guarantees that the lender can call on.

Why companies use project finance rather than corporate borrowing: limited recourse (parent company's other businesses are insulated if the project fails), off-balance-sheet in some accounting treatments, and the ability to fund a single big project without disturbing the parent's existing capital structure.

Syndicated loans

A loan too large for one bank to make alone. A group of banks ("syndicate") collectively provides the loan, each taking a share. Concentration risk is shared; the borrower gets a larger facility than any single bank would extend.

One or more lead arrangers structure the deal and bring in other lenders. Two main commercial models:

Underwritten syndication
Lead arrangers guarantee the full amount to the borrower up front. If they cannot find enough other lenders, they keep the unsold portion on their own books. Higher fees for the arrangers; certainty for the borrower.
Best-efforts syndication
Lead arrangers commit only to use best efforts. The amount the borrower receives depends on how successful those efforts are. Lower fees; less certainty.
Club deal
A small syndicate of pre-agreed banks (no formal syndication process). Common for refinancings where the relationship banks are already known.

Commercial real estate (CRE) lending

Loans secured against commercial property (offices, retail, industrial, hotels). Almost always secured; usually with a maximum LTV. Specific covenants include maintaining property insurance and preventing material deterioration. The three product types map to different phases of the building's life.

Bridging finance
Short-term (3 months to 2 years), interest-only, used to fund refurbishment or to bridge until a sale or longer-term refinancing.
Development finance
Funds construction. Drawn down in tranches matching build phases. Interest-only until completion; capital repaid from sales or refinancing once the development is income-producing.
Investment finance
Long-term loan secured on an income-producing property. Like a residential mortgage but typically shorter (15 to 20 years) and may allow payment holidays during void periods.

Equipment leasing

Three structural variants, distinguished by who owns the asset at the end and who bears residual-value risk.

Finance lease
Effectively a secured loan. The customer uses the equipment, the bank holds title until the loan is repaid. Customer takes residual-value risk: at the end of the lease, the equipment is theirs. Usually three or more years.
Operational lease
Customer rents the equipment but never owns it. Bank takes residual-value risk: monthly charge must cover interest plus expected depreciation. If the asset depreciates faster than expected (used aircraft market in 2020, for instance), bank loses on resale. Common for aircraft, ships, rolling stock.
Contract hire / contract lease
A managed operational lease. The leasing company also handles maintenance, servicing, sometimes insurance. Common for fleet vehicles.

Trade finance

A family of short-term products that smooth international trade and cash-flow timing. The core problem: a buyer and seller in different countries do not trust each other. Trade finance interposes one or more banks to make the trade workable.

Three main instruments:

Letter of credit
The buyer's bank promises to pay the seller a defined amount when defined documents (bill of lading, inspection certificate) are presented. The seller's credit risk transfers from the buyer to the buyer's bank. Variants: commercial (bank pays directly), standby (bank pays only if buyer cannot), revolving (multiple uses up to a limit), confirmed (a second bank also guarantees).
Bill of exchange
A formal written promise to pay a specified amount on a specified date (a "time draft") or on demand (a "sight draft"). Can be issued by banks or by companies. Crucial feature: transferable, so it can be discounted (sold to a third party at a discount to face value) to give the seller cash earlier and offload counterparty risk.
Trade loan
Short-term revolving facility funding the gap between paying a supplier and being paid by customers. Drawdowns require invoice and shipping documents to protect the bank. Tenor typically 1 to 12 months.

Investment banking products

Investment banking serves the largest corporates, financial institutions, and governments. Two distinct activities: transactional services (markets, trading, securities) and advisory services (M&A, capital raising).

Derivatives

A derivative is a contract whose value depends on an underlying asset, rate, or index. Used to hedge, to speculate, and to offer risk-management services to corporate clients. The three families:

Swaps
Two parties agree to exchange cash flows on a notional principal for an agreed period. Interest rate swap (fixed against floating in one currency); currency swap (interest and / or principal in two currencies); credit default swap (buyer pays a periodic premium, seller pays out if a reference entity defaults).
Futures and forwards
Obligation to buy or sell an asset at an agreed price on an agreed date. Futures are exchange-traded and centrally cleared, so counterparty risk is mutualised. Forwards are OTC, so both parties have bilateral counterparty risk.
Options
Right but not obligation. A call gives the right to buy; a put gives the right to sell. European exercisable only at expiry; American exercisable at any time. Vanilla (exchange-traded) options have standard contracts; exotic (OTC) options can be customised heavily. Selling a naked call has theoretically unlimited downside.

Securities financing (repo)

A repurchase agreement is short-term borrowing collateralised by securities. One party sells a security (usually a high-quality government bond) and agrees to repurchase it at a slightly higher price on a future date. The difference is interest (the "repo rate"). Used for liquidity management, financing long securities positions, and as a primary central-bank monetary-policy tool.

Both OTC derivatives and repo transactions carry counterparty credit risk: the counterparty may default before settlement, leaving the surviving party to replace the position at a worse price. Post-2008 reforms (mandatory central clearing, initial and variation margin) have reduced this risk substantially.

Asset-backed securities

A bank takes a pool of loans, transfers them to an SPV, and the SPV issues bonds backed by the loan cash flows. Investors are repaid from interest and capital on the underlying loans. The underlying pool can be mortgages (MBS), credit card receivables, car loans, personal loans, or other receivables.

Risk is managed two ways. First, diversification: the pool contains thousands of loans, so individual defaults wash out. Second, tranching: the SPV issues different tiers of bonds, each absorbing losses in a defined order. From most junior to most senior:

The originating bank often retains all or part of the equity tranche, both because regulators require "skin in the game" (so the originator stays incentivised to underwrite well) and as a signal of confidence.

CDOs (collateralised debt obligations) generalise ABS by including not just loans but also bonds and even other ABS tranches as collateral. CDO² (CDO-squared) uses tranches of other CDOs as its underlying. CDO³ goes one level deeper still. Modelling the tail risk of these structures is mathematically demanding; in 2007 to 2008, the correlation assumptions baked into the models broke down completely, leading to losses on senior CDO² tranches that supposedly could not happen.

Advisory services

Investment banks earn fees by advising corporates and governments on deals. Four main activities:

M&A
The most visible (and historically most profitable) advisory work. Banks help buyers identify targets, value businesses, structure deals, negotiate price, and handle the "social" terms (head office location, key appointments, brand). They also help sellers run auctions and defend against hostile bids.
IPOs and follow-on offerings
Banks bring private companies to the public market. Their key role is price discovery: setting an IPO price that attracts investors while paying the seller fairly. They also handle prospectus preparation, regulatory submissions, and (often) underwriting. Fees are typically 3% to 7% of capital raised.
Bond issuance
Banks advise corporates and governments on issuing bonds: tenor, coupon, covenants, target investor base, rating-agency engagement. They typically also act as bookrunners, building the order book from investors. May underwrite the whole issue or syndicate the underwriting risk.
Leveraged buyouts
A private-equity buyer takes a listed company private using a high proportion of debt. Banks advise the PE buyer, provide acquisition financing, and (later) refinance the resulting debt stack.

Markowitz's modern portfolio theory (1952) gave investors a path to diversification through portfolios of single-business firms, removing the rationale for the old conglomerate model. The resulting decades of conglomerate breakups have generated a steady stream of M&A and IPO work for investment banks.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The product mix tells you the bank

A bank dominated by retail mortgages (Nationwide, Bradford and Bingley pre-2008) is fundamentally different from one dominated by corporate lending (HSBC's Asia book) or trading and advisory (Goldman Sachs). Each mix produces different ROEs in normal times, different stress losses in bad times, different funding profiles, and different regulatory capital intensities. The first question to ask of any bank balance sheet is "what is the product mix?" Everything else flows from that.

Cross-subsidy is everywhere and it is fragile

Free banking in the UK is funded by overdraft fees. Cheap mortgages are funded by expensive PPI cross-sells (until they were not). Discounted corporate loans are funded by FX bid-offer and advisory fees. Whenever a product looks cheap, look for the cross-subsidy paying for it. Regulators sometimes unbundle these (PPI redress, interchange caps, unauthorised-overdraft caps); when they do, the underlying business model needs to be rebuilt, often painfully.

Residual-value risk is the hidden tail in vehicle and equipment leasing

Banks running PCP / GFV books and operational-lease books are taking a directional bet on used-asset prices. 2020 to 2022 was a windfall for these books (used-car shortage drove prices to 130% of expectations). 2024 onwards has been the unwind as EV depreciation curves prove much steeper than ICE ones. Watch operational-lease books carefully in any aircraft, shipping, or vehicle-finance house.

The CDO² lesson is about model risk, not greed

The popular narrative on the 2008 crisis emphasises greed and reckless lending. The technical narrative is more uncomfortable: the rating-agency and bank models for senior CDO² tranches were based on correlation estimates from a benign period. When the housing market turned, correlations jumped, and the senior tranches that the models said could lose at most 1% lost 60%. The lesson is generic: any model whose output depends on tail-correlation assumptions is dangerous when those assumptions are extrapolated from the recent past.

Open Banking and PSD2 are slowly hollowing the franchise

For a century, the current account was the moat. The bank that held your payments held your trust, your data, and your cross-sell opportunities. Open Banking lets third parties access your account data with your consent, which means new entrants can pitch you mortgages, savings accounts, and insurance products without ever holding your current account. The competitive geography is still settling, but the incumbent advantage is smaller than it was ten years ago.

Common F107 traps

Watch out Four traps that recur on this material. (1) Confusing the four-party (Visa / Mastercard) and three-party (Amex) credit card schemes. (2) Forgetting that money market mutual funds are not bank deposits and are not protected by deposit insurance. The Reserve Primary Fund break-the-buck event is the canonical example. (3) Treating finance leases and operational leases as economically the same. They differ on who owns the asset at the end and who carries residual-value risk. (4) Confusing an ABS tranche's rating with its risk. The AAA on a CDO² senior tranche in 2007 did not mean what the same rating meant on a sovereign bond.

Test yourself

  1. State the four Basel tests for retail-exposure classification. Why does the classification matter?
  2. Compare authorised and unauthorised overdrafts. Why have regulators intervened on the latter?
  3. Describe the four-party credit card scheme. Who pays whom, and what does each party earn?
  4. What is the difference between an instant-access savings account, a notice account, and a term deposit in terms of value to the bank (LCR / NSFR treatment)?
  5. Explain residual-value risk in a PCP / GFV agreement and one way a bank mitigates it.
  6. In a project finance loan, why is the loan made to an SPV rather than to the project's parent company?
  7. Describe the standard ABS tranche structure. Which tranche is the originating bank likely to retain, and why?
  8. What was the technical (model-risk) failure that turned CDO² senior tranches from safe to disastrous in 2007 to 2008?
