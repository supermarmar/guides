Lectures / Banking / Internal environment

The business model

Why a bank is a deeply strange institution: it promises to give you your money back tomorrow while having lent it out for thirty years.

Lecture 1 of Phase 1 Reading time, 15 minutes Source: docs/wiki/application/banking/01_internal_environment/01-business_model.md
Step 1

The 30-second version

A bank is a clever middleman. Lots of people lend small amounts of money to the bank for a short time (we call this a "deposit"). The bank pools all that money together and lends it out in large chunks for a long time (we call this a "loan"). The bank pockets the difference in interest rates as profit, in exchange for taking on the awkward problem that the lenders might all want their money back before the borrowers are due to repay.

That single trick, called maturity transformation, is the heart of every other lecture in this course. Capital rules, liquidity rules, stress tests, deposit insurance, central-bank lending, every one of them exists because banks promise instant access to money that is, in reality, locked up in a thirty-year mortgage somewhere.

Feynman test Imagine ten friends each lend you 100 GBP that they want back whenever they ask. You take all 1,000 GBP and lend it to your cousin for a year. You make money on the deal because your cousin pays you interest. But if even three friends ask for their 100 GBP back tomorrow, you are in trouble. That is exactly what a bank does, every single day, on a much larger scale. Everything else flows from this.
Step 2

Why this matters

In March 2023, Silicon Valley Bank failed in less than 48 hours. It was the second-largest bank failure in US history. The bank was profitable, well-capitalised on paper, and held mostly US Treasury bonds, supposedly the safest assets in the world. So how did it die in two days?

Because the depositors (mostly tech startups with very large balances) demanded their money back faster than SVB could sell its long-dated Treasury bonds without taking a loss. The bonds were fine in the long run; the bank just did not live to see the long run. That is maturity transformation breaking. Once you understand the business model in this lecture, the entire SVB story reads as a textbook example, not a freak accident.

Conversely, the reason the F107 syllabus is built the way it is, with separate areas for capital, liquidity, credit risk, market risk, IRRBB, is that each of those is a different angle on managing the same fundamental tension: assets and liabilities do not behave the same way over time.

Step 3

The mental model

Hold this picture in your head whenever a banking exam question hits you. The bank sits in the middle of a flow: short-dated, on-demand money comes in from one side; long-dated, illiquid loans go out the other side. Two cushions sit between them, because the timing does not match.

Notice the asymmetry. The depositors are small, many and impatient. The borrowers are large, few and locked in. The bank is the only thing that makes the asymmetry workable, and it does so by holding two cushions: capital (so it can survive if some borrowers do not repay) and a liquidity buffer (so it can pay depositors who do not want to wait).

Step 4

The detail

Maturity transformation, defined precisely

Two activities happen in parallel:

Long lending
The bank advances money to customers for as long as they need it. Typical examples: a 25-year residential mortgage; a 7-year corporate term loan; a 15-year project-finance facility. These are long-dated assets on the bank's balance sheet.
Short borrowing
The bank accepts deposits that can mostly be withdrawn on demand or at very short notice. These are short-dated liabilities on the bank's balance sheet. The customer is effectively the lender; the bank is the borrower.

The bank's balance sheet is therefore structurally mismatched. Assets are long, liabilities are short. The bank assumes, every day, that it can either keep rolling its short funding or replace it with new funding. The moment that assumption fails, the model breaks.

Bank runs and the role of confidence

Were every depositor to demand their money back simultaneously (a bank run), the bank could not sell its long loans fast enough at fair value. It would either dump them at fire-sale prices (incurring losses) or simply fail to pay. Crucially, the trigger is not insolvency in the usual sense: a perfectly solvent bank, with assets worth more than its liabilities at maturity, can still fail because it cannot meet a sudden withdrawal demand. This is why liquidity risk and solvency risk are not the same thing, a distinction we return to repeatedly later in the course.

The structural vulnerability creates the demand for two non-negotiables.

Liquidity
Holding enough liquid assets and stable funding to meet withdrawal demands at all times. Measured in F107 by the Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) and Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR).
Capital
Holding enough loss-absorbing equity (and equity-like instruments) so that depositors are protected if some of the long loans go bad. Measured in F107 by CET1, AT1 and T2 ratios.

The second business: facilitating payments

Banks are also the plumbing of the payments system. Cards (Visa, Mastercard), domestic clearing systems, SWIFT for cross-border wire transfers, ATMs and increasingly real-time online payments all run through bank balance sheets. This service generates fee income (covered in the next lecture on financial management) and is the reason a typical retail customer keeps their current account at one bank for decades. It also creates operational and cyber risk that has nothing to do with credit or maturity transformation, which is why operational risk has its own Basel pillar.

Practitioner insight Payments are also where banks compete most fiercely now. A new entrant cannot easily replicate a deposit franchise, but it can build a payments app. Hence the rise of neobanks and fintechs, and the regulatory interest in open banking. None of this changes the underlying business model; it changes the unit economics of customer acquisition.

Investment banks, the asterisk

Not every institution called a "bank" operates the model above. Investment banks make most of their money from advising on transactions (M&A, capital markets), trading securities, and hedging or selling risk through derivatives rather than holding it on the balance sheet. They are less reliant on maturity transformation and more exposed to market risk and counterparty risk. Throughout F107, "bank" defaults to the retail / commercial type unless the question specifies otherwise.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The whole regulatory edifice traces back to this lecture

Look at the topics across F107: capital ratios, LCR, NSFR, stress testing, ICAAP, ILAAP, recovery and resolution planning, deposit insurance, lender of last resort. Every one of them is an answer to a single question: "given that banks promise instant access to money they have lent out long-term, how do we prevent the next 1933 / 2008 / 2023?" When you can frame any F107 exam question this way, you have the right scaffolding.

Diamond and Dybvig, in one sentence

The 1983 Diamond-Dybvig model formalises why maturity transformation is both economically valuable and fragile. Banks pool depositors so that, on average, only a small fraction need their money at any one time. This is welfare-enhancing. But if depositors collectively expect a run, the rational individual response is to run too, and the bank fails even though it was sound. The model is the foundation for deposit insurance schemes (which break the run incentive) and for the lender-of-last-resort role of central banks.

Moral hazard, the inevitable consequence

Deposit insurance solves the run problem but creates a different one: depositors stop policing the bank, so the bank can take more risk than depositors would otherwise allow. Risk-based deposit insurance premiums, prudential supervision, and capital ratios are all responses to that moral hazard. Expect F107 questions that ask you to connect these dots.

Common F107 trap

Watch out Candidates often confuse liquidity and capital. A bank with healthy CET1 ratios can still fail if it runs out of cash, and a bank with adequate liquidity can still fail if losses wipe out its capital. SVB had high regulatory capital ratios at the time it died. The answer in an exam is rarely "more capital"; sometimes it is "more liquid assets" or "a more stable deposit base". Read every liquidity question with that distinction primed.
Step 6

Test yourself

Try to answer each prompt out loud, in plain English, as if to a curious non-banker friend. Do not look back at the lecture until you have tried.

  1. In one paragraph, explain why a profitable, well-capitalised bank can still fail.
  2. Why is a 25-year mortgage difficult for a bank to fund using one-day deposits? What two cushions does the bank hold to make it work?
  3. What is the difference between a liquidity problem and a solvency problem? Give a one-sentence example of each.
  4. What would the banking system look like with no maturity transformation? Who would lose, who would gain, and would loans still exist?
  5. If deposit insurance solves the bank-run problem, why do regulators still require capital ratios?
