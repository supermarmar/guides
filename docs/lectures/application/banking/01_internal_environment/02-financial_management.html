Financial management

How to read a bank's annual report: balance sheet, income statement, and the ratios that matter.

Lecture 2 of Phase 1 Reading time, 25 minutes Source: docs/wiki/application/banking/01_internal_environment/02-financial_management.md
Step 1

The 30-second version

A bank's annual report has two main stories. The balance sheet is a snapshot: what the bank owns, what it owes, and the cushion in between. The income statement is a film: how much money came in, how much went out, and what was left for shareholders. A handful of ratios compress those two stories into numbers you can compare across banks and years.

Once you have lecture 1 in your head (banks borrow short and lend long), the accounts are easy: the balance sheet shows you the mismatch, the income statement shows you the spread, and the ratios tell you whether the bank is doing it well, safely and efficiently.

Feynman test Pretend a bank is a shop that buys apples from one customer (a deposit) and sells them to another (a loan). The balance sheet says how many apples are in the warehouse, how many it has promised to deliver, and how much money it would have left if every promise came due tomorrow. The income statement says how much it made selling apples over the last twelve months, minus the wages it paid its staff and the apples that rotted before they sold.
Step 2

Why this matters

Two banks can report the same headline profit and be very different beasts. One earns most of it from stable retail deposits and mortgages; the other from short-dated wholesale funding and trading. The first survives a credit cycle; the second can vanish in a quarter. You cannot tell which is which from the press release. You can tell from the accounts, but only if you know where to look.

For an actuary, the bank annual report is also the only place where the credit risk models you build (IFRS 9 ECL, Basel EL) show up as an actual line item: the cost of credit. Understanding how that line connects back to your portfolios is half of what makes you useful in a banking finance function.

Step 3

The mental model

Think of a bank as a sandwich. The top slice is what the bank owns (assets). The bottom slice is what the bank owes (liabilities). The filling in between is equity, the part that absorbs losses if the top slice is worth less than the bottom slice.

And the income statement, in one line:

Revenue (NII + NIR) - Operating expenses - Cost of credit - Tax = Earnings

Everything that follows is detail on what goes in each of those buckets and how to compare them across banks.

Step 4

The detail

The balance sheet: assets

The first thing to know is that a bank's balance sheet reverses the customer's view. Your deposit is your asset; it is the bank's liability. Your mortgage is your liability; it is the bank's asset. Whenever a question feels confusing, mentally flip the perspective.

Banking book

The banking book contains assets the bank intends to hold to maturity. It is dominated by loans and advances, the primary earning asset for retail and commercial banks. Three categories sit here:

Loans and advances
Retail loans (mortgages, credit cards, personal loans), commercial loans (SME, corporate, project finance), shown net of provisions (more on provisions when we cover IFRS 9).
Undrawn commitments
Off-balance-sheet credit lines. The bank has promised to lend, but the customer has not yet drawn. These attract regulatory capital because they can convert into on-balance-sheet exposure (a "credit conversion factor" applies).
HQLA
High-quality liquid assets. Cash, central bank reserves, sovereign and certain corporate bonds. Held specifically to be sold quickly under stress to fund deposit withdrawals.

Banking-book assets are not marked to market. They are held at amortised cost, with any deterioration recognised through impairment (IFRS 9 ECL). The exception is if an asset becomes credit-impaired ("stage 3"), in which case its carrying value is reduced.

HQLA tiers

SARB (and Basel internationally) classifies HQLA into three tiers, with progressively larger haircuts. The tiering reflects observed behaviour in stress events, not credit ratings alone.

TierHaircutTypical assetsWhy it sits here
Level 10%Cash, SARB reserves, treasury bills, government bonds, reposRetains value and liquidity even in severe stress
Level 2A15%Corporate and SOE debt rated AA- or higherHigh quality but does widen in stress
Level 2B50% (illustrative)Corporate and SOE debt rated BBB- or higher; some equityLiquid in normal times, less so under stress

The LCR (covered later under liquidity risk) is built from these tiers: it weights HQLA by these haircuts and asks whether the result covers 30 days of stressed outflows.

Trading book

The trading book is the bank's "shop window" of positions held for short-term gain, arbitrage, or to hedge other trading-book risks. Basel defines the boundary strictly because the two books are treated very differently for capital purposes (the trading book attracts market-risk capital; the banking book attracts credit-risk capital).

Trading-book positions are marked to market daily. Three valuation levels apply, as defined by IFRS 13:

Level 1
Quoted prices for identical assets in active markets. Most listed equities, deep-market sovereign bonds.
Level 2
Quoted prices for similar assets, or for the same asset in inactive markets. Most vanilla swaps and corporate bonds.
Level 3
No observable market price. The bank values using its own model ("mark-to-model"). Bespoke derivatives, illiquid structured credit.
2008 lesson The lack of public information on CDO values, plus the limitations of the models used to value them, was a central problem in the 2007 to 2008 banking crisis. Banks held large Level 3 positions in mortgage-backed structured products whose model valuations diverged badly from any realistic sale price. Hence Basel's later push to tighten the trading-book boundary (the trading-book / banking-book boundary became the FRTB battleground).

The balance sheet: liabilities and equity

The right side of the balance sheet (how the bank is funded) has three main blocks: deposits, wholesale funding and debt, and equity. The mix matters enormously, because retail deposits behave very differently from wholesale funding under stress.

Deposits
Customer money, by far the biggest funding source for most retail banks. Retail deposits in particular are valuable: depositors are price-insensitive, slow to switch banks, and protected by deposit insurance, so they tend to stay even when the bank looks shaky.
Wholesale funding
Senior bonds, certificates of deposit, repos, interbank loans, foreign deposits. Owners are sophisticated and price-sensitive. They withdraw fast when the bank's risk profile worsens, making this funding "non-sticky". The 2007 "credit crunch" was really a wholesale funding crunch.
Debt instruments (T2 / AT1)
Long-dated, loss-absorbing debt. T2 absorbs losses in gone-concern (after the bank has failed); AT1 (contingent convertibles) converts to CET1 in going-concern when triggers breach. Covered properly in the capital management lecture.
Equity
Share capital + share premium + retained earnings. The first line of defence against losses. The CET1 ratio (covered separately) is the headline regulatory measure.

The income statement

Walk through it from top to bottom. The bank's revenue has three sources: net interest income, non-interest income, and trading income.

Net interest income (NII)

The headline. NII is gross interest earned on assets, minus gross interest paid on funding:

$$ \text{NII} = \text{Interest earned on loans, HQLA, securities} - \text{Cost of funding} $$

For a retail / commercial bank, NII is typically 60% to 80% of total income. It is relatively stable compared to trading income, but it is sensitive to:

  • Competition: when rivals cut loan rates, NII compresses
  • Rate changes: especially when policy rates approach zero, banks cannot fully pass the cut on to depositors (the "zero lower bound")
  • Volume mix: a shift toward longer-duration assets earns more spread but increases IRRBB

Non-interest revenue (NIR)

Two sub-buckets:

Non-interest income
Fees from banking-book activity: account fees, commitment fees, transaction fees, asset management, insurance. Initial, ongoing, or termination, with some spread over time under IFRS 15 for accounting purposes.
Trading income
Daily mark-to-market gains and losses on trading-book positions: equities, fixed income, commodities, FX. Volatile by nature. Where no market price exists, mark-to-model applies (with the risks just discussed).

Operating expenses

Staff costs (the biggest line for most banks), property, IT, marketing, professional advisers, depreciation. Typically classified three ways:

  • Fixed: head office, central functions, treasury infrastructure (unchanged by lending volume)
  • Semi-fixed: product, credit risk, marketing, distribution (scale, but slower than volume)
  • Variable: credit-bureau fees, intermediary commissions (scale with volume)

Cost of credit

The line where the credit risk modelling work shows up. Under IFRS 9, banks hold provisions for expected credit losses on every loan, allocated to one of three stages. The annual impairment charge — the cost of credit — is built from five moving parts:

$$ \text{Cost of credit} = \Delta\text{provisions} - \text{interest on stage 3} + \text{gross write-offs (net of debt collection)} - \text{PWOR} + \text{MGL} $$

Δ provisions is the year-on-year change in the provisions stock on the balance sheet — closing balance minus opening balance, aggregated across stage 1, stage 2 and stage 3. It nets newly recognised expected losses (loans deteriorating, stage migrations) against releases (loans curing, stage migrations back, and provisions used up when a loan is written off). It is a balance-sheet movement, not a cash flow.

Why interest on stage 3 is deducted. Under IFRS 9, a stage 3 (credit-impaired) loan accrues interest at the effective rate applied to its net carrying value (gross less provision), not the gross. The shortfall between the gross-rate interest and the IFRS 9 statutory net-rate interest — the unwinding of discount on the provision — is not new credit loss; it is the time-value of the existing provision releasing as the loan moves closer to its expected resolution date. Banks typically present this unwind inside NII so that margin metrics stay comparable across stages, which means the same amount also flows through Δ provisions as a release. Deducting it here strips out the double-count and leaves only the genuinely new economic loss in the cost of credit line.

MGL stands for Modifications, Gains and Losses: the net P&L impact of contractual modifications such as forbearance, term extensions, rate concessions and restructurings, which change the present value of a loan's future cash flows without writing it off. PWOR is post-write-off recoveries: cash collected on loans that had previously been written off the balance sheet, which mechanically reduces the impairment charge.

Provisions reduce the carrying value of loan assets and are recognised as an impairment charge in the income statement. They affect the timing of loss recognition but not the actual loss amount over the life of a loan.

Why this matters for an actuary For a credit risk modeller, the IFRS 9 ECL number you produce is exactly this line in the income statement. Senior management cares because it is volatile (it moves with the macro outlook) and pro-cyclical (it rises just when the bank's capital is under most pressure). Understanding the link between your PD / LGD / EAD models, the staging logic, and this single income-statement line is what makes you genuinely useful in a banking finance team.

Tax and the bottom of the page

UK corporation tax is 18%; South African is 28%. UK banks also pay a bank levy, introduced in 2011 in response to the financial crisis. After tax:

Total income = NII + NIR EBIT = Total income - Operating expenses - Cost of credit Profit = EBIT - Tax - Debt interest Earnings = Profit - Preference shareholder dividends EPS = Earnings / Number of shares

The ratios that matter

You cannot read a bank annual report without these. Memorise them.

Ratio Formula What it tells you Pitfall
Revenue & margin
NII / Total income NII ÷ (NII + NIR) How "boring" the bank is. Higher means more stable revenue mix. Too high can also mean limited fee diversification.
Net interest spread (NIS) Avg asset yield − Avg liability cost The "raw" spread before allowing for the mix of earning vs non-earning assets. Ignores that not all assets earn interest.
Net interest margin (NIM) NII ÷ Average interest-earning assets The "true" spread. The headline profitability measure for a retail bank. A rising NIM could mean strong franchise or a shift to riskier lending. Always read alongside cost of credit.
Efficiency & return
Cost-to-income Opex ÷ Total income Efficiency. Lower is better. UK retail banks target 50% or below. Can fall because revenue jumped (one-off trading gain), not because costs dropped.
Return on assets (ROA) Earnings ÷ Average assets Profit generated per unit of asset. Higher leverage banks can show lower ROA but higher ROE.
Return on equity (ROE) Earnings ÷ Average equity The shareholder's headline. Can be boosted by leverage rather than skill.
Asset quality
Stage 3 / Total loans Credit-impaired loans ÷ Gross loans Quality of the loan book. Definition of "stage 3" can vary across banks; check the policy note.
Liquidity & funding
Loan-to-deposit ratio (LDR) Loans ÷ Deposits Funding stability. Below 100% means deposits fund the book. Some safe banks run above 100% if they have stable wholesale funding.
Liquidity coverage ratio (LCR) HQLA ÷ 30-day stressed net outflows Short-term liquidity strength. Minimum is 100%. Survives 30 days, not the whole crisis. Pair with NSFR.
Capital strength
CET1 ratio CET1 capital ÷ RWAs Headline regulatory capital strength. RWAs are model-dependent; not directly comparable across banks using different methodologies.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The mismatch is hiding in plain sight

Every concept in this lecture is the maturity-transformation story from lecture 1, written down in accounting. The banking book is the long, illiquid side. Deposits are the short, sticky side. HQLA is the buffer. Capital is the cushion. The income statement is what is left after running the machine for a year. If you ever get lost in a bank annual report, return to the maturity diagram in lecture 1 and locate yourself.

NIM and cost of credit are siblings

A bank with a strong NIM but a benign cost of credit is genuinely well-run. A bank with a strong NIM and a rising cost of credit is harvesting risk: it is charging more for loans because the loans are worse. Always read these two lines together. The same logic applies on the funding side: a low cost of deposits is great only if the deposits are sticky; if the bank is funding itself cheaply from wholesale markets, the cost will spike at exactly the wrong moment.

IFRS 9 created income statement volatility, on purpose

Under the old IAS 39 "incurred loss" model, banks recognised provisions only after credit deterioration was evident. The result was sharp cliff-edge provisions in downturns. IFRS 9's "expected loss" model is meant to be smoother: provisions move with the macroeconomic outlook (via forward-looking information, "FLI") and with stage migrations (via "significant increase in credit risk", "SICR"). The cost is that the income statement now responds to economic forecasts, not just to realised losses. For an actuary, that means your scenario weights and stage triggers directly drive reported earnings volatility. Treat them with the seriousness that implies.

Why the ECL vs Basel EL gap is a real management lever

Regulatory expected loss (Basel) is built from through-the-cycle PDs and downturn LGDs. Accounting ECL (IFRS 9) is built from point-in-time PDs and probability-weighted scenarios. They almost never match. Where IFRS 9 ECL is lower than regulatory EL, the gap ("EL shortfall") is deducted from CET1 capital. Where ECL is higher, a slice of the excess (up to 0.6% of RWAs) is eligible as T2 capital. We will come back to this gap repeatedly when we hit the A-IRB and IFRS 9 lectures.

Common F107 traps

Watch out A high CET1 ratio at one bank vs another tells you very little on its own. RWA densities differ because of different model approaches (standardised vs A-IRB), different business mixes, and different sovereign exposures. The leverage ratio (which ignores risk weights) was added precisely to backstop the CET1 ratio. F107 exam questions love asking you to interpret a single ratio in isolation; the safer answer is almost always "I would need to see X alongside it".
Step 6

Test yourself

As before, answer aloud first, then check.

  1. Without looking, sketch a bank balance sheet. Label five assets on the left, four liability or equity blocks on the right.
  2. What is the difference between the banking book and the trading book? What different valuation regime applies to each?
  3. Define NIM in words. Why might a rising NIM be a bad sign?
  4. The bank reports a 12% CET1 ratio. Why is this less informative than it sounds?
  5. A bank reports ECL provisions of 1,200 and a regulatory EL of 950. What is the capital treatment, and why?
  6. Walk through the income statement from total income to EPS. Name every line.
