Bank structure

A large bank is never one company. It is a holding company on top of operating subsidiaries on top of branded franchises, with the regulatory perimeter drawn in carefully chosen places. The structure exists to ring-fence risk, isolate failure, and let one group serve many markets.

Lecture 6 of Phase 1 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: docs/wiki/application/banking/01_internal_environment/06-structure.md
Step 1

The 30-second version

When you read "Barclays" or "FirstRand" or "JPMorgan", you are reading about a group, not a single company. The group sits as a holding company at the top. Beneath it are licensed banking subsidiaries (one per major jurisdiction). Beneath those sit franchises (the consumer-facing brands like FNB or Barclaycard). Insurance, asset management and other non-bank activities live in separate subsidiaries on the side. Each level is its own legal entity, with its own regulator, its own balance sheet, and its own capital.

This separation is not bureaucratic decoration. It exists so that (1) regulators can supervise the bank properly in each country, (2) losses in one part of the group cannot bleed into another, and (3) when a bank fails it can be resolved cleanly without taking down its retail depositors. The structure is the operating chassis on which every product in lecture 5 actually sits.

Feynman test Think of a bank group like a fast-food franchise system. McDonald's Corporation at the top sets the brand and the strategy. Below it sit the regional companies that hold the operating licences in each country. Below those sit the individual restaurants. If one restaurant burns down, the regional company is hurt but the global brand survives. If a regional company goes bankrupt, the corporation can replace it. A bank group works the same way: the holding company sets strategy and holds capital; the operating subsidiaries hold the banking licences in each country; the franchises (FNB, RMB, WesBank, Barclaycard) face customers. Ring-fencing is the deliberate decision to put fire-doors between rooms so that a fire in one cannot consume the building.
Step 2

Why this matters

The structure determines who is on the hook when things go wrong. A 2008-era universal bank had a single balance sheet supporting retail deposits, investment-bank trading, and everything in between. When the trading book lost money, retail depositors were exposed. Post-2008, the UK responded with the Financial Services (Banking Reform) Act 2013, mandating ring-fencing: the retail bank had to be a separate legal entity from the rest of the group. Barclays Bank UK PLC was created in 2018 for exactly this reason. The structural choice is now part of every G-SIB's risk profile.

For an actuary in central functions, structure also determines where your work applies. An ICAAP for the holding company is a different document from an ICAAP for the ring-fenced bank. Solvency II reporting in the UK lands on different legal entities depending on which subsidiary writes the policy. Resolution planning (covered in Lecture 3 under TLAC and MREL) only works if the legal structure cleanly separates "critical functions" from "everything else".

Step 3

The mental model

A pyramid with three layers and a sidecar. The holding company at the apex; banking subsidiaries underneath; franchises at the base. Non-bank financial services (insurance, asset management) sit off to the side, often as separate subsidiaries of the holding company.

Two important variants. Ring-fenced banks (UK only, since 2018) are a separate banking subsidiary that holds only retail and SME activities, structurally isolated from the rest of the group. International subsidiaries and branches are licensed locally (PRA for FirstRand's London Branch, Central Bank of Ireland for Barclays Bank Ireland PLC), so each operates under both home and host supervision.

Step 4

The detail

The holding company layer

At the top of every large bank group is a holding company, listed on a stock exchange. FirstRand Limited lists on the JSE; Barclays PLC lists on the LSE; JPMorgan Chase & Co. lists on NYSE; Standard Chartered PLC on LSE; HSBC Holdings PLC on LSE with secondary listings in Hong Kong and Bermuda. The holding company's job:

  • Strategy and capital allocation: decide how capital flows between subsidiaries.
  • Consolidated reporting: produce the group's IFRS accounts and regulatory disclosures.
  • Group-level governance: the board overseeing risk appetite, audit, compensation and nominations.
  • Group-level regulatory engagement: dealing with SARB / Prudential Authority and FSCA in SA, with PRA / FCA and Bank of England in UK, with the Fed (and home supervisor) in US.

The holding company is not itself a bank. It does not hold deposits or make loans. It holds shares in the operating subsidiaries. This is important for resolution: if a subsidiary fails, the holding company's other subsidiaries are insulated.

Banking subsidiaries

One level down. Each banking subsidiary holds an actual banking licence and is the legal entity that takes deposits and makes loans. In FirstRand's case, FirstRand Bank Limited is the single home-country banking entity, housing retail, commercial, corporate and investment banking under one banking licence (issued under the SA Banks Act). Barclays splits these activities across two banking subsidiaries because of the UK ring-fencing regime.

The UK ring-fencing regime

Introduced by the Financial Services (Banking Reform) Act 2013, fully effective from 1 January 2019. The rule applies to UK banks with more than 25 billion GBP of "core deposits" (retail and SME) and requires them to ring-fence those activities into a separate legal entity. The ring-fenced bank cannot:

  • Carry on most trading, market-making or proprietary investment activity.
  • Operate outside the EEA (broadly speaking).
  • Have direct exposures to relevant financial institutions (other banks, broker-dealers, insurance companies) above narrow limits.

The effect: Barclays now has Barclays Bank UK PLC (the ring-fenced bank, holding retail, SME and wealth UK operations) and Barclays Bank PLC (the non-ring-fenced bank, holding corporate, investment, and international banking). Both are PRA-authorised, FCA-regulated, with independent boards. The two entities cannot lend to each other except on strict third-party terms.

The argument for ring-fencing is that retail depositors should not lose money because of trading losses elsewhere in the group. The argument against is that it raises costs (duplicate boards, duplicate IT, duplicate capital) and reduces the diversification benefits of a universal bank. Empirically, costs ended up at the higher end of forecasts but the policy survived its first stress test (Covid).

Franchises

The customer-facing brand layer. These are not separate legal entities; they are divisions of the licensed banking subsidiary above them. FNB and RMB and WesBank are all divisions of FirstRand Bank Limited, sharing one banking licence, one regulatory capital pool, and one balance sheet.

FirstRand's franchises map roughly to customer segments:

FNB (First National Bank)
Retail and commercial banking. Individuals, SMEs, mid-sized corporates. Deposits, mortgages, personal loans, credit cards, transactional banking. The brand most South Africans interact with daily.
RMB (Rand Merchant Bank)
Corporate and investment banking. Large corporates, institutional clients, government. Advisory, structured finance, trading, treasury.
WesBank
Instalment finance specialist. Vehicle and asset finance for individuals and corporates. Has long been one of the dominant vehicle financiers in SA.

Barclays Bank PLC similarly hosts Barclays Corporate Banking, Barclays Investment Bank, and Barclays International. Inside Barclays Bank UK PLC sit the UK retail bank and Barclaycard UK. The franchise structure exists because customers buy products under recognisable brand names, even if the legal entity behind them is the same.

International subsidiaries and branches

Banks operating in multiple countries face a structural choice in each market: open a branch (an extension of the home-country bank) or a subsidiary (a separately-incorporated entity licensed locally).

Branch
Legally part of the home bank. Operates under the home licence, supplemented by host-country rules. Capital is shared with the parent. FirstRand Bank London Branch is an example, PRA-authorised and FCA-regulated but sharing FirstRand Bank Ltd's capital base.
Subsidiary
Separate legal entity, locally incorporated, locally capitalised, locally licensed. Insulates the parent from host-country losses; insulates the host country from parent failure. FirstRand Namibia, FirstRand Botswana and Barclays Bank Ireland PLC are subsidiaries. Barclays restructured into Barclays Bank Ireland PLC post-Brexit to maintain EU passporting rights.

Subsidiaries are heavier (separate capital, separate board, separate IT) but cleaner for resolution. Branches are lighter but expose the parent to host-country risk. Regulators in some emerging markets prefer subsidiaries for exactly this reason: they want the local depositors backed by ring-fenced local capital, not by promises from a parent in another jurisdiction.

Non-bank financial services subsidiaries

Insurance, asset management, securities and wealth-management activities live in separate subsidiaries of the holding company, not inside the banking subsidiary. The reason: each activity has its own regulator and its own capital regime. Insurance follows Solvency II (UK / EU) or SAM (SA). Asset management follows MiFID II conduct rules. Securities broker-dealers follow market-specific regulation.

FirstRand's non-bank stack includes Ashburton Investments (asset management), FirstRand Life Assurance (life insurance), and securities broker-dealer entities. Barclays' includes Barclays Life Assurance Company Limited, Barclays Capital Securities Limited, and the Wealth Management division. Both groups generate meaningful non-interest income from these activities, complementing the bank-side NII.

Retail and commercial banks

The core of the system. Need a banking licence to call themselves banks and accept retail deposits. Listed and shareholder-owned in most developed markets. Subject to the full prudential and conduct regime, including deposit insurance contributions. The dominant funding source is retail deposits (sticky, cheap), with wholesale supplementing where needed.

Investment banks

Two main activity blocks: transactional services (sales and trading of bonds, equities, derivatives, currencies, commodities) and advisory services (M&A, capital raising). Often house specialised lending (project finance, trade finance, commodity finance). Modern standalone investment banks include Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley in the US; most other large banks (JPMorgan, UBS, Barclays, Deutsche, BNP) run investment banking as a division of a universal bank.

Universal banks

The dominant large-bank model. Combine commercial banking (including retail) with investment banking, plus often wealth management, fund management and bancassurance. The argument in favour: diversification of revenue and a one-stop-shop for corporate clients. The argument against: deposits made by retail customers can be put at risk by losses from trading activities elsewhere in the group. The 2008 crisis sharpened that argument; the UK responded with ring-fencing, the US with the Volcker Rule, the EU with various structural-reform proposals (mostly abandoned).

Challenger banks

New entrants exploiting low-cost digital channels. UK examples: Atom, Monzo, Starling, Revolut (now a bank in some jurisdictions, an e-money institution in others). Some "challengers" are not banks at all: PayPal and Stripe are payments firms; Ant Group runs a vast Chinese fintech without (most of) a banking licence; M-Pesa operates banking-like services in Kenya, Tanzania and Ghana via mobile-network operators.

Open APIs (PSD2 in the EU, Open Banking in the UK) lower the barriers to entry. A new firm can offer mortgage advice or savings comparison without ever holding a current account, by reading customers' account data via API. The competitive geography is still shifting.

Shadow banks

Institutions that offer banking-like services without a banking licence and without (most) prudential regulation. The Financial Stability Board now uses the broader term "non-bank financial intermediation" (NBFI). Estimated by the FSB at around 60 trillion USD globally (2023), comparable in size to the banking sector itself.

Shadow banks were at the centre of the 2008 crisis: Lehman Brothers, Bear Stearns, AIG's CDS book, the money-market funds that broke the buck, the SIVs that funded mortgage assets short-term off-balance-sheet. Each was performing credit intermediation outside the banking perimeter, with limited capital and limited regulatory oversight.

Post-2008 reforms have re-regulated parts of shadow banking (money-market funds, securitisation, repo) but the perimeter remains contested. The 2022 UK LDI crisis was a shadow-banking crisis in pension form. The 2023 Credit Suisse collapse was partly triggered by losses on shadow-banking exposures (Archegos, Greensill).

Mutual banks

Owned by their members (depositors or borrowers) rather than by external shareholders. Building societies in the UK and South Africa, credit unions in the US, stokvels and rotating savings groups in Africa. Strong values, community focus, often offering lower fees and better savings rates than commercial peers.

The disadvantage: they cannot raise external equity. In a downturn or to fund major investment (the digital banking transition, say), they have only retained earnings and members' subscriptions. Many UK building societies demutualised between 1989 and 2000 (Halifax, Northern Rock, Bradford and Bingley, Bristol & West) to access equity capital. The 2008 crisis was a poor advertisement for that strategy: most of the demutualised entities failed or were rescued, while most still-mutual peers survived.

Wholesale-funded banks

Banks with a corporate-lending focus and limited retail deposit base. They fund themselves through bond issuance, interbank borrowing, certificates of deposit, and commercial paper. The economic profile is:

  • Lower NIM: wholesale funding is more expensive than retail deposits, and corporate loans pay lower spreads than retail loans.
  • Lower credit losses: corporate borrowers are higher quality than the typical retail borrower.
  • Lower costs: no branch network, no deposit operations, no card programmes.
  • Higher funding fragility: wholesale funders are price-sensitive and disappear under stress. Northern Rock's 2007 failure was a textbook wholesale-funding run.

Development banks

Government-backed lenders that fund projects in developing economies. Their mandate is not commercial returns but development impact: infrastructure, climate-aligned investment, SME support, sovereign credit during stress.

The multilateral development banks include the World Bank Group, European Investment Bank (EIB), Asian Development Bank (ADB), Inter-American Development Bank (IADB) and African Development Bank (AfDB). In Africa specifically: the Trade and Development Bank (operating across COMESA), the Arab Bank for Economic Development in Africa, and SA's Land and Agricultural Development Bank (the Land Bank). These institutions issue their own bonds, typically AAA-rated thanks to sovereign backing, and on-lend at concessional rates.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Legal structure determines resolution mechanics

The whole point of complicated bank structures is to make resolution possible without taxpayer bailouts. The single point of entry (SPE) resolution model puts the holding company into resolution and recapitalises the operating subsidiaries from above. The multiple point of entry (MPE) model resolves each significant subsidiary in its home jurisdiction. UK G-SIBs broadly run SPE; HSBC historically ran MPE because of its globally-distributed deposit base. The TLAC and MREL requirements from Lecture 3 only work if the legal structure supports the chosen resolution model. Structure is resolution made concrete.

The Glass-Steagall debate never died

The US separation of commercial and investment banking ran from 1933 (Glass-Steagall) to 1999 (Gramm-Leach-Bliley repealed it). Within a decade of repeal, the 2008 crisis hit; many commentators blamed the universal-banking model. The post-crisis response was the Volcker Rule (banning proprietary trading by deposit-taking banks) and UK ring-fencing. Both are softer than full Glass-Steagall but capture the spirit. The 2023 collapse of Credit Suisse re-opened the debate; some now argue the universal-banking model has structural fragilities that no amount of capital can fix. The pendulum swings every 25 years or so.

Mutuals were not actually safer in 2008, but the survivors look smart now

The demutualised UK building societies (Northern Rock, B&B, Halifax, Alliance & Leicester) all failed or were rescued in 2007 to 2008. The still-mutual ones (Nationwide, Skipton, Yorkshire, Coventry) survived comfortably. The lesson is not that mutuals are intrinsically safer; it is that mutuals had not pursued the high-risk strategies that the demutualised banks chose to pursue once they had access to equity. Capital structure shapes strategy and risk-taking, often more than risk-takers admit.

The shadow-banking perimeter is the next big regulatory problem

Capital and liquidity requirements on banks have pushed credit intermediation out into the shadow-banking system. Private credit funds, BDCs, money-market funds, securitisation vehicles, and increasingly insurance companies (via private-market commitments) now do what banks used to do. The 2022 UK LDI crisis was the canonical shadow-banking-in-disguise event: long-dated pension fund leverage that no bank capital rule could see, until it broke the gilt market. The next crisis is more likely to come from NBFI than from a regulated bank, and the regulatory response is still being designed.

Common F107 traps

Watch out Four traps. (1) Confusing the holding company (not a bank, holds shares) with the banking subsidiary (holds the licence). The Lehman holding company filing for bankruptcy in 2008 did not mean Lehman's UK and Japanese subsidiaries were instantly defunct; they continued separately. (2) Treating a "branch" and a "subsidiary" as the same. They differ on legal personality, capital, and resolution treatment. (3) Forgetting that UK ring-fencing applies only to UK banks above the 25 billion GBP threshold; not all banks ring-fence. (4) Calling the FSB-defined shadow banking sector "unregulated". It is less regulated than banking, but parts of it (money-market funds, regulated AIFs, insurance) are squarely inside the regulatory perimeter.
