Capital management

The shock absorber. Why banks hold equity (and equity-like instruments), how regulators stack the requirement, and how losses move through it.

Lecture 3 of Phase 1 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: docs/wiki/application/banking/01_internal_environment/03-capital_management.md
Step 1

The 30-second version

Capital is the part of the bank that gets hurt first when losses arrive. Equity holders, then certain junior bondholders, then senior bondholders. Depositors are at the back of the queue, which is exactly the point. Regulators take that ordering and write rules requiring banks to hold enough of each layer so that even in a serious downturn, losses are absorbed before they ever reach depositors or taxpayers.

Three things to keep clear: capital is not the same as liquidity, capital is on the liability side of the balance sheet (not an asset), and capital exists to absorb unexpected losses, while expected losses are meant to be covered by ordinary pricing and provisions.

Feynman test Imagine a bank as a car. The chassis is the loans. The fuel is the deposits. The crumple zones are the capital. When the car crashes (a recession, a default wave), the crumple zones deform first so the passengers (depositors) survive. Different layers of the crumple zone deform at different stages of the crash. Common equity is the soft outer panel; senior debt is the rigid frame. Regulators set the minimum thickness of each layer.
Step 2

Why this matters

Before 2008, banks routinely operated with very thin equity (commonly 2% to 4% of total assets) and topped up with hybrid instruments that, when stress arrived, did not absorb losses as advertised. Governments had to step in with public money. The political and economic damage from that has shaped every banking rule since.

Basel III, finalised through Basel d424 ("Basel IV"), did three things in response. It made the definition of capital much stricter (CET1, AT1, T2 with hard eligibility criteria). It increased the quantity required. And it added layers on top: buffers, Pillar 2 add-ons, leverage ratio, TLAC, MREL. Every component you are about to learn was a response to a specific failure pattern from the crisis.

In 2023, the Credit Suisse rescue made AT1 famous: 17 billion CHF of AT1 instruments were written down to zero before equity, which was a controversial inversion of the usual order but technically within the contract terms. SVB the same week showed that adequate capital ratios do not save a bank from a liquidity run. Both events sit on top of the framework you are about to learn.

Step 3

The mental model

The capital stack is a layer cake, read from the bottom up. Each layer absorbs losses before the next layer is touched. The first three layers (CET1, AT1, T2) are "regulatory capital". The layers above (senior bail-in-able debt) only matter once the bank has entered resolution.

The picture above answers three different questions at once:

  • Order of loss: who gets hit first when the bank loses money?
  • Going-concern vs gone-concern: which layers absorb while the bank is still operating (CET1, AT1) vs only in resolution (T2, TLAC senior)?
  • Regulatory minimum: how big must each layer be, as a percentage of risk-weighted assets?
Step 4

The detail

Capital is funding, not an asset

A common beginner mistake is to picture capital as a pile of money "set aside" somewhere. It is not. Capital is a source of funding, sitting on the liability side of the balance sheet alongside deposits and bonds. The difference: it has no fixed coupon (CET1 pays only discretionary dividends), no maturity date (CET1 is perpetual), and it absorbs losses before any other funding source. That combination is exactly what makes it loss-absorbing.

Expected vs unexpected losses, and what each is funded by

Banks plan for some loan defaults every year. That number, the expected loss, is built into pricing and into IFRS 9 provisions. It is a cost of doing business, not what capital is for.

What capital is for is the unexpected loss: the gap between what actually happens in a bad year and what you planned for. The gap is what knocks a bank below the regulatory minimum and triggers a run. Most bank failures sit on the unexpected-loss side of this distinction.

$$ \text{EL} = \text{PD} \times \text{LGD} \times \text{EAD} \qquad \text{UL} \approx \text{std dev of the loss distribution} $$

The IRB capital formula (covered in detail in the A-IRB lectures later) sets capital equal to the loss at the 99.9th percentile of the portfolio loss distribution, minus the EL. In other words, capital is sized to absorb the gap between a really bad year and an average year.

The three tiers, side by side

Each tier is defined by three things: what it looks like as an instrument, when it absorbs losses, and how much of the minimum requirement it can fill.

The single most important distinction Going-concern loss absorption (CET1, AT1) happens while the bank is still operating. Gone-concern (T2, TLAC senior) only kicks in after the bank has been declared non-viable and put into resolution. AT1 is the only instrument that converts before the bank dies, which is why it carries such a high coupon, and why its 2023 write-down at Credit Suisse was so controversial.

Pillar 1: the minimum requirement

Pillar 1 sets the minimum capital a bank must hold against its risk-weighted assets (RWAs). RWAs are computed across three risk types:

Credit risk
Standardised approach (fixed risk weights by exposure class) or internal ratings based approach (F-IRB or A-IRB, with the bank's own PD / LGD / EAD estimates).
Market risk
Standardised approach or an internal VaR / ES model. Basel d424 introduces the FRTB framework.
Operational risk
Historically BIA, TSA, AMA. The AMA is being retired in Basel d424; the SMA replaces all three.

Total Pillar 1 capital must be at least 8% of RWAs, decomposed as 4.5% CET1 + 1.5% AT1 + 2.0% T2. A bank can hold more CET1 in place of AT1 or T2, but never less than 4.5% CET1.

The buffers, stacked on top

Above Pillar 1 sit three CET1-only buffers, all introduced by Basel III. Breaching any of them triggers automatic restrictions on dividend, AT1 coupon and bonus payments (the "maximum distributable amount" or MDA mechanism). Banks therefore manage to their buffer-inclusive ratio, not to the Pillar 1 minimum.

CCoB
Capital conservation buffer. Fixed at 2.5% of RWAs in CET1. Standard everywhere. Designed to be drawn down in a downturn; regulators reduced it to 0% in the 2020 pandemic.
CCyB
Countercyclical buffer. Set by the national regulator between 0% and 2.5% of RWAs in CET1. Tightens when household and corporate credit-to-GDP is rising unsustainably.
G-SIB / D-SIB
Systemic-importance surcharge. G-SIB amounts set by the FSB in consultation with BCBS (1% to 3.5%). D-SIB amounts set domestically (up to 2.5% in South Africa, up to 2% for UK ring-fenced banks).

Pillar 2: the supervisory add-on

Pillar 1 captures credit, market and operational risk in a standardised way. Pillar 2 catches everything else, plus stress-testing outcomes. There are two flavours.

Pillar 2A
Capital for risks Pillar 1 misses or under-captures: concentration, IRRBB, business model, pension, model, reputational, strategic, climate, cyber. Disclosed; expected to be met at all times.
Pillar 2B
In the UK, the "PRA buffer", set as guidance based on stress-test results. In South Africa, the "ICR" (individual capital requirement), set by the Prudential Authority. Generally not disclosed publicly. Effectively a private stress-test buffer.
Pillar 1 minimum 8.0% (4.5 CET1 + 1.5 AT1 + 2.0 T2) + Combined Buffer (CCoB + CCyB + D-SIB) 4.5% (CET1 only) + Pillar 2A 2.0% (mostly CET1) + Pillar 2B (guidance) 2.5% (CET1) + Management buffer 1.0% (internal target) ========================================= = Internal capital target ~18.0% of RWAs

TLAC: capital plus bail-in debt for G-SIBs

The 2008 dilemma was binary: let a G-SIB fail in a disorderly way (systemic contagion) or bail it out (moral hazard). The FSB designed TLAC (total loss-absorbing capacity) as a third option: orderly resolution without public funds. The mechanism is bail-in: creditors absorb losses and are converted to equity, the bank is recapitalised, and it emerges from resolution still performing its critical functions.

TLAC is set as the higher of 18% of RWAs and 6.75% of the leverage ratio exposure. It sits below the combined buffer requirement, so the bank's total stack is TLAC + buffers.

The crucial point: TLAC is not just regulatory capital. It also includes bail-in-able senior unsecured debt, which can be written down or converted in resolution. To qualify, the senior debt must be structurally or contractually subordinated to operating liabilities (so a write-down does not trigger cross-default on operational counterparties), have remaining maturity over one year, and be issued or guaranteed by the resolution entity.

Common confusion Calling TLAC a "capital requirement" misses the point. CET1 + AT1 + T2 covers loss absorption. TLAC additionally covers recapitalisation, the cost of getting the bank back to viable capital ratios after losses have already been absorbed. That is why the percentages are higher than the Pillar 1 minimum.

MREL: the EU and UK parallel

MREL (minimum requirement for own funds and eligible liabilities) is the EU and UK equivalent under the BRRD. It applies to all banks, not just G-SIBs, but is calibrated bank by bank. It is built from two components:

Loss absorption
The higher of (Pillar 1 + Pillar 2A) RWA capital, the leverage requirement, or the Basel I capital floor. Reflects what the bank needs to satisfy ongoing capital rules post-resolution.
Recapitalisation
0% to 100% of the loss-absorption amount, set by the resolution authority based on the bank's size and systemic significance.

For G-SIBs, MREL broadly tracks TLAC. For smaller systemically relevant banks, MREL is the binding constraint and TLAC does not apply.

The leverage ratio: a non-risk-weighted backstop

RWAs are model-dependent. Two banks with similar businesses can report very different RWAs because they use different IRB models, different model parameters, or different exposure mixes. A bank that lowers its RWAs through model optimisation also lowers its capital requirement, even if the underlying risk is unchanged.

The leverage ratio bypasses that. It is the simplest possible measure: Tier 1 capital divided by a total exposure measure that ignores risk weights.

$$ \text{Leverage ratio} = \frac{\text{Tier 1 capital}}{\text{On-balance + derivatives + SFT + off-balance exposures}} $$

Basel sets a 3% minimum. The South African Prudential Authority requires 4%. The leverage ratio is a backstop: it binds when RWA-based ratios look comfortable but the bank is, in fact, very large relative to its capital.

The ECL provision link

IFRS 9 ECL feeds the capital calculation, in different ways under each approach.

Standardised banks
Provisions split into general (GP) and specific (SP). GPs may count in T2 capital up to 1.25% of credit RWAs. SPs reduce the asset exposure before applying RWAs.
IRB banks
No GP / SP split. Where ECL exceeds regulatory EL, the excess may count in T2 up to 0.6% of credit RWAs. Where regulatory EL exceeds ECL ("EL shortfall"), the gap is deducted directly from CET1.

Practical consequence: when the macroeconomic outlook deteriorates and IFRS 9 ECL jumps, CET1 falls (through retained earnings). This is procyclical: it tightens capital just as the bank most needs flexibility. The transitional arrangements that the EU allowed in 2020 (phased in ECL impact) and South Africa did not (full recognition) illustrate how regulators can soften this.

Economic capital, the internal view

Regulatory capital is what you must hold. Economic capital is what the bank's own internal models say it needs. The two diverge because regulatory capital uses standardised assumptions (notably the ASRF model's single systematic factor and infinite granularity) while economic capital can model concentration, diversification across risk types, and the bank's actual loss distribution.

Credit risk EC (internal model, includes concentration) + Market risk EC (VaR / ES, stressed scenarios) + Operational risk EC (scenario analysis, loss distribution approach) + IRRBB EC (EVE sensitivity at confidence level) + Other Pillar 2 risks (pension, model risk, strategic, often judgemental) ========================================================= = Gross Internal Capital Requirement - Diversification benefit (if inter-risk correlation is modelled) ========================================================= = Net Internal Capital Requirement (ICR)

For a well-diversified bank, EC is typically lower than regulatory capital, because diversification benefits are captured. For concentrated or small banks, EC can exceed regulatory capital. Either way, the binding constraint is the larger of the two: you cannot run below regulatory capital just because your internal models say you should.

Capital allocation, where management actually thinks

Holding capital is half the problem. Allocating it across business lines is the other half. The principles:

  • Capital is "owned" by the centre, not by the business units. Treasury acts as "bank to the bank", charging each business line for the capital it consumes (via RWAs and EC) and rewarding it for capital it generates.
  • Endowment benefit is the return on assets funded by capital (typically central bank deposits or sovereign bonds). It accrues to the centre or ALCO book, not to individual business lines, otherwise the bank's calculated value-added would be distorted.
  • Business cases: each line submits proposals for capital based on RWAs, EC, expected return on capital, and economic profit. The centre allocates against a hurdle rate set by the board (reflecting the bank's cost of equity).
  • Performance measurement: bonuses linked not just to returns but to risk-adjusted returns, so traders cannot game the system by taking high-capital, high-return positions.

Pillar 3: market discipline through disclosure

The third Basel pillar requires banks to publish information about their capital structure, risk exposures, and risk management processes. The idea is that informed shareholders and counterparties act as an additional layer of discipline beyond regulators. Disclosures cover at minimum: which group entities are in scope, the terms of capital instruments, total amounts of each capital tier, capital requirements by risk type, the bank's risk management structure, and a breakdown of credit exposures by asset class.

The EBA prescribes a standardised set of tables that every European and UK bank must publish. The headline table is KM1, "key metrics" — a one-page summary of the bank's capital, leverage, liquidity, and (for G-SIBs) TLAC positions. Here is an abridged extract from a UK G-SIB's most recent disclosure to show what one looks like:

Three things are worth noticing in any G-SIB KM1. First, the capital ratios sit well above the binding minimum — for HSBC, 14.8% CET1 vs roughly 11.5% required (Pillar 1 + buffers + Pillar 2A). The headroom is the management buffer plus the firm's voluntary cushion. Second, the leverage ratio at 5.6% is a long way above the 3.25% UK minimum — for a bank with substantial low-risk-weight exposures (sovereign bonds, prime mortgages), the leverage ratio rarely binds. Third, the TLAC ratios are reported on both bases (RWA and leverage exposure); the rule binds at the higher of the two minima, and the bank must comfortably clear both.

Pillar 3 is the least quantitative of the three pillars and the most often underestimated. In an ASSA F107 question, "market discipline" is the keyword.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Read the stack from the bottom up, always

When you see a bank's reported CET1 ratio, mentally subtract the buffers, P2A, and P2B before judging it. A 12% CET1 ratio for a UK D-SIB might be only 1% to 2% above its true binding constraint. The first management lever the bank pulls in a stress is to cut dividends and bonuses to preserve CET1 above the MDA threshold.

The AT1 problem, and why Credit Suisse hurt

AT1 instruments were designed to absorb losses while the bank is still operating, ahead of T2 but after CET1. In the Credit Suisse rescue in March 2023, FINMA used a contractual write-down clause to zero the AT1 stack while equity holders received a residual recovery from the UBS share-exchange. Legally defensible, but economically inverted. The AT1 market briefly seized up because the implicit assumption (AT1 ranks above equity in resolution) had been broken. EU and UK regulators rushed out statements to reassure the market that their resolution regimes would not do the same. This is a real F107-relevant case study on the limits of the going-concern / gone-concern model.

Why high RWA-based ratios can mislead

Two banks with 14% CET1 ratios can be very different. Bank A uses A-IRB and gets RWAs of 40% of total assets (low risk weights, low denominator, high ratio). Bank B uses standardised and gets RWAs of 65% of total assets (higher denominator, lower ratio for the same loans). The leverage ratio backstop exists precisely because of this. In an F107 exam, the safe answer when asked to compare ratios across banks is "I would need to see leverage ratio and RWA density alongside CET1."

Procyclicality runs through everything

Capital ratios fall in downturns: RWAs rise as ratings deteriorate, ECL provisions jump, retained earnings drop. This is exactly when the bank most needs to lend to support the economy. Basel's response was to design buffers that can be drawn down (CCoB, CCyB) and to allow transitional ECL relief in crises. To see why this matters, walk through one full cycle in the life of a single bank.

Before: the build-up (years of expansion)

The economy is growing. Unemployment is low, asset prices are rising, defaults are well below the through-the-cycle average. For the bank, this shows up everywhere at once:

  • RWAs fall per unit of lending. IRB PD estimates drift down as recent benign default data rolls into the calibration window. Stage migrations are mostly stage 2 → stage 1 (cures).
  • IFRS 9 ECL is small. Stage 3 inflows are low, forward-looking macro overlays are benign, lifetime ECL on the stage 2 book shrinks.
  • Cost of credit drops, so the cost-of-credit line in the income statement is small or negative (net release). Earnings rise, retained earnings build CET1.
  • The CET1 ratio climbs — numerator up (more capital), denominator down (lower RWAs). The bank reports a comfortable 15% CET1.
  • The regulator raises CCyB from 0% toward 1% or 2%, "leaning against the wind" precisely because the build-up itself creates the eventual crash. CCyB is the only buffer designed to be set higher in good times.

This is the dangerous part of the cycle, even though it looks calm. The bank's reported ratios make everything look fine; the regulator is the only party with an explicit incentive to take capital away from the bank (via the CCyB) when it most appears not to need it.

During: the crunch (the recession hits)

Unemployment rises, asset prices fall, borrowers miss payments. The CET1 ratio collapses from three directions at once:

  • RWAs rise. Stage 1 → 2 and 2 → 3 migrations push IRB PDs up; downgraded counterparties pick up higher risk weights under the standardised approach; LGD assumptions worsen as collateral values fall. The denominator can easily grow 10% to 20% with no change in book size.
  • IFRS 9 ECL jumps. Stage 2 ECL flips from 12-month to lifetime as SICR triggers; new stage 3 inflows take large specific provisions; forward-looking macro scenarios bias the central case downward. The cost-of-credit charge in the P&L can be three to five times its normal level.
  • Retained earnings drop or turn negative. The cost-of-credit line eats into operating profit. CET1 capital falls in absolute terms.
  • The CET1 ratio drops sharply — numerator down, denominator up, the two effects compounding. A bank entering the cycle at 15% can sit at 11% within a year.
  • MDA restrictions threaten. As the combined buffer is eroded, the bank approaches the threshold that triggers automatic restrictions on dividends, AT1 coupons and bonuses. The first management lever is therefore to suspend distributions, preserving CET1.

The regulator's instinct is to give banks room. The CCyB is released back to 0% (its design purpose, finally activated). Transitional ECL relief may be granted (the EU did this in 2020; South Africa did not). Pillar 2B (PRA buffer) guidance can be eased. The hope is that banks use the headroom to keep lending. In practice, banks usually deleverage anyway, because their internal management buffer is now the binding constraint, not the regulatory minimum.

After: the rebuild (recovery)

Defaults peak and start to fall. The economy stabilises. The reverse process plays out, but more slowly than the crunch:

  • ECL releases. Stage 3 inflows slow; some stage 2 loans cure back to stage 1; forward-looking overlays normalise. Provision charges turn into provision releases, which lift the P&L.
  • RWAs fall. PDs improve as recent default data trends back toward the through-the-cycle average; collateral values recover, LGD assumptions improve. The denominator shrinks.
  • CET1 rebuilds through both retained earnings and provision releases.
  • The CET1 ratio recovers — typically over two to three years, not months. Dividends and AT1 coupons resume once the bank is comfortably above the MDA threshold; bonuses are reinstated.
  • The regulator gradually rebuilds CCyB back to a neutral 1% to 2%, ready for the next cycle.

The asymmetry of the cycle matters. The build-up of vulnerability happens slowly and invisibly; the crunch happens fast and very visibly; the recovery is slow again. Banks that did not have an internal management buffer big enough to absorb the crunch (the ones that ran at 12% CET1 with no management cushion) end up forced into emergency rights issues, business sales, or — in the worst case — resolution. That is why every bank you read about runs to an internal target a few percentage points above the regulatory minimum, even though that "wastes" capital in good times. The cost of holding excess capital in a boom is well worth paying to avoid being the bank that has to call its shareholders during a panic.

The 2020 live test The COVID-19 shock was the first real-world test of the full Basel III procyclicality toolkit. UK and EU regulators released CCyB to 0% in March 2020; the EBA granted phased-in IFRS 9 ECL relief (a five-year transitional add-back to CET1); the PRA eased Pillar 2B guidance; UK banks suspended dividends and AT1 coupons voluntarily under regulatory pressure. The result was unusual: the hoped-for "banks keep lending into the recession" outcome didn't really happen on bank balance sheets. Instead, governments invented huge state-guaranteed lending schemes (BBLS, CBILS in the UK; PPP in the US) that put credit risk on the sovereign rather than the bank. The lesson the regulators took away was that releasable buffers help, but they are not sufficient on their own to overcome the bank's internal incentive to deleverage in a panic.

Common F107 traps

Watch out Three traps I would expect F107 to set. (1) Confusing TLAC with regulatory capital, then forgetting the bail-in-able senior debt component. (2) Forgetting that the leverage ratio numerator is Tier 1, not CET1 and not total capital. (3) Assuming buffers are part of the 8% Pillar 1 number; they sit on top of it. Read every capital question with these three primed.
