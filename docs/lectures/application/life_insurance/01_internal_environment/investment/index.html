Life insurance, internal environment

Investment

Four lectures covering F102 chapter 33. The only module in the track that starts from the liabilities and works outwards. A life insurer does not choose assets because it likes them; it chooses assets whose cashflows, term, currency and real-or-nominal character answer the liabilities it has already written. Everything else in the chapter is a consequence of that constraint, or a regulatory limit on how far it can be relaxed.

How this module is organised

Principles first, then the asset classes one by one, then matching, then the strategy and the models used to test it. Four lectures for one chapter, because the chapter is long and because the exam treats investment as an application topic: it gives you a liability profile and asks you to justify an asset mix, which requires all four lectures at once.

Where this connects

Investment strategy is not a free choice and the exam rewards saying so. The product basis decides who bears investment risk, which decides how much freedom the office has: a unit-linked fund is invested as the policyholder chose, a without-profits annuity book is matched as closely as the bond market allows, and only the with-profits fund and the free assets leave real discretion. The reserving basis then constrains it further, because a market-consistent liability moves with the discount curve and an office that is mismatched sees its solvency position move with it.

Index-linked bonds are the case worth thinking hardest about. They are the natural match for a liability that escalates with inflation, which describes most annuities in payment and much of the income protection book, and the supply of them is limited in most markets. That mismatch between what offices need and what governments issue is a real constraint rather than a textbook one.

Asset class mechanics and valuation are covered in financial management and financial engineering. This module assumes them and concentrates on the suitability argument.
