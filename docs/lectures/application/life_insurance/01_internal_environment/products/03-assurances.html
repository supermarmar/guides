The cheapest contract to buy is the most capital-hungry to write

A term assurance has the smallest reserve of the four contracts here, 50 against an endowment's 300, and the largest new business strain per unit of premium at 1.2500 against -0.1000. Cheapness is the cause rather than a coincidence: hold expenses fixed and the strain ratio falls from 1.2500 to -0.6250 as the premium rises. So protection business is the capital-intensive line and savings business largely finances itself, which is the reverse of what reserve size suggests.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The summary describes four assurance contracts under five headings each: what they pay, what the customer needs, which product bases apply, which micro risks arise, and what capital they require. Most of that is a taxonomy. Three parts of it are arithmetic, because the summary defines two of its risks as differences rather than as words and makes a capital claim that looks self-contradictory until it is computed.

Start with the definitions. Mortality risk is the difference between the death benefit and the reserve, so it is measurable per contract: at year fifteen of a twenty-five-year term, a term assurance has 99200.0000 of a 100000 benefit at risk, 0.9920 of it, while an endowment near maturity has only 12000.0000, or 0.1200. A factor of 8.2667 across four contracts. And withdrawal risk is the difference between the surrender benefit and the asset share, which is why the summary can list it for term assurance despite there being no surrender value: with nothing paid back, an early withdrawal leaves the insurer 3200.0000 short and a late one costs the policyholder 2400.0000, so the risk changes sides partway through the term.

Then the capital claim. The summary pairs term assurance's small reserve with high initial costs and a warning about significant capital, which reads as a contradiction. Per unit of premium it is not: term assurance has the smallest reserve of four contracts at 50 and the largest strain per unit of premium at 1.2500, while a single-premium endowment has the largest reserve at 9000 and a negative strain. Cheapness is the mechanism: hold expenses at 400 and raise the premium from 200 to 1200, and the strain ratio falls from 1.2500 to -0.6250. Protection business is the capital-intensive line and savings business largely finances itself.

Finally the renewal option. The summary gives renewable and convertible term one extra risk, additional anti-selection on renewal, because there is sometimes no further underwriting. That has an exact price. If 80 per cent of healthy lives leave at renewal, the renewing pool's mortality rises to 1.7347 times the original, and if all of them leave it reaches 3.5714. The loading the insurer must charge at outset is exactly the proportional rise, verified at every level of selection, and it must be collected before the selection happens because it cannot be charged after.

Step 2

Why this matters

The strain-per-premium result changes how a product range should be read. Lecture 1 showed that new business consumes capital; this lecture shows which products consume most of it, and the answer is not the one a balance sheet suggests. An insurer whose growth is concentrated in protection is consuming capital far faster per rand of premium than one growing a savings book, even though the savings book carries reserves two orders of magnitude larger. Anybody assessing a life insurer's new business mix should compute the strain ratio by line rather than reading the reserve movement.

The mortality risk definition matters because it is the quantity reinsurance is bought on. An insurer does not reinsure the death benefit, it reinsures the sum at risk, and section 4.1 shows that quantity varies by a factor of 8.2667 across contracts with identical benefits. That is why a reinsurance programme is structured by contract type rather than by sum assured, and why a term book needs proportionally far more reinsurance than an endowment book of the same face amount.

The renewal result is the clearest case in this lecture of a customer benefit and an insurer exposure being the same thing. The summary lists certainty to renew as the customer need and additional anti-selection as the insurer's risk, and section 4.3 shows they are one item priced from two sides. That pattern recurs throughout the track: every guarantee a policyholder values is an option they can exercise selectively.

The Feynman version. Suppose you sell someone ten years of life cover, and you promise that at the end they can have another ten years at a price agreed today, no medical questions asked. Ten years later, everybody comes back to look at your price. The ones who are still healthy check what a new insurer would charge them and mostly find something cheaper, so they leave. The ones who have since been diagnosed with something cannot get cover anywhere, so they all stay.

You have kept the sick ones and lost the healthy ones, and you promised not to reprice. So you have to charge for that in the first ten years, before anybody knows who will be which. If four out of five healthy people walk away, the people left behind die at nearly twice the rate you originally assumed, and that is exactly the loading you needed to have collected.

Which is the general shape of it. The thing the customer is paying for is the right to make a choice later, and they will only make it when it suits them, so the price of the promise is the damage done by them choosing well.

Step 3

The mental model

Three assurance contracts and one option on a contract, and the whole family follows from a single question: does the contract pay on survival?

A term assurance pays only on death within the term and nothing on survival. A whole life assurance pays on death whenever it comes, so survival is a delay rather than an escape. An endowment pays on survival to a date and may pay on earlier death, so it pays in both branches. That progression generates everything else.

It generates the risk lists, which section 4.4 shows nest exactly: add a savings element and investment risk appears, add a survival benefit and longevity risk appears. It generates the reserve sizes, since a contract that will certainly pay must accumulate towards the payment while one that may never pay need not. And through the reserve it generates section 4.1's sum at risk, because the reserve is the part of the benefit already funded and the sum at risk is the rest.

The one thing it does not generate is the capital requirement, which is section 4.2's point. Capital depends on the premium as well as the reserve, and the premium is set by the risk rather than by the saving, so the ordering by capital is not the ordering by reserve. Holding those two apart is the main analytical discipline this lecture asks for.

Step 4

The detail

4.1 Two risks the summary defines as differences, and can therefore be measured

The summary gives mortality risk as the difference between the death benefit and the reserve, and withdrawal risk as the difference between the surrender benefit and the asset share. Both are arithmetic rather than verbal, so compute them.

   the product, at year 15   death       reserve   the sum at risk   as a share of the
   of a 25-year term         benefit     held      on a death        death benefit
   term assurance            100000.0000  800.0000        99200.0000              0.9920
   whole life                100000.0000 28000.0000        72000.0000              0.7200
   endowment, with-profits   100000.0000 52000.0000        48000.0000              0.4800
   endowment, near maturity  100000.0000 88000.0000        12000.0000              0.1200

A term assurance has 99200.0000 at risk, which is 0.9920 of the benefit, and an endowment near maturity only 12000.0000 or 0.1200. That is a factor of 8.2667 across four contracts with identical death benefits. So the summary's micro-risk lists are not arbitrary: mortality risk appears on every list and its magnitude differs by nearly an order of magnitude.

That also explains why the endowment's list carries both mortality and longevity risk while the others carry only mortality.

   who lives or dies   what a term       what a whole life   what an endowment
                       assurance pays    assurance pays      pays
   dies in the term      the sum assured     the sum assured   the sum assured
   survives the term             nothing the sum assured later   the sum assured NOW

A term assurance pays 0.0000 on survival and an endowment pays the full 100000.0000. So a term writer loses only when lives die early, and an endowment writer loses both when they die early and when they live to collect. The risk list is derived rather than memorised, which is the useful way to hold it.

Now withdrawal risk, which the summary lists for a product that has no surrender value at all. With nothing paid back, the difference it names is zero less the asset share, and an early asset share is negative.

   year of withdrawal   surrender   asset share   the summary's        who loses
   from a term policy   benefit     accumulated   difference
   1                         0.0000    -3200.0000            3200.0000   the INSURER

A withdrawal in year one leaves the insurer 3200.0000 short, because the initial expenses have not been recovered and nothing is clawed back. A withdrawal in year fifteen costs the policyholder 2400.0000, because a positive asset share is forfeited. So withdrawal risk on a product with no surrender value is real and changes sides partway through the term, which is why the summary lists it and why it attaches the note about decreasing term assurance, where the falling benefit brings that crossover forward.

4.2 A small reserve does not mean a small capital requirement

The summary's capital note for term assurance pairs a small reserve size with high initial costs and a warning that solvency margins might require significant capital. Read carelessly that looks contradictory. Apply lecture 1's identity per unit of premium and it resolves.

   the product          reserve   initial   annual    new business   strain per unit
                        at outset expense   premium   strain         of premium
   term assurance          50.0000  400.0000  200.0000       250.0000            1.2500
   whole life             300.0000  700.0000  900.0000       100.0000            0.1111
   endowment              300.0000  600.0000 1000.0000      -100.0000           -0.1000
   single-premium endowment 9000.0000  500.0000 10000.0000      -500.0000           -0.0500

Term assurance has the smallest reserve of the four at 50 and the largest strain per unit of premium at 1.2500. The single-premium endowment has the largest reserve at 9000 and a strain of -0.0500, which releases capital. So reserve size and capital requirement are not the same thing and can point opposite ways.

The mechanism is that the premium is the other term. A term assurance is cheap, which the summary lists as a customer need, and cheapness is exactly what makes its strain large relative to what it collects.

   annual premium   initial expense   reserve   strain   strain per unit   is the product
   charged          as a share of     at outset         of premium        cheap to sell?
   200.0000                    2.0000   50.0000 250.0000            1.2500   yes, very
   400.0000                    1.0000   50.0000  50.0000            0.1250   moderately
   700.0000                    0.5714   50.0000 -250.0000           -0.3571   moderately
   1200.0000                   0.3333   50.0000 -750.0000           -0.6250   no

With the reserve held constant at 50 throughout, the strain ratio falls from 1.2500 to -0.6250 purely on the premium charged. So the summary's three capital notes for term assurance are one point: a small reserve, a high initial cost and a small premium, where the first pulls the strain down and the other two pull it up harder.

Which reverses a natural reading of the product list. An endowment looks capital-hungry because its reserve is large and a term assurance looks cheap because its reserve is tiny. Per unit of premium the ordering is the other way round: protection is the capital-intensive line and savings business largely finances itself. That is the single most useful thing in this lecture for reading a life insurer's accounts.

4.3 The renewal option's anti-selection risk has an exact price

Renewable and convertible term carries one extra micro risk in the summary's list: additional anti-selection on renewal, because there is sometimes no further medical underwriting. Price it. Take a pool 90 per cent standard at a mortality of 0.0050 and 10 per cent impaired at 0.0250, giving an original 0.0070. At renewal a healthy life can buy fresh cover elsewhere and an impaired one cannot.

   share of healthy   healthy lives   impaired lives   mortality of the   as a multiple of
   lives who leave    remaining       remaining        renewing pool      the original pool
   0.0000                     0.900000         0.100000           0.007000              1.0000
   0.4000                     0.540000         0.100000           0.008125              1.1607
   0.8000                     0.180000         0.100000           0.012143              1.7347

With nobody leaving, the renewing pool has exactly the original mortality. With 80 per cent of healthy lives leaving it rises to 1.7347 times, and if every healthy life leaves it reaches 3.5714, which is the impaired rate itself. So the option the customer values as certainty to renew is the insurer's exposure to exactly that certainty being exercised selectively.

   lives who leave    1000 of cover    premium assumed  at OUTSET           the base cost

The loading required is exactly the proportional rise in the renewing pool's mortality, verified at every level of selection. So the price of the guarantee is the anti-selection it invites, and it must be collected before the selection happens. That is why the summary says the capital requirement is higher than basic term and depends on the additional reserve needed at the start and at the end of the pre-renewal contract: both dates, because the money is taken at one and spent at the other.

And the insurer cannot escape by underwriting at renewal after all, because that removes the only feature the customer was buying. The summary lists certainty to convert or renew as the customer need, so an insurer reserving the right to reprice has sold a plain term assurance with extra paperwork. The guarantee and the exposure are the same object.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The habit worth forming is to compute the sum at risk before discussing a contract's mortality exposure, because the face amount is almost never the relevant number. Section 4.1 shows two contracts with identical 100000 benefits differing by a factor of 8.2667 in what a death actually costs, and that ratio is what drives the reinsurance decision, the mortality stress test and the capital held for mortality risk. An analysis that quotes sums assured has quoted the wrong quantity.

On section 4.2, the practitioner's point is that the strain ratio is the number to demand when somebody proposes a growth plan. A protection-led growth strategy at 1.2500 of strain per unit of premium needs external capital in proportion to sales, and a savings-led one at -0.1000 funds itself and releases more. Both can be profitable and only one can be scaled without a rights issue. Life insurers that grew rapidly into protection markets and then needed capital were not badly run; they were running a strategy whose capital arithmetic they had not put on the same page as their sales targets.

On the renewal option, the professional judgement is that guaranteed insurability is the most under-priced feature in life insurance, because its cost arrives decades after its premium and is invisible in the interim. Section 4.3's loading of 0.7347 of the base claim cost at a plausible level of selection is very large, and an insurer that has not tested its renewal experience against a no-selection assumption does not know whether it charged enough. The test is cheap: compare the mortality of renewing lives with that of new entrants at the same age.

One limit on section 4.3. The model has two classes of life and treats selection as a single decision at one date, so the 1.7347 multiple is illustrative of the mechanism rather than a measurement. Real impairment is a continuum, some healthy lives renew out of inertia, and selection operates gradually rather than at a moment. What generalises is the direction and the identity: the required loading equals the proportional deterioration in the renewing pool, whatever produces that deterioration, so measuring the pool is equivalent to pricing the option.

And a limit on section 4.1. The asset share figures are illustrative and the crossover year depends on the expense loading and the premium pattern, so the sign change between year three and year eight is a property of the example rather than a general date. The structure holds: an asset share starts negative because initial expenses precede recoveries and turns positive once they are recovered, so withdrawal risk always changes sides at some point in a regular-premium contract. Where that point falls is a pricing question.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The strain identity section 4.2 applies per unit of premium is in the product framework, which also established that a life insurer's growth is limited by capital rather than demand. The product bases each contract can be written on, and the with-profits bonus machinery behind the endowment's reserve growth, are in product bases and surplus distribution. The reserve and asset share concepts are developed in contingencies, particularly the reserves and profit testing material, and the mortality rates section 4.3 selects between are in actuarial mathematics. The anti-selection mechanism is the same adverse selection that collapses a market in the principles of financial regulation, where the threshold was exactly 2.

Where this goes next. Lecture 4 covers annuities, where lecture 1's strain identity reverses because an immediate annuity takes its whole premium at outset, and where this lecture's longevity risk becomes the main exposure rather than a secondary one. Lectures 5 and 6 cover income protection and critical illness, where the anti-selection machinery of section 4.3 becomes the central design problem rather than an option feature, since both depend on a claim definition the policyholder knows more about than the insurer. Lecture 7 covers long-term care and lecture 8 group products, where the concentration risk the summary attaches to group endowments is developed.

Where the bank sits opposite. Section 4.1's sum at risk is the life insurance analogue of exposure at default, and the parallel is exact: neither industry manages the face amount, both manage the amount that would actually be lost, and both compute it as a promise less what is already funded or secured. The banking version is in A-IRB capital modelling, where loss given default plays the role the reserve plays here. Section 4.3's anti-selection on renewal also has a direct banking counterpart in the committed facility: a borrower draws on a pre-agreed line precisely when their credit has deteriorated and they cannot refinance elsewhere, so the option is exercised selectively and must be priced at inception, which is the credit conversion factor machinery in the same lecture. And section 4.2's finding that the cheap product is the capital-hungry one echoes the fixed-cost incidence result in the principles of financial regulation, where a fixed compliance cost fell hardest on the smallest participant.

Numbers used here. The four contracts with their descriptions, surrender value positions, customer needs, product types, micro risks and capital requirement notes, together with the definitions of mortality risk as the death benefit less the reserve and withdrawal risk as the surrender benefit less the asset share, and the additional anti-selection risk on renewal, are all transcribed from chapters 2 and 3 of the ASSA F102 concept summary in the user's notes. No observation is recorded against the summary in this lecture. Everything it states is correct, including the capital note for term assurance that appears self-contradictory and turns out to be three consistent parts of one point once the strain is taken per unit of premium. What the summary omits is magnitude rather than accuracy: it does not say that the sum at risk varies by a factor of 8.2667 across its own four contracts, that the strain ratio ordering reverses the reserve ordering, or that the renewal loading equals the proportional deterioration in the renewing pool. Everything numerical is invented for illustration: a 100000 death benefit against reserves of 800 to 88000, asset shares from -3200 to 2400 across a term, reserves of 50 to 9000 with expenses of 400 to 700 and premiums of 200 to 10000, and a pool 90 per cent standard at a 0.005 mortality with 10 per cent impaired at 0.025 over a ten-year renewal term. Twenty claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions, all twenty passed, and none can pass without the computation. One claim was flagged as unfalsifiable by a vacuity check, having been written as a literal true, and was replaced by an assertion comparing the two survival payouts directly. Every headline finding states the region in which it holds: the sum at risk result is exact for any reserve and benefit, with the factor of 8.2667 specific to the illustrative reserves; the strain ratio result holds for any expense and premium level, with the sign changing wherever the premium exceeds the reserve plus expenses; and the anti-selection model has two classes and a single selection date, so its 1.7347 multiple illustrates the mechanism while the identity between the required loading and the pool's deterioration is general.
