The summary's three claims about bonus methods are one claim, and all three check out

Its example rates are not comparable, since 2 per cent simple reaches 140.0000 and 1 per cent compound only 122.0190. Step 1

The 30-second version

Lecture 1 counted which risks each product basis leaves with the policyholder. This lecture takes the with-profits basis apart, because it is the one where the insurer has discretion and therefore the one where the arithmetic decides something. The summary gives three ways to calculate a reversionary bonus and makes three comparative claims about them, and all three claims turn out to be exactly right and to be the same claim.

First, a measurement problem the summary creates for itself. Its example rates are 2 per cent simple, 1 per cent compound and a super-compound two-tier of 1 and 2 per cent, and those reach terminal benefits of 140.0000, 122.0190 and 124.2974. They differ by 17.9810, so comparing the paths directly confounds deferral with generosity. Solve each method instead for the rate delivering the same terminal benefit of 150.000000, which needs 0.025000 simple, 0.020480 compound and only 0.017632 on the basic benefit for super-compound.

Now the claims. The summary says super-compound defers more than compound and compound more than simple. With terminal benefits equalised, at year ten the three stand at 125.0000, 122.4745 and 120.7107, and the strict ordering holds at years five, ten and fifteen. It says simple weights early profits and super-compound late ones: simple delivers 0.5000 of its total bonus in the first half against super-compound's 0.4142. And it says super-compound keeps reserves low and is more capital efficient: the present value of the guarantee is 246.7509 under simple and 214.1788 under super-compound, a saving of 13.2004 per cent on an identical terminal benefit.

Those are three bullet points in the summary and one fact underneath: a benefit not yet granted is a benefit not yet reserved for. All three orderings follow from the single choice of which base the percentage applies to, and the capital consequence connects straight to lecture 1, since a smaller reserve is a smaller term in the strain identity. The choice of bonus method is a capital decision dressed as a distribution decision.

The summary then names the limit itself. Deferring everything to a terminal bonus takes the guarantee's present value to 0.0000, so it costs no capital until declared, and four of its six listed consequences of deferral are benefits to the insurer. But one is a cost, unpopularity and harder marketing, and the sixth is the resolution: smooth what you defer. Because once policyholders expect a terminal bonus, it is a guarantee in everything but name.

Step 2

Why this matters

The with-profits basis is where actuarial discretion actually lives. A without-profits contract is priced once and the answer is fixed; a unit-linked contract passes investment outcomes straight through. With-profits sits between them, and every year somebody decides how much of the surplus to hand over and in what form. Section 4.2 shows that decision is not primarily about generosity but about the timing of a guarantee, and the timing has a capital price.

The methodological point matters as much as the result. The summary invites a comparison of three methods and supplies rates that make the comparison meaningless, because a method giving a bigger benefit will naturally look more generous early. Equalising the outcome first is what turns a plausible story into a tested claim, and it is the general move: when comparing designs, hold the outcome fixed and compare the paths. That is worth carrying beyond this lecture.

For the exam, the useful content is that super-compound is not a piece of arcana. It exists because it is the cheapest way to promise a given eventual benefit, and section 4.2 gives the saving as 13.2004 per cent of the guarantee's present value. A candidate who can say why, in terms of which base each tier applies to, has understood with-profits design rather than memorised a list.

The Feynman version. You promise somebody a hundred pounds now and you want it to grow to a hundred and fifty over twenty years. Once you promise an increase you cannot take it back, and you have to put money aside for every promise you have made.

So you have a choice about pacing. Add the same slice every year and you have promised a lot early, which means a lot set aside early. Or add a small slice of the original hundred and a larger slice of whatever you have already added: at the start there is barely anything added, so the promises stay small, and they only accelerate near the end. Same hundred and fifty at the finish, much less locked up along the way.

That is all super-compound is. And it explains why it looks like an accountant's trick and is actually the whole point: the promise you have not yet made is the promise you do not have to fund.

Step 3

The mental model

Hold surplus distribution as a question about when a promise becomes binding, because that single question organises the whole of the summary's chapters 8 and 9.

Surplus arises from three sources the summary names: savings profit, insurance profit, and surplus from without-profits or unit-linked business. What happens to it is then three choices in sequence. How much to distribute, which is the amount. When, which is the timing. And in what form, which is cash, a premium reduction or a benefit increase.

Each of those choices has a capital consequence and the mechanism is always the same: a guaranteed amount must be reserved for and an ungranted one need not be. That is why the summary's solvency claim is arithmetic rather than prudential opinion, and it is why a terminal bonus is the cheapest form of distribution and a declared reversionary bonus the dearest.

The constraint on all of it is policyholders' reasonable expectations, and section 4.3 makes the mechanism precise. Two of the four things a with-profits policyholder expects are neither guaranteed nor reserved for, and that gap is what the constraint is about. An expectation is not a liability and cannot be ignored either, which is why the summary requires distribution to be equitable, and defines equitable as fair but not equal.

Two of the four are expected without being guaranteed or reserved for, and that gap is what policyholders' reasonable expectations means as a constraint. The summary requires distribution to be consistent with it and equitable, which it defines as fair but not equal, and that distinction is the whole of with-profits practice: different policyholders get different amounts by design, and the design has to be defensible rather than uniform.

The summary's remaining machinery follows from the same idea. Bonus earning capacity is the rate a contract can sustain over its future lifetime, and the summary notes that long-term reversionary rates are set below it precisely to leave room for a terminal bonus. That is section 4.2's capital result stated as a practice rule: hold the guaranteed rate below what you can afford, and pay the difference at the end where it costs nothing until it is paid.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The habit worth forming is the one section 4.1 demonstrates: before comparing two designs, equalise the outcome. The summary's own example rates would have supported an entirely wrong story, because 2 per cent simple pays more than 1 per cent compound at every point including the end, so the simple method looks both more generous and less deferred when only the first is true. Holding the terminal benefit fixed is what separates the two, and the same move applies to comparing charge structures, premium patterns or guarantee designs anywhere in this track.

On the capital result, the practitioner's point is that 13.2004 per cent of a guarantee's present value is a large number in an industry where lecture 1 showed new business already consumes more capital than a year's premium. A with-profits insurer choosing super-compound over simple is buying the same policyholder outcome for materially less locked-up capital, which means it can write more business from the same capital base. That is the real reason the two-tier structure exists, and it is worth stating plainly rather than presenting it as a technical variant.

On expectations, the professional judgement is that the gap in section 4.3's table is the most dangerous thing on a with-profits balance sheet, precisely because it appears nowhere on it. A terminal bonus paid consistently for fifteen years is not a liability in any accounting sense and is very nearly a promise in every practical one, and the cost of disappointing it is a conduct problem rather than a solvency one. The summary's answer, smoothing, manages the expectation and does not remove it.

One limit on section 4.2. The three paths assume level bonus rates throughout, and the summary itself notes that level rates are unusual in practice since economic and investment conditions change. Real declarations vary year by year, so the neat orderings would be perturbed in any actual series. What survives is the structural result, which is that for any given rate schedule the method weighting accumulated bonuses defers most, because the base it applies to starts near zero and grows.

And a limit on section 4.3. The present value of the guarantee is computed at a single 6 per cent discount rate, so the 13.2004 per cent saving depends on that rate: a higher rate discounts the later promises more heavily and shrinks the difference between the methods, while a lower rate widens it. The direction is unaffected, since super-compound's promises are strictly later at every point, so it is cheaper at any positive discount rate. The magnitude is not, and it should be quoted with the rate attached.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The strain identity that section 4.2's capital result feeds into is in the product framework, which also counted which BIIFIL risks the with-profits basis leaves with the policyholder and found investment risk sitting with the insurer, which is what creates the surplus this lecture distributes. The reserving and profit-emergence machinery is developed in contingencies, particularly the reserves and profit testing material. The discounting of a deferred promise is standard from financial mathematics, and the smoothing that section 4.3 identifies as the resolution is the same anchoring mechanism quantified in unlisted and illiquid indices, where a smoothed series understated its true variance by half.

Where this goes next. Lecture 3 covers assurances, the endowment, whole life and term contracts, and applies this lecture's bases to each: the same promise on a without-profits, with-profits or unit-linked basis is three different products for both parties. Lecture 4 on annuities is where the strain identity of lecture 1 reverses, since an immediate annuity takes its whole premium at outset. The revalorisation and contribution methods this lecture named but did not work through belong to the later surplus distribution material, and the unit-linked charge structures belong with lecture 3, where they can be set against the with-profits bonus machinery developed here.

Where the bank sits opposite. A bank has no with-profits analogue, and the absence is instructive. A bank's depositor receives a contractual rate and its shareholder takes the residual, so there is no discretionary middle where surplus is allocated between generations of customers. That is why a bank needs no equivalent of policyholders' reasonable expectations and why its capital management, set out in capital management, is a question of ratios rather than of equity between customer cohorts. The closest banking parallel to section 4.2's result is the treatment of a guarantee: a committed but undrawn facility consumes less capital than a drawn one for exactly the reason a terminal bonus consumes less than a declared one, which is the credit conversion factor machinery in A-IRB capital modelling. And the smoothing question has a direct banking counterpart in provisioning, where the choice between recognising expected losses early or late is the same timing decision with the same capital consequence, treated in IFRS 9 impairments.
