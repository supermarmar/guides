The first thirteen weeks of cover are the worst value in the contract

The summary gives four reasons for a deferred period and lists two of them, lower claim costs and lower administration costs, as though they were the same size. They are not. A thirteen-week deferred period removes 0.8300 of sickness episodes and only 0.2403 of the benefit cost, a ratio of 3.4535, because sickness durations are skewed. Price the two tranches separately and the first thirteen weeks cost 1.3035 per rand of benefit delivered against 1.0197 for everything after them, a factor of 1.2783, and the ordering survives even a trivial administration fee. That is why the deferred period is never zero.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Income protection is the first product on this syllabus where the policyholder influences the claim after it has started. Every earlier contract turned on an event outside anybody's control, death or survival, and priced it from a table. Here the benefit continues until the claimant recovers, and how quickly they recover depends on what they are being paid not to. That single fact generates the deferred period, the replacement ratio, the occupational definition, and the whole apparatus of over-insurance controls, and four parts of it are computable.

Start with the deferred period, because it is the design feature the summary explains least and the arithmetic explains best. Sickness durations are skewed: most episodes are short and most weeks of benefit are owed to a few long ones. So a thirteen-week deferred period removes 0.8300 of episodes and 0.2403 of the benefit cost, and the summary's two cost reasons differ by a factor of 3.4535 rather than being parallel. Price the tranches separately with a fixed administration cost per claim, and the first thirteen weeks cost 1.3035 per rand of benefit against 1.0197 afterwards. The cheapest cover to omit is the cover nobody notices omitting.

Then the replacement ratio. The summary warns that escalation rates should not exceed expected earnings rates, and calls that prudence. It is more than prudence: it is the exact condition for the ratio not to drift. A benefit escalating at 0.0500 against earnings at 0.0300 takes a ratio set at 0.6000 to full replacement in 26.5621 years with nothing else changing, and across nine combinations of starting ratio and escalation gap the ratio drifts up in exactly the cases where escalation exceeds earnings. The starting level decides when the problem arrives, never whether it arrives.

Then the claim definition. The summary lists four and orders their cost, highest for own occupation and lowest for any occupation, without saying why the market settled on the third. Because cost sits in the long tail and claimants sit in the short bulk: an initial period of 104 weeks captures 0.5549 of the available saving while leaving 0.9500 of claimants paid exactly as under the dearest definition. And the initial period reaches both end stops exactly, so the four definitions are one product with a dial.

Finally the premium guarantee. The summary says a guaranteed premium is very expensive and that morbidity guarantee loadings exceed mortality ones. Split the uncertainty and both claims follow from the part that does not diversify: the morbidity-to-mortality uncertainty ratio rises with portfolio size, from 2.4210 at one policy to 3.9999 at a million, converging on the ratio of the two parameter uncertainties. Scale helps a mortality guarantee and barely helps a morbidity one.

Step 2

Why this matters

The deferred period result is the clearest example on this syllabus of a product feature that looks like a cost-cutting compromise and is actually the efficient design. A buyer offered cover from day one at a modestly higher premium is being offered the worst value tranche in the contract, and would be better off buying a longer benefit term or a higher benefit with the same money. That reframes the sales conversation: the deferred period is not an exclusion to be apologised for, it is the reason the rest of the cover is affordable. The same arithmetic explains why every insurance line with per-claim administration costs has an excess, a deductible or a waiting period, and why removing one is always disproportionately expensive.

The escalation result matters because it turns a soft warning into a testable condition. An insurer offering benefit escalation at a fixed 0.0500 against a workforce whose earnings grow at 0.0300 has not taken a small risk of over-insurance, it has guaranteed over-insurance on a known timetable. Anybody reviewing an income protection product should compute the drift directly, because the answer is a closed form in two growth rates and the starting ratio, and there is nothing to estimate. The same test applies to any contract where a benefit and its reference quantity grow at different fixed rates, which includes escalating annuities against inflation and index-linked policies against the index they claim to track.

The claim definition result matters because it shows that a product taxonomy can conceal a continuum. Examiners ask for the four definitions and their cost ordering, and the ordering is worth knowing, but the professionally useful fact is that the initial period spans the entire range continuously and reaches both ends exactly. So the pricing question is never which of four definitions to use, it is where on one axis to sit, and the answer follows from the shape of the duration distribution rather than from a preference about fairness.

The guarantee result matters because it contradicts an instinct that scale solves uncertainty. It does, for the part of the risk that is independent across policies, and not at all for the part that is a shared misunderstanding of the underlying rate. An insurer with a million policies has essentially eliminated policy-level noise and is left holding pure parameter uncertainty, and on a morbidity risk that residue is four times the mortality equivalent. Which is why the largest insurers are the most reluctant to guarantee morbidity rates for long terms, a pattern that looks like conservatism and is arithmetic.

The Feynman version. Think about what it costs to run a claim. Somebody has to read the doctor's note, open a file, check the policy, set up the payment, and close it again. That work is the same whether you end up paying for two weeks or two years. Now think about who claims. Nearly everyone who claims at all has a short illness; a handful have long ones. So if you pay from day one, you are doing all that paperwork over and over for tiny payments, and the paperwork costs more per pound delivered than the long claims ever do.

Which means the fairest-sounding version of the product, cover from the moment you fall ill, is the version where the least of the customer's premium reaches the customer. Making them wait three months does not save you much in benefits, only a quarter of them. It saves you nearly all the paperwork, because five sixths of the illnesses never become claims at all.

And here is the part that makes this product different from everything before it. If you pay someone nearly all their salary while they are off sick, some of them get better more slowly. Not because they are dishonest, mostly, but because going back is unpleasant and there is no longer a financial reason to hurry. So the more generously you pay, the longer you pay, and the cost goes up faster than the benefit does. Pay ninety per cent instead of fifty and the benefit is not quite twice as big, but the bill is nine times as big.

Step 3

The mental model

Hold four pictures. The first is a skewed distribution with a fixed toll at the gate. Sickness durations are a mass of short episodes with a thin long tail; the benefit cost lives in the tail and the administration cost lives in the mass. Any feature that filters by duration therefore trades the two off at a very unfavourable exchange rate for the buyer at the short end, which is the whole content of the deferred period, the waiting period, and the linked-claims period.

The second is a race between two indices. The replacement ratio is a quotient of a benefit and a salary, both growing, so its path depends on nothing except which grows faster. Every over-insurance source in the summary's list is a way of making the numerator grow faster than the denominator: escalation exceeding earnings, salary stalling, tax falling on the benefit, undisclosed income adding to it. Four sources, one mechanism.

The third is a dial rather than a menu. Own occupation and any occupation are the two end stops, and the initial period slides continuously between them. Nothing about the product changes across that range except when the retest happens, so the four names in the summary are four settings of one parameter and the market's preference for the middle follows from where cost and claimants sit.

The fourth is a variance decomposition. Any uncertainty about a future claim rate splits into the part that averages out across policies and the part that is a shared error in the rate itself. Volume kills the first and leaves the second untouched. So a premium guarantee is a sale of the second component, its price does not fall with scale, and it compounds with the term because the shared error has longer to be wrong.

Those four pictures also explain why this product carries moral hazard as a named micro risk when nothing before it did. Death is not influenced by the payout. Recovery is. Once the claimant's behaviour enters the cash flows, the insurer is pricing a response function rather than a hazard rate, and every control in the summary's over-insurance list exists to keep that response function inside the region where it was priced.

Step 4

The detail

4.1 The deferred period's two cost reasons differ by a factor of three

The summary gives four reasons for a deferred period: integrating with short-term employer benefits, reducing the cost of claims and therefore premiums, reducing administration costs and therefore premiums, and meeting the customer's true need, which it glosses as claiming for serious ill health. The middle two are both costs and both said to reduce premiums, which invites reading them as one point stated twice. They are very different in size.

Take a sickness duration distribution in weeks with the shape every published continuance table has, a mass of short episodes and a thin long tail, and vary the deferred period. Two quantities move: the weeks of benefit paid, and the share of episodes that survive long enough to become claims at all.

   13                            17.2900            0.2403            0.1700              0.8300
   26                            15.0800            0.3374            0.1000              0.9000
   52                            12.4800            0.4517            0.0500              0.9500

The duration distribution is invented for illustration, with episode lengths from one to 520 weeks and most weight on the short ones. Both shares rise with the deferred period, at very different speeds.

A thirteen-week deferred period removes 0.8300 of episodes and only 0.2403 of the benefit cost, a ratio of 3.4535. So the administration saving is the large one and the claim cost saving is the small one, which is why a deferred period survives in markets where premium competition is not the binding constraint, and why insurers resist shortening it even when they can afford the extra benefit.

Which raises the question the summary does not ask. Why is the deferred period never zero, even at the top of the market? Because the first tranche of cover is the most expensive per rand of benefit delivered. Price the two tranches separately, with a fixed administration cost per claim whatever its length.

                           per episode    administer                              of benefit
   the first 13 weeks           5470.0000         0.8300    1660.0000    7130.0000          1.3035

A weekly benefit of 1000 and a claim administration cost of 2000, both invented for illustration. The whole cover necessarily sits between its two tranches.

The first thirteen weeks cost 1.3035 per rand of benefit delivered and everything after them 1.0197, a factor of 1.2783. The benefit is identical from the claimant's side and the cost of supplying it is not, because administration is charged per claim and the short claims are nearly all the claims. So a deferred period of zero sells the worst-value cover in the contract, and the summary's fourth reason, meeting the customer's true need, is the same point from the buyer's side rather than a piece of paternalism.

And the ordering does not depend on the administration cost being large. Drop it from 2000 per claim to 1.0000 and the ratio is still 1.0001, above one. It is the per-claim structure that does the work, not the size of the fee, so the two tranches converge only in the limit as administration goes to zero. That generalises: any cost charged per claim rather than per unit of benefit makes short cover dearer per unit delivered, whatever the level of that cost, which is why excesses and deductibles appear across every insurance line with claims handling.

One boundary. The distribution is invented, so the 3.4535 ratio and the 1.2783 factor are illustrative magnitudes. What is general is the direction, which follows from skew alone: whenever the duration distribution has more mass below the deferred period than above it, the episode share removed exceeds the benefit share removed. The result would reverse only for a distribution with most weight at long durations, which no sickness table has.

4.2 The escalation prudence rule is the no-drift condition

The summary defines the replacement ratio as post-claim income to pre-claim income net of tax, states that a higher ratio means a lower incentive to return to work and worse morbidity experience, and lists four sources of over-insurance. Three of those four happen after the contract starts: pre-claim salary not keeping up with benefits, tax reduction on claims, and undisclosed post-claim income. So the ratio drifts even where the insurer set it correctly at outset.

The summary also says, under escalation, that it is not prudent to have escalation rates exceeding expected earnings rates. Put the two together and the reason for the warning becomes exact. Take a benefit escalating at 0.0500 against earnings growing at 0.0300, starting from a ratio of 0.6000.

   inception     index      index      ratio         yet?

   5                 1.2763     1.1593        0.6606   no
   10                1.6289     1.3439        0.7272   no

   26                3.5557     2.1566        0.9892   no

Escalation and earnings rates are invented for illustration. Nothing about the policyholder, the illness or the insurer changes down this table.

The crossing year is 26.5621, and a benefit set prudently at 0.6000 of salary reaches full replacement purely because the two indices differ by 0.0200 a year. That is not a risk the insurer runs, it is an outcome the insurer has scheduled. Now the part worth remembering, which is that the condition for no drift does not depend on the level of the ratio at all.

Three starting ratios against three escalation gaps. The drift column depends only on the comparison of the two rates.

In every one of the nine combinations the ratio drifts up if and only if the escalation rate exceeds the earnings rate. The starting ratio changes when the problem arrives and never whether it arrives. So the summary's prudence rule is not a conservative margin around a judgement, it is the exact condition for the ratio to be stable, and an insurer that breaks it has built over-insurance into the contract at outset rather than risking it later. Stated that way it is also easier to remember and impossible to fudge.

Which leaves the question of why a maximum ratio belongs among the summary's financial solutions rather than its operational ones. Make the moral hazard explicit: let the annual recovery rate fall linearly with the ratio, reaching zero at full replacement where there is no financial reason to recover.

   ratio         recovery rate of claim         amount           of one claim    of benefit
   0.2500               0.4500           2.2222       25000.0000      55555.5556          2.2222

A recovery rate of 0.6000 times one minus the ratio, on a pre-claim income of 100000. The response function is invented; its direction is the summary's own statement.

The ratio rises by a factor of 1.8000 from 0.5000 to 0.9000 and the expected cost by 9.0000, from 166666.6667 to 1500000.0000. The cost is convex in the ratio and grows without bound at full replacement while the benefit stays linear and bounded. So a maximum ratio is a solvency device rather than a fairness device, and the summary is right to file it under financial solutions.

And the moral hazard is what makes the limit necessary rather than merely tidy. A policyholder who priced their own recovery behaviour would never ask for cover at 0.9000, because the premium rises 5.0000 times faster than the benefit across that range. The limit exists precisely because the buyer does not internalise the cost of their own response to it, which is what moral hazard means and why the summary lists it as a micro risk in its own right alongside morbidity. Morbidity risk is uncertainty about the transition rates; moral hazard is the claimant moving them.

One boundary. The linear response function is a modelling choice, and a real recovery rate does not fall to exactly zero at full replacement. What survives any plausible alternative is the convexity and the direction, because the benefit enters the cost linearly while the duration also rises with the benefit, so the product of the two rises faster than either. The unboundedness is an artefact of the linear form; the convexity is not.

4.3 The four claim definitions are one dial

The summary distinguishes the effect of occupation on the price charged, where occupations are grouped into classes reflecting claims experience, from its effect on the claim payment, where the contract defines what incapacity means. Four definitions are given: inability to perform one's own occupation; inability to perform one's own or any other suited occupation; inability to perform one's own for an initial period followed by inability to perform any; and inability to perform any occupation. The cost is stated as highest for own and lowest for any. The market standard is the third, and the summary does not say why.

Model it. Suppose some claimants could do a different suited job and so recover, on the any-occupation test, in a fraction of their own-occupation duration, while the rest could not work at all under either test. The hybrid pays on the own-occupation basis for the initial period and on the any-occupation basis afterwards.

   the claim definition          expected weeks   share of the      share of claimants
                                 of benefit       maximum saving    paid as own-occupation
   own occupation                         22.7600            0.0000                 1.0000
   own for 104 weeks, then any            17.4560            0.5549                 0.9500
   any occupation                         13.2008            1.0000                 0.0000

Six in ten claimants assumed able to do some other suited occupation, recovering in 0.3000 of their own-occupation duration. Both figures are invented for illustration.

The summary's ordering holds, with own occupation dearest at 22.7600 weeks and any occupation cheapest at 13.2008. The hybrid at 17.4560 captures 0.5549 of the available saving while leaving 0.9500 of claimants paid exactly as under the dearest definition, because they never reach the 104-week retest. That is the asymmetry: the insurer's cost is concentrated where the claimants are not. A product that saves more than half the money while changing nothing for nineteen claimants in twenty is not a compromise, it is a dominant design.

The initial period as a continuous parameter. The cost rises monotonically and the two end stops are reached exactly.

The cost rises monotonically with the initial period, from 14.7884 weeks at thirteen to 22.7600 at 520, and both end stops are exact: a zero initial period costs 13.2008, which is the any-occupation cost, and one covering the longest possible claim costs 22.7600, which is the own-occupation cost. So the four definitions are not four products, they are one product with a dial, and the summary's cost ordering names the two ends of it.

But a complete cost dial is not a complete product range. The summary's aside about the pianist's fingers names a case the dial cannot express: a pianist who cannot play but could do other work is a total loss under own occupation and has no claim at all under any occupation, so the choice there is genuinely discrete even though the cost function between the two ends is continuous and reaches both exactly. That is why the summary calls own occupation not useful for minor disability risks, which is a statement about the definition failing rather than about it costing. A dial that prices every setting still cannot price a definition that does not describe the loss.

The summary also lists alternatives that ignore occupation entirely: activities of daily living, functional assessment tests, activities of daily working, and personal capability assessment. Those sit outside the dial, because they replace the occupational reference point rather than adjusting when it is tested, and they are what an insurer reaches for when the occupational definition is the thing that has failed.

4.4 The guarantee loading is parameter uncertainty, so volume cannot reduce it

The summary makes three claims about guaranteed premium rates: long-term contracts have large standard deviations, so a guarantee carries a large contingency loading and is very expensive; larger reserves are needed; and guarantee loadings are higher than for mortality products. All three follow from splitting the uncertainty into the part that averages out across policies and the part that does not.

   the portfolio  mean mortality rate  mean morbidity rate  morbidity   near the floor?

   10000                     0.050249             0.200360      3.9873   no

Relative parameter uncertainty of 0.0500 for mortality and 0.2000 for morbidity, with policy-level randomness of 0.5000 and 1.2000. All four figures are invented for illustration.

The ratio rises with portfolio size, from 2.4210 at one policy to 3.9999 at a million, converging on 4.0000, which is the ratio of the two parameter uncertainties and nothing else. So the summary's claim that a morbidity guarantee loading exceeds a mortality one is not about the level of the rates, it is about how well each is known, and the largest insurer suffers the widest relative gap. That inverts an instinct: scale is a powerful argument for guaranteeing mortality and a weak one for guaranteeing morbidity, because scale only removes the component that morbidity has least of in proportion.

A thirty-year guarantee carries 1.0954 of accumulated parameter uncertainty against 0.2000 for one year, a factor of 5.4772, and a two-deviation loading on it is 2.1909 of the expected claim cost. An insurer would have to more than treble the price. That is precisely why the summary says a guaranteed premium is very expensive, why larger reserves are needed, and why reviewable rates exist at all.

But reviewable rates have their own failure, which the summary names: limits on premium increases may dissuade lives with better experience from renewing, and it calls this selective lapsing. Iterate it and the problem is not a one-off loss.

The portfolio rate rises from 0.012400 to 0.022489 over five reviews and heads for the bad lives' own 0.030000. Each increase is justified by the experience the previous increase created. Which recasts the summary's instruction that a review should be for the future rather than to recover past losses: that is not primarily an ethical statement, it is the only thing standing between a review clause and a self-feeding spiral. An insurer that prices each review to recover the last one's shortfall guarantees the next shortfall.

The summary's remaining review points fit the same frame. Its list of legitimate reasons to revise, that diseases become curable, treatment improves, workplaces automate, and employer expectations change, are all changes in the underlying rate rather than in the mix of lives, so acting on them does not accelerate selection. Its observation that some insurers reduce premiums when experience is better than expected is the same mechanism run in reverse, and it is what keeps the good lives in the pool.

4.5 The two reserve claims are about different quantities

The summary's capital section says income protection needs lower reserves than an endowment or whole life because there is a chance benefits will never be paid, and higher reserves because sickness experience is harder to predict. Read as one quantity that is a contradiction, and it is the same shape as lecture 3's apparently self-contradictory capital notes for term assurance. Read as a best estimate plus a margin it is two compatible statements about different components.

The crossover margin is 3.3750. Below it the summary's first claim governs and above it the second does, so both are true and neither is complete without the margin. Which gives a general habit worth carrying: when a summary looks self-contradictory, find the quantity the two halves are measuring and check it is the same one. It usually is not, and the two halves usually turn out to be a best estimate and a margin, a level and a rate, or a total and a per-unit figure.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The habit this lecture should install is to ask, of any threshold in a contract, what it removes from the frequency distribution and what it removes from the severity distribution, and to expect the two answers to differ by a large factor. Deferred periods, excesses, deductibles, minimum claim sizes and attachment points are all the same device, and they all work because frequency and severity live in different parts of a skewed distribution. Anybody who prices one of them by asking only what share of the money it saves has missed the larger half of the answer.

The second habit is to convert every prudence rule into a condition and then test it. The summary's escalation warning reads like judgement and turns out to be an identity: the ratio is stable if and only if the two rates match. Most well-worn actuarial cautions have that character, and finding the condition behind one is worth more than remembering the caution, because a condition can be checked against a specific contract while a caution cannot. It also protects against the failure mode where a rule is applied where it does not bite and ignored where it does.

The third habit is to look for the continuum behind a taxonomy. Four claim definitions turned out to be one parameter with two exact end stops, which means the professional question is never a choice among four labelled products. The same is true of bonus methods in lecture 2, of annuity types in lecture 4, and of most syllabus lists: they enumerate settings, and the settings usually vary continuously. Examiners test the list; practice tests the dial. Both are worth knowing, but only one of them prices anything.

The fourth habit is to decompose uncertainty before deciding whether scale helps. The instinct that a bigger book is a safer book is correct for independent risk and worthless for shared risk, and the guarantee result shows the difference is measurable and can run to a factor of four. Any conversation about whether an insurer is large enough to take a risk should begin by asking which component of the risk is being discussed. That applies as much to a bank's operational risk, where a single control failure hits every transaction at once, as to a morbidity guarantee.

The last habit is to treat the claimant as a participant rather than a subject. Every product before this one priced an event; this one prices a response. Once that is true, the contract's terms are inputs to the very behaviour being modelled, and a change in the benefit level changes the claim duration it will have to fund. The consequence is that the cost function is convex and no single best-estimate assumption describes it, which is why over-insurance controls are financial rather than merely administrative, and why the summary's inclusion of moral hazard as a distinct micro risk alongside morbidity is precise rather than redundant.

Connections

What this assumes. The product bases section 4.1's types refer to, and the with-profit machinery behind the bonus-on-expiry variant, are in product bases and surplus distribution. The anti-selection mechanism that section 4.4's selective lapsing reproduces, and the resolution pattern section 4.5 reuses for two apparently contradictory capital notes, are both in assurances. The reserve and margin decomposition section 4.5 depends on is in the product framework. Section 4.1's duration distribution and section 4.3's retest are multiple-state model problems, developed in multiple state models, where the healthy-sick-dead process and its transition intensities are the standard treatment. Section 4.4's split between parameter uncertainty and policy-level randomness is the credibility and parameter-error material in risk modelling, particularly the reinsurance and parameter uncertainty lecture.

Where this goes next. Lecture 6 covers critical illness, which replaces this lecture's continuing income with a lump sum on a defined condition, and so removes the moral hazard in the recovery rate while introducing a far sharper definitional risk: the claim turns entirely on whether a diagnosis meets a written condition. Lecture 7 covers long-term care, which combines this lecture's continuing benefit and duration risk with that definitional problem and the longevity exposure of lecture 4, which is why it is the hardest product on the syllabus. Lecture 8 covers group business, where the summary's group income protection notes, benefits on gross or net salary, cover for pension and State contributions, and the continuation option, are taken up alongside the group versions of the other products. The morbidity assumptions this lecture treats as given are set in the pricing module, and the reserving basis in the risk measurement module.

Where the bank sits opposite. Section 4.1's deferred period is the same device as a bank's minimum loss threshold in operational risk, where losses below a de minimis level are excluded from the loss data set for exactly this reason: they are numerous, cheap, and cost more to collect and validate than they contribute to the capital number. That threshold and its consequences are in operational risk measurement. Section 4.2's moral hazard has a direct lending counterpart in loan-to-value: the more of a borrower's own money sits in the asset, the harder they work to keep it, so a high advance changes the default rate it is priced against rather than merely covering more of the same risk, which is the affordability and forbearance machinery in A-IRB capital modelling. Section 4.4's decomposition is the same distinction a bank draws between idiosyncratic and systematic credit risk, where diversification removes the first and leaves the second, and it is the reason the asymptotic single risk factor model has a correlation parameter at all. And section 4.4's review spiral is the pricing analogue of a bank raising rates on a deteriorating book and losing exactly the customers who can refinance elsewhere, which is why risk-based repricing on existing exposures is treated with such caution.

No observation is recorded against the summary in this lecture. Its two reserve claims look contradictory and section 4.5 shows they measure different components; its two cost reasons for a deferred period look parallel and section 4.1 shows they differ by a factor of 3.4535, which is an omission of magnitude rather than an error; and its escalation prudence rule turns out to be exactly the right condition rather than an approximation to one. Everything numerical is invented for illustration: a sickness duration distribution over episode lengths from one to 520 weeks with 0.3000 of weight at one week and 0.0200 at 520, a weekly benefit of 1000 with 2000 of administration cost per claim, escalation at 0.0500 against earnings at 0.0300 from a 0.6000 starting ratio, a recovery rate of 0.6000 times one minus the ratio on a 100000 income, six in ten claimants able to work in another occupation at 0.3000 of their own-occupation duration, relative parameter uncertainties of 0.0500 and 0.2000 with policy-level randomness of 0.5000 and 1.2000, a pool eighty per cent good at 0.0080 and twenty per cent bad at 0.0300 losing four in ten good lives per review, and best-estimate reserves of 5000 and 1200. Twenty-eight claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions, all twenty-eight passed, and none can pass without the computation. One claim was flagged as unfalsifiable by a vacuity check, having been written around a literal identity, and was replaced by an assertion that the cost at a 0.9900 ratio exceeds twenty times the cost at 0.5000. A first run also asserted that a 520-week initial period would not reach the own-occupation cost; it reaches it exactly, which is the better result and is what the lecture now states. And a first run mixed weeks with currency in the tranche table, giving a nonsensical cost per rand of benefit; the benefit was converted at the weekly rate and the figures are now 1.3035 against 1.0197. Every headline finding states the region in which it holds: the deferred period result depends on the distribution having more mass below the deferred period than above it, which is true of every sickness table but is a property of the shape rather than a theorem; the convexity of cost in the replacement ratio survives any plausible response function while the unboundedness at full replacement is an artefact of the linear one; the occupational dial's two end stops are exact for any duration distribution, with the 0.5549 saving specific to the illustrative retraining assumptions; and the guarantee result's factor of 4.0000 is the ratio of the assumed parameter uncertainties, with only the direction, that scale widens rather than narrows the gap, being general.
