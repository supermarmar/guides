Premium frequency alone flips the sign of the capital a new policy requires

The summary gives the whole of life insurance finance in one line: the capital needed at outset is the reserve plus initial expenses less the premium received. On identical reserves and expenses an annual premium gives a strain of -100.0000 and a monthly premium gives +816.6667, so the same product consumes or releases capital depending on how the customer chooses to pay. At 816.6667 of strain against 40.0000 of profit per policy, growth cannot be funded from its own year's profit at any volume.

Step 1

The 30-second version

This opens the life insurance track. The summary's first two chapters give a framework rather than a product: a four-stage financial life cycle, a one-line capital identity with five factors attached, four purposes the product range serves, and a six-risk mnemonic applied across four product bases. Everything later in this module is an instance of that framework, so it is worth getting the arithmetic of it right first.

The identity is C0+ = V0+ + e0+ - P0+: the capital needed the moment a policy is written is the reserve that must be set up, plus the initial expenses paid out, less the premium received. Its sign is not a property of the product. A modest saving plan with a 300 reserve, 600 of initial expense and a 1000 annual premium has a strain of -100.0000 and releases capital; raise the initial expense to 900 and it becomes +200.0000; strengthen the reserving basis to 700 instead and it becomes +300.0000.

Of the summary's five factors, premium frequency dominates, and it is the one that changes a sign. A monthly premium delivers a twelfth of the cash at outset against the same reserve and the same expenses, so the identical policy moves from -100.0000 to +816.6667. Nothing else in the list does that. And the consequence for a growing insurer is exact: at 816.6667 of strain against 40.0000 of profit per policy, the capital absorbed rises in proportion to volume and cannot be funded from the year's own profit at any volume. A life insurer's growth is limited by capital rather than by demand, which inverts the banking intuition entirely.

The summary's BIIFIL mnemonic then shows that no product basis removes risk from the policyholder. Across the four bases the holder bears between three and five of the six risks, never none and never all. Investment risk is the one that moves, sitting with the insurer under without-profits and with the holder under unit-linked, which is the entire point of the unit-linked design. Lapse risk sits with the holder under every basis, so it is not a basis question at all.

And the life cycle is not a partition. The summary names nine products across its four stages and five of the nine serve more than one stage, so a product designed for one will be sold into another. The first stage, graduates with no assets, has no products listed against it, and that is not an omission: insurance protects something.

Step 2

Why this matters

The strain identity is the reason life insurance looks strange to anybody arriving from banking or investment management. A bank lends and earns a margin from the first day, so growth builds capital through retained profit and the constraint on it is regulatory rather than arithmetic. A life insurer pays the whole acquisition cost at outset and collects the premium over decades, so growth destroys capital first and rebuilds it later. Section 4.1 puts a number on that, and the number is larger than the annual premium itself.

That reframes several things this track will return to. It explains why life insurers care so much about persistency, since a policy that lapses early never repays the strain it caused. It explains why the reserving basis is contested, because it appears directly in the identity and a stronger basis is capital the insurer must find. And it explains the summary's third purpose, maximising economies of scale within available capital, which reads as a platitude until the identity makes the constraint concrete.

The BIIFIL result matters for how to read a product description. A sales narrative describes what a product provides; section 4.2 shows what every product still leaves with the customer. The useful question about any life product is therefore not what it covers but which of the six risks it moves and to whom, and the answer is never "all of them".

The Feynman version. Somebody signs up for a policy. You have to pay the salesperson today, do the medical today, print the paperwork today. And you have to put money aside today, because you have promised to pay a claim one day and the regulator wants that promise backed. Against all that you have received one premium.

If they pay you a whole year up front, that one premium might cover it. If they pay monthly, you have received a twelfth as much and everything else is unchanged, so you are badly out of pocket on day one. The policy is identical. The customer's payment habit decided whether writing it made you richer or poorer this morning.

Now sell fifty thousand of them. Every one digs the same hole, so the more successful your salespeople are, the more money you need to find. That is why a growing life insurer can be perfectly profitable and still run out of capital, and it is the single most important thing to understand about the industry.

Step 3

The mental model

Hold a life insurance product as three separate objects that happen to be sold together, because the summary's framework separates them and every later lecture works on one at a time.

The first is a promise: what gets paid, on what event, to whom. That is the description line in the summary's grid and it is the part the customer buys.

The second is a risk allocation: which of the six BIIFIL risks the customer keeps and which the insurer takes. That is the product basis, and section 4.2 shows it is a redistribution rather than a removal. Two products with identical promises and different bases are different investments for both parties.

The third is a financing pattern: money out at the start and money in over time. That is the strain identity, and it is the part nobody discusses in a sales conversation and everybody discusses in a board meeting. The first two are about the customer and the third is about the insurer's survival, and the summary's genius is to put the identity on the same page as the product list rather than in a finance chapter.

Step 4

The detail

4.1 New business strain, and the factor that changes its sign

The summary gives the identity C0+ = V0+ + e0+ - P0+: the capital needed the moment a policy is written is the reserve set up plus the initial expenses paid less the premium received. Work out when that is positive.

   the policy                    reserve   initial   premium   new business   does writing it
                                 set up    expense   received  strain         consume capital?
   a modest saving plan           300.0000  600.0000 1000.0000      -100.0000   no, it releases capital
   the same, heavy initial cost   300.0000  900.0000 1000.0000       200.0000   yes
   a strong reserving basis       700.0000  600.0000 1000.0000       300.0000   yes
   a protection policy             50.0000  400.0000  200.0000       250.0000   yes

The modest saving plan has a strain of -100.0000, which is negative: the first premium more than covers the reserve and the expenses, so writing it releases capital. Raise the initial expense to 900 and the strain becomes 200.0000. Strengthen the reserving basis instead and it becomes 300.0000. So the sign is not a property of the product, it is a property of the three numbers, and two of the three are chosen by the insurer rather than by the market.

Now the factor the summary lists second, which dominates all the others. A monthly premium delivers a twelfth of the cash at outset against the same reserve and the same initial expense.

   premium frequency   cash at outset   new business   strain as a share   multiple of the
                       from the policy  strain         of annual premium   annual case
   annual                     1000.0000      -100.0000             -0.1000            1.0000
   quarterly                   250.0000       650.0000              0.6500           -6.5000
   monthly                      83.3333       816.6667              0.8167           -8.1667

An annual premium gives a strain of -100.0000 and a monthly one gives 816.6667, on identical reserves and expenses. So premium frequency alone flips the sign of the capital requirement. That is why the summary lists it among the five factors and why it deserves to be second: nothing else in the list changes a sign, and the monthly strain is 0.8167 of a whole year's premium.

The consequence for a growing insurer is the one that matters. Take a monthly-premium book and write more of it, assuming 40.0000 of profit per policy in the year.

   this year          policy       absorbed       of annual premiums   from profit?
   1000.0000              816.6667     816666.6667               0.8167    NO
   5000.0000              816.6667    4083333.3333               0.8167    NO
   20000.0000             816.6667   16333333.3333               0.8167    NO

The capital absorbed rises exactly in proportion to volume, and at 816.6667 of strain against 40.0000 of profit per policy it cannot be funded from the year's own profit at any volume. So a life insurer's growth is limited by capital rather than by demand, which is what the summary's third purpose is about when it says maximise economies of scale within the capital available.

And that inverts the banking intuition. A bank lends and earns from the first day, so growth builds capital through retained profit. A life insurer pays its acquisition cost up front and collects over decades, so growth destroys capital first and rebuilds it later. The same word has opposite signs in the two industries, which is worth holding when reading anything that treats the two as variants of one business.

4.2 The four product bases redistribute risk rather than removing it

The summary gives policyholder risks the mnemonic BIIFIL, for benefits, investment, inflation, flexibility, insolvency and lapse, and lists which apply to each of four bases. Count them.

   the product basis   B   Inv  Infl F   Ins L   how many of the six
                                                 the POLICYHOLDER bears
   without-profits       y   .   y   y   y   y                   5
   with-profits          .   .   y   y   y   y                   4
   unit-linked           y   y   y   .   y   y                   5
   index-linked          y   y   .   y   .   .                   3

The policyholder bears between three and five of the six risks under every basis. None of the four leaves them bearing none, and none leaves them bearing all six. So choosing a product basis is choosing which risks to keep, not whether to keep any, and a sales narrative that presents a basis as removing risk is describing a transfer.

The risks that move are the interesting ones.

   risk           without-profits   unit-linked   index-linked   without-profits to
                                                                unit-linked: the shift
   benefits       the holder        the holder    the holder     unchanged
   investment     the insurer       the holder    the holder     moves to the holder
   inflation      the holder        the holder    the insurer    unchanged
   flexibility    the holder        the insurer   the holder     moves to the insurer
   insolvency     the holder        the holder    the insurer    unchanged
   lapse          the holder        the holder    the insurer    unchanged

Investment risk is the one that moves: the insurer bears it under without-profits and the holder bears it under unit-linked, which is the entire point of the unit-linked design and the reason the two bases exist side by side. Lapse risk sits with the holder under every basis, so it is not a basis question at all, and any discussion of product design that treats lapse as something a basis can address has confused two problems.

One entry in the summary's listing is worth querying rather than memorising. Index-linked is the only basis for which insolvency risk is not listed, and that looks like an omission rather than a design feature: an index-linked policy is still a claim on the insurer's balance sheet, so its holder faces the same insolvency exposure as any other. The examinable position is the summary's list as written, with the observation that insolvency risk applies to all four bases and differs only in degree, which is how the summary itself describes it for with-profits and unit-linked.

4.3 The life cycle is a set of overlapping needs, not a partition

   life stage           the objective           products the summary lists
                        the summary gives
   graduates 16-25      start saving            none: no assets to protect yet
   young adult 25-35    saving for dependants   endowments, renewable or convertible term, decreasing term, income protection
   adult 35-65          saving for retirement   endowments, whole life, deferred annuities, critical illness, income protection
   retirees over 65     transfer of wealth      whole life, deferred annuities, immediate annuities, long-term care insurance, critical illness

The summary names nine distinct products and five of the nine serve more than one stage: critical illness, deferred annuities, endowments, income protection and whole life. So the life cycle is not a partition of the product range, it is a set of overlapping needs, and a product designed for one stage will be sold into another. That is worth knowing before the later lectures describe each product, because it means the product-to-need mapping is many-to-many rather than one-to-one.

The first stage is the instructive one, because it has no products at all.

   life stage           income     liabilities            assets       what an insurer
                                                                       can sell
   graduates 16-25      low        student and car loans  none         nothing yet
   young adult 25-35    moderate   home loans             low          4 products
   adult 35-65          high       reduced debt           moderate     5 products
   retirees over 65     moderate   no debt                high         5 products

The stage with no assets is the stage with no products, which is not a coincidence: insurance protects something, so a customer with nothing to protect and nobody depending on them is not a customer. The summary's own grid says so by leaving the row empty, and reading that emptiness as information rather than as an oversight is the right instinct.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The habit worth forming from the first lecture of this track is to compute the strain before forming a view on any product. It takes three numbers, two of which the insurer controls, and it answers the only question that decides whether the product can be sold at scale. A product with attractive margins and a large positive strain is a product the insurer cannot afford to succeed at, and that combination is common rather than exotic, because the same features that make a product attractive to a customer, a low premium paid monthly, are the features that make its strain large.

On section 4.1's frequency result, the practical consequence is that premium frequency is a pricing variable and not an administrative convenience. Insurers that charge a loading for monthly payment are charging for the capital, not for the paperwork, and the size of that loading should be the strain difference rather than a nominal fee. Anybody analysing a life insurer's new business figures should look at the premium frequency mix before the volume, because a shift towards monthly collection raises the capital requirement of unchanged sales.

On BIIFIL, the professional judgement is that the mnemonic is more useful as a checklist against a product description than as something to recite. Take any product's marketing material and mark off which of the six it addresses; what remains unmarked is what the customer is keeping, and the gap between what the material implies and what section 4.2 shows is where most mis-selling complaints originate.

One limit on section 4.1. The identity is stated at outset only, so it says nothing about how quickly the strain is repaid, and two products with identical strain can recover it over three years or thirty. The summary's later chapters cover that as the emergence of surplus, and the honest position for now is that strain measures the size of the hole and not how long it takes to fill. A complete view needs both, and this lecture supplies one.

And a limit on section 4.3. The product-to-stage map is the summary's own and reflects a particular market at a particular time, so the specific assignments are conventional rather than derived. What generalises is the structure: needs overlap across stages, the map is many-to-many, and a stage with no assets has no products. The specific nine products are worth learning because they are examinable, not because the mapping is a theorem.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. This is the first lecture of the life insurance track and assumes nothing from within it. From elsewhere in the course it uses the reserving concept developed in contingencies, particularly the reserve and profit testing material, and the capital arithmetic of bank capital management as the contrast section 4.1 draws. The risk-transfer framing of section 4.2 is the same conservation structure found in trust law and fiduciary duty, where a benefit to one party was exactly a cost to another, and the product-basis choice parallels the vehicle choice priced in vehicles and wrappers.

Where this goes next. Lecture 2 covers the product bases in their own right, taking the four this lecture counted risks across and examining how each works, including how a with-profits bonus is declared and how a unit-linked charge structure operates. Lectures 3 to 8 then work through the products themselves, and every one of them will be assessed against this lecture's three objects: what it promises, which risks it moves, and what it does to capital on day one. Lecture 4 on annuities is where the strain identity reverses most sharply, since an immediate annuity receives its whole premium at outset, and lecture 8 on group products is where the summary's four payer arrangements interact with it.

Where the bank sits opposite. Section 4.1's contrast is the substance of the comparison and it runs deeper than the timing. A bank's capital requirement is proportional to its outstanding exposure, so it scales with the balance sheet and falls as loans repay; a life insurer's is concentrated at inception, so it scales with new business rather than with the book. That means a bank in run-off releases capital gradually while a life insurer in run-off releases it immediately, which is why a closed life book is a capital-generative asset and a closed loan book is merely a shrinking one. The bank side is in capital management and the underlying business model in the bank business model. The BIIFIL risks also have a banking analogue worth noting: a depositor bears insolvency risk and nothing else, whereas a policyholder bears between three and five of six, which is why deposit protection is a simpler instrument than policyholder protection and why the two are regulated by different machinery.

Numbers used here. The four financial life cycle stages with their objectives, incomes, liabilities, assets and expenses, the new business strain identity, the five capital requirement factors, the four purposes for the insurer, the BIIFIL policyholder risks across four product bases, the AIMEW insurer risks and the product-to-stage assignments are all transcribed from chapters 1 and 2 of the ASSA F102 concept summary in the user's notes. One observation is recorded against the summary: its BIIFIL listing gives insolvency risk for without-profits, with-profits and unit-linked and omits it for index-linked, which appears to be an omission rather than a design feature, since an index-linked policy remains a claim on the insurer's balance sheet; the examinable answer gives the four listings as written and adds that insolvency risk applies to all four bases and differs in degree, which is how the summary itself qualifies it for two of the three it does list. Everything numerical is invented for illustration: reserves of 50 to 700, initial expenses of 400 to 900, annual premiums of 200 and 1000 split annually, quarterly and monthly, volumes of 1,000 to 50,000 policies and a profit of 40 per policy. Nineteen claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions, all nineteen passed, and none can pass without the computation. One claim failed on the first run, asserting that four of the named products serve more than one life stage where the count is five of nine, and was corrected to a majority. Two table column widths were widened after labels overflowed, and one column header was relabelled once it became clear it compared only two of the three bases shown rather than all three. Every headline finding states the region in which it holds: the strain identity is exact at outset and silent on how quickly the strain is repaid, so it sizes the hole rather than timing its filling; the frequency result holds for any reserve and expense level, with the sign change occurring wherever the annual premium exceeds the reserve plus expenses and a fraction of it does not; and the product-to-stage map is the summary's own convention rather than a derivation, with the many-to-many structure generalising and the specific assignments not.
