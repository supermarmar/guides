Lectures / Life insurance / Internal environment / Products

One set of medical advances makes a term policy cheaper and a critical illness policy dearer

The summary names the rate of diagnosis as critical illness's first micro risk and says medical advances will cause unexpected changes. The direction is the whole point. Progress that finds conditions earlier and lets people survive them cuts mortality and raises diagnosis at the same time, so on the illustrative figures a term assurance gets 0.2000 cheaper while a stand-alone critical illness policy gets 0.2500 dearer. The accelerated benefit moves only 0.0211, because bringing a claim forward is not the same as adding one, which is a better argument for that structure than any the summary offers.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Critical illness pays a chosen lump sum on a defined diagnosis. That single design choice, a fixed sum rather than an indemnity, drives everything difficult about the product, because it severs the payment from the loss it is meant to cover. Lecture 5's income protection had a claimant who could influence the duration; this product has an insurer who cannot control what the words in its own contract will come to mean.

Start with the sign flip, which is the deepest result in the chapter. The summary lists the rate of diagnosis first among the micro risks and notes that medical advances cause unexpected changes. Work out the direction: the same progress that reduces mortality raises diagnosis, because conditions get found earlier and survived. So a term assurance becomes 0.2000 cheaper and a stand-alone critical illness policy 0.2500 dearer from one set of advances, while an accelerated benefit moves only 0.0211. A life office holding both books is not diversified against medical progress, it is exposed twice in opposite directions.

Then the condition list, where the summary appears to contradict itself. It says lengthening the list increases the cost minimally, and that offices nonetheless decline to cover everything. Both are true because they are about different costs: twenty rare conditions raise the claim cost by 0.0121, and their marginal cost per unit of risk actually transferred is 700000.0000 against the majors' 101600.0000, a factor of 6.8898, because each condition needs a definition, data and a claims process whether anybody claims on it or not. The summary's own general conditions, that a covered event be clearly defined and have sufficient data, are that fixed cost stated in words.

Then the three benefit structures. A rider costs 22000.0000, exactly the stand-alone 11400.0000 plus the term 10600.0000, because it is those two contracts sold together. An accelerated benefit costs 19000.0000, between the two. And the survival period the summary attaches to stand-alone alone is worth 600.0000 there and exactly 0.0000 on an accelerated benefit, because a claimant who dies inside the period is paid as a death claim instead. The condition is not an anti-fraud measure, it is a definition of the insured event.

Finally tiering, where one clause passes without comment in the summary and turns out to be a price rise on a schedule. Further claims come from the balance of the sum, but premiums are not reduced with every claim payment, so a policyholder who has claimed through the third severity level holds 50000.0000 of cover and pays the same 1200.0000. The effective rate goes from 0.012000 to 0.024000, exactly double, and the ratio is fixed by the tier shares rather than by the premium.

Step 2

Why this matters

The sign flip changes how a life office should think about its own diversification. Holding a protection book alongside a critical illness book looks like a spread of risks and is closer to a doubled bet on one variable, because medical progress is a single systematic driver moving the two in opposite directions. That is not a hedge unless the two exposures are deliberately sized against each other, and nothing in ordinary product management does that sizing. The practically useful consequence is that an accelerated benefit, which the summary presents as one structure among three, is the structure that is largely immune to the risk the summary itself names first.

The condition list result matters because it is a general lesson about product scope disguised as a detail about illness definitions. Whenever a benefit is extended by adding named triggers, the claim cost of the extension is governed by the frequency of the new triggers and the total cost by a fixed charge for each one. Frequency falls away much faster than the fixed charge does, so the scope always stops well short of comprehensive, and it stops for administrative rather than actuarial reasons. Anybody who argues for a wider list on the grounds that the extra claims are cheap has computed the smaller half of the cost.

The benefit structure result matters because the three structures are routinely compared on price as though they covered the same thing. They do not. The rider can pay twice on one life and the accelerated benefit cannot, which is why the rider costs exactly the sum of its two components and the accelerated benefit costs less than that sum. A buyer choosing between them on premium alone is choosing between different quantities of cover, and a comparison table that lists them side by side under one benefit heading is misleading whatever the numbers in it.

The tiering result matters because it is the clearest instance on this syllabus of value transferred through a mechanism nobody looks at. The claim definition is scrutinised, the severity levels are marketed, and the premium clause sits unremarked. Yet the premium clause is worth a doubling of the effective rate by the third level, and the summary is unusually candid that three of its five reasons for offering tiered benefits favour the insurer, including making comparisons more difficult in order to raise profits. Reading a product's small print for what it does to the rate per unit of remaining cover is a habit worth having.

The Feynman version. Imagine you sell two policies to the same person. One pays out if they die in the next twenty years. The other pays out if a doctor tells them they have cancer. Now medicine gets better. Scanners find things earlier, treatments work, people survive. Your death policy just got cheaper, because fewer of them die. Your cancer policy just got dearer, because more of them get told. Same hospital, same decade, opposite effect on your two books.

That is a strange position to be in, and it gets stranger. Because you promised a fixed sum rather than the cost of treatment, the better medicine gets, the bigger the gap between what you pay out and what the illness actually cost them. If curing it costs ten thousand and you handed over a hundred, ninety thousand of that is a windfall. Not a mistake in your pricing. The design of the product.

So what do you do if you want to promise not to raise the premium? You cover fewer illnesses. Not because the rare ones cost much, they barely cost anything. Because an illness you have not named cannot have its definition overtaken by a doctor in ten years' time. You are not buying a cheaper book, you are buying a book whose meaning is less likely to change underneath you.

Step 3

The mental model

Hold four pictures. The first is two dials turned by one hand. Medical progress is a single driver with two outputs, lower mortality and higher diagnosis, and every product on a life office's shelf is exposed to some combination of the two. A term assurance sits at one extreme, a stand-alone critical illness policy at the other, and the accelerated benefit sits where the two effects almost cancel. Once that picture is in place, the question about any healthcare product becomes where on that axis it sits rather than how much it costs.

The second is a toll per named thing. Each condition on the list carries a fixed charge for defining it, gathering data on it and being able to adjudicate a claim under it, and that charge is the same for the rarest condition as for the commonest. Since incidence falls away steeply down the list and the charge does not, the cost per unit of risk transferred rises without limit and the list stops. This is the same structure as lecture 5's per-claim administration cost, and it produces the same shape.

The third is a Venn diagram for benefit structures. Diagnosis and death are two events, and the three structures are three different ways of combining them: the union paid once, both paid separately, or diagnosis alone with a survival test. The prices follow arithmetically, and the survival period only bites where diagnosis and death are substitutes rather than the same claim, which is exactly the stand-alone case.

The fourth is a shrinking denominator. Under tiering the sum at risk falls with each payment while the premium does not, so the rate per unit of remaining cover rises on a schedule set by the tier shares. That picture generalises to any contract where a benefit is drawn down against a fixed charge, including a decreasing term assurance with a level premium and a facility fee on a reducing loan.

Those four pictures also explain what distinguishes this product from income protection. There the difficulty was behavioural, a claimant influencing the duration, and the controls were financial limits. Here the difficulty is definitional, a contract's words being reinterpreted by a profession outside the contract, and the controls are narrower scope and reviewable rates. Moral hazard is largely absent, which is why the summary does not list it among the micro risks and does list it for income protection, and why it says childhood critical illness carries low moral hazard as a specific reassurance.

Step 4

The detail

4.1 Medical progress moves the two books opposite ways

The summary's first micro risk is the rate of diagnosis, qualified twice: limited data makes industry estimates unreliable for any single insurer, and medical advances cause unexpected changes. Take the second qualification seriously and work out the sign. Medical progress does two things at once. It reduces mortality, because conditions that used to kill are now survived. And it raises diagnosis, because conditions are found earlier, including in people who would previously have died of something else undiagnosed.

Price three contracts over one term on one population, before and after a set of advances that cuts deaths by a fifth and raises diagnoses by a quarter, with half the additional diagnoses drawn from lives who would otherwise have died undiagnosed.

   the contract          cost before   cost after    change      which way does
                         progress      progress      in cost     medical progress push?
   term assurance           10600.0000     8480.0000     -0.2000   in the insurer's favour
   accelerated benefit      19000.0000    19400.0000      0.0211   against the insurer
   stand-alone CI           11400.0000    14250.0000      0.2500   against the insurer

A 100000 sum assured with a 0.1200 chance of diagnosis and a 0.1060 chance of death during the term. The improvement and diagnosis factors are invented for illustration.

The term policy gets 0.2000 cheaper and the stand-alone critical illness policy 0.2500 dearer from one set of medical advances. That is not a diversified position, it is the same variable entering two books with opposite signs, and a life office that holds both without sizing them against each other has an unmanaged exposure rather than a natural hedge.

The accelerated benefit moves only 0.0211, under a fifth of either of the others. The reason is structural rather than lucky: an accelerated benefit pays once, on illness or death, whichever comes first, so an earlier diagnosis mostly brings forward a payment the contract was going to make anyway rather than adding one. It is only exposed where a diagnosis now happens inside the term for a life whose death would have fallen outside it. So the accelerated benefit is roughly immune to the summary's own first-named risk, which is a stronger argument for it than any of the ones the summary gives.

And this explains a sentence in the summary that otherwise looks like a non-sequitur. It says competition has forced insurers to offer guarantees, and that insurers are beginning to cover fewer diseases in order to offer them. Narrowing the list is not primarily a claim cost saving, because section 4.2 shows that saving is minimal. It is a reduction in exposure to diagnostic drift: a condition not on the list cannot have its definition overtaken by medical practice. What narrowing buys is variance rather than expected cost, and it is the same parameter uncertainty lecture 5 priced for morbidity guarantees.

Which leaves the windfall. The summary makes two statements in different sections: that critical illness is not designed to indemnify, since the sum assured is chosen by the insured and meets neither an income loss nor a medical cost; and that improving techniques could produce windfall claims where the benefit outweighs the medical costs. They are one statement.

   medical cost of the   sum assured   the windfall   as a share of   is this a
   condition             chosen        to the holder  the sum         pricing error?
   90000.0000              100000.0000     10000.0000          0.1000   no, it is the design
   60000.0000              100000.0000     40000.0000          0.4000   no, it is the design
   30000.0000              100000.0000     70000.0000          0.7000   no, it is the design
   10000.0000              100000.0000     90000.0000          0.9000   no, it is the design

Treatment costs falling against a fixed chosen sum. All figures invented for illustration.

The windfall rises from 10000.0000 to 90000.0000 as treatment costs fall, with the sum assured fixed throughout, and every rand saved on treatment accrues to the holder one for one. So non-indemnity plus medical progress equals windfall, necessarily rather than as a risk. An indemnity product self-corrects because the payment tracks the loss; a chosen-sum product cannot. That is why the summary is careful to say in its description that critical illness meets neither an income loss nor a direct medical cost, and it is worth reading that sentence as a warning rather than a definition.

One boundary. The illustrative improvement factors are invented, so the -0.2000 and +0.2500 are magnitudes rather than forecasts. What is general is the opposition of the signs, which follows from the fact that survival is the same event as diagnosis-without-death, and the near-invariance of the accelerated benefit, which follows from it paying once on a union of the two events. Both would survive any calibration in which progress lowers mortality and raises detection.

4.2 The condition list stops for administrative reasons

The summary makes two statements about the condition list that sit awkwardly together. Lengthening it increases the cost minimally. And offices are not attempting to cover all conditions, since it is still cheaper to offer a specific product in order to increase sales. Read as economics the first appears to undercut the second. Both are correct, and the reconciliation is that they are about different costs.

The five additional conditions raise the claim cost by 0.1000 and the twenty rare ones by 0.0121, so the summary's word minimal is exactly right about claim cost. Now divide by the risk actually covered, which is where the second statement comes from.

   the condition set        cost per unit of   marginal cost per unit   is the last tranche

   the four major only                101600.0000              101600.0000   yes

The majors cost 101600.0000 per unit of incidence covered and the rare tranche 700000.0000 at the margin, a factor of 6.8898. So both statements hold: the claim cost of lengthening the list is minimal, and the cost per unit of risk actually transferred becomes ruinous, because each condition needs a definition, data and a claims process whether anybody claims on it or not.

And the summary has already given the reason, in a different section. Its general policy conditions require that a covered event be perceived as serious, occur frequently, be clearly defined, and have sufficient data. The last three of those four are the fixed cost. So the summary states the constraint under policy conditions and states the cheapness under the description, and the apparent contradiction is an artefact of the two statements sitting in different places rather than a defect in either.

The same arithmetic explains the summary's note on total and permanent disability, that it is included because it complements critical illness and there is significant overlap at little additional cost.

At a 0.9000 overlap the marginal cost is 0.1000 of the standalone cost, and the saving is exactly the overlap. So the summary's two clauses, significant overlap and little additional cost, are not two observations that happen to coincide, they are the same number stated twice. Which is why total and permanent disability gets bundled into a critical illness product and Alzheimer's does not: one shares its claim events with the existing list and the other adds new ones.

4.3 The survival period is a definition, not an anti-fraud measure

The summary gives three benefit structures. Stand-alone pays on diagnosis with no benefit on death, and the policyholder must survive a period after diagnosis to receive it. A rider pays the critical illness benefit on diagnosis and another policy benefit on death. An accelerated benefit pays on illness or death, whichever occurs first, with some policies splitting the sum between the two. Price all three against a plain term assurance on one population.

   the contract          pays on                   probability of   expected cost   dearer than
                                                   paying           per policy      a term policy?
   term assurance        death only                        0.106000      10600.0000   -
   stand-alone CI        diagnosis, then survival          0.114000      11400.0000   yes
   accelerated benefit   illness or death, once            0.190000      19000.0000   yes
   CI rider              diagnosis AND death               0.220000      22000.0000   yes

A 0.1200 chance of diagnosis, a 0.0700 chance of death without prior diagnosis, a 0.0360 chance of death after one, and 0.0500 of diagnoses dying inside the survival period. All invented for illustration.

The rider is the dearest at 22000.0000, which is exactly the stand-alone 11400.0000 plus the term 10600.0000, because it is those two contracts sold together and is the only structure that can pay twice on one life. The accelerated benefit costs 19000.0000, between the term policy and the rider, because it pays once on whichever event arrives first. So the three structures are not three prices for one cover, they are three different covers, and any comparison that lines them up under a single benefit heading is comparing unlike things.

Now the survival period, which the summary attaches to stand-alone alone. Test whether it matters on the others.

   the contract          cost with the     cost without      what the survival   why
                         survival period   survival period   period is worth
   stand-alone CI               11400.0000        12000.0000            600.0000   no death benefit
   accelerated benefit          19000.0000        19000.0000              0.0000   the death claim pays anyway
   CI rider                     22000.0000        22600.0000            600.0000   the death benefit pays too

The same contracts with the survival requirement removed. The accelerated benefit is unchanged to the last decimal place.

The survival period is worth 600.0000 on the stand-alone contract and exactly 0.0000 on the accelerated benefit. That is why the summary attaches it only to stand-alone: it is the only structure where diagnosis and death are substitutes rather than the same claim, so it is the only one where a deathbed diagnosis would create a payment that would not otherwise have happened. Read that way the condition is not an anti-fraud measure at all, it is part of the definition of the insured event, and the exam-relevant sentence is that stand-alone cover insures survival with a diagnosis rather than the diagnosis itself.

And the same reasoning fixes the rider, where the period saves money for the opposite reason. There the death benefit pays as well, so removing the survival requirement would have the contract pay twice on a single life inside a fortnight. That is a duplication rather than a substitution, and it happens to be worth the same 600.0000, which is a coincidence of the illustrative figures rather than a result: the two amounts coincide because both equal the diagnoses that die inside the period times the sum assured, but they are compensating for different things.

The summary's remaining structural notes fit the same frame. It says the lump sum may be paid in instalments with any outstanding amount payable on death, which converts an accelerated benefit into something closer to a rider at the margin. It says terminal illness cover relates to the effect on life expectancy rather than to a named condition, and is usually offered as an acceleration product, which is consistent: a terminal diagnosis is the case where diagnosis and death are nearest to the same event, so acceleration costs almost nothing extra over the death benefit. And the children's benefit is a rider to age eighteen that does not terminate when the child claims, with the summary noting low moral hazard because childhood critical illness is very rare.

4.4 Tiering leaves the premium alone while the sum at risk falls

The summary's tiered benefit pays a proportion of the sum at each of usually four severity levels, with further claims taken from the balance as the disease advances. It then adds one clause without comment: premiums are not reduced with every claim payment. Price what that clause is worth.

   after a claim at     paid so far   sum remaining   premium still   effective rate on
                        as a share    at risk         payable         the cover remaining
   no claim yet                0.0000     100000.0000       1200.0000            0.012000
   level 1, earliest           0.1000      90000.0000       1200.0000            0.013333
   level 2                     0.2500      75000.0000       1200.0000            0.016000
   level 3                     0.5000      50000.0000       1200.0000            0.024000

Tier shares of 0.1000, 0.1500, 0.2500 and 0.5000 on a 100000 sum with a 1200 annual premium. Invented for illustration; the premium clause is the summary's.

A policyholder who has claimed through the third level holds 50000.0000 of cover and pays the same 1200.0000, so the effective rate has gone from 0.012000 to 0.024000, exactly double. And that ratio does not depend on the premium at all: it is one over the share of cover remaining, so it is fixed by the tier shares the insurer chose. The summary's uncommented clause is a price rise on a schedule, known at outset, disclosed nowhere in the marketing of the severity levels.

And the summary is unusually candid about where the value goes. Of its five reasons for offering tiered benefits, two favour the policyholder, a more comprehensive product and the possibility of multiple claims, and three favour the insurer: a reduced incentive for anti-selection, the ability to differentiate, and making comparisons more difficult leading to higher profits. That last phrase is the summary's own, not an inference, which makes tiering the most openly two-sided feature on this syllabus and worth quoting in an answer that asks why insurers offer it.

The anti-selection reason is worth unpacking, because it is the one that genuinely serves both sides. Under an all-or-nothing benefit a policyholder at an early stage of a covered condition has an incentive to wait, to press for a more severe classification, or to shop for a diagnosis, since anything short of the full definition pays nothing. A tiered benefit pays something at the early stage, so the incentive to game the boundary falls. That is the same insight as lecture 5's rehabilitation benefit, where a partial payment for part-time work removes the reason to stay wholly off it.

4.5 The late notification reserve is the one capital item this product does not share

The summary says critical illness capital requirements are similar to income protection, but that a reserve is needed to cover late notified claims after the original incident. That is an incurred but not reported reserve, and its size is the reporting delay expressed as a share of one year's claims.

A reporting delay distribution against annual claims of 825.0000 per policy. Invented for illustration.

The reserve is 195.9375, or 0.2375 of a year's claims, even though half of all claims are reported immediately, and the mean delay is only 0.2675 years. It is driven by the tail rather than the average: the rarest delay group contributes at full weight while the quarter-year group contributes a quarter of its share, so a small number of very late notifications does most of the work.

Which is why the summary singles this out as the one capital item critical illness does not share with income protection. On an income protection policy the claim and the notification are effectively the same event, because the benefit only begins once the insurer has been notified and the deferred period has run, so there is no meaningful gap to reserve for. Here the insured event is a diagnosis that has already happened, and the insurer may not learn of it for years, so the liability exists before any claim file does.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The habit worth installing is to trace every systematic driver through the whole product range and check the signs, rather than assessing products one at a time. Medical progress is the case here, and the answer is that a protection book and a critical illness book respond in opposite directions to the same event. The same exercise on interest rates would show an annuity book and a deposit-funded lending book responding in opposite directions, and on inflation would separate index-linked liabilities from nominal ones. A risk register organised by product hides these oppositions; one organised by driver reveals them, and the second organisation is the one that tells you whether you are hedged.

The second habit is to distinguish expected cost from variance when a scope decision is on the table. Narrowing a condition list saves almost no expected claims and buys a large reduction in definitional uncertainty, which is why it is the price of a premium guarantee. Anybody who evaluates that decision on expected cost alone will conclude it is not worth doing, and will be wrong for the same reason that lecture 5's morbidity guarantee could not be made affordable by volume: the binding constraint is what you do not know, not what you expect.

The third habit is to read the payment mechanism as carefully as the claim definition. Tiering is scrutinised on its severity thresholds and passes unexamined on its premium clause, and the premium clause is worth a doubling of the effective rate. That pattern recurs wherever a benefit is drawn down against a charge that is not: level premiums on decreasing cover, fixed fees on reducing facilities, flat management charges on funds in drawdown. Compute the rate per unit of remaining exposure, always, and compute it at the end of the schedule rather than the beginning.

The fourth habit is to ask whether a product indemnifies a loss or pays a chosen sum, and then to ask what happens to the gap between them over time. An indemnity product self-corrects as circumstances change; a chosen-sum product accumulates a windfall or a shortfall depending on which way the world moves. Critical illness accumulates a windfall as medicine improves, which is favourable to the policyholder and hostile to the insurer's pricing, and no clause in the contract adjusts for it. The general point is that non-indemnity is not a simplification, it is a long option on the divergence between the sum and the loss.

The last habit is to notice which risk a product does not carry, because that is often what distinguishes it from its neighbour. Critical illness has almost no moral hazard where income protection is defined by it, and the reason is that a diagnosis is not something a policyholder can extend. So the two products need entirely different controls, financial limits for one and definitional discipline for the other, and an insurer that applies income protection's over-insurance machinery to a critical illness book has bought the wrong protection. The summary's micro risk lists differ for a reason, and the differences are more informative than the overlaps.

What this assumes. Where the bank sits opposite. Section 4.2's fixed cost per named condition is the same structure as a bank's cost of adding a product or a jurisdiction: each one carries a fixed charge for policy, controls, reporting and model validation whether or not volume follows, which is why banks rationalise product ranges that look individually profitable, and why the compliance-cost incidence result in the principles of financial regulation falls hardest on the smallest participant. Section 4.1's driver-level analysis is exactly what a bank's stress testing does when it shocks a macroeconomic variable through every book at once rather than assessing portfolios separately, which is the point of the scenario design in stress testing. Section 4.4's shrinking denominator has a direct lending counterpart in a fixed facility fee on a reducing balance, where the effective rate rises as the loan amortises, and section 4.5's late notification reserve is the life insurance form of the reporting lag that makes operational risk loss data incomplete at the most recent dates, treated in operational risk measurement.

No observation is recorded against the summary in this lecture. Its two statements about the condition list look contradictory and section 4.2 shows they concern claim cost and total cost respectively, with the summary's own general policy conditions supplying the fixed cost it does not name as one. Its two clauses about TPD turn out to be a single number stated twice. And its decision to attach the survival period only to stand-alone cover is exactly right, since the period is worth nothing on an accelerated benefit. Everything numerical is invented for illustration: incidence rates from 0.0035 for cancer down to 0.000005 for each rare condition, a fixed cost of 3.0 per condition per policy, a standalone TPD incidence of 0.0025, a 0.1200 diagnosis probability with 0.0700 dying undiagnosed and 0.0360 dying after diagnosis, 0.0500 of diagnoses failing the survival period, a mortality improvement factor of 0.8000 with diagnoses rising by a quarter and half the increase drawn from lives who would have died undiagnosed, medical costs from 90000 down to 10000 against a 100000 sum, tier shares of 0.1000, 0.1500, 0.2500 and 0.5000 with a 1200 premium, and a reporting delay distribution with half of claims reported at once and 0.0300 delayed two years. Twenty-four claims and counterexamples were encoded as assertions, all twenty-four passed, and none can pass without the computation. One claim was flagged as unfalsifiable by a vacuity check, having been written against numeric literals rather than the delay distribution, and was rewritten to draw both contributions from that distribution. A second claim asserted only that the sum assured never changed, which is true by construction; it was replaced by an assertion that the windfall moves one for one against the medical cost, which tests the mechanism. Every headline finding states the region in which it holds: the opposition of signs under medical progress follows from survival being diagnosis-without-death and holds for any calibration in which progress lowers mortality and raises detection, with the -0.2000 and +0.2500 specific to the illustrative factors; the condition list result depends only on incidence falling faster down the list than the fixed cost does; the survival period being worth exactly zero on an accelerated benefit is exact and structural, while the equality of the stand-alone and rider amounts is a coincidence of the figures rather than a result; and tiering's doubling at the third level is a property of the tier shares alone, independent of the premium.
