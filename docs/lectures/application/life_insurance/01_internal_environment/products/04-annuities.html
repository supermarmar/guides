For an annuity the reserving basis is not one input to the capital requirement, it is the capital requirement

The summary gives an immediate annuity essentially one capital note, that the requirement is significant and depends on the pricing and reserving basis, where a term assurance got four. That asymmetry is correct rather than careless. A single point off the reserving rate moves an annuity's reserve by 7922.5313 on a 100000 premium, 0.0792 of the whole consideration, and moves the strain from -10038.4796 to 13866.2019 across a three-point band. The same proportional change is worth 0.0225 of premium on a term assurance, a factor of 3.8202, because for an annuity the premium and the reserve are nearly the same number.

Step 1

The 30-second version

An annuity is an assurance run backwards. The policyholder pays once and receives many times, the insurer loses money when the policyholder lives rather than when they die, and the risk the healthy life poses is the risk the impaired life poses on an assurance. Three consequences of that reversal are computable, and each one changes a conclusion reached earlier in this track.

The first is capital. Lecture 1 established that new business consumes capital and that a single premium at outset makes the strain negative, because the cash arrives before the reserve is needed. For an annuity that reasoning fails, and the arithmetic shows why: the single premium and the reserve it must establish are nearly the same number, so the strain is the small difference between two large ones. On the illustrative contract the strain is -10038.4796 at a reserving rate of 0.0600 and 13866.2019 at 0.0300, crossing zero between 0.0500 and 0.0400. A single-premium contract is not automatically capital-generative, and whether it generates or consumes capital is decided entirely by the gap between the pricing and reserving bases.

The second is selection. The summary lists anti-selection as a micro risk for both assurances and annuities, and the same word points at opposite populations. An annuity bought only by healthy lives costs the insurer 12037.8061 more than it charged; one bought only by impaired lives costs 27640.5208 less. That is the whole reason the impaired-life annuity exists, and section 4.2 shows the summary's phrase, higher benefits for a shorter life, is not a concession but the removal of a cross-subsidy: priced separately, the healthy life receives 7255.4818 and the impaired life 11497.6117 for the same money.

The third is the guarantee. The summary attaches additional capital on a deferred annuity to any guaranteed terms for converting between a lump sum and a pension, and that is an option rather than a contingency. Its payoff is exactly zero while market annuity rates exceed the guaranteed rate and exactly FUND × (guaranteed rate - market rate) below it, so a guarantee at 0.1100 costs nothing at a market rate of 0.1200 and 40000.0000 at 0.0700. Worse, the two risks the summary names as its causes, additional investment risk and additional mortality risk, both push the same way. Falling interest rates and improving longevity each raise the cost of the promised annuity, so they reinforce rather than diversify.

Step 2

Why this matters

Annuities are where a life insurer's capital position is decided rather than merely recorded. A protection book consumes capital in proportion to premium, which lecture 3 showed, but the amounts are small per contract and an insurer can slow the drain by writing less. An annuity book consumes or releases capital in proportion to the whole consideration, and the sign is set by a basis assumption rather than by sales volume. An insurer that strengthens its reserving basis by a point across a large annuity book has just written a very large cheque without selling anything, and the same insurer holding an equivalent term book would barely notice.

The selection result matters because the usual remedy is unavailable. The summary notes in passing that anti-selection on an annuity is subject to legislation, and that qualification carries the whole design problem. An insurer can decline a life assured, defer them, or load their premium after underwriting. It generally cannot decline an annuitant, particularly where pension legislation compels annuity purchase from an accumulated fund, so the risk cannot be underwritten away. The only available response is to price into it, which means either accepting the average and losing on favourable selection or segmenting the market and pricing the segments, and the second of those is what an impaired-life annuity is.

The guarantee result matters because it is history rather than theory. Guaranteed annuity rates written in high-interest-rate decades became catastrophically valuable when rates fell and longevity improved together, and the reason they were destructive rather than merely expensive is the correlation shown in section 4.3: both drivers move the option the same way at the same time, so there is no state of the world in which one offsets the other. Any product feature whose two named risk drivers reinforce rather than diversify deserves the treatment given to an option, which means a price at inception and a reserve that moves with the market, not a best-estimate assumption.

The Feynman version. Someone hands you a hundred thousand and you promise to pay them eight thousand a year until they die. How much of that hundred thousand is yours to keep? None of it yet. You have to put nearly all of it aside, because you owe nearly all of it. Whether you have to put aside ninety-five thousand or a hundred and twelve depends on what interest rate you are allowed to assume you will earn on it. Assume six per cent and you can hold back ten thousand. Assume three and you have to find twelve thousand of your own money on top. Nothing about the customer, the promise, or the market has changed between those two sentences. Only the assumption has.

That is the difference between an annuity and a life policy. On a life policy you take in two hundred a year and set aside fifty, so an argument about the assumption is an argument about a few pounds. On an annuity the money you take in and the money you set aside are almost the same amount, so an argument about the assumption is an argument about the whole business.

And now notice who wants to buy one from you. Not the person who has just been given six months to live. The person who runs marathons at seventy and whose parents both reached ninety-five. On a life policy the sick queue up; on an annuity the healthy do. Same word, anti-selection, opposite queue.

Step 3

The mental model

Hold three pictures. The first is a balance: on one side the single premium, on the other the reserve, and the strain is the small gap between them. Because both sides are large, the gap is exquisitely sensitive to anything that moves either side, and the reserving basis moves one side without touching the other. This is the opposite of a protection contract, where the premium is small and the reserve is smaller still, so the gap is dominated by expenses rather than by assumptions.

The second is a mirror. Every risk on an assurance has an annuity counterpart with the sign reversed. Mortality risk becomes longevity risk. The impaired life who selects against an assurance is the healthy life who selects against an annuity. Underwriting, which lets an insurer refuse the assurance risk, is largely unavailable on the annuity side, so the mirror is imperfect in the insurer's disfavour. And where an assurance's exposure shrinks as its reserve grows into the benefit, an annuity's exposure grows as the annuitant survives.

The third is a hockey stick. Any guaranteed conversion term, whether from lump sum to pension or between bases, has an option payoff: flat at zero on one side of a strike and linear on the other. That shape is not what a best-estimate reserve captures, because a best estimate sits in the middle of a distribution and the option's value comes from one tail. An insurer that reserves an option at its expected outcome has systematically under-reserved it, and the error grows with volatility rather than with the mean.

Those three pictures generate the whole lecture. The first says the capital requirement is a basis question. The second says the selection problem cannot be underwritten. The third says the guarantee must be priced as an option. The summary's three annuity capital notes, one for the immediate annuity and two for the deferred, correspond exactly to those three pictures, which is a useful check that the taxonomy is derived rather than assembled.

Step 4

The detail

4.1 The basis gap is the capital requirement

The summary's capital note for an immediate annuity reads that there are significant requirements depending on the pricing and reserving basis, with solvency margins also contributing to the initial strain. For a term assurance the basis was one factor among five. Here it is close to the only one, and the reason is arithmetic rather than emphasis.

Take a single premium of 100000 buying a level income for an expected eighteen years, priced at 0.0500 with 1500 of initial expense and a small profit margin, so the payment is 8170.0000 a year. Lecture 1's strain identity is C0+ = V0+ + e0+ - P0+: the capital needed at outset equals the reserve established plus the initial expense less the premium received. Vary only the reserving rate.

   interest rate         required      received   expense   strain         of the premium

   0.0500                   95503.9250 100000.0000 1500.0000     -2996.0750            -0.0300

The strain rises monotonically as the reserving basis weakens. A reserving rate above the pricing rate releases capital; one point below it already consumes capital. Reserve, premium and expense are invented for illustration; the identity is the summary's.

Three things follow. A reserving rate above the pricing rate gives a negative strain, so the contract releases capital at outset. One point below it the strain is already positive, at 4926.4563, and two points below it reaches 13866.2019. And the move for a single point of basis, 7922.5313, is 0.0792 of the whole premium, which is larger than the entire profit margin priced into the contract. An insurer arguing over the reserving rate on an annuity book is arguing over a quantity comparable to the business's whole economics, not over a rounding.

Compare that with lecture 3's term assurance, where the reserve was 50 against a 200 premium. The sensitivity of the strain scales with the reserve, because the reserve is the only term in the identity a basis change touches, so the same proportional weakening barely registers on a protection contract.

A nine per cent proportional strengthening of the reserve, expressed as a share of the premium received. Reserves and premiums for the two assurance contracts are lecture 3's illustrative figures.

The same proportional change is worth 0.0225 of premium on a term assurance and 0.0860 on an annuity, a factor of 3.8202. So the summary gives term assurance four separate capital notes and the annuity essentially one, and the asymmetry is correct: on a protection contract the strain is an expense-and-premium story with several moving parts, and on an annuity it is a basis story with one.

The counterexample matters more than the result. Lecture 1 established that a single premium at outset makes the strain negative, because the reserve is established out of money already received. That is true in general and false here. The cash at outset is the entire premium, and the strain is still positive whenever the reserving basis is stronger than the pricing basis. So the rule to carry forward is not that single-premium business generates capital; it is that single-premium business generates capital only to the extent the premium exceeds the reserve, and for an annuity that margin is thin enough for a basis assumption to reverse it.

One boundary. The illustration replaces a life annuity with an annuity certain over an expected term, which is exact for the interest-rate sensitivity being measured but understates the mortality sensitivity, since a real annuity's cash flows are themselves probability-weighted. The direction and rough magnitude of the basis result are unaffected; the precise figures would need a mortality table.

4.2 Anti-selection reverses, which is why impaired-life annuities exist

The summary lists anti-selection as a micro risk for the assurance contracts and again for annuities, and the repetition hides a sign change. On an assurance the impaired life selects against the insurer, because early death is what costs money. On an annuity the healthy life does, because a long life is what costs money. Price the same contract against three populations.

   who buys            expected years   annuity value   priced on the   the insurer's
                       of payment       at 5%           average          result
   only impaired lives                 11      67863.4042       95503.9250    a PROFIT of 27640.5208
   a mixed pool as priced               18      95503.9250       95503.9250    as expected
   only healthy lives                  22     107541.7311       95503.9250    a LOSS of 12037.8061

The same 8170.0000 annual income priced on an eighteen-year average and sold to populations living eleven and twenty-two years. Terms and rates are invented for illustration.

An annuity bought only by healthy lives costs 12037.8061 more than priced; one bought only by impaired lives costs 27640.5208 less. A candidate who writes anti-selection without saying which direction has said nothing, because the word points at opposite populations in the two halves of the summary's own product list. The exam-relevant sentence is that on an annuity the risk is favourable selection by the long-lived, and the mechanism is that the policyholder knows more about their own health than the insurer and buys only when that knowledge is good news for them.

Now the summary's own remedy, which appears in its list of annuity types by contingency: the impaired-life annuity, described as paying higher benefits for a shorter life. Price the two classes separately and the cross-subsidy disappears.

   the arrangement          healthy life gets   impaired life gets   who subsidises whom
   one rate for everyone              8170.0000            8170.0000   the impaired subsidise the healthy
   separately priced                  7255.4818           11497.6117   nobody

Both rows purchased with the same 100000. Under a single rate the impaired life receives the same income for eleven years against the healthy life's twenty-two.

Under one rate both receive 8170.0000 and the impaired life collects it for eleven years against twenty-two, so the impaired annuitant subsidises the healthy one. Priced separately the healthy life receives 7255.4818 and the impaired life 11497.6117 for the same money, a ratio of 1.5847. So the summary's phrase, higher benefits for a shorter life, is not a concession granted to the sick. It is the removal of a subsidy the sick were paying to the healthy, and the fairness argument runs in the segmenting direction, which is the reverse of how segmentation usually reads.

And it is the only remedy available. The summary qualifies annuity anti-selection as subject to legislation, and that clause does the work. An insurer cannot decline an annuitant the way it can decline a life assured, particularly where pension legislation compels an annuity purchase out of an accumulated fund, so the risk cannot be underwritten away. Every response available is a pricing response. That is why the annuity market segments on health where the assurance market underwrites on it: same information asymmetry, different legal ability to refuse.

Two boundaries. The model has two health classes and a single purchase date, so the 1.5847 ratio illustrates the mechanism rather than calibrating it. And the argument that separate pricing removes a subsidy holds only where the classification is accurate; a misclassified healthy life priced as impaired receives a windfall, which is why impaired-life annuity underwriting is intrusive despite the insurer being unable to decline.

4.3 The guaranteed conversion terms are an option that bites when it hurts most

The summary's deferred annuity carries two additional capital notes beyond the endowment-like requirement for the lump sum. The first is additional investment and mortality risk where there is a guarantee for receiving a pension and lump sum at the vesting date. The second is additional capital for any guaranteed terms for converting between a lump sum and a pension. That second item is a guaranteed annuity rate, and it is an option rather than a contingency, because the policyholder takes it only when the market alternative is worse.

Take a fund of 1000000 at vesting with a guaranteed conversion rate of 0.1100, so the promised income is 110000. The holder compares that with whatever the market would give.

The guarantee costs nothing while market rates exceed it and costs the insurer the whole shortfall once they fall below it, and the cost is exactly the fund times the gap between the guaranteed and market rates. Flat at zero on one side of a strike, linear on the other. That is an option payoff, and it has the consequence noted in section 3: a best-estimate reserve set at the expected market rate captures none of the value that comes from the tail, so the reserve must respond to the market rate rather than to an assumption about it.

Which raises the question of what moves the market annuity rate. The summary names the two risks itself, additional investment and mortality risk, and the point worth extracting is that they push the same way rather than offsetting.

A lower discount rate raises the cost of the promised annuity from 52838.8252 to 82182.7401 on the illustrative cash flows, and longer life raises it from 80871.8965 to 112734.9035. Both push the same way, so the two risks the summary names reinforce each other rather than diversifying. That is the difference between an expensive guarantee and a fatal one. An insurer that wrote guaranteed annuity rates in a high-rate era subsequently faced falling rates and improving longevity simultaneously, which is why this particular option closed life offices rather than merely costing them money.

And the natural hedge runs the wrong way too. A life office holding both annuities and assurances has some longevity offset, since longer life delays claims on the assurance book. But the guarantee sits only on the annuity side, and lecture 3 showed that an assurance's sum at risk shrinks as its reserve grows into the benefit, so the offsetting exposure is smallest exactly where the annuity book is largest, on the older lives. The hedge is real and too small to rely on.

Worth stating in an exam answer, because the shared heading invites the assumption that they are variants of one idea.

The deferred annuity's needs list adds one item to the immediate annuity's, loan repayment through a cash option, and its micro risks are described as similar to an endowment assurance and to an immediate annuity combined. That combination is exactly right structurally: the deferral period behaves like an endowment accumulating to a maturity, and the payment period behaves like an immediate annuity, so the risks are the union of the two plus whatever the joint between them creates. The joint is section 4.3's guarantee, which is why the additional capital note sits precisely there.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The instinct to develop is that an annuity's economics live in the difference between two large numbers, and every professional habit should follow from that. When someone quotes an annuity book's reserve, ask what basis it is on before asking how large it is, because the basis is worth more than the difference between a good year and a bad one. When someone proposes writing more annuity business, ask what the pricing basis is relative to the reserving basis, because that single comparison determines whether growth funds itself or requires capital. And when someone reports that the annuity book released capital this year, check whether anything happened other than a basis change.

The second habit is to distrust the word anti-selection whenever it appears without a direction. It is one of a small set of terms that name a mechanism rather than an outcome, and the mechanism produces opposite outcomes on opposite products. The same applies to longevity risk, which is a liability for an annuity writer and an asset for an assurance writer, and to interest rate risk, which helps a bank's deposit margin and hurts its annuity subsidiary. Naming the mechanism is the easy half; naming which side of it you are on is the half that gets marks and makes money.

The third habit is to look for option payoffs in anything described as a guarantee, and then to ask what moves the option into the money. Most guarantees have a single driver, and those can be priced with a distribution. The dangerous ones have two or more drivers that move together, because then the tail is fatter than any single-driver analysis suggests and the diversification an insurer expects to rely on is absent exactly when it is needed. Guaranteed annuity rates are the canonical case in life insurance; the banking equivalent is a portfolio of committed facilities to correlated borrowers, drawn together in a downturn.

The fourth habit is to treat legal ability to refuse as a first-class variable in risk management rather than a compliance footnote. The summary's four-word qualification, subject to legislation, is what converts annuity anti-selection from an underwriting problem into a pricing problem, and the entire structure of the impaired-life market follows from it. Wherever an insurer or a bank cannot decline, it must price, and where it must price it needs segmentation, and where it needs segmentation it needs data. That chain runs from a legal constraint to a data strategy in three steps, and it is invisible if the constraint is read as boilerplate.

The last habit is arithmetic humility about which risk is largest. Ask an actuary what the biggest risk on an annuity book is and the answer is usually longevity, because that is the risk the product is named for. Section 4.1 says otherwise for the initial capital question: a point of interest basis moves 0.0792 of the premium, and no plausible one-year longevity surprise does that. Longevity is the largest risk over the life of the book and the interest basis is the largest risk at the moment of writing, and confusing the two leads an insurer to hedge the wrong exposure at the wrong time.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. The strain identity section 4.1 varies is established in the product framework, along with the BIIFIL classification that section 4.4's amount dimension reproduces and the conclusion that a life insurer's growth is capital-limited rather than demand-limited. The with-profit, index-linked and unit-linked bases the amount dimension selects between are in product bases and surplus distribution. The sum at risk that shrinks as a reserve grows, which is why section 4.3's natural hedge is too small, and the anti-selection mechanism whose sign reverses here, are both in assurances. Annuity valuation and the mortality tables the impaired-life split needs are developed in contingencies and actuarial mathematics. The option payoff in section 4.3 is priced properly in options, where the hockey stick and the reason an expected-value reserve misses its value are the standard results.

Where this goes next. Lectures 5 and 6 cover income protection and critical illness, where the information asymmetry of section 4.2 becomes the central design problem rather than a pricing adjustment, because both products turn on a claim definition the policyholder knows more about than the insurer and can influence after inception. Lecture 7 covers long-term care, which combines this lecture's longevity exposure with those products' claim-definition problem and is the hardest product on the syllabus for exactly that reason. Lecture 8 covers group products, where the compulsion that removes the insurer's ability to decline becomes an advantage rather than a constraint, since a whole-scheme purchase is not a selected one. The investment and matching problem this lecture's liabilities create is taken up in the investment module, where the duration of an annuity book sets the asset strategy.

Where the bank sits opposite. Section 4.1's finding, that the capital requirement is the difference between two large numbers and therefore hostage to an assumption, is the same structure as a bank's regulatory capital under the A-IRB approach, where the requirement is a small residual after subtracting expected loss from a stressed loss and is correspondingly sensitive to the correlation and confidence parameters; that machinery is in A-IRB capital modelling. Section 4.3's guarantee is a written option on the insurer's balance sheet, and its banking twin is the committed facility whose drawdown rises exactly when the borrower's credit falls, priced through the credit conversion factor in the same module. The correlation point is sharper still: a bank facing both falling rates and rising defaults has the same two-drivers-one-direction problem, which is why interest rate risk in the banking book and credit risk are stressed jointly rather than separately. And section 4.2's legal inability to decline has a direct banking analogue in the lending obligations imposed by national credit legislation, where an institution that cannot refuse must price, which is the same three-step chain from a legal constraint to a data strategy.
