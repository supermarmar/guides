A life insurer is a borrower before it is an underwriter

The premium arrives at a mean time of 9.5000 years and the benefit leaves at 20.0000, a gap of 10.5000 years which is 0.5250 of the term. That gap is the business. Step 1

The 30-second version

A life insurer sells a promise contingent on human life or health, takes the money now, and pays out decades later. That single ordering of cash flows generates every problem the rest of this track solves, and four consequences of it are computable directly from the summary's own income statement and balance sheet, without any additional theory.

Start with the ordering itself. Premiums on the illustrative endowment arrive at a mean time of 9.5000 years and the benefit leaves at 20.0000, so the insurer holds the money for 10.5000 years, which is 0.5250 of the term. On a cash basis the contract collects 20000.0000 and promises 30000.0000, a loss. At five per cent it accumulates 34719.2518 against the same promise, a profit of 4719.2518. The break-even rate is 0.037189 and the underwriting is identical on both sides of it, which is why the summary places policyholder investment income second in its revenue list, ahead of everything except the premium.

Then the income statement, which contains two costs that are not payments to anybody: the increase in reserves and the increase in solvency capital. Both rise with new business, so writing profitable business reduces this year's reported profit. A static book reports 602.5000 per policy in force and one growing at 0.4000 reports 378.2466, a fall of 0.3722, on identical per-policy experience. That also puts two of the summary's own stakeholder aims in direct conflict, since it lists management's short-term aims as maximising short-term profits and maximising new business levels.

Then the balance sheet, where free assets are what remains after reserves and solvency capital. On the illustrative figures free assets are 0.1000 of the balance sheet, so a 0.0500 strengthening of the reserving basis costs 0.4000 of them: a gearing of 8.0000, which is exactly the ratio of reserves to free assets. And the summary notes that the solvency capital requirement's place in the balance sheet is country specific, which matters because moving it out of the liabilities takes the gearing from 8.0000 to 4.0000 with nothing real having changed.

Finally the conflicts. The summary lists four aims for the actuarial department, two of which are increasing company profits and maximising policyholder bonuses. Its own income statement subtracts bonus from surplus to reach profit, so the two sum to a constant at every split, and the trade-off is exactly one for one at any surplus level. Two of the four aims are constraints and the two that are objectives are that zero-sum pair, which is the structural reason the role needs a mandate rather than a target.

Step 2

Why this matters

The ordering result matters because it explains what a life insurer actually sells and therefore what can go wrong with it. If the profit came from underwriting, the risks would be mortality and expenses and the discipline would be selection. Because the profit comes substantially from holding money over a long interval, the dominant risks are the ones attached to that interval: the interest rate assumed, the assets bought, and the duration mismatch between them and the liabilities. That is why an investment module exists in a life insurance syllabus at all, and why an insurer can be perfectly good at underwriting and still fail.

The reported profit result matters because it makes an insurer's income statement partly a statement about its sales volume. Two companies with identical products, identical experience and identical prospects will report materially different profits if one is growing and one is not, and the growing one will look worse. Anybody reading a life insurer's results needs to separate the effect of experience from the effect of volume before drawing any conclusion, and anybody running one needs to know that a good sales year is a bad reporting year.

The gearing result matters because it explains why actuarial assumptions are contested rather than merely estimated. A five per cent difference of opinion about the reserving basis is a forty per cent difference in the free assets, so a debate that looks technical is a debate about most of the shareholders' cushion. It also explains why solvency ratios cannot be compared across jurisdictions casually: the summary's own note that the solvency capital requirement's placement varies means two identical insurers can report different gearing depending on the convention their supervisor uses.

The conflict result matters because it is the clearest statement on the syllabus of what the actuarial role is. An optimisation problem needs an objective, and the summary's list gives the actuarial department two constraints and two objectives that sum to a constant. There is no maximum to find. What remains is judgement exercised under a mandate, which is why professional guidance appears in the list alongside regulation and why the with-profits actuary's discretion is regulated rather than delegated.

The Feynman version. Someone gives you a thousand pounds a year for twenty years, and you promise to hand back thirty thousand at the end. Count it up: they gave you twenty thousand and you owe thirty. On those numbers you have just agreed to lose ten thousand pounds. Every year for twenty years, on purpose.

Except you have the money in the meantime. The first thousand sits with you for twenty years, the second for nineteen, and so on. Invest it at five per cent and by the end you are holding nearly thirty-five thousand, so you pay the thirty and keep almost five. Same promise, same customer, same mortality. The difference between a bad business and a good one is the interest rate, and the switch flips at about three point seven per cent.

Which tells you what you actually are. You are not really in the business of guessing who will die. You are in the business of holding other people's money for a decade and a half and earning more on it than you promised them. The guessing matters, but it is the second thing.

Step 3

The mental model

Hold four pictures. The first is a long gap between two cash flows. Money in early, money out late, and the insurer standing in the middle earning on the difference. Every structural feature of a life insurer follows from the length of that gap: the size of the asset portfolio, the importance of matching, the sensitivity to interest rates, and the fact that a mistake made today is discovered in twenty years.

The second is an income statement with two costs that nobody receives. Increases in reserves and in solvency capital are transfers from this year's profit to future years' security, so a growing insurer reports the cost of its own growth and a shrinking one reports the release. Reported profit is therefore a function of the shape of the book as well as of its quality, and the two have to be separated deliberately.

The third is a thin residual between two thick numbers. Free assets are assets less reserves less solvency capital, so they move by a large multiple of any move in the assumptions underlying either subtraction. This is the fourth time this track has met that structure, after the new business strain identity, the annuity basis gap and the sum at risk, and it is worth treating as a general pattern: whenever a quantity is defined as a small difference, expect it to be the sensitive one.

The fourth is a fixed pie with two claimants. Surplus splits into bonus and profit, so policyholders and shareholders are dividing one number, and any statement that both should be maximised is a statement without content. That picture recurs whenever an institution has two constituencies with a claim on the same residual, which is most mutuals, most with-profits funds, and any bank deciding between retained earnings and dividends.

Step 4

The detail

4.1 The interest rate decides whether the business exists

The summary's income statement lists four sources of revenue in order: premiums received, policyholder investment income, shareholder investment income, and reinsurance benefit received. Investment income on policyholders' money comes second, ahead of everything except the premium itself. Take a twenty-year endowment paying 30000 at maturity for 1000 a year and settle it on a cash basis first.

Nominally the insurer collects 20000.0000 and promises 30000.0000, a cash loss of 10000.0000. At five per cent the same contract accumulates 34719.2518 and shows a profit of 4719.2518. The break-even rate is 0.037189, and nothing about the mortality, the expenses or the policyholder differs on either side of it. The underwriting is identical and the business is not.

Why that works is the gap between when the money arrives and when it leaves.

   the cash flow          amount        mean time     what the insurer
                          per policy    in years      does in between
   premiums in               20000.0000        9.5000   invests it
   benefit out               30000.0000       20.0000   pays it away
   the gap                   10000.0000       10.5000   earns the difference

The mean time of the premium stream against the single maturity payment.

Premiums arrive at a mean time of 9.5000 years and the benefit leaves at 20.0000, a gap of 10.5000 years, which is 0.5250 of the term. So a life insurer is a borrower before it is an underwriter, and the summary's placement of investment income high in the revenue list is a statement about where the money comes from rather than an accounting convention.

And this is the exact opposite of the banking case. A bank's money goes out first and comes back with interest, so it is a lender before it is a credit assessor. A life insurer's money comes in first and goes out later, so it is a borrower before it is an underwriter. Every difference in how the two are capitalised follows from that one reversal of sign: a bank earns from day one and needs capital against the possibility of not being repaid, while a life insurer pays its acquisition cost up front and needs capital against having already spent money it will not see back for years. The banking side of that comparison is developed in the banking track's own business model lecture.

One boundary. The illustration excludes mortality and expenses so the interest effect stands alone, which overstates the insurer's position on a real endowment where deaths bring some benefits forward and expenses reduce the amount invested. Including both moves the break-even rate up without changing the structure: there is still a rate below which the contract loses and above which it gains, and the gap between the mean premium time and the mean benefit time is still the reason.

4.2 Growth reduces reported profit

The summary's income statement lists six costs. Four of them are payments to somebody: reinsurance premiums, benefit payments, marginal expenses and a portion of fixed expenses. Two are not. The increase in reserves and the increase in solvency capital are transfers from this year's profit to future years' security, and both rise with new business.

   annual growth in   policies in   reported profit   profit per      is the book
   new business       force         for the year      policy in force reporting well?
   0.0000                   20.0000        12050.0000         602.5000   yes
   0.0500                   13.0853         7555.4586         577.3996   yes

A first-year contribution of -300 against a renewal-year contribution of 650, over a twenty-year book. Both invented for illustration; the two non-cash costs are the summary's.

A static book reports 602.5000 per policy in force and one growing at 0.4000 reports 378.2466, a fall of 0.3722 on identical per-policy experience. Nothing has gone wrong. The growing book simply has proportionally more first-year policies, and a first-year policy contributes -300.0000 where a renewal year contributes 650.0000. So an insurer's income statement is partly a statement about its sales volume, and the summary's two non-cash costs are the mechanism.

Which puts two of the summary's own stakeholder aims in direct conflict. Under internal stakeholders it splits management into long-term and short-term aims, and the short-term pair is maximising short-term profits and maximising new business levels. Those are the two ends of this table. The growth rate that maximises new business is the one that minimises reported profit, so the two cannot both be pursued, and the summary states them side by side without comment. That is not an error in the summary, it is an accurate description of a real institutional tension, and section 4.5 shows it is not the only one.

4.3 Free assets are a geared residual

The summary's balance sheet puts two things on the asset side, policyholder investments and shareholder investments, and three on the liability side: the solvency capital requirement, the reserves, and share capital or free assets. So free assets are what remains, and they are a small difference between large numbers.

                                                                       assets
   nothing, the base case  10000.0000  8000.0000 1000.0000     1000.0000              -
   reserving basis +0.0500 10000.0000  8400.0000 1000.0000      600.0000      -400.0000
   new business strain 200 9800.0000  8000.0000 1000.0000      800.0000      -200.0000
   SCR rises by 0.1000     10000.0000  8000.0000 1100.0000      900.0000      -100.0000

Assets of 10000 against reserves of 8000 and solvency capital of 1000. All invented for illustration; the balance sheet structure is the summary's.

Free assets are 1000.0000, which is 0.1000 of the balance sheet. A 0.0500 strengthening of the reserving basis costs 400.0000, or 0.4000 of the free assets: a gearing of 8.0000, which is exactly the ratio of reserves to free assets. So an insurer's solvency position moves by a large multiple of any move in its assumptions, and three different events drain the same residual: a stronger basis, new business, and a higher capital requirement.

This is the fourth time this track has met a quantity defined as a small difference between two large ones. The new business strain was the gap between a reserve and a premium; the annuity basis result was the gap between a single premium and the reserve it establishes; the sum at risk was a death benefit less a reserve. Free assets are the same structure at the level of the whole company, and the lesson is the same: the sensitivity of a difference is the ratio of the subtracted quantity to the difference itself, so a thin residual is always the volatile number.

And the summary flags that this presentation is not universal. It notes that the solvency capital requirement's place in the balance sheet is country specific and depends on whether the report is prepared for shareholders or for supervisory bodies. That matters because if the requirement is not shown as a liability, free assets become 2000.0000 and the gearing falls from 8.0000 to 4.0000. The insurer is identical. Which is why a solvency ratio cannot be compared across jurisdictions without knowing which convention is in use, and why the summary bothers to say so in a document that otherwise compresses hard.

4.4 The policy cycle's weight sits on the first day

The summary gives the policy cycle in five stages and, unusually for a compressed document, prints its own count of the items in each. Check the counts against the lists, then ask which stage carries the weight.

The summary's parenthetical counts, checked against its own enumerations.

The counts agree at all five stages, totalling 26 items. The first day has 7, more than the entire sale process at 6, and of those seven items exactly one is income: the premium earned. The other six are reserves established, solvency capital established, acquisition cost paid, administration cost paid, the initial portion of fixed expenses, and capital drawn from free assets to meet the resulting loss.

So the policy cycle is not a smooth progression through a product's life. It is a cliff on day one followed by decades of recovery, and the summary's own phrase for the day-one event is valuation strain. Read that way, the six items are not a list of administrative tasks but a description of a single event: the insurer spends money, establishes a liability, is required to hold capital against it, and funds the shortfall from its own resources, all before the policy has done anything.

The product cycle, which the summary gives separately as eight stages from product design through pricing, administration, marketing and sales, underwriting, claims management and experience monitoring to valuation, is the institutional counterpart: it names the departments rather than the events, and it is the map the rest of this module follows. Each of the sub-modules owns some of those stages, which is why the module index reads as a division of that list.

4.5 The actuarial department is asked to maximise a constant

The summary lists eight external stakeholder groups with their aims, from policyholders through competitors and reinsurers to fiscal authorities and government, and six internal ones. Most of the conflicts are between groups and are obvious: shareholders want the highest possible return and policyholders want good value for money, which is one number viewed from two sides. One conflict is inside a single group, and it is exact.

A surplus of 1000 split between bonus and pre-tax profit. The ordering that makes them sum to the surplus is the summary's own income statement.

Bonus and pre-tax profit sum to the surplus at every split, because the summary's income statement reaches surplus first, then subtracts the bonus declared, then arrives at pre-tax profit. The actuarial department's listed aims include both increasing company profits and maximising policyholder bonuses. Those are not competing priorities to be balanced, they are two halves of a constant, and a department asked to maximise both has been asked to maximise a number that cannot move.

   the actuarial department's aim        is this a constraint   who does it serve?
                                        or an objective?
   comply with regulations               constraint             the regulator
   comply with professional guidance     constraint             the profession
   increase company profits              objective              shareholders
   maximise policyholder bonuses         objective              policyholders

The four aims the summary lists, classified. The classification is this lecture's; the aims are transcribed.

Of the four aims, two are constraints and two are objectives, and the two objectives are the zero-sum pair. So the summary has described the actuarial position exactly without saying so: two things that must be obeyed, and two things that trade off one for one. That is why the role is defined by professional judgement rather than by optimisation, and it is the structural reason a with-profits actuary needs a mandate rather than a target.

And growing the surplus does not resolve it, which is the obvious escape. The rate at which profit falls as bonus rises is exactly -1.0000 at a surplus of 1000.0000, at 2000.0000 and at 10000.0000. The trade-off is one for one at every scale, so a bigger surplus enlarges the argument without changing its shape. What a larger surplus buys is a less painful negotiation, not a different one, and an actuary who promises both sides more has promised the same thing twice.

The other internal conflicts follow the same pattern once looked for. The marketing department's single listed aim is maximising new business levels in the short term, which section 4.2 showed reduces reported profit. The sales department and other employees both aim to maximise their own remuneration, which is a cost in the income statement. And management is explicitly split into long-term and short-term aims that differ, which is the summary acknowledging that the conflict is not only between departments but within the same people over different horizons.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The habit worth installing first is to ask, of any financial institution, which way its money flows in time. Money out then in makes a lender, and the risks are credit and recovery. Money in then out makes a borrower, and the risks are the ones attached to holding: the return earned, the duration matched, and the promise's real value when it falls due. Life insurers, general insurers, pension funds and banks divide cleanly on that question, and almost every difference in their regulation, capital and failure modes follows from it. Anybody who can state which side an institution sits on has most of the analysis.

The second habit is to separate experience from volume in any reported result. A growing life insurer reports the cost of its growth; a shrinking one reports the release of its past. Neither figure describes how good the business is, and the correction requires knowing the shape of the book rather than only its size. The same distortion appears in a bank's impairment charge when the loan book is growing, and in any accounting system where a provision is established on origination.

The third habit is to compute the gearing of any residual before arguing about the inputs. Free assets, new business strain, sum at risk and the annuity basis gap are all thin differences between thick numbers, and in every case the sensitivity is the ratio of what was subtracted to what remains. That single ratio tells you how much a disagreement about assumptions is worth, and it is usually far more than the size of the disagreement suggests. It also tells you when a presentational change matters, as the solvency capital placement does here.

The fourth habit is to check whether stated objectives can be jointly maximised before treating a mandate as a management problem. When two objectives sum to a constant there is no optimum, only a distribution, and pretending otherwise produces either paralysis or a covert decision. The right response is to convert one of them into a constraint and optimise the other, which is exactly what a with-profits mandate does when it fixes a bonus policy and lets profit follow. Naming which objective has become a constraint is the useful act.

The last habit is to read a summary's own counts and orderings as claims rather than as formatting. The income statement's ordering makes bonus and profit a zero-sum pair, and nothing else in the document says so. The policy cycle's counts locate the whole weight of the business on day one. The revenue list's ordering puts investment income second. None of those is stated as a finding, and all three are derivable from the layout. Documents that compress hard put their arguments in their structure, and reading the structure is part of reading the document.

Step 7

Connections

What this assumes. Nothing in this track, which is where it belongs: this is the first lecture of the life insurance module and everything it needs is arithmetic. The strain identity that section 4.4's day-one cliff describes is developed with its five factors in the product framework, and the products whose cash flows section 4.1 illustrates are catalogued through the products module, which is complete. The accumulation and discounting in section 4.1 are the compound interest material in financial mathematics, and the mean-time calculation is the Macaulay duration idea developed properly there. The with-profits surplus that section 4.5 divides is the subject of product bases and surplus distribution, where the bonus mechanics that make the split a real decision are set out.

Where this goes next. Overview lecture 2 reads the income statement and balance sheet line by line, taking this lecture's two structural results and turning them into a working knowledge of where surplus arises and how it is recognised. Lecture 3 is the capital hinge: it takes the portfolio view of new business strain, how much capital a given growth plan needs and over what period it returns, which is the aggregate counterpart to the per-policy identity already established in the products module. Lecture 4 develops the stakeholder conflicts that section 4.5 opened, and lecture 5 maps the policy lifecycle onto the sub-modules so that each stage has an owner. After the overview, the intended sequence is products, then pricing, then risk management, then risk measurement, then investment.

Where the bank sits opposite. Section 4.1's reversal is the cleanest comparison in either track. A bank's money goes out and comes back, so it holds capital against not being repaid; an insurer's money comes in and goes out, so it holds capital against having already spent what it will not see for years. The banking business model is in the banking business model, and its financial statements in financial management, where the same income statement exercise runs on a net interest margin rather than a premium. Section 4.2's growth distortion has an exact banking counterpart in expected credit loss provisioning, where a growing loan book charges impairment on origination and depresses reported profit, developed in IFRS 9 impairment modelling. Section 4.3's geared residual is the common equity tier 1 ratio, a thin difference between large numbers that moves by a multiple of any change in risk-weighted assets, in capital management. And section 4.5's zero-sum split is the bank's retained earnings decision, where the same residual is claimed by dividends and by capital adequacy.
