Life insurance, internal environment

Risk management

Seven lectures covering F102 chapters 13 to 15, 29 to 31 and 34. Identification and mitigation, not quantification: what can go wrong, and what a life office does about it before the actuary has to put a number on it. The quantification layer sits next door in risk measurement.

How this module is organised

Three lectures inventory the risks, then four cover the mitigants. That split mirrors the banking track, where risk identification and risk mitigation are separate lectures inside the same module.

The two mitigants that matter are underwriting and reinsurance. Underwriting controls what enters the book; reinsurance controls how much of it stays. Between them they account for four of the seven lectures and considerably more than four sevenths of the exam weight in this module.

Status badges show what is built (built) or planned. Planned lectures show their intended filename.

Sources of risk

  • planned01. Risk sources 01-risk_sources.html — the full inventory, then the two that are easiest to underestimate: policy and other data, and the assumptions built on it (Ch 13)
  • planned02. New business and other risks 02-new_business_and_other_risks.html — volume and mix risk, the strain that unexpected success causes, plus operational, expense and mismatching risk (Ch 14)
  • planned03. Aggregation and credit failure 03-aggregation_and_credit_failure.html — where exposures accumulate without anyone deciding they should, catastrophe concentration, and failure of a reinsurer, a bond issuer or a distributor (Ch 15)

Writing too much profitable business can be as dangerous as writing unprofitable business, because new business strain is proportional to volume while capital is not. That is the counter-intuitive result in lecture 02, and it connects directly to the capital management lecture in the internal environment overview.

Lecture 03 is the natural bridge to the banking track. Credit failure here is the same phenomenon covered in depth under credit risk; the difference is that a life insurer meets it through its bond portfolio and its reinsurance counterparties rather than through lending, and it is measured with far less machinery.

Underwriting

  • planned04. Underwriting 04-underwriting.html — the purpose and the process, medical and financial underwriting, choosing the level of underwriting, interpreting the evidence, specifying ordinary and special terms, claims underwriting, the marketability cost, and how reinsurers influence the terms (Ch 31)

One lecture for a long chapter, because the material is a single connected argument rather than a set of separable topics. The argument runs: anti-selection exists, underwriting is the defence, every additional question costs sales, so the level of underwriting is an optimisation rather than a maximisation. Financial underwriting is the part most often forgotten in exam answers, and it is the part that catches fraud rather than adverse health.

Reinsurance

  • planned05. Reinsurance types 05-reinsurance_types.html — facultative against obligatory, original terms and coinsurance, risk premium reinsurance and how the two compare, excess of loss, stop loss, catastrophe cover, and financial reinsurance in its risk premium and contingent loan forms (Ch 29)
  • planned06. Reinsurance use 06-reinsurance_use.html — the primary and secondary reasons for reinsuring, what to consider before doing it, how the choice varies by class of business, the cost, how retention limits are set, and the counterparty and legal risks it introduces (Ch 30)

The arrangements themselves are covered from a general insurance angle in risk modelling, where the interest is in what proportional and non-proportional treaties do to the claim distribution. These lectures take the life office view instead: reinsurance as a source of capital and of underwriting expertise, not only as a way to cut the tail. Financial reinsurance in particular has no general insurance analogue, and it is the topic in this module most likely to be examined as a numerical question.

Further risk management

  • planned07. Further risk management 07-further_risk_management.html — the remaining levers: expense control, managing policy retention, managing new business mix and volume, managing the options written to policyholders, and what can and cannot be done about systematic risk (Ch 34)

The residual lecture, and the one that closes the loop. Each lever here responds to a risk named in lectures 01 to 03, and each has a measurement counterpart in experience analysis: you cannot manage retention without a withdrawal analysis, or control expenses without an expense analysis.
