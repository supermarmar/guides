Risk measurement, life insurance

Policyholder values

Five lectures covering F102 chapters 25 and 26. What the office pays, or credits, when a policyholder does not see the contract through to its original terms. A surrender ends the contract for cash; an alteration keeps it alive on different terms. Both require the actuary to put a value on a partly completed promise, and both leave the remaining book different from the book that was priced.

How this sub-module is organised

Two chapters over five lectures, and the ratio is deliberate rather than generous. Three things make this material examinable out of proportion to its length in the syllabus: the two alteration methods give different answers to the same question, the selection effects are subtle and routinely missed, and the whole topic sits on the boundary between actuarial judgement and treating customers fairly, which is where professional guidance bites hardest.

Surrender values come first, split into principles and then methods, because a paid-up value is a surrender value that stays inside the contract and the alteration methods reuse the surrender machinery. Alterations then take three lectures: paid-up values, the two general methods, and the consequences.

Read the asset shares lectures before any of it. The asset share is the natural upper bound on what a surrendering policyholder can fairly be paid, and the principles of surrender are largely statements about how close to it the office should come.

Surrender values

Surrender value principles 01-surrender_value_principles.html — what a surrender value is and what it is not, why it is rarely guaranteed, the principles the office should observe, the asset share as the anchor and the equity between surrendering and continuing policyholders, and what the office must disclose about its basis (Ch 25)
  • planned02. Surrender value methods 02-surrender_value_methods.html — the retrospective and prospective methods, the calculation under each, the assumptions each requires, and a direct comparison of what they produce and when an office would prefer either (Ch 25)

The retrospective method accumulates what the policyholder has contributed; the prospective method values what the office is being released from. They answer different questions and they do not agree, and the choice between them is a statement about whether the office is paying back a contribution or settling a liability.

Two methods, two answers

Equating policy values sets the value of the altered contract equal to the value of the original, which requires a basis and is sensitive to it. The proportionate paid-up value method scales the benefit by the proportion of premiums paid, which requires almost nothing and is correspondingly crude. Being able to say which is more appropriate for a given alteration, and why, is a recurring question, and it is a question about materiality and practicality rather than about which method is theoretically superior.

Selection, the part that is missed

Policyholders who surrender are not a random sample of policyholders, and policyholders who convert a contract to paid-up are not either. Healthy lives surrender term assurance because they can replace it more cheaply; impaired lives keep it, so the surviving book is worse than the book that was priced. The direction reverses on an annuity, where the lives most likely to want out are the ones least likely to live long. Lecture 05 makes that argument product by product rather than stating it as a general principle.

The consequences reach outwards. The withdrawal assumption in setting assumptions has to anticipate it, the withdrawal analysis in experience analysis has to detect it, and the retention levers in further risk management are what an office does about it. This sub-module is the point where those three connect.
