Capital and embedded value

Four lectures covering F102 chapter 24. Reserves cover the expected; capital covers the adverse. This sub-module sets out what capital a supervisor requires and why, how a one-year value-at-risk requirement is constructed, and then turns the same projection machinery to a different question: not how much the office must hold, but what its existing book is worth.

How this sub-module is organised

Principles, then the requirement, then the two families of technique for calculating it, then embedded value. The syllabus treats capital requirements and embedded value in a single chapter, which reads oddly at first, but the connection is real: both are projections of the same cashflows under a stated basis, and the difference is only what question is being asked of the projection.

Read reserving first. A capital requirement is defined relative to a reserve, so the answer moves whenever the reserving basis moves, and that dependency is the single most common source of confusion in the topic.

What transfers from banking, and what does not

A great deal transfers. A one-year value-at-risk requirement at a high confidence level is the same construction as the Basel capital formula: pick a horizon, pick a confidence level, measure the distance from the expected outcome to the adverse quantile, and hold that. The tiering of admissible capital is the same idea as the bank loss-absorbing stack. Even the aggregation across risk types uses correlation matrices in both worlds.

Three things do not transfer. First, the horizon: a bank measures credit loss over one year on a portfolio it can reprice, whereas a life insurer holds a thirty-year promise it cannot reprice, so the one-year formulation is a convention rather than a description of the exposure. Second, the direction of the risk: a bank's assets are risky and its liabilities are nearly certain, and for a life insurer it is the other way round. Third, embedded value has no banking analogue at all.

Where the exam catches people

Embedded value is not a capital measure and not a reserve. It is the shareholder's value in the existing book: net assets plus the discounted value of future shareholder profits, less the cost of holding the required capital, and with no credit for business not yet written. Every part of that sentence is examinable, and the exclusion of future new business is the part most often forgotten.
