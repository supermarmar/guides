Experience analysis

Three lectures covering F102 chapters 32 and 35. The feedback loop that closes the track. Every assumption set in pricing was a claim about the future; experience analysis is where those claims are checked against what happened, and where the difference is decomposed into causes specific enough to act on. An office that prices well but never analyses its experience is guessing repeatedly rather than learning.

How this sub-module is organised

Data quality first, because every analysis downstream inherits its errors. Then the decrement and expense analyses, which is the routine monitoring work. Then the two reconciliations, analysis of surplus and analysis of embedded value movement, which take the same experience and express it as movements in the balance sheet and in shareholder value.

The syllabus splits data checks into chapter 32 and puts monitoring in chapter 35, thirteen chapters apart. They belong together and are taught together here.

Lectures

  • planned02. Monitoring experience 02-monitoring_experience.html — why experience is monitored at all, the data an analysis needs and the exposure conventions it depends on, the expense analysis, the withdrawal analysis, and how the results are used (Ch 35)
  • planned03. Analysis of surplus and embedded value 03-analysis_of_surplus_and_ev.html — decomposing the change in surplus into its sources, the same decomposition applied to the movement in embedded value, and what each variance tells the office to change (Ch 32, 35)

What the analysis is for

Four uses, and they pull in different directions. Monitoring checks whether experience is running to assumption. Updating feeds the next assumption review. Management information tells the business which product lines and which distribution channels are actually working. Pricing takes the updated assumptions and repeats the cycle.

The analysis of surplus is the one that ties the module together. Actual surplus in a period differs from expected surplus, and the analysis attributes the difference to mortality, morbidity, withdrawals, expenses, investment return, new business and the change of basis. Done properly the attributions sum back to the total, which is a genuine check on the whole apparatus: if the analysis does not reconcile, either the experience data or the valuation is wrong. Done carelessly it produces a list of numbers that sum to something else and nobody notices.

Order matters

Variances are calculated one at a time against a moving baseline, so the order in which the items are taken changes the individual attributions even though the total is unchanged. The convention must be stated and applied consistently between periods, or the resulting trend in any single variance is an artefact of the ordering rather than a feature of the experience.

The credibility question sits underneath all of it: how much experience is enough before an assumption should move. The statistical machinery for that judgement is in risk modelling, under credibility theory and empirical Bayes, and these lectures point at it rather than repeating it.
