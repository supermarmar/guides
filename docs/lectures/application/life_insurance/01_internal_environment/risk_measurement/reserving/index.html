Reserving

Four lectures covering F102 chapter 23. A reserve is the amount an insurer holds now so that it can meet a promise later. That definition is simple and the arithmetic is standard; what is neither simple nor standard is the choice of method, the choice of basis, and the treatment of future premiums, and those three choices are what separate a supervisory reserve from a best-estimate liability from a market-consistent value of the same contract.

How this sub-module is organised

Purpose first, then the two traditional methods together so the comparison is direct, then the unit-linked case which behaves differently enough to need its own lecture, then market-consistent valuation which is where modern supervisory regimes have landed.

Prospective and retrospective reserves, the equation of value, and the conditions under which the two agree are established in actuarial mathematics. Reserves inside a profit test, including zeroisation, are in reserves under profit testing. This sub-module assumes both.

Where the marks are

Lecture 02 is the classic examinable comparison. The net premium valuation ignores the actual office premium and values a notional net premium instead, which caps the credit taken for future premium income and so builds in an implicit margin. The gross premium valuation uses the real premium and the real expenses, which is more informative and less automatically prudent. Knowing which one a regime mandates matters less than being able to say what each does to the reserve and why a supervisor might prefer either.

Lecture 03 carries the subtlest point in the sub-module. A negative non-unit reserve says the future charges on a contract exceed the future non-unit outgo, which is true and which would, if recognised, let the office release capital today against profit it has not yet earned. Whether that is permitted, and under what conditions, is a supervisory judgement rather than an actuarial one.
