Life insurance, ASSA F102

External environment

Seven lectures built on F102 chapters 11 and 12. Everything the insurer operates inside and does not control: who sells its products, what the economy does to them, what the law and the supervisor permit, how the accounts must present them, how they are taxed, and what the profession requires of the actuary who signs them off. Two chapters stretched over seven lectures, because the regulatory and accounting layers need more than the source summary gives them.

How this module is organised

The first lecture is the framework: the full list of external factors, so that later lectures can be recognised as instances of it rather than as a miscellany. Distribution then gets its own lecture because it drives product design more directly than any other external factor. The remaining five take one environment each.

This module mirrors the banking external environment in shape and in the division of labour with the regulation track. The exam framing lives here: why the regime exists, what it forces the actuary to do, and what would change if it changed. The standard-by-standard treatment lives in regulation, organised by jurisdiction.

Framework and distribution

  • planned01. General business environment 01-general_business_environment.html — the complete list of external factors and how each one reaches the insurer's product range, its pricing basis and its capital position (Ch 11)
  • planned02. Distribution channels 02-distribution_channels.html — the channels available, what each costs, the business each brings, and the effect the channel has on product design, on the expense basis and on persistency (Ch 11)

Distribution is the external factor with the most direct grip on the product. A tied agent selling on commission produces a different persistency profile, a different expense loading and a different anti-selection risk from a direct-to-consumer channel selling the same contract, and the pricing basis has to reflect it. That connection back to pricing is what makes this a technical lecture rather than a descriptive one.

The five environments

  • planned03. Economic environment 03-economic_environment.html — inflation, interest rates, investment markets and the state of the economy, and how each feeds the assumption basis and the demand for savings and protection products (Ch 12)
  • planned04. Regulatory environment 04-regulatory_environment.html — what insurance supervision is for, prudential against conduct regulation, and the South African regime under the Insurance Act 18 of 2017 and the prudential standards that sit under it (Ch 12, plus external sources)
  • planned05. Accounting environment 05-accounting_environment.html — how insurance contracts are reported, the contractual service margin and the risk adjustment under IFRS 17, and the variable fee approach for with-profits and unit-linked business (external addition, not in Ch 12)
  • planned06. Fiscal regime 06-fiscal_regime.html — how the insurer and the policyholder are taxed, why the answer differs by product and by fund, and what tax does to product design and to the investment strategy (Ch 12)
  • planned07. Professional guidance 07-professional_guidance.html — the actuary's professional obligations, the standards and practice notes that bind the work, and where professional judgement is required rather than permitted (Ch 12)

The two gaps worth naming

Lecture 04 and lecture 05 both go substantially beyond the source summaries, and for the same reason: the summaries are written to a syllabus that treats the regulatory and accounting environments generically, while the exam is South African and the accounting standard changed.

On regulation, the local regime matters. The Insurance Act 18 of 2017 and the prudential standards issued under it govern how a South African life office holds capital, and the summary treats regulation as a generic external factor without reaching them. The lecture will close that, and the numbering of individual standards will be verified against the Prudential Authority's own published set rather than reproduced from memory.

On accounting, IFRS 17 has no chapter in the source at all. It changes how insurance liabilities are measured and presented, and the contractual service margin in particular changes when profit is recognised on a contract, which reaches directly into how an office reports the business that pricing wrote. Leaving it out would be a real hole rather than a stylistic one. The standard itself is treated in regulation; the lecture here covers what it does to the actuary's work.
