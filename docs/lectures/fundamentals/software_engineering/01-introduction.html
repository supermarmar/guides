Lectures / Software engineering

What is software engineering?

A program is a recipe. Software engineering is what gets a thousand bakers, in a thousand kitchens, over the course of a decade, to keep producing the same cake reliably while the recipe is edited, the kitchens are renovated, and someone has started asking for it vegan. This lecture defines the field, traces it from the 1968 software crisis, and names the four ideas that the rest of the course is built on.

Lecture 1 of 16 Reading time, 40 minutes Part I: the craft of writing code
Step 1

The 30-second version

A program is a sequence of instructions that a machine can execute. Programming is the act of writing one. Software engineering is the discipline of producing programs that other people, including a future version of yourself, can read, run, modify, and trust. The same instructions can be brilliant when written by one person for one machine on one Tuesday afternoon, and worse than useless when handed to a colleague six months later. Software engineering is what closes that gap.

As a recognised field, software engineering begins in 1968, at a NATO-sponsored conference in Garmisch convened to address what the attendees called the software crisis. Projects were over-running budgets by orders of magnitude, defects in production were endemic, and capable people were leaving the field in despair. The diagnosis was that the building of programs had been treated as an art and a craft, when the scale of the systems being attempted now demanded an engineering discipline, with methodologies, professional standards, and lifecycle accountability. The conference proposed the term software engineering as a deliberate provocation: a discipline that did not yet exist, named so that it might.

Sixty years on the crisis has not gone away. The cost of poor-quality software in the United States alone was estimated at 2.84 trillion US dollars in 2020 (CISQ, after the Consortium for Information & Software Quality). But the discipline has matured: we have languages that prevent whole categories of error, version control that lets us undo any change, automated tests that warn us before users do, and deployment pipelines that bring a fix to production in minutes instead of months. The rest of this course is the toolbox.

Feynman test A program is a recipe. Programming-in-the-small is what gets the recipe to make a cake once. Software engineering is what gets a thousand bakers, in a thousand kitchens, over the course of a decade, to keep producing the same cake reliably while the recipe is being edited, the kitchens are being renovated, the bakers are coming and going, and someone has started asking for the same cake but vegan. The cake is the deliverable; everything else is the practice. The cost of getting the practice wrong is paid not in the first cake, but in the thousandth.
What's beyond the syllabus The 1968 conference's choice of name is itself an artefact worth noticing. Engineering implies repeatable methods, calculable tolerances, and a body of theory underwriting the practice. Software has, even now, very little of that: there is no equivalent of structural analysis for code, no proof procedure that survives contact with industrial systems. Bauer, who chaired the Garmisch conference, later said the term was chosen partly to provoke. The provocation worked; the body of theory remains a work in progress.
Step 2

Why this matters

Two empirical findings should be enough to take the discipline seriously. The first is Boehm's cost-of-defects curve (Boehm 1981, refined ever since): the cost of fixing a defect rises by roughly an order of magnitude with each lifecycle stage at which the defect survives. A bug caught while writing the requirements costs one unit; the same bug caught in design costs five; in code, ten; in integration testing, fifty; in production, a hundred or more. The exact multiplier varies with the system, but the shape of the curve is invariant. Every practice in this course exists, in part, to push the detection of defects further to the left.

The second is the DORA programme (DevOps Research and Assessment, summarised in Forsgren, Humble, & Kim 2018, Accelerate). Across a decade of survey data, four metrics, deployment frequency, lead time for change, mean time to restore service, and change failure rate, predict both organisational performance and individual job satisfaction. The high performers do not trade off speed against quality; they get more of both, because their engineering practices, automated tests, version control, continuous delivery, are co-determined. The DORA finding contradicts the older view that quality is what you sacrifice to ship faster; the modern view is that quality is what lets you ship faster.

The implication for an individual practitioner is that the techniques in this course are not luxuries, paid for out of an output budget. They are the very things that pay for the output budget. A small investment in setup, conventions, tests, and review compounds into a much faster shipping cadence, because the cost of the next change does not grow without bound.

Step 3

The mental model

Three lenses are worth holding at once. They are not competing theories of software; they are complementary descriptions of the same artefact viewed at different scales.

Code as text for humans (Knuth)

Donald Knuth's literate programming agenda (Knuth 1984) starts from the observation that any source file has two audiences: the compiler, which must accept it, and the human reader, who must understand it. The two are not equally demanding. The compiler will accept any syntactically valid program; the human reader will give up on most of them. Knuth's prescription, that we should write programs as expository documents from which the machine-readable form is extracted, was always too radical for the mainstream. The underlying observation, that the human reader is the binding constraint on a program's life, is now textbook orthodoxy. Every modern style guide, every code-review practice, every naming convention is a downstream consequence.

Programs as theory (Naur)

Peter Naur's "Programming as theory building" (Naur 1985) is the most read-and-rereadable essay in software engineering. Naur argues that the "real" program is not the source code, but the theory the development team has formed about the problem domain: what the world contains, how the code models it, why each design choice was made, and what changes would or would not preserve the model. The source code is a partial dump of this theory; the rest lives in the team's heads, in conversations, in mailing lists, and, increasingly, in commit messages and design documents.

The consequence is uncomfortable. If the program is a theory, replacing the team while retaining the source code is not a continuity operation but a discontinuous one, equivalent to a translator producing a new theory from a foreign-language text. Naur uses this argument to explain why so many "rewrite from scratch" projects fail, why "we still need the original developer in the room" so often, and why documentation written for compliance is so much less useful than documentation written to articulate the theory.

Essential vs accidental complexity (Brooks)

Fred Brooks's "No Silver Bullet" (Brooks 1986) is the most quoted essay in the field. Brooks divides the difficulty of building software into two species. Essential complexity is the irreducible difficulty inherent in the problem itself: a payroll system is hard partly because payroll, with its tax law, edge cases, and stakeholder disagreements, is hard. Accidental complexity is the difficulty added by our tools and our practices: the language we chose, the build system that does not work on a colleague's laptop, the deployment process that needs eight people on a call.

Brooks's pessimistic claim, which has held up well, is that the history of software engineering is the steady elimination of accidental complexity (higher-level languages, automatic memory management, declarative builds, integrated test runners), and that the curve of remaining gains is asymptotic. We have already picked the big fruit. Essential complexity is, by definition, what we cannot reduce; no single innovation, "no silver bullet", will give us another order-of-magnitude improvement in productivity. This is why advances come from accumulated practice, not heroic invention.

Why three lenses, not one The lenses answer different questions. Knuth answers "for whom is the code written?" (humans, primarily). Naur answers "what is the code, really?" (a partial record of a theory). Brooks answers "why is this hard?" (essentially hard, plus the accidents we have not yet eliminated). Together they explain why no methodology has ever made software simple: the difficulty is essential (Brooks), the artefact is incomplete (Naur), and the principal reader is irreducibly human (Knuth).

The four properties a program is judged on

Set the lenses aside for a moment. When practitioners argue about whether a piece of code is "good", they are usually arguing about four properties:

CorrectnessThe program does what it is supposed to do, on the inputs it will be given, in the time it will have to do it. Tested for via automated tests, type checks, formal proofs, code review, and finally users.
MaintainabilityA competent reader who has not seen the program before can make a correct change to it in reasonable time. Tested for via style consistency, naming, modular structure, and the absence of "clever" code.
PerformanceThe program uses the resources (time, memory, network, money) appropriate to the value it produces. Tested for via profiling, benchmarks, and capacity planning.
DeployabilityThe program can be moved into production reliably and reversibly, by someone who is not the author. Tested for via continuous integration, reproducible builds, and deployment automation.

The four are not independent; an unmaintainable program eventually becomes incorrect because nobody can change it without breaking it, and a non-deployable program may as well be incorrect because nobody can run the corrected version. The argument over which of the four to optimise for first is the central organising debate of the field, and it is what most "religious wars" between teams and tools are really about.

Step 4

The detail

A short history

The 1968 Garmisch conference is the conventional starting point, but the practices it tried to codify had been accumulating for two decades. The 1950s saw the first compilers (Backus's FORTRAN, 1957) and the first general-purpose business languages (COBOL, 1959). The 1960s saw the first operating systems large enough to demand a team of engineers (IBM's OS/360, 1964), and Brooks's experience managing that project produced the second-most-cited book in the field, The Mythical Man-Month (1975, with the famous claim that "adding people to a late project makes it later").

The 1970s gave us structured programming (Dijkstra), the relational model (Codd), and Unix (Thompson and Ritchie). The 1980s gave us object orientation and the personal computer. The 1990s gave us the web, the open-source movement, and version control (CVS, then Subversion). The 2000s gave us agile methodologies (the Agile Manifesto, 2001), test-driven development (Beck 2003), and continuous integration. The 2010s gave us git, GitHub, container deployment, and DevOps. The 2020s have given us large-language-model assistants, of which this lecture is partly a product, and an unsettled debate over how they will reshape every practice in the course.

The pattern across the seven decades is that almost every advance has been about reducing accidental complexity, exactly as Brooks predicted in 1986. The languages are higher-level; the tools are more automated; the feedback loops are tighter; the unit of deployment has shrunk from a printed deck of cards to a containerised microservice. The essential complexity, that programs model the world and the world is intricate and changing, is mostly unchanged.

Brooks's four essential difficulties

Brooks names four properties of software that make it essentially, not accidentally, hard. They are worth committing to memory, because they survive every change in language, tooling, or methodology.

PropertyWhat it meansWhy it cannot be eliminated
ComplexitySoftware systems have more distinct parts, with more distinct interactions, than physical systems of comparable size.The complexity is the function. A simpler model is a different program, not a refactor.
ConformitySoftware must conform to its environment, to legacy data, to external regulations, to other software it integrates with.The world is not designed by the engineer. The interfaces are given.
ChangeabilitySuccessful software is pressed to do more, and to be modified, much faster than physical artefacts.Cost of physical change limits hardware revision; software has no equivalent friction.
InvisibilitySoftware has no spatial form. We cannot pick it up, walk around it, point at the part that is broken.The structure exists only in the mind and in tools that visualise small slices of it.

Each of the four maps onto a discipline in this course. Complexity is what Part I (style, idiom, language) tries to control at the line and file level. Conformity is what Parts III and IV (documentation, version control, collaboration) handle at the artefact and team level. Changeability is what Part II (testing, debugging) makes survivable. Invisibility is what Part III (diagrams as code, literate documentation) tries to compensate for.

What "good code" means: Beck's four rules

Kent Beck, who wrote the foundational book on test-driven development, also gave us the most operational definition of "good code". A program is well designed, Beck claims (Beck 2008, after Fowler), if and only if it satisfies four rules, in priority order:

  1. Passes its tests. The program does what it is supposed to do, and there is automated evidence of that. Without this, none of the other rules matter.
  2. Reveals intention. A reader who has never seen the code before can tell, from the names and structure, what each part is for. This is what most style guides are trying to enforce.
  3. Contains no duplication. Every fact about the system, every constant, every rule, lives in exactly one place. Duplication is the most common source of bugs in modified code: a change in one copy is forgotten in the other.
  4. Uses the fewest elements. Among solutions that satisfy 1, 2, and 3, the shorter one is better. Lines of code, classes, methods: fewer is better, all else equal.

The priority order matters. A program with no duplication that fails its tests is worse than one with some duplication that passes. The four rules also give a definition of "refactoring" that is not vacuous: a refactor is a change that improves at least one of rules 2 to 4 without violating rule 1. If the tests still pass, the change is safe; if they do not, you have introduced a regression, and the "refactor" was something else.

The Zen of Python as an executable style guide

Most languages have community norms; Python has the unusual property that its community norms are themselves a piece of source code. Tim Peters's Zen of Python (PEP 20, 2004) ships with every Python distribution and is recited by typing import this at the REPL. It is the closest the field has come to a one-page expression of good engineering taste:

Beautiful is better than ugly.
Explicit is better than implicit.
Simple is better than complex.
Complex is better than complicated.
Flat is better than nested.
Sparse is better than dense.
Readability counts.
Special cases aren't special enough to break the rules.
Although practicality beats purity.
Errors should never pass silently.
Unless explicitly silenced.
In the face of ambiguity, refuse the temptation to guess.
There should be one-- and preferably only one --obvious way to do it.
Although that way may not be obvious at first unless you're Dutch.
Now is better than never.
Although never is often better than *right* now.
If the implementation is hard to explain, it's a bad idea.
If the implementation is easy to explain, it may be a good idea.
Namespaces are one honking great idea -- let's do more of those!

Read the Zen as a checklist for the four properties from Step 3. "Beautiful is better than ugly", "readability counts", and "if the implementation is hard to explain, it's a bad idea" all serve maintainability. "Errors should never pass silently" serves correctness. "Simple is better than complex" and "complex is better than complicated" hint at performance, in the sense that simpler programs are easier to reason about and optimise. The Zen is the spine of Lecture 2, where we connect each line to a specific style-guide consequence.

The Zen is not a rulebook Peters wrote the Zen as a half-joke at a 1999 conference, and it includes one deliberately ironic line ("Although never is often better than *right* now"). Treating it as a strict rulebook misreads its tone. The intent is to articulate a sensibility, a habit of mind. A practitioner who has the sensibility breaks any one of the lines, on occasion, deliberately, with reason. A practitioner who treats it as a rulebook ends up writing code that satisfies the letter and violates every principle the Zen actually expresses.

The lifecycle and where each practice lives

Every change to a real codebase moves through roughly six stages: specify (decide what is to be built), code (write the change), review (have a colleague check it), test (run automated checks against it), deploy (put it in front of users), and monitor (watch how it behaves there). Boehm's curve from Step 2 is a statement that the cost of fixing a defect rises as the change travels through these stages. The practices we will learn correspond, roughly, to the stages.

Why software is different from other engineering

The 1968 conference's choice of name invited the obvious comparison: if software is engineering, why is it so unlike civil, mechanical, or electrical engineering? The answer is in Brooks's four essential difficulties, but it is worth stating directly.

Civil and mechanical engineers build artefacts that are physical: a bridge has a location, a weight, a maximum load; the cost of modifying it after construction is enormous, and the modifications are visible. Software has none of these. A program has no weight and no location; modifying it costs almost nothing in materials, only in human attention; and the modification is invisible until you read the diff. The discipline of software engineering is therefore not analogous to the discipline of bridge-building. It is closer to the discipline of writing a constantly-edited legal document by a rotating team of authors, where any error in the text causes the building described by the document to collapse.

One consequence of the analogy is that the techniques of software engineering have more in common with the techniques of academic writing, peer review, citation discipline, archival care, than with traditional engineering. This is not an accident. Knuth's literate-programming agenda, Naur's theory-building view, and the modern practice of code review all treat code as a kind of writing, with editors, drafts, and revision. Lectures 8, 9, and 14 take this seriously.

Worked example: a one-line change, end to end

You discover that a constant in your codebase, the discount-rate cap used in a pricing model, is set to 0.40 when it should be 0.45. The fix is one line. Trace it through the lifecycle:

1. Specify: a colleague writes a one-line ticket; you confirm the number with the product owner. (Cost so far: 5 minutes.)

2. Code: you change the constant in src/pricing/config.py. (1 minute.)

3. Review: you open a PR with the diff and a sentence of context. A reviewer notices that you changed the constant but did not update the test that pins it at 0.40. (10 minutes, two engineers.)

4. Test: you fix the test. CI runs the full suite in 4 minutes and catches that a downstream calibration test was also pinned to 0.40. You fix it. (15 minutes.)

5. Deploy: the PR is merged. The deployment pipeline ships it to staging in 3 minutes; you smoke-test the price endpoint; it goes to production within an hour. (1 hour.)

6. Monitor: a dashboard alarm catches that revenue on one product line dropped 3 per cent; a colleague suspects the new cap. You investigate; the new cap is correct, but the upstream attribution model was over-weighting the capped segment. The fix involved is unrelated. (3 hours.)

Total cost: ~5 hours of human attention for a one-line change. Without the test, review, CI, deploy pipeline, and monitoring, the same change might have shipped in 15 minutes, but the calibration regression would have surfaced as a customer complaint a week later, costing several days of investigation and a credit. The practices in this course are not overhead. They are the cheapest way to make the change.

Step 5

Synthesis and what's next

You now have the field's working definition (software engineering is what makes a program survive other people, other machines, and the passage of time), its founding diagnosis (the 1968 software crisis and Boehm's curve), three intellectual lenses (Knuth, Naur, Brooks), and four properties to judge code on (correctness, maintainability, performance, deployability).
