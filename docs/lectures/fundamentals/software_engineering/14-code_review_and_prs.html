Collaboration: branching, code review, pull requests

Code review is the social workflow that runs on top of version control. Done well it is the highest-leverage quality practice a team has; done poorly it is a tax that produces both worse code and lower morale. This lecture covers the empirical evidence on what review does and does not catch, the mechanics of the pull request as it has settled across GitHub and GitLab, and the etiquette that distinguishes a productive review from a bruising one.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A pull request (GitHub, 2008; called merge request on GitLab, review on Gerrit, change on Phabricator) is a proposal to integrate a branch into a longer-lived one, opened against a forge with a review interface attached. The author opens the PR; one or more reviewers read the diff, leave comments, suggest changes; CI runs automated checks in parallel; when both reviewers and CI are satisfied, the PR is merged. The artefact that survives is the diff plus the conversation: a record of what changed, why, and what the team thought about it. Every modern git host has converged on roughly this model; the variations are cosmetic.

The empirical record on what code review actually does is clear, and not always what teams expect. The largest study to date is Bacchelli and Bird (2013), "Expectations, outcomes, and challenges of modern code review", an observational study at Microsoft of 6,000 pull requests across multiple product teams. Their findings, broadly: reviewers do not primarily catch bugs (although they catch some); they primarily transfer knowledge, propose alternative designs, and enforce team norms. The same finding repeats in follow-up work at Google (Sadowski et al., 2018) and at Microsoft again (Rigby and Bird, 2013). The implication is the opposite of what many teams act on: a review is not a quality gate; it is a teaching and norming forum.

The mechanics across GitHub, GitLab, and Bitbucket are similar enough that this lecture treats them as one interface with cosmetic differences. The author writes a description, links any related issue, requests reviewers, and pushes; reviewers add line comments, file-level comments, or general comments; the author replies, pushes fix-up commits, and re-requests review; the merge button waits for required reviewers to approve and required CI checks to pass. The variations worth knowing are the merge strategies (merge commit, squash-and-merge, rebase-and-merge), the branch-protection rules (required reviewers, required checks, restrict who can push), and the automation hooks (CODEOWNERS files, auto-assignment, draft PRs).

Feynman test A pull request is a structured conversation about a diff. The diff is the proposal; the conversation is the review; the merge is the decision. The author's job is to make the proposal easy to read; the reviewer's job is to make the conversation useful; the host's job is to record both and enforce the team's gating rules. If you can see those three roles as separable, you can design or critique any team's review process.
What's beyond the syllabus The deep historical fact about code review is that it predates pull requests by 30 years. Michael Fagan's "Design and code inspections to reduce errors in program development" (IBM Systems Journal, 1976) defined the formal "Fagan inspection": four reviewers, a moderator, a recorded session, a defect log. The defect-detection rates Fagan reported (60 to 90% of bugs) are still the benchmark today. Modern pull-request review is a lighter-weight descendant: asynchronous, opportunistic, focused on knowledge transfer rather than defect counts. The full Fagan inspection still appears in safety-critical industries (aerospace, medical devices) where defect rates have a legal floor.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that code review is, in most engineering organisations, the single longest-running coordination practice. The 2024 GitHub Octoverse counted a median of three reviewers per merged PR across active enterprise repositories, and a median PR lifetime of 19 hours; multiplied by the volume of PRs, this is a substantial fraction of every developer's working week. A practice that consumes that much engineering time deserves to be done deliberately. The default ("look at the diff, comment if anything jumps out") produces a wide spread of outcomes from the same nominal activity.

The second reason is that the published evidence on what review accomplishes is unfamiliar to most teams. The intuition that "review catches bugs before they ship" survives because it sounds correct, but the Microsoft and Google studies above show that fewer than 15% of review comments are bug-catching; the remainder are about readability, design, knowledge transfer, and conformance to team patterns. A team that aims its review process at "find the bugs" allocates effort to the smallest output and demoralises both authors and reviewers, because the activity rarely produces its claimed result. A team that aims at "transfer knowledge and enforce norms" gets results aligned with the practice's actual mechanism.

The third reason is that risk modelling and regulatory work depend on review's audit trail as a separate value. A model change that lands without a recorded review is, from an audit perspective, an unsupervised change. The IFRS 9 governance expectations of paragraph BC5.270, the EBA's RTS on the IRB process, and the PRA's SS3/24 on model risk all assume a documented review step. The pull-request workflow gives that audit trail for free: the diff, the reviewers, the comments, the approvals are all timestamped and immutable. A team that runs review well already produces the audit artefact; a team that does not has to construct it after the fact.

Step 3

The mental model

The four purposes of review

Code review serves four purposes simultaneously, in proportions that depend on the team. Naming them lets a reviewer decide which one a given comment is in service of.

PurposeWhat a reviewer is doingWhat kind of comment results
Knowledge transferReading code to learn the system; teaching the author about it"Have you seen the existing helper at utils.py:42?"
Design feedbackAsking whether the approach is the right one"Could this be a Strategy pattern instead of the if-chain?"
Norm enforcementChecking against team conventions for style, naming, testing"Our convention is to test the public method, not the private one."
Defect detectionLooking for bugs the author missed"This off-by-one breaks on an empty list."

Bacchelli and Bird's finding is that the bottom row is the smallest, even though it is the one most people think they are doing. A useful self-discipline as a reviewer is to label internally which row your next comment falls into. If three of your last five comments are norm-enforcement nits, ask whether the team should encode them in a linter (ruff) so they stop being review-time activity.

What "ready for review" actually means

The other side of the conversation is the author. A PR that is "ready for review" satisfies, at minimum, four conditions, which the author owes the reviewer:

One concern per PR. The change addresses one logical change: one feature, one bug fix, one refactor. Bundling unrelated changes into one PR forces reviewers to context-switch and dilutes the review quality. The right answer for "while I was here I also fixed X" is to split X into its own PR.

Reasonable size. The empirical sweet spot for review quality is around 200 lines changed; above 400 lines, review quality degrades sharply (Cohen, 2006, "Best Kept Secrets of Peer Code Review"). A 2,000-line PR receives the same scrutiny as a 50-line PR, because the reviewer's attention is bounded.

Local checks pass. Pre-commit hooks have run (ruff format, ruff check, mypy, nbstripout). CI is expected to be green by the time the reviewer sees the PR. A PR with a red CI badge is communicating "I have not finished" and wastes reviewer time.

Description tells the story. The PR body explains the why, not the what (the diff shows the what). Three short paragraphs: the problem this fixes, the approach taken, what to look for in review. Screenshots if the change is visual. Links to the relevant issue, ADR, or model methodology document if the context is non-obvious.

The pull-request shape

Pull request. A proposal, hosted on a forge with a review interface, to merge one branch into another. The PR groups a diff, a description, a thread of comments, the CI results, and a set of approvals into one reviewable artefact.
CODEOWNERS. A text file at the root of a repository that maps file patterns to GitHub teams or users. PRs touching those files automatically request review from the listed owners; if branch protection is configured to require owner approval, the PR cannot merge without one.
Step 4

The detail

Conventional Commits and the commit-message contract

The single commit that lands on main after a squash-merge is the row that appears in git log for the next ten years. Its message is the team's institutional memory. The Conventional Commits specification (Marco Ferrari et al., 2017, conventionalcommits.org) is the most-adopted format: a structured one-line subject (type(scope): description) followed optionally by a body and a footer.

feat(pd): add point-in-time term-structure adjustment

The previous through-the-cycle PD struggled in a downturn because
the AR(1) decay was calibrated on the post-crisis sample. This commit
introduces a regime-switching adjustment that re-weights the long-run
average by recent macroeconomic conditions.

Refs: MODEL-218, RISK-1742

The type prefixes are conventional: feat for a new feature, fix for a bug fix, refactor for a non-behavioural change, docs for documentation, test for tests, chore for build / dependency updates. The format is machine-readable as well as human-readable; tools like semantic-release can derive the next version number automatically by counting commit types since the last release. For credit-risk work, the convention pays dividends in audit: a regulator who asks "what changed in the LGD model between Q1 and Q2?" gets a clean list when commit messages follow the pattern, and a forensic exercise when they do not.

The CODEOWNERS file

For larger repositories, a CODEOWNERS file at the repository root (or in .github/) automates reviewer assignment based on which files are touched. The format is one rule per line: a glob pattern, then a list of teams or users. When a PR touches a file matching a pattern, the named owners are automatically requested for review. If branch protection is set to require owner approval, the PR cannot merge until they sign off.

# .github/CODEOWNERS

# Default ownership: any change in the repo needs at least one risk-team review
*                                       @bank/risk-modelling

# Anything in the PD codebase needs the PD lead
/src/credit_risk_models/pd/             @bank/pd-leads

# Anything in regulatory documentation needs compliance
/docs/regulation/                       @bank/compliance

# Notebooks: needs the notebook gatekeeper to spot reproducibility issues
*.ipynb                                 @bank/notebook-reviewers

# CI and config: needs the platform team
/.github/                               @bank/platform
/pyproject.toml                         @bank/platform

The pattern that works on a multi-team monorepo is: a permissive default for any-team review, plus tightly-scoped owners for the high-stakes directories. The CODEOWNERS file is the codification of the team's "who looks at what" intuition; like any documentation it drifts, and a quarterly audit of "is this file's owner still the right person" is worth doing.

Branch protection rules

The merge gate is enforced by the forge, not by social agreement. On GitHub, the branch protection settings on a branch (typically main) can require: a minimum number of approving reviews; reviews from designated CODEOWNERS; specific CI checks to be green; that the branch be up to date with the base before merging; that conversations be resolved; that no force pushes are allowed; that only certain users or apps can merge. GitLab and Bitbucket have analogous configurations.

# Conceptual branch protection for main:
required_approvals: 2
require_codeowner_reviews: true
required_status_checks:
  - lint
  - typecheck
  - tests
  - notebooks-execute
strict: true                  # branch must be up to date with main
require_conversation_resolution: true
allow_force_pushes: false
restrict_push: [release-managers]

The set of rules above is appropriate for a regulated codebase. For an internal experimental repo, lighter rules suffice (one approval, lint and tests, no force-push restriction). The discipline is to pick the rules deliberately, write them down (the GitHub UI is the canonical source for now; GitHub's "repository rulesets" feature in 2024 made them YAML-configurable), and audit them quarterly. The principle is the same as for code: the rules should be reviewable and version-controlled.

Reviewer etiquette

The technical mechanics are only half the practice. The other half is how reviewers and authors speak to each other. The accumulated wisdom from twenty years of open-source projects, distilled in the Google "Code Review Developer Guide" (2019), the Microsoft "Engineering Fundamentals Playbook", and Bacchelli & Bird's anti-patterns, is roughly the following.

Comment on the code, not the coder. "This loop runs in O(n²)" not "you wrote a quadratic loop". The fix is not just politeness; it shifts attention to the artefact, which can be changed, away from the person, who cannot.

Distinguish blockers from suggestions. The convention used in this course is the conventional comments tag system (conventionalcomments.org): start each comment with a tag like nit:, suggestion:, question:, blocking:, praise:. The author then knows whether they have to address the comment to merge or whether it is optional. Without the tag system, every comment looks equally weighty, and authors either over-respond (and lose time) or under-respond (and miss real issues).

Ask questions, do not assume malice. "Why this approach?" is better than "this approach is wrong". Often the author had a reason the reviewer was not aware of; the question surfaces it without forcing a defence. The phenomenon Hanlon's razor warns against ("never attribute to malice that which is adequately explained by stupidity") applies even more strongly to "never attribute to error that which is adequately explained by context you don't have".

Praise as a deliberate practice. Comments are almost never used to flag good work, because there is no obvious moment that prompts them. The deliberate practice is to add at least one praise: comment on every PR you review where it is honest. The reciprocal effect on author morale is large, and it costs the reviewer nothing.

Be specific. "This could be clearer" tells the author nothing. "This could be clearer; consider extracting the inner expression into a variable named excess_capital" tells them what to do.

Common pitfall The most damaging pattern in review is the silent rejection: a reviewer who keeps requesting small changes without explaining the underlying objection. After three rounds of "could you also fix this nit?", the author begins to suspect a hidden disagreement that has not been named. The remedy is for reviewers to surface the underlying issue early: "Stepping back, I think this whole approach has a problem; let's discuss before fixing the small things". Saying it explicitly is uncomfortable in the moment and much cheaper than the alternative.

Draft PRs and the early-feedback workflow

GitHub's draft pull requests (2019) and GitLab's work-in-progress (WIP) marker serve the same purpose: open the PR before the work is done, get early feedback on the approach, mark it ready when CI is green and the design has settled. The pattern is particularly useful for non-trivial changes (a new model, an architectural refactor, a notebook with model-validation implications), where finding out at line-1500 that the approach is wrong is much more expensive than finding out at line-50.

The convention this course recommends: any PR larger than 500 lines, or any PR that introduces a new module, should open as a draft after the first commit, with a clear "what I'm doing and why" description, and only become "ready" after a reviewer has nodded at the approach.

Merge strategies

Once the PR is approved and CI is green, the merge button is enabled. Three strategies are available on GitHub:

Create a merge commit produces a true merge in the DAG sense: a commit with two parents, preserving the feature branch's commit history. Useful when the feature branch's commits are themselves meaningful (a tutorial series, a multi-step migration where each step is a separate fact). Most teams find the result noisy.

Squash and merge collapses all commits on the feature branch into a single commit on main, with a message editable by the merger. This is the dominant pattern on most modern teams: one PR becomes one commit on main, which is the granularity at which git log and git bisect become useful.

Rebase and merge replays each individual commit on the feature branch onto the tip of main, preserving them as separate commits but in a linear history. The middle option; cleaner than merge commits, more detailed than squash. Used by teams that put effort into making each individual commit on the feature branch coherent.

The recommendation, in absence of strong reason otherwise, is squash-and-merge. It pairs well with Conventional Commits, gives a clean trunk history, and removes the cognitive load of "should I clean up these intermediate commits before the PR lands?".

Worked example: reviewing a credit-risk model change

Worked example: reviewing PR #482, "Add macroeconomic overlay to LGD"

A colleague opens a 380-line PR adding a macroeconomic overlay to the LGD model. The PR description is two paragraphs. CI is green. You have been requested as one of three reviewers. The walkthrough below is the actual mechanics of producing a useful review in 30 minutes.

Step 1 (read the description, then the issue link). Spend two minutes understanding what the PR is trying to do. The description references issue MODEL-318, which proposes the overlay; open it, read the requirements. Decide what success looks like.

Step 2 (sweep the file changes). Look at the "Files changed" tab. Skim every file just to know what was touched: src/lgd/overlay.py (new), src/lgd/calibrate.py (modified), tests/test_overlay.py (new), docs/methodology/lgd.md (updated). Note that the methodology doc was updated; that is a positive signal.

Step 3 (read the test file first). The tests tell you what the author thinks the new code is supposed to do. Read them as a specification. If you cannot understand the tests, that is your first comment.

Step 4 (read the main change). Now read src/lgd/overlay.py top to bottom. Tag your thoughts as you go: question: for things you do not understand, nit: for style issues, suggestion: for design alternatives, blocking: for things that must change before merge. You find: two nits (variable naming), one suggestion (the if-elif chain could be a dict lookup), one question ("how is this calibrated against historical recoveries?"), one blocker (the macroeconomic series is loaded with no caching, so a backtest would re-download it thousands of times).

Step 5 (read the integration). Read the changes in src/lgd/calibrate.py. Note that the overlay is hard-coded as enabled; suggest a feature flag so the team can toggle it during validation. Add the comment as a suggestion:.

Step 6 (one praise). The test for the boundary case (recession indicator at exactly the threshold) is a careful piece of work. Leave a praise: comment naming it.

Step 7 (overall comment, decision). Write a short overall comment summarising: "One blocker (caching), one suggestion (feature flag), one question, two nits, one bit of praise. The methodology doc update is appreciated. I'm requesting changes; happy to re-review once the blocker is addressed." Click "Request changes".

The author now has a clear, ranked list of what needs to happen. The conversation is structured around the conventional-comments tags; no one has to wonder which comments are blocking. The whole review was 30 minutes; the resulting PR is better and the author is told what they got right as well as what they got wrong.

Step 5

Synthesis and what's next

Code review is the social practice that runs on top of git branching. The mechanics (open a PR, request reviewers, comment on the diff, merge when approved) are standardised across every modern forge; the variations are configuration. The empirical record (Bacchelli & Bird 2013, Sadowski 2018) is that review's value comes mostly from knowledge transfer, design feedback, and norm enforcement, not from defect detection; teams that aim at the right purpose get better results from the same effort. The two halves of the practice are the author's responsibility ("one concern, reasonable size, CI green, description tells the story") and the reviewer's responsibility ("specific, tagged, comment-on-the-code, ask-don't-assume"). The branch-protection rules, CODEOWNERS file, and merge-strategy choice are the configuration that supports the social practice.

The deeper point is that review is a learning loop. Every PR is a chance for the author to learn the codebase from someone else's perspective and for the reviewer to learn the change from the author's. A team that runs review as a quality gate misses the learning; a team that runs it as a teaching forum gets the learning and the quality, because reviewers who understand the system catch real issues. The next lecture turns to the automation layer that runs alongside review: continuous integration and delivery, the build system that triggers on every PR and produces the green or red check that branch protection consults.
