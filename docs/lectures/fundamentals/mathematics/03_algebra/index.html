Algebra

Six lectures on structure and symmetry: linear algebra in R^n, then linear algebra without coordinates, then the group, ring, and field theory that explains what the linear case was a special case of. The first three lectures follow the UP modules (WTW 124, 211, 221, 381). The last three go beyond the degree into Galois theory, number theory, and category theory, which is where the pattern of the whole course finally becomes explicit.

What this course is

Algebra begins with a computation and ends with a classification. The computation is solving a linear system, and the classification is the statement that two structures which look different are the same structure wearing different notation. Everything in between is a stripping away of assumptions: matrices become linear maps once you stop naming coordinates, linear maps over a field become module homomorphisms once you stop assuming inverses exist, and the whole apparatus becomes a diagram of arrows once you stop looking inside the objects at all.

Linear algebra deserves separate mention because it is the single most reused course in this repository. Regression is a projection, principal components are eigenvectors, covariance matrices are quadratic forms, and the numerical stability of every fitted model is a statement about conditioning. Read the first two lectures even if the rest of the course is not for you.

Prerequisites

One block, plus one soft dependency.

  • Foundations, lectures 1 and 3: proof technique, functions, and above all equivalence relations. Cosets, quotient groups, and quotient rings are all one construction applied three times, and that construction is an equivalence relation.
  • Foundations, lecture 4 is a soft dependency for lecture 2, where "every vector space has a basis" in infinite dimension needs Zorn's lemma. The finite-dimensional case, which is all most readers need, does not.

How to read this

Lectures 1 to 3 are the spine and are strictly sequential. Lecture 4 depends on lecture 3 and on nothing else. Lecture 5 is the most self-contained in the track and can be read at any time. Lecture 6 is best read last, because its content is a commentary on constructions the earlier lectures have already performed, and read too early it is an unmotivated list of definitions. Status badges show what is built (built) or planned; the module code in brackets names the UP module the lecture is drawn from.

Course sequence

Part 1 · Linear algebra (lectures 1 to 2)

The concrete case, then the same results with the coordinates removed.

  • builtLecture 1: linear algebra I. Vectors and geometry in R^n, lines and planes. Matrix algebra, systems of linear equations, Gaussian elimination, determinants. Linear combinations, spans, independence, subspaces, basis and dimension. Eigenvalues, eigenvectors, similarity, and diagonalisation. (WTW 124, WTW 211)
  • builtLecture 2: linear algebra II. Abstract vector spaces over a field. Linear transformations, their matrix representations, and change of basis. Inner product spaces, orthogonality, and Gram-Schmidt. The spectral theorem for symmetric matrices. Quadratic forms and positive definiteness. (WTW 221)

Part 2 · Abstract structures (lectures 3 to 4)

Groups, rings, and fields, and then the theorem that made them necessary.

  • builtLecture 3: groups, rings and fields. Groups, subgroups, cyclic and permutation groups. Homomorphisms, cosets, Lagrange's theorem, normal subgroups and factor groups. Rings, ideals, factor rings. Polynomial rings, irreducibility, and factorisation. Integral domains, fields, and field extensions. (WTW 381)
  • builtLecture 4: Galois theory. Splitting fields, normal and separable extensions. The Galois correspondence between subfields and subgroups. Solvable groups, and the insolubility of the general quintic by radicals. The classical straight-edge and compass constructions settled for good. (extension, developing WTW 381)

Part 3 · Beyond the degree (lectures 5 to 6)

The oldest branch of the subject, and the newest.

  • builtLecture 5: number theory. Divisibility and the Euclidean algorithm. Primes and unique factorisation. Congruences and modular arithmetic, the Chinese remainder theorem. Fermat's little theorem, Euler's theorem, primitive roots. Quadratic reciprocity. An introduction to algebraic number fields, and the RSA construction as an application. (extension)
  • builtLecture 6: category theory. Categories, functors, natural transformations. Universal properties, limits and colimits, products and coproducts. Adjunctions. The Yoneda lemma. Monads. Why the quotient constructions in lectures 2 and 3, the completion in analysis, and the free constructions everywhere are all instances of one thing. (extension)

Where this course is used later

Lectures 1 and 2 are load-bearing for almost everything downstream. Least squares is orthogonal projection onto a column space, principal components are the eigenvectors of a covariance matrix, the singular value decomposition underlies both, and multicollinearity is a rank statement. In statistics the covariance matrix must be positive semi-definite, which is lecture 2, and in machine learning every embedding, factorisation, and attention mechanism is a linear map before it is anything else. Within this track, lecture 2 supplies the algebraic half of functional analysis in analysis, and lecture 3 supplies the groups that algebraic topology attaches to spaces. Lecture 5 stands apart from the modelling work and connects instead to cryptography, which is why the RSA construction closes it.
