Linear algebra I

This is the most reused lecture in the entire track. Regression is a projection onto a column space, principal components are eigenvectors of a covariance matrix, a Markov chain's long-run behaviour is an eigenvector of its transition matrix, and every gradient step in a fitted model is a matrix acting on a vector. The material looks like arithmetic with grids of numbers and is not: a matrix is a linear map written in coordinates, and almost every confusion in the subject comes from forgetting that the coordinates were a choice.

Lecture 1 of 6 Reading time, 65 minutes Source: UP WTW 124 and WTW 211, extended
Step 1

The 30-second version

A matrix multiplies vectors, and the operation is linear: $A(x + y) = Ax + Ay$ and $A(\lambda x) = \lambda Ax$. Those two rules are the whole subject. Solving $Ax = b$ asks which vectors the map can produce and how many inputs give the same output, and the answers are the column space and the null space. Gaussian elimination computes both, and the rank-nullity theorem says their dimensions always add to the number of columns.

The determinant measures how the map scales volume, so it is zero exactly when the map collapses space into a lower dimension, which is exactly when the matrix is not invertible. Eigenvectors are the directions the map does not rotate, merely stretches: $Av = \lambda v$. When enough of them exist, the matrix is diagonalisable, meaning that in the right coordinates it is just a list of scaling factors, and powers, exponentials, and long-run behaviour all become trivial to compute.

Feynman test Take a sheet of rubber with a circle drawn on it, and stretch it uniformly in one direction while squashing it in another. The circle becomes an ellipse. Almost every arrow drawn from the centre changes both length and direction. But two special arrows, along the ellipse's axes, change only in length: they still point where they pointed. Those are the eigenvectors, and how much they stretch are the eigenvalues. If you know them, you know everything the stretch does, because any other arrow is a combination of those two and gets scaled componentwise. That is diagonalisation, and it is why finding eigenvectors is worth the effort of solving a polynomial.
What's beyond a first course A first course teaches elimination, determinants by cofactor expansion, and eigenvalues by characteristic polynomial. This lecture adds three things a modelling audience needs. The four fundamental subspaces are presented together, because the orthogonality relations between them are what regression diagnostics rely on. The determinant is introduced as a volume scaling rather than as a formula, since the formula explains nothing and the geometry explains invertibility, change of variables, and singularity at once. And the numerical section is not optional: computing a determinant to test invertibility, or inverting a matrix to solve a system, are both wrong in practice, and the reasons are quantitative.
Step 2

Why this matters

Ordinary least squares is $\hat\beta = (X'X)^{-1}X'y$, and every part of that expression is from this lecture. Whether $X'X$ is invertible is a rank question about X; when it fails, the cause is exact collinearity among predictors, meaning the columns are linearly dependent and the null space is non-trivial. When it nearly fails, the matrix is ill conditioned and the coefficients become unstable, which is the formal content of multicollinearity: the same fitted values are produced by wildly different coefficient vectors, and small data changes move them a lot. Variance inflation factors are a diagnostic for exactly this.

Eigenvectors carry the rest. Principal component analysis diagonalises a covariance matrix, so the components are eigenvectors and the variances explained are eigenvalues. A Markov chain's stationary distribution is the eigenvector of its transition matrix for eigenvalue one, and the speed of convergence to it is governed by the second-largest eigenvalue, which is what determines how quickly a rating-migration model forgets its starting state. Factor models decompose a correlation matrix into a small number of leading eigenvectors plus idiosyncratic noise, which is how a large credit portfolio is reduced to a handful of systematic drivers.

Beyond specific formulas, this lecture supplies the language. When the analysis course says a Hessian is positive definite, when machine learning says an embedding has rank k, or when a covariance matrix must be positive semi-definite before a simulation will run, the statement being made is one from here.

Step 4

The detail

Vectors, matrices, and what multiplication means

A vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a list of numbers, added componentwise and scaled by reals. The dot product $x \cdot y = \sum x_i y_i$ gives length $\|x\| = \sqrt{x \cdot x}$ and angle through $x \cdot y = \|x\|\|y\|\cos\theta$, so orthogonality is $x \cdot y = 0$. In statistics, centring two data vectors and taking their dot product gives the covariance, and the cosine of the angle between them is the correlation, so the geometric and statistical vocabularies coincide exactly.

The product $Ax$ is best read as a linear combination of the columns of A, with the entries of x as the coefficients. This reading makes the column space obvious, since the set of all $Ax$ is precisely the span of the columns, and it makes matrix multiplication associative for a reason rather than by computation: $AB$ is the map "do B, then do A", and composition of functions is associative. Matrix multiplication is not commutative because composing a rotation with a stretch depends on the order, which the rubber-sheet picture makes obvious.

Solving systems by elimination

Gaussian elimination, fully worked, with the three outcomes. Solve: 2x + y - z = 8 -3x - y + 2z = -11 -2x + y + 2z = -3 Augmented matrix and row reduction: [ 2 1 -1 | 8 ] [ -3 -1 2 | -11 ] [ -2 1 2 | -3 ] R2 <- R2 + (3/2) R1; R3 <- R3 + R1: [ 2 1 -1 | 8 ] [ 0 1/2 1/2 | 1 ] [ 0 2 1 | 5 ] R3 <- R3 - 4 R2: [ 2 1 -1 | 8 ] [ 0 1/2 1/2 | 1 ] [ 0 0 -1 | 1 ] Back substitution: -z = 1 => z = -1 (1/2)y + (1/2)(-1) = 1 => y = 3 2x + 3 - (-1) = 8 => x = 2 Solution (2, 3, -1). Check in the first equation: 4 + 3 + 1 = 8. Correct. THE THREE OUTCOMES, read off the reduced form: - A pivot in every column and no contradictory row: unique solution. - A row of zeros with a non-zero right side (0 = c): NO solution; b lies outside the column space. - A free column (no pivot) and no contradiction: INFINITELY many solutions, one for each vector in the null space added to any particular solution. That last case is the general structure of a linear system: the solution set is a particular solution plus the entire null space. In regression, a free column means two predictors carry the same information and no unique coefficient vector exists.

The rank is the number of pivots, and it equals the dimension of the column space and of the row space, which is not obvious and is one of the first real theorems of the subject. Rank-nullity then states that rank plus nullity equals the number of columns, which the first figure summarises: every input direction is either mapped injectively or annihilated.

Determinants as volume

Determinant
$\det A$ is the signed factor by which the map scales volume: the unit cube maps to a parallelepiped of volume $|\det A|$, and the sign records whether orientation is reversed. Consequences follow without computation. $\det A = 0$ means volume is crushed to zero, so the map is not injective and A is not invertible. $\det(AB) = \det A \det B$ because scalings compose. $\det(A^{-1}) = 1/\det A$. And the change-of-variables Jacobian in multivariable calculus is this same factor computed locally.

For a $2\times2$ matrix the determinant is $ad - bc$, which is the area of the parallelogram spanned by the columns. Cofactor expansion generalises this and is fine for small matrices and useless for large ones, costing on the order of $n!$ operations; in practice determinants are computed from an LU factorisation as the product of pivots, at cost $n^3$.

Span, independence, basis, dimension

A set of vectors is linearly independent when no non-trivial combination gives zero, equivalently when none is a combination of the others. Their span is the set of all combinations. A basis is an independent spanning set, and every basis of a given space has the same number of elements, which is the dimension. Coordinates are the coefficients relative to a chosen basis, and this is where the lecture's opening claim bites: a vector is a geometric object, its coordinate list is a description relative to a basis, and a matrix is the description of a linear map relative to bases on each side. Changing basis changes every number in the matrix while changing nothing about the map, which is what lecture 2 makes precise.

Eigenvalues and diagonalisation

$Av = \lambda v$ with $v \ne 0$ makes $\lambda$ an eigenvalue and v an eigenvector. Rearranged, $(A - \lambda I)v = 0$ requires the matrix $A - \lambda I$ to be singular, so $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$: the characteristic polynomial, whose roots are the eigenvalues. If an $n \times n$ matrix has n independent eigenvectors, gathering them as columns of P gives $A = PDP^{-1}$ with D diagonal, and then $A^k = PD^kP^{-1}$, which reduces powers to scalar powers.

Diagonalisation applied: the long-run behaviour of a rating migration. A two-state transition matrix, rows summing to one (states: performing, delinquent): P = [ 0.9 0.1 ] [ 0.4 0.6 ] Eigenvalues from det(P - lambda I) = 0: (0.9 - L)(0.6 - L) - (0.1)(0.4) = 0 L^2 - 1.5 L + 0.54 - 0.04 = 0 L^2 - 1.5 L + 0.5 = 0 (L - 1)(L - 0.5) = 0 so L1 = 1, L2 = 0.5. Eigenvalue 1 always appears for a stochastic matrix, because the rows sum to one, so the all-ones vector is a right eigenvector. LEFT eigenvector for L = 1 (this is the stationary distribution): pi P = pi, with pi = (p, 1-p): 0.9 p + 0.4 (1 - p) = p 0.4 + 0.5 p = p p = 0.8 pi = (0.8, 0.2). Interpretation: in the long run 80% of accounts are performing, whatever the starting mix. SPEED OF CONVERGENCE. Write any starting distribution as pi plus a multiple of the second eigenvector. After k steps the second component is multiplied by 0.5^k, so the deviation from the stationary distribution halves every step: start (1, 0), all performing: after 1 step: (0.900, 0.100) deviation from 0.8 is 0.100 after 2 steps: (0.850, 0.150) deviation 0.050 after 3 steps: (0.825, 0.175) deviation 0.025 after 4 steps: (0.8125, 0.1875) deviation 0.0125 after 8 steps: (0.80078, ...) deviation 0.00078 Each deviation is exactly 0.5 times the previous: the second eigenvalue IS the memory of the chain. A model with second eigenvalue 0.95 would take roughly fourteen times as long to forget its starting point (log 0.5 / log 0.95 = 13.5), which is the quantitative content of "this rating model is sticky".

Not every matrix is diagonalisable. A matrix with a repeated eigenvalue may lack a full set of independent eigenvectors, the standard example being $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, which has eigenvalue 1 twice and only a one-dimensional eigenspace. Such matrices are handled by the Jordan form, which lecture 2 discusses. Symmetric matrices, by contrast, are always diagonalisable with orthogonal eigenvectors and real eigenvalues, which is the spectral theorem and the reason covariance matrices behave so well.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Never invert a matrix, and never test singularity with a determinant

Both instructions are standard numerical practice and both contradict what a first course implies. To solve $Ax = b$, factorise and substitute rather than forming $A^{-1}$: the factorisation costs about a third as many operations, uses less memory, and is more accurate, because computing an inverse and then multiplying accumulates rounding twice. The formula $\hat\beta = (X'X)^{-1}X'y$ is a statement about the answer, not an instruction; every serious implementation solves the normal equations by Cholesky factorisation or, better, avoids forming $X'X$ at all and uses a QR factorisation of X, since forming $X'X$ squares the condition number.

Determinants are equally misleading as a singularity test, because they scale badly. Multiplying a $20\times20$ matrix by $1/2$ divides its determinant by more than a million while changing its invertibility not at all, so a small determinant means nothing on its own. The right diagnostic is the condition number, the ratio of largest to smallest singular value, which measures how much a relative error in the data is amplified in the answer. A condition number of $10^k$ means losing roughly k digits of accuracy, and it is scale invariant. Multicollinearity in a regression is a large condition number, not a small determinant, and this is the same quantity that governed convergence of gradient descent in multivariable calculus: the Hessian's condition number there and the design matrix's here are the same idea applied to different matrices.

The matrix is not the point

A linear map exists before any coordinates are chosen, and the matrix is what appears once a basis is fixed on each side. Two matrices representing the same map in different bases are similar, related by $B = P^{-1}AP$, and everything genuinely about the map is invariant under that change: rank, determinant, trace, eigenvalues, characteristic polynomial. Everything else is an artefact of the coordinates. This is why diagonalisation is worth pursuing, since it amounts to finding the coordinates in which the map is as simple as possible, and it is why eigenvalues rather than matrix entries appear in every theorem. Lecture 2 takes the coordinates away entirely and works with abstract vector spaces, at which point this observation becomes the definition rather than a remark.

Watch out Three habits worth forming now. Read $Ax$ as a combination of columns rather than as a grid of dot products, because every structural fact about solvability follows from that reading. Distinguish "singular" from "nearly singular": the first is a yes-or-no algebraic fact, the second is a quantitative numerical one measured by the condition number, and only the second matters for data. And check whether a matrix is symmetric before assuming real eigenvalues or orthogonal eigenvectors, since a general matrix may have complex eigenvalues even with entirely real entries, as any rotation does.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Solve the system $x + 2y + z = 4$, $2x + 5y - z = 3$, $3x + 7y = 7$ by elimination, and state which of the three outcomes occurs and why.
  2. Explain why the solution set of $Ax = b$, when non-empty, is a particular solution plus the null space, and say what this means for a regression with two identical predictors.
  3. State the rank-nullity theorem and verify it for a $3 \times 4$ matrix of rank 2, identifying all four fundamental subspaces and their dimensions.
  4. Explain why $\det A = 0$ is equivalent to non-invertibility using the volume interpretation, without computing any determinant.
  5. Compute the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 3\end{pmatrix}$ and confirm the eigenvectors are orthogonal. Say which property of the matrix guaranteed that.
  6. For the transition matrix in the worked example, verify that $(0.8, 0.2)$ is stationary, and compute how many steps are needed for the deviation from stationarity to fall below one percentage point.
  7. A rating model has second-largest eigenvalue 0.95. How many steps does it take to halve a deviation from the stationary distribution, and what does that say about the model's memory?
  8. Show that $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is not diagonalisable by computing its eigenspace, and state what makes symmetric matrices different.
  9. Explain why forming $X'X$ before solving squares the condition number, and name the factorisation that avoids it.
  10. A colleague reports that a determinant of $10^{-14}$ proves a design matrix is singular. Give the two reasons this inference is wrong and state the diagnostic they should use instead.
