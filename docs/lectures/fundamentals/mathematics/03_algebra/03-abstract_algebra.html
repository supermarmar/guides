Groups, rings and fields

Linear algebra assumed the scalars were real numbers, so every non-zero one could be divided by. Drop that and the subject splits into three. A group has one operation and every element invertible, and it is the mathematics of symmetry. A ring has two operations with division not guaranteed, and the integers and the polynomials are the models. A field has two operations with division available, so linear algebra works over it. The same three moves recur at each level: find the substructures, find the structure-preserving maps, and quotient by the right kind of substructure.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A group is a set with one associative operation, an identity, and an inverse for every element. The symmetries of any object form a group under composition, and that is what groups are for. A ring adds a second operation, distributing over the first, with multiplicative inverses not required: the integers, the polynomials, and the $n\times n$ matrices are rings. A field is a ring where every non-zero element is invertible, so the rationals, the reals, the complexes, and the integers modulo a prime are fields.

Three structural results carry the lecture. Lagrange's theorem: the size of a subgroup divides the size of the group, proved by observing that the cosets partition it. The first isomorphism theorem: any homomorphism factors as a quotient followed by an injection, so the image of a map is the source modulo what the map forgets. And the quotient construction itself, which needs a normal subgroup for groups and an ideal for rings, both being exactly the substructures for which the operation descends to equivalence classes.

Feynman test Pick up a square and put it back down so it occupies the same space. You can rotate it by a quarter, a half, or three quarters of a turn, leave it alone, or flip it about any of four axes: eight moves in total, and no others. Do two of them in succession and the result is one of the eight. Every move can be undone. Doing nothing is one of the moves. Those three observations are the group axioms, and you verified them physically rather than algebraically. The eight symmetries of the square are a group, and every group is the symmetry group of something, which is Cayley's theorem. Group theory is the study of what "sameness under transformation" can look like.
What's beyond a first course A first course defines the structures and proves the isomorphism theorems. This lecture adds two things. Lagrange's theorem is used to prove Fermat's little theorem in three lines, which shows abstract structure delivering a concrete arithmetic fact and sets up both the number theory and Galois lectures. And a finite field with four elements is constructed explicitly, with its full addition and multiplication tables, because finite fields are where the abstraction stops feeling abstract and because they are what error-correcting codes and cryptographic primitives are built from.
Step 2

Why this matters

The honest position is the one the course landing page takes: this lecture's payoff is structural rather than operational for a risk modeller, with three concrete exceptions. Modular arithmetic is a ring, and the invertibility of elements modulo n is exactly what makes RSA work, which the number theory lecture develops. Finite fields are the arithmetic of error-correcting codes and of most cryptographic primitives, so anyone working near data integrity or security is using them. And symmetry as a group is the formal content of equivariance in machine learning: a convolutional layer is designed so that translating the input translates the output, which is a statement that the layer commutes with the action of the translation group, and the same framing produces rotation-equivariant and permutation-equivariant architectures.

The larger return is the quotient construction, met three times already and now met in general. Foundations lecture 3 proved that equivalence relations partition a set; modular arithmetic quotients the integers; and this lecture quotients groups by normal subgroups and rings by ideals. In each case the same question arises, whether an operation defined on representatives is well defined on classes, and normality and the ideal property are precisely the conditions that make the answer yes. Once that pattern is visible, the quotient topology in geometry and the $L^p$ spaces as classes of functions in measure theory are recognisably the same construction rather than four separate definitions to learn.

The detail

Groups

Group
A set G with an operation satisfying closure, associativity, an identity element e, and an inverse for each element. It is abelian when the operation commutes. The order of the group is its size; the order of an element is the least positive n with $g^n = e$.

A subgroup is a subset that is itself a group under the same operation, and the test is closure under the operation and inverses. A coset of a subgroup H is $gH = \{gh : h \in H\}$, and cosets are the equivalence classes of the relation "$a^{-1}b \in H$", so by the partition theorem from foundations lecture 3 they partition G. Each coset has the same size as H, since $h \mapsto gh$ is a bijection. That is the whole proof of Lagrange.

LAGRANGE'S THEOREM. For a finite group G with subgroup H, the order of H divides the order of G. Proof. The cosets of H partition G (equivalence classes), and each has exactly |H| elements (left multiplication by g is a bijection from H to gH). So |G| = (number of cosets) times |H|. QED Immediate corollaries: - The order of any element divides |G|, since the powers of g form a subgroup of that order. - g^|G| = e for every g. - A group of prime order is cyclic and has no proper subgroups. FERMAT'S LITTLE THEOREM, in three lines. Let p be prime and a not divisible by p. The non-zero residues mod p form a group under multiplication, of order p - 1. By the corollary above, a^(p-1) = 1 (mod p). That is Fermat's little theorem, proved with no number theory at all: only the fact that a subgroup's order divides the group's. Check with p = 7, a = 3: 3^6 = 729 = 104 * 7 + 1, so 729 = 1 mod 7. Correct. Euler's generalisation follows the same way with the group of units mod n, whose order is Euler's totient phi(n), giving a^phi(n) = 1 mod n whenever a and n are coprime. That statement is the mathematical engine of RSA, and it has just been derived from Lagrange.

A homomorphism satisfies $\varphi(ab) = \varphi(a)\varphi(b)$; its kernel is the preimage of the identity and its image is a subgroup of the target. A subgroup is normal when $gNg^{-1} = N$ for every g, and this is exactly the condition under which the coset multiplication $(aN)(bN) = abN$ is well defined, giving the quotient group $G/N$. Kernels are always normal, and every normal subgroup is the kernel of the quotient map, so the two notions coincide. The first isomorphism theorem then states $G/\ker\varphi \cong \operatorname{im}\varphi$, which is the second figure.

Rings

Ring
A set with two operations: an abelian group under addition, associative under multiplication, with multiplication distributing over addition. A commutative ring has commutative multiplication; an integral domain is a commutative ring with identity and no zero divisors ($ab = 0$ forces $a = 0$ or $b = 0$); a field is a commutative ring with identity in which every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse.

The integers form an integral domain that is not a field, since only $\pm 1$ have inverses. Polynomials over a field form an integral domain. The $n\times n$ matrices form a non-commutative ring with zero divisors, since two non-zero singular matrices can multiply to zero. And $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is a field exactly when n is prime: if $n = ab$ with both factors exceeding 1, then a and b are non-zero with product zero, so there are zero divisors and no inverses for them.

The ring analogue of a normal subgroup is an ideal, a subgroup under addition that absorbs multiplication from the ring: $rI \subseteq I$ for every r. Absorption is exactly what makes multiplication descend to cosets, so $R/I$ is a ring, and the first isomorphism theorem holds verbatim with ideals in place of normal subgroups. In $\mathbb{Z}$ every ideal is the multiples of a single integer, so $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is a quotient ring and modular arithmetic is an instance of the general construction rather than a special trick.

Polynomials, irreducibility, and building fields

For a field F, the polynomial ring $F[x]$ has division with remainder, which makes it a principal ideal domain: every ideal is generated by one polynomial, the greatest common divisor of its elements. A polynomial is irreducible when it does not factor into lower-degree polynomials over F, which is the polynomial analogue of primality. The key construction follows: $F[x]/(p(x))$ is a field exactly when p is irreducible, and it contains a root of p by construction, namely the class of x. This is how $\mathbb{C}$ is built from $\mathbb{R}$, as $\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2+1)$, and it is how all finite fields are built.

Constructing the field with four elements. There is no field of order 4 among the integers mod n: Z/4Z has zero divisors (2 x 2 = 0), so it is not a field. Build one properly. Take F2 = {0,1}, the field with two elements (arithmetic mod 2), and the polynomial p(x) = x^2 + x + 1. It has no root in F2: p(0) = 1, p(1) = 1 + 1 + 1 = 1 (mod 2). Being quadratic with no root, it is irreducible over F2. Form F4 = F2[x] / (x^2 + x + 1). Elements are the remainders on division by p, so all polynomials of degree below 2: {0, 1, a, a+1} where a denotes the class of x. Four elements, as required. The defining relation is a^2 = a + 1, because a^2 + a + 1 = 0 and -1 = +1 in characteristic 2. ADDITION (componentwise mod 2): + | 0 1 a a+1 -----+-------------------------- 0 | 0 1 a a+1 1 | 1 0 a+1 a a | a a+1 0 1 a+1 | a+1 a 1 0 MULTIPLICATION (reduce using a^2 = a + 1): x | 0 1 a a+1 -----+-------------------------- 0 | 0 0 0 0 1 | 0 1 a a+1 a | 0 a a+1 1 a+1 | 0 a+1 1 a Check one entry: a * (a+1) = a^2 + a = (a + 1) + a = 2a + 1 = 1 (mod 2). So a and a+1 are inverses. Every non-zero element has an inverse, so F4 is a field. Note also that the non-zero elements {1, a, a+1} form a cyclic group of order 3 under multiplication: a^1 = a, a^2 = a+1, a^3 = 1. The multiplicative group of any finite field is cyclic, which is the fact that makes discrete-logarithm cryptography possible. GENERAL RESULT. A finite field exists for every prime power p^k and for no other order, and it is unique up to isomorphism. Reed-Solomon codes, used in storage and transmission, do their arithmetic in fields of order 256, which is 2^8, built exactly this way from an irreducible degree-8 polynomial over F2.

Field extensions generalise the construction. If K contains F, then K is a vector space over F, and its dimension is the degree $[K:F]$; degrees multiply in towers, $[M:F] = [M:K][K:F]$. That single multiplicative fact settles the classical construction problems: doubling the cube requires a length of degree 3 over the rationals, while straight-edge and compass constructions only produce degrees that are powers of 2, and 3 does not divide any power of 2. The Galois lecture develops this into a complete correspondence between field extensions and groups.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

One pattern, applied at every level

Every algebraic theory follows the same four steps, and recognising them turns a long syllabus into one idea repeated. Define the objects by axioms. Define the substructures closed under the operations: subgroups, subrings, subspaces. Define the structure-preserving maps: homomorphisms, ring homomorphisms, linear maps. Then identify which substructures permit a quotient, which is where the theories differ in detail and agree in shape: normal subgroups for groups, ideals for rings, all subspaces for vector spaces (because addition is commutative, so every subspace is automatically normal). The isomorphism theorems then hold in every case with identical proofs. This is why the category theory lecture exists at the end of the course: once the same theorem has been proved four times with only the nouns changed, the right response is to prove it once about arrows.

Symmetry is a group, and this has become practical

Noether's theorem in physics says every continuous symmetry of a system corresponds to a conserved quantity, which is the most consequential statement in the subject. The machine learning version is equivariance: a model should transform predictably when its input is transformed. A convolutional layer is equivariant under translation, meaning shifting the image shifts the feature map, and this is a design constraint expressed as commuting with a group action. Graph neural networks are permutation equivariant, since relabelling the nodes should relabel the output and change nothing else. Rotation-equivariant networks for molecular and physical data use representations of the rotation group directly. In each case the group is chosen first, from the symmetries the data genuinely has, and the architecture is derived to respect it. Baking in a symmetry that the data has is one of the few reliable ways to improve sample efficiency, and it is group theory doing the work.

Classification is what algebra aims at

The ambition of a structural theory is to list all the objects up to isomorphism. Finite abelian groups are completely classified as direct products of cyclic groups of prime power order, a clean and usable theorem. Finite simple groups, the building blocks from which all finite groups are assembled, were classified over roughly fifty years and thousands of pages, finishing in the 1980s, and the answer includes eighteen infinite families plus twenty-six sporadic groups that fit no pattern, the largest of which has about $8 \times 10^{53}$ elements. Finite fields are classified completely and simply: one for each prime power. That the difficulty varies so wildly across three classification problems in the same subject is worth noticing, since it is a reminder that structural mathematics does not get uniformly easier as the axioms get stronger.

Watch out Two errors. Assuming commutativity: groups of transformations rarely commute, and in $D_4$ or in matrix groups the order of operations changes the answer, so writing $ab = ba$ in a proof is an assumption that must be justified. And assuming a quotient exists: an arbitrary subgroup does not give a quotient group, and an arbitrary subring does not give a quotient ring. Normality and the absorption property are precisely what make the operation well defined on classes, and checking well-definedness is the standing obligation whenever a quotient appears anywhere in this track.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Verify the group axioms for the symmetries of an equilateral triangle, list all six elements, and find every subgroup. Confirm Lagrange's theorem holds for each.
  2. Prove Lagrange's theorem, stating where the partition theorem from foundations is used and why every coset has the same size.
  3. Derive Fermat's little theorem from Lagrange, and verify it numerically for $p = 11$ and $a = 7$.
  4. Show that the kernel of a group homomorphism is a normal subgroup, and that every normal subgroup is the kernel of some homomorphism.
  5. Explain why coset multiplication $(aN)(bN) = abN$ is well defined only when N is normal, giving a concrete failure for a non-normal subgroup of $S_3$.
  6. State the first isomorphism theorem and apply it to the map $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, identifying the kernel, the image, and the isomorphism.
  7. Show that $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ has zero divisors while $\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z}$ is a field, and state the general criterion.
  8. Verify that $x^2 + x + 1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{F}_2$, then construct $\mathbb{F}_4$ and reproduce both operation tables.
  9. Show that the multiplicative group of $\mathbb{F}_4$ is cyclic by exhibiting a generator, and say why this matters for cryptography.
  10. Use the multiplicativity of extension degrees to explain why the cube cannot be doubled with straight-edge and compass.
