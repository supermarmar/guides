Galois theory

There is a formula for the roots of a quadratic, and messier ones for cubics and quartics. For the general quintic there is no formula at all, not because nobody has found one but because none can exist. Galois proved this by inventing a translation: attach to each polynomial the group of symmetries permuting its roots, and questions about solving the equation become questions about the group. Solvable by radicals turns out to correspond exactly to a group property, and the symmetric group on five letters lacks it. The method matters more than the theorem.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Given a polynomial over a field F, its splitting field is the smallest extension in which it factors completely. The Galois group is the set of automorphisms of that extension fixing F pointwise, and since an automorphism must send roots to roots, it acts by permuting the roots. The group records which permutations are consistent with the algebraic relations among the roots, and it is usually far smaller than all permutations.

The fundamental theorem is a dictionary. Subgroups of the Galois group correspond exactly to intermediate fields between F and the splitting field, the correspondence reverses inclusion, indices of subgroups match degrees of extensions, and normal subgroups correspond to normal extensions. Questions about fields become questions about a finite group, which is a much smaller object. Applying the dictionary to solvability by radicals shows that a polynomial is solvable in that sense exactly when its Galois group is solvable, and since $S_5$ is not, the general quintic is not.

Feynman test Take the polynomial $x^2 - 2$, whose roots are $\sqrt{2}$ and $-\sqrt{2}$. Now ask: working only with rational numbers, can you tell the two roots apart? Every rational relation satisfied by one is satisfied by the other, since both square to 2 and neither is rational. So swapping them changes nothing expressible in rational terms, and that swap is a symmetry. Now take $x^2 - 4$, with roots 2 and $-2$. Here you can tell them apart: one is positive and rational, the other is not, and the swap is not a symmetry. The Galois group measures exactly how much the roots are interchangeable from the base field's point of view, and the whole theory follows from taking that measurement seriously.
What's beyond a first course Most undergraduate treatments state the fundamental theorem and prove insolubility of the quintic. This lecture does both and adds an explicit lattice: the extension $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2, \sqrt3)$ is worked out in full, with all four subgroups, all four intermediate fields, and the inclusion-reversing correspondence visible in a single diagram. It also exhibits a specific quintic with Galois group $S_5$, since "the general quintic" is unsatisfying without a concrete polynomial no formula can solve.
Step 2

Why this matters

This is the least applied lecture in the mathematics track, and the course landing page says so. Nothing in credit risk, pricing, or statistics uses the Galois correspondence. Three honest reasons for its inclusion follow.

The first is that it settles questions nothing else can. Why is there no quintic formula, why can an angle not be trisected with straight-edge and compass, and why is the cube not doubleable? All three are impossibility results, and impossibility requires a theory of what is possible. Degree arguments over field extensions supply that, and the answers are clean: constructible lengths have degree a power of two over the rationals, trisection requires degree three, and three does not divide a power of two.

The second is that the method generalises far beyond its origin. "Study an object by the group of symmetries that preserve its structure, and let the subgroup lattice describe the substructure lattice" is one of the most productive patterns in mathematics. It reappears as the correspondence between covering spaces and subgroups of the fundamental group in algebraic topology, which is Galois theory with spaces in place of fields and the same inclusion-reversing dictionary. Recognising the shape once means recognising it later.

The third is finite fields, which do have applications. The Galois group of a finite field extension is cyclic, generated by the Frobenius map $x \mapsto x^p$, and this single fact makes finite field arithmetic tractable enough to underpin error-correcting codes and elliptic curve cryptography. The number theory lecture uses that tractability without deriving it; this lecture is where it comes from.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture one: two lattices, one upside down

Picture two: which shuffles of the roots are allowed

An automorphism fixing the base field must send each root of a polynomial to another root of the same polynomial, since applying it to the equation $p(r) = 0$ gives $p(\sigma(r)) = 0$. So the Galois group embeds in the permutations of the roots. What it usually is not is all permutations, because algebraic relations among the roots constrain which shuffles preserve them.

Which permutations survive? Two contrasting examples. EXAMPLE A. x^4 - 5x^2 + 6 = (x^2 - 2)(x^2 - 3), roots +/-sqrt2, +/-sqrt3. Four roots, so at most 4! = 24 permutations. But the relation (root 1)^2 = 2 must be preserved, so sqrt2 can only map to +/-sqrt2, never to +/-sqrt3. Similarly for sqrt3. The allowed permutations are therefore: identity; flip the sqrt2 pair; flip the sqrt3 pair; flip both. Four of the twenty-four survive. The Galois group has order 4, matching the degree of the extension, and it is the Klein four-group of the figure. EXAMPLE B. x^3 - 2, roots c, cw, cw^2 where c is the real cube root of 2 and w is a primitive cube root of unity. Here the relations are weaker: no root is distinguished from the others by any rational relation that pins it down individually. The splitting field is Q(c, w) of degree 6 over Q, and the Galois group has order 6, acting as ALL permutations of the three roots: it is S3. The contrast is the point. Both polynomials have several roots; the first has extra rational structure linking them in pairs, which cuts the group down, and the second does not. The Galois group measures exactly how much structure is present, and a large group means the roots are hard to tell apart from the base field's point of view.
Step 4

The detail

Extensions, minimal polynomials, and degree

If K contains F then K is a vector space over F, and $[K:F]$ denotes its dimension. An element $\alpha \in K$ is algebraic over F when it satisfies some non-zero polynomial with coefficients in F, and the monic one of least degree is its minimal polynomial, necessarily irreducible. Then $F(\alpha) \cong F[x]/(m(x))$ and $[F(\alpha):F] = \deg m$, which is the construction from lecture 3 read in reverse. Degrees multiply in towers: $[M:F] = [M:K][K:F]$, proved by taking a basis of M over K and one of K over F and checking the products form a basis.

Two conditions make an extension well behaved. It is normal when every irreducible polynomial over F with one root in K splits completely in K, so no root is stranded outside. It is separable when minimal polynomials have no repeated roots, which is automatic in characteristic zero and for finite fields. An extension both normal and separable, and finite, is a Galois extension, and for those the correspondence below holds exactly.

The fundamental theorem

Fundamental theorem of Galois theory
Let $K/F$ be a finite Galois extension with group $G = \operatorname{Gal}(K/F)$. Then the maps $H \mapsto K^H$ (the field fixed by H) and $L \mapsto \operatorname{Gal}(K/L)$ are mutually inverse bijections between subgroups of G and intermediate fields. The correspondence reverses inclusion; $[K : K^H] = |H|$ and $[K^H : F] = [G:H]$; and $K^H$ is a Galois extension of F exactly when H is normal in G, in which case $\operatorname{Gal}(K^H/F) \cong G/H$.
The correspondence computed in full for Q(sqrt2, sqrt3) over Q. Degree. [Q(sqrt2):Q] = 2 since x^2 - 2 is irreducible over Q. Then x^2 - 3 remains irreducible over Q(sqrt2), because sqrt3 = a + b sqrt2 with a, b rational leads on squaring to 3 = a^2 + 2b^2 + 2ab sqrt2, forcing ab = 0 and then either 3 = a^2 or 3 = 2b^2, neither solvable in rationals. So [Q(sqrt2, sqrt3) : Q] = 2 x 2 = 4. The group. An automorphism fixing Q must send sqrt2 to +/-sqrt2 and sqrt3 to +/-sqrt3, giving at most four automorphisms, and all four occur since the degree is 4: e : sqrt2 -> sqrt2, sqrt3 -> sqrt3 s2 : sqrt2 -> -sqrt2, sqrt3 -> sqrt3 s3 : sqrt2 -> sqrt2, sqrt3 -> -sqrt3 s2s3: sqrt2 -> -sqrt2, sqrt3 -> -sqrt3 Every element squares to the identity, so G is the Klein four-group Z2 x Z2, not the cyclic group of order 4. The dictionary, subgroup by subgroup: subgroup order fixed field degree over Q ---------------------------------------------------------- {e} 1 Q(sqrt2, sqrt3) 4 {e, s3} 2 Q(sqrt2) 2 {e, s2} 2 Q(sqrt3) 2 {e, s2s3} 2 Q(sqrt6) 2 G 4 Q 1 Check the third row: s2 sends sqrt3 to sqrt3 and sqrt2 to -sqrt2, so it fixes Q(sqrt3) exactly. Check the fourth: s2s3 negates both square roots, so it fixes their product sqrt2 * sqrt3 = sqrt6, and Q(sqrt6) is its fixed field. Note the index and degree relation holds throughout: a subgroup of order 2 has index 2, and its fixed field has degree 2 over Q. And since G is abelian, every subgroup is normal, so every intermediate field is itself Galois over Q, which is why all three appear as quadratic extensions.

Solvability by radicals

An equation is solvable by radicals when its roots can be written using the coefficients, the four arithmetic operations, and root extractions. Formally, the splitting field sits inside a tower $F = F_0 \subset F_1 \subset \cdots \subset F_m$ where each step adjoins an n-th root. A group is solvable when it has a chain of subgroups, each normal in the next, with abelian quotients. Galois's theorem states that the equation is solvable by radicals exactly when its Galois group is solvable, and the reason the two notions match is that adjoining a root of unity or an n-th root produces a cyclic, hence abelian, step in the group.

Small symmetric groups are solvable: $S_2$ and $S_3$ and $S_4$ all have suitable chains, which is why quadratic, cubic, and quartic formulas exist and why the quartic is already ugly. $S_5$ is not, because its only non-trivial normal subgroup is $A_5$, and $A_5$ is simple and non-abelian, so no chain with abelian quotients reaches the identity. Hence no general quintic formula. That is the theorem; the more satisfying statement is a specific polynomial.

A specific quintic with no radical solution: x^5 - 6x + 3. Step 1: irreducible over Q. Eisenstein's criterion with p = 3: the prime 3 divides the coefficients -6 and 3, and 9 does not divide the constant term 3, and 3 does not divide the leading coefficient 1. So the polynomial is irreducible over Q, and the Galois group acts transitively on the five roots. Step 2: exactly three real roots. f(x) = x^5 - 6x + 3, f'(x) = 5x^4 - 6, critical points at x = +/-(6/5)^(1/4) = +/-1.0466. f(-1.0466) = -1.2555 + 6.2795 + 3 = 8.024 (local maximum, positive) f(+1.0466) = 1.2555 - 6.2795 + 3 = -2.024 (local minimum, negative) Since f goes from minus infinity up to a positive maximum, down to a negative minimum, then up to plus infinity, it crosses zero three times. Three real roots, and therefore one conjugate pair of complex roots. Step 3: the group is S5. By step 1 the group is a transitive subgroup of S5, so its order is divisible by 5, and Cauchy's theorem supplies an element of order 5, which in S5 must be a 5-cycle. Complex conjugation fixes the three real roots and swaps the two complex ones, so it is a transposition in the group. A transitive subgroup of S5 containing a 5-cycle and a transposition is all of S5 (the two generate). Step 4: conclude. S5 is not solvable, so x^5 - 6x + 3 = 0 cannot be solved by radicals. Its roots exist, are perfectly well defined real and complex numbers, and can be computed numerically to any accuracy: the real ones are approximately 0.50550, 1.40989, and -1.67046. What cannot exist is a formula for them built from the coefficients using arithmetic and root extraction. The distinction between "no closed form" and "no solution" is the one worth carrying away, and it recurs: most differential equations, most integrals, and most fixed points have no closed form and perfectly good solutions.

Straight-edge and compass constructions

Starting from two points, the constructible points are those reachable by intersecting lines and circles, and each new intersection solves at most a quadratic over the field generated so far. So every constructible length lies in a tower of quadratic extensions, and its degree over $\mathbb{Q}$ is therefore a power of two. Three classical problems fall immediately. Doubling the cube requires $\sqrt[3]{2}$, of degree 3, which is not a power of two. Trisecting a general angle requires solving a cubic, again degree 3: trisecting sixty degrees would need a root of $8x^3 - 6x - 1$, which is irreducible over the rationals. Squaring the circle requires $\sqrt{\pi}$, and $\pi$ is transcendental, so it has no finite degree at all. Two thousand years of failed attempts were explained by one degree argument.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The correspondence pattern, and where it appears again

Strip Galois theory of fields and what remains is a template: given an object with a group of symmetries, the subgroups classify the intermediate structures, with inclusion reversed. The same template governs covering spaces in algebraic topology, where the fundamental group plays the role of the Galois group, connected covering spaces correspond to subgroups, and normal subgroups give the regular coverings whose deck transformation group is the quotient. The analogy is not loose; Grothendieck's reformulation makes both instances of a single theorem about categories with a suitable notion of connectedness. For a reader of this track the practical value is recognition: when a lattice of substructures matches a lattice of subgroups in reverse, the machinery for one transfers to the other.

Finite fields, and why they are so easy

The Galois group of $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$ is cyclic of order n, generated by the Frobenius automorphism $x \mapsto x^p$. That single fact makes finite fields the most tractable objects in algebra: their subfields correspond to divisors of n, they are unique up to isomorphism for each prime power, and every extension is normal and separable automatically. The practical consequences run through coding theory and cryptography. Reed-Solomon codes exploit the structure of $\mathbb{F}_{256}$; elliptic curve cryptography works over finite fields and relies on the difficulty of the discrete logarithm in their multiplicative or curve groups; and the AES block cipher's byte substitution is an inversion in $\mathbb{F}_{256}$. When the next lecture asserts that finite field arithmetic is well behaved, this is why.

What remains open

The inverse Galois problem asks whether every finite group arises as the Galois group of some extension of the rationals. It is known for solvable groups, for symmetric and alternating groups, and for most sporadic simple groups, and it remains open in general after more than a century. This is worth noting for perspective: Galois theory is often presented as a closed and classical subject, finished in the 1830s, and its central existence question is unresolved. Mathematics rarely finishes a subject; it answers the question it started with and leaves the converse open.

Watch out Two confusions. Insolubility by radicals is not insolubility: the quintic above has five perfectly good roots, computable to any precision, and the theorem denies only a formula of a specific algebraic shape. And the Galois correspondence requires the extension to be Galois, meaning normal and separable; for a non-normal extension such as $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, the automorphism group is trivial while the degree is 3, the correspondence fails, and the fixed field of the trivial group is the whole extension rather than the base.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Find the minimal polynomial of $\sqrt2 + \sqrt3$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ and use it to confirm that $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2,\sqrt3):\mathbb{Q}] = 4$.
  2. Prove that degrees multiply in a tower, by constructing a basis of the top field over the bottom from bases of each step.
  3. List all four automorphisms of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, verify the group is Klein rather than cyclic, and identify the fixed field of each subgroup.
  4. Explain why $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ is not a Galois extension, and compute both its degree and its automorphism group to show the correspondence fails.
  5. Determine the Galois group of $x^3 - 2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, giving the splitting field and its degree.
  6. Define a solvable group and exhibit a chain for $S_4$ showing it is solvable. Then explain what goes wrong for $S_5$.
  7. Apply Eisenstein's criterion to $x^5 - 6x + 3$, and then show it has exactly three real roots by locating its critical points.
  8. Complete the argument that the Galois group of that quintic is $S_5$, naming the theorem that supplies the 5-cycle and the source of the transposition.
  9. Show that trisecting a sixty-degree angle requires a root of $8x^3 - 6x - 1$ and explain why that makes the construction impossible.
  10. State the Galois group of $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$ and its generator, and say what its subgroup lattice implies about subfields.
