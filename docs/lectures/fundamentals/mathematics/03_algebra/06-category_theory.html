Category theory

This course has now proved the first isomorphism theorem three times with only the nouns changed, constructed quotients four times with the same well-definedness check, and met a correspondence between subgroups and subfields that reversed inclusion for reasons that had nothing to do with fields. Category theory is the response: stop looking inside objects, look only at the arrows between them, and prove each of those theorems once. The cost is a layer of abstraction that is genuinely unmotivated until you have done the repetition, which is why this lecture comes last.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A category is a collection of objects and arrows between them, with associative composition and an identity arrow on each object. That is all: no elements, no membership, no internal structure. Sets and functions form one; groups and homomorphisms another; vector spaces and linear maps another; topological spaces and continuous maps another. A single partially ordered set is also a category, with one arrow whenever $a \le b$.

A functor maps one category to another, sending objects to objects and arrows to arrows while preserving composition. A natural transformation maps one functor to another, coherently at every object. Universal properties then define constructions by the arrows into or out of them rather than by their contents, and the payoff is that products, quotients, free objects, and completions all become instances of two or three definitions. Adjunctions pair functors going opposite ways, and the Yoneda lemma says an object is determined completely by how everything else maps into it.

Feynman test Ask what a pair is. One answer describes its contents: a box holding a first thing and a second thing. Another describes what it does: whenever you have a way of producing a first thing and a way of producing a second thing, from the same source, you get exactly one way of producing the pair, and reading off the two components recovers what you started with. The second answer never opens the box. It also applies unchanged to pairs of sets, of groups, of vector spaces, of spaces, and to the greatest lower bound of two elements in an ordering, all of which turn out to be the same construction. Category theory is the discipline of preferring the second kind of answer.
What's beyond a first course There is no first course to be beyond here; the subject is not in the UP curriculum and is rarely met before graduate work. The lecture is scoped to the parts that explain things this track has already done: universal properties, which unify the constructions of lectures 2 and 3; adjunctions, which explain both free objects and the Galois correspondence of lecture 4; and the Yoneda lemma, which is the sharpest statement of the methodological shift. Monads are included briefly because they are the one part of the subject a working programmer meets directly.
Step 2

Why this matters

Nothing in credit risk, pricing, or statistics uses category theory, and this lecture claims no such application. Its justification is internal to the track and one external application that is real.

The internal case is compression. This course proved the first isomorphism theorem for groups and stated it for rings and vector spaces, and the proofs are identical once the words change. It constructed quotients by normal subgroups, by ideals, by equivalence relations, and by null sets, each time checking that an operation was well defined on classes. In every case the check is the same check. Category theory names the pattern precisely enough that the theorem can be proved once, in any abelian category, and the four instances inherited. Whether that compression is worth an extra abstraction layer depends on how many instances you expect to meet; after this course, the count is high enough to be worth naming.

The external case is programming. The functor, applicative, and monad interfaces in Haskell, Scala, Rust, and increasingly in mainstream languages are the categorical definitions taken literally: a functor is anything with a structure-preserving map, and a monad adds a way to flatten nesting. Optional and nullable types, futures and promises, parsers, and state-threading abstractions are all monads, and the laws they must satisfy are the categorical axioms. Anyone who has wondered why flatMap appears in so many unrelated libraries has met the answer here. Lenses and optics, used for immutable data updates, are another direct application.

Step 4

The detail

Categories

Category
A collection of objects; for each ordered pair of objects a collection of arrows between them; a composition operation on compatible arrows that is associative; and an identity arrow on each object acting neutrally under composition. Nothing else is assumed, and in particular objects need not be sets and arrows need not be functions.

Two degenerate examples make the definition's reach clear. A monoid, meaning a set with an associative operation and an identity, is a category with exactly one object whose arrows are the monoid elements; composition is the operation. A poset is a category with at most one arrow between any two objects, present exactly when $a \le b$; transitivity is composition and reflexivity is the identity. Neither has anything recognisable as a function, and both satisfy the axioms, which is why theorems proved categorically apply to orderings and to algebraic structures at once.

An isomorphism is an arrow with a two-sided inverse, and this definition recovers the right notion in every category met so far: bijection in sets, group isomorphism in groups, linear isomorphism in vector spaces, homeomorphism in spaces. That one definition produces four correct notions is not a coincidence; each of those was originally defined as a structure-preserving bijection, and the categorical version says the structure preservation is what matters and the bijection is a consequence.

Universal properties

The same argument twice, then never again. CLAIM (in Set). The Cartesian product A x B with the two projections satisfies the universal property. Given f: C -> A and g: C -> B, define h(c) = (f(c), g(c)). Then p1(h(c)) = f(c) and p2(h(c)) = g(c), so both triangles commute. If h' also works then p1 h' = f and p2 h' = g force h'(c) to have first component f(c) and second g(c), so h' = h. Unique. CLAIM (in Grp). The direct product G x H with the two projections satisfies the universal property. Given homomorphisms f: K -> G and g: K -> H, define h(k) = (f(k), g(k)). It is a homomorphism because the operation on G x H is componentwise and f and g are homomorphisms. Commutativity and uniqueness follow exactly as above. The two proofs differ in one sentence: the check that h preserves the group operation. Everything else is identical, and the same is true in Vect, in Top, and in Ring. CONSEQUENCE OF THE UNIVERSAL PROPERTY ALONE. Any two objects satisfying it are uniquely isomorphic. Let P and Q both be products of A and B. Each factors through the other, giving arrows u: P -> Q and v: Q -> P. Then v u : P -> P commutes with both projections, and so does the identity on P; uniqueness in the universal property forces v u = id. Similarly u v = id. So P and Q are isomorphic, and canonically so. This proof mentions no elements and applies in every category at once. That is the whole argument for the categorical style: it is the last time this uniqueness argument needs to be given.

Other universal properties recover other familiar things. An initial object has exactly one arrow to every object, a terminal object exactly one from every object. A quotient is characterised by the property that any arrow killing the relevant substructure factors uniquely through it, which is the first isomorphism theorem restated as a definition and is why the theorem holds in every algebraic category. Limits and colimits generalise products and coproducts to arbitrary diagrams, capturing pullbacks, equalisers, direct limits, and completions in one framework.

Functors and natural transformations

A functor $F$ assigns to each object an object and to each arrow $f : A \to B$ an arrow $F(f) : F(A) \to F(B)$, preserving identities and composition. Contravariant functors reverse the arrows instead. Examples run through the whole track: the forgetful functor from groups to sets discards the operation; the free functor from sets to groups builds the free group on a set; the fundamental group is a functor from pointed spaces to groups, which is exactly what makes it useful in algebraic topology, since a continuous map of spaces induces a homomorphism of groups; the dual space is a contravariant functor on vector spaces, which is why the dual of a linear map goes the other way.

A natural transformation between functors $F$ and $G$ gives, for each object A, an arrow $\eta_A : F(A) \to G(A)$ such that the obvious square commutes for every arrow of the source category. The word natural is doing real work: it distinguishes constructions that are canonical from those requiring arbitrary choices. The classic illustration is that a finite-dimensional vector space is isomorphic to its dual, but only after choosing a basis, so the isomorphism is not natural; whereas the map into the double dual, sending a vector to evaluation at that vector, requires no choice and is natural. Category theory was invented in order to make that distinction precise, and everything else in the subject followed.

Adjunctions

Adjunction
Functors $F : \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ and $G : \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{C}$ are adjoint, written $F \dashv G$, when there is a natural bijection between arrows $F(X) \to Y$ in $\mathcal{D}$ and arrows $X \to G(Y)$ in $\mathcal{C}$. Informally, specifying a structured map out of a freely built object is the same as specifying an unstructured map into the underlying object.
Free is left adjoint to forgetful, and what that means concretely. Let F : Set -> Grp build the free group on a set, and U : Grp -> Set forget the group structure. The adjunction says { group homomorphisms F(X) -> G } corresponds bijectively to { functions X -> U(G) }. In words: to define a homomorphism out of a free group, it is necessary and sufficient to say where the generators go, with no constraints, because the free group has no relations to respect. Every reader has used this without naming it. "Define a linear map by specifying its values on a basis" is the same adjunction for vector spaces: the free vector space on a set is the one with that set as basis, and a linear map out of it is exactly an arbitrary function on the basis. The same statement covers free monoids (strings), polynomial rings (the free commutative ring on a set of variables), and free modules. OTHER ADJUNCTIONS ALREADY MET IN THIS TRACK. - Completion of a metric space is left adjoint to the inclusion of complete spaces into all metric spaces, which is the formal content of "the completion is the smallest complete space containing it" from real analysis II. - The discrete and indiscrete topologies on a set are respectively left and right adjoint to the forgetful functor from spaces to sets. - The Galois correspondence of lecture 4 is an adjunction between the poset of subgroups and the poset of intermediate fields, viewed as categories, and its inclusion-reversing character is exactly what makes it a contravariant adjoint pair. That last item is why the Galois lecture said the correspondence pattern would reappear. It is not an analogy; both are adjunctions between posets, and the general theory of such pairs (Galois connections) covers both.

Yoneda, and monads

The Yoneda lemma states, in its usable form, that an object is determined up to isomorphism by the collection of arrows into it from every other object, together with how those collections transform. Informally: you know everything about an object once you know how everything else maps into it. This is the sharpest statement of the lecture's thesis, and it has a recognisable analogue outside mathematics, in the principle that a component is characterised by its interface rather than its implementation.

A monad is a functor $T$ from a category to itself together with two natural transformations, one embedding an object into $T$ of it and one flattening $T$ of $T$ of it down to $T$ of it, satisfying coherence laws. In programming these are the operations usually called pure or return and join or flatten, and flatMap is their combination. The optional type is a monad, where flattening collapses a doubly-optional value; lists are a monad, where flattening concatenates; futures are a monad, where flattening removes a nested asynchronous wrapper. The monad laws are what guarantee that chained operations can be regrouped without changing behaviour, which is why they are worth stating in a library's documentation rather than left implicit.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Define things by their relationships

The methodological shift is the content. A traditional definition says what an object is made of; a categorical one says how it relates to everything else, and the second turns out to determine the first up to isomorphism while being far more portable. The pattern recurs outside mathematics in any discipline that has learned to specify interfaces rather than implementations: a module's contract, a service's API, an abstract data type. In each case the discipline is that consumers depend on the relationships and not on the internals, and the benefit is that the internals can change. Category theory is that idea pursued to its conclusion inside mathematics, and encountering it in that form makes the software version look less like a convention and more like a theorem.

The honest limits

Category theory organises; it rarely solves. Knowing that products in four categories share a universal property does not construct any of them, and knowing that a theorem holds in every abelian category does not prove the hard specific results that make each subject worth studying. The hard content of measure theory is the construction of Lebesgue measure and the convergence theorems, not the observation that measurable spaces form a category. There is a failure mode, familiar in both mathematics and software architecture, in which abstraction is mistaken for progress and considerable effort produces a very general framework containing very little. The corrective is to insist on instances: an abstraction that unifies four things already understood is earning its place, and one that unifies none is decoration.

Where the subject has genuinely delivered

Three places, none of them in this repository's applied work. Algebraic geometry was rebuilt on categorical foundations by Grothendieck, and the reconstruction is what made modern arithmetic geometry possible, including the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem. Homological algebra, which computes invariants by systematically resolving objects, is categorical throughout and is the engine of algebraic topology. And functional programming has taken the definitions literally rather than analogically, which is why the laws in a Haskell typeclass are the categorical axioms verbatim. The subject's reputation for being abstract without payoff is a fair description of a bad first exposure and a poor description of the field.

Watch out Two errors. Treating a universal property as a construction: it characterises an object if one exists, and existence must be proved separately in each category, which is why some categories lack products or colimits. And believing that categorical language makes an argument rigorous: a diagram that is claimed to commute must be checked to commute, and the check is the same work as the elementwise verification it replaced, done once instead of four times rather than not at all.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Verify the category axioms for a poset with one arrow whenever $a \le b$, identifying what composition and identities correspond to.
  2. Show that a monoid is a one-object category, and say what the arrows and composition are.
  3. State the universal property of the product and verify it for the Cartesian product in Set and the direct product in Grp, noting the single sentence that differs.
  4. Prove that any two objects satisfying the product's universal property are uniquely isomorphic, without mentioning elements.
  5. Reverse all the arrows in the product definition and identify the resulting construction in Set, in Vect, and in a poset.
  6. Show that the fundamental group is a functor, saying what it does to objects and to arrows, and why functoriality is what makes it useful for proving non-homeomorphism.
  7. Explain why the isomorphism between a finite-dimensional vector space and its dual is not natural while the map to the double dual is.
  8. State the free-forgetful adjunction for groups and explain what it says about defining a homomorphism out of a free group.
  9. Explain in what sense the Galois correspondence of lecture 4 is an adjunction between posets, and why it reverses inclusion.
  10. Define a monad and identify the two natural transformations for the optional type and for lists, saying what each does concretely.
