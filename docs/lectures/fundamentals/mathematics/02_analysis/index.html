Analysis

Nine lectures on limits and what can be built from them: calculus in one variable, in several, and along curves and surfaces; then the rigorous re-derivation of all of it in real analysis; then complex analysis, measure theory, functional analysis, and the measure-theoretic construction of probability. The first six lectures follow the UP calculus and analysis modules (WTW 114, 124, 218, 248, 220, 310, 320). The last three go beyond the degree and are the reason this course exists in a risk-modelling repository.

What this course is

Analysis is the study of approximation. Every statement in it has the same shape: some object you cannot compute is the limit of objects you can, and the work is proving that the limit exists and behaves. Calculus is the first instalment, taught concretely. Real analysis is the second, where the same results are proved rather than asserted and the proofs reveal that only two properties of the real line were ever used, completeness and the triangle inequality. Measure theory is the third, where the Riemann integral is replaced by one that survives the limits you actually want to take. Functional analysis is the fourth, where the objects being approximated are themselves functions.

The last lecture is the payoff for anyone in this repository. A random variable is a measurable function, an expectation is an integral against a probability measure, conditional expectation is a projection, and a martingale is what a fair game looks like once you have filtrations. Without that lecture, the stochastic calculus in financial engineering is a set of rules to memorise. With it, the rules are theorems.

Prerequisites

Two blocks. Read them first if either is unfamiliar.

  • Foundations, lectures 1 and 3: quantifiers, proof technique, induction, and equivalence relations. Real analysis is written entirely in nested quantifiers, and a reader who is not fluent in them will struggle from lecture 4 onwards.
  • Algebra, lectures 1 and 2: vector spaces, linear maps, inner products, and the spectral theorem. Needed from lecture 8, where the spaces being studied are infinite-dimensional vector spaces and the operators on them generalise matrices.

How to read this

The nine lectures are sequenced by dependency. Lectures 1 to 3 are the computational course most readers already have; skim them for notation and move on. Lectures 4 and 5 are the pivot of the whole track, and they repay slow reading. Lecture 6 is largely independent of what follows and can be read at any point after lecture 4. Lectures 7 to 9 form a single arc and should be read together. Status badges show what is built (built) or planned; the module code in brackets names the UP module the lecture is drawn from.

Course sequence

Part 1 · Calculus (lectures 1 to 3)

The computational layer: differentiate, integrate, and do both in more than one dimension.

  • builtLecture 1: single-variable calculus. Functions, limits, and continuity. Differentiation and rates of change. The mean value theorem and L'Hopital's rule. Curve sketching and optimisation. Definite and indefinite integrals, the substitution rule and integration techniques, the fundamental theorem of calculus. (WTW 114, WTW 124)
  • builtLecture 2: multivariable calculus. Functions of several variables, partial and directional derivatives, the gradient and the multivariable chain rule. Extrema, the second derivative test, and Lagrange multipliers. Multiple integrals in polar, cylindrical, and spherical coordinates. (WTW 218)
  • builtLecture 3: vector calculus. Vector functions and the differential geometry of curves. Vector fields, line integrals, conservative fields and potentials. Surfaces and surface integrals. The theorems of Green, Gauss, and Stokes. (WTW 248)

Part 2 · Rigour (lectures 4 to 6)

The same material proved rather than asserted, and then extended to the complex plane where the theory turns out to be far stronger.

  • builtLecture 4: real analysis I. The completeness of the reals. Sequences and series, tests of convergence, power series. The Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem. The intermediate value theorem, continuity, and differentiability on an interval. Construction and properties of the Riemann integral. (WTW 220)
  • builtLecture 5: real analysis II. Metric spaces and the topology of finite-dimensional spaces: open and closed sets, compactness, connectedness, completeness. The Heine-Borel theorem. Uniform versus pointwise convergence and why the distinction matters. Sequences and series of functions. (WTW 310)
  • builtLecture 6: complex analysis. Complex functions, the Cauchy-Riemann equations, holomorphy. Contour integration, Cauchy's theorem and integral formulas. Taylor and Laurent series, classification of singularities. The residue theorem, and real integrals evaluated by residues. (WTW 320)

Part 3 · Beyond the degree (lectures 7 to 9)

The modern foundations: a better integral, infinite-dimensional spaces, and probability rebuilt on both.

  • builtLecture 7: measure theory and Lebesgue integration. Sigma-algebras and measures, Lebesgue measure, the existence of non-measurable sets. Measurable functions and the Lebesgue integral. Monotone and dominated convergence. Fubini-Tonelli. Absolute continuity and the Radon-Nikodym theorem. (extension)
  • builtLecture 8: functional analysis. Normed, Banach, and Hilbert spaces. The L^p spaces. Orthogonality, projection, and orthonormal bases. Bounded linear operators. Hahn-Banach, open mapping, and closed graph theorems. Duality and weak convergence. The spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators. (extension)
  • builtLecture 9: measure-theoretic probability. Probability spaces, random variables as measurable functions, expectation as an integral. Independence and the Borel-Cantelli lemmas. Modes of convergence and the laws of large numbers. Characteristic functions and the central limit theorem. Conditional expectation as a projection, filtrations, and martingales. (extension)

Where this course is used later

The statistics course uses lectures 1 and 4 constantly, since every estimator is a limit and every asymptotic result is a convergence theorem, and lecture 9 is the formal justification for the probability lecture there. The machine learning course lives on lecture 2, because every gradient step is a directional derivative and every regularisation argument is a constrained optimisation. The financial engineering course needs lecture 9 in full before stochastic calculus makes sense, and Radon-Nikodym from lecture 7 is precisely the change of measure that turns a real-world drift into a risk-neutral one. On the credit side, the Vasicek model, copulas, and extreme value theory all reduce to statements about limits of sums, which is lecture 4 with more structure.
