Real analysis I

This is the pivot of the whole track. The first three lectures computed; this one proves, and in proving it reveals that every theorem of calculus rests on a single property of the real numbers that the rationals lack. Completeness is that property, and the chain runs: completeness gives suprema, suprema give the monotone convergence theorem, that gives Bolzano-Weierstrass, that gives the extreme value theorem, and that is what made Rolle's proof work three lectures ago. Pull completeness out and the entire structure collapses to nothing.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The rationals are missing points. There is no rational number whose square is 2, so the set of rationals below $\sqrt{2}$ is bounded above with no rational least upper bound. The completeness axiom says the reals have no such gaps: every non-empty set bounded above has a least upper bound, its supremum. That one axiom is the entire difference between $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{R}$, and every existence theorem in calculus is a consequence of it.

From completeness the results cascade. Bounded monotone sequences converge. Every bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence (Bolzano-Weierstrass). Cauchy sequences converge, so the reals are complete as a metric space too. Continuous functions on closed bounded intervals attain their bounds (the extreme value theorem) and hit every intermediate value. Continuous functions on such intervals are uniformly continuous, which is what makes them Riemann integrable. Each of these is an existence claim, none can be proved by computation, and all of them fail over the rationals.

Feynman test Suppose you only had the rational numbers, and you tried to prove that a continuous function which is negative at 0 and positive at 2 must be zero somewhere between. Take $f(x) = x^2 - 2$. It is negative at 0, positive at 2, continuous everywhere, and never zero at any rational point, because $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational. The intermediate value theorem, which looks like an obvious statement about unbroken curves, is simply false over the rationals. Nothing about continuity failed; the number system had a hole exactly where the root should have been. Everything in this lecture is the work of establishing that the reals have no holes and then harvesting the consequences, and the intermediate value theorem is the first harvest.
What's beyond a first course A first course states the theorems and drills epsilon-delta exercises. This lecture makes the dependency structure explicit: it proves Bolzano-Weierstrass by bisection, derives the extreme value theorem from it, and notes at each stage exactly which earlier result is doing the work, so that the reader can see the whole edifice standing on completeness. It also treats conditional convergence seriously, including Riemann's rearrangement theorem, because absolute convergence is precisely the condition under which sums and integrals may be manipulated freely, and that condition reappears as a hypothesis in Fubini's theorem, in the dominated convergence theorem, and in the definition of an expectation.
Step 2

Why this matters

Three reasons, in increasing order of practical bite. The first is that this lecture supplies the proofs the calculus lectures borrowed. Rolle's theorem needed the extreme value theorem, which needs Bolzano-Weierstrass, which needs completeness. A reader who has taken those on trust has a working subject; a reader who has seen the chain has a subject that cannot be shaken by an unfamiliar case.

The second is convergence, which is what every asymptotic claim in statistics is about. Consistency of an estimator is convergence in probability, which is built on the definition of a limit given here. The central limit theorem is a convergence statement about distribution functions. Iterative algorithms across machine learning and numerical work, from Newton-Raphson to expectation-maximisation to gradient descent, are sequences whose convergence is proved with the tools of this lecture, usually by showing the sequence is monotone and bounded or that it is Cauchy. When a training run fails to converge, the diagnosis is nearly always that one of these hypotheses fails.

The third is that absolute convergence is a genuine practical constraint rather than a technicality. Riemann's rearrangement theorem says a conditionally convergent series can be reordered to sum to any value whatsoever. In a summation over states, scenarios, or cashflows where the positive and negative parts are each infinite, the answer depends on the order of summation, which is not a property any reported number should have. The same condition, absolute integrability, is the hypothesis of Fubini's theorem from the previous lecture and the definition of an expectation existing at all. Knowing where that line falls is knowing when a computation is meaningful.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture one: the hole in the rationals

Picture two: the epsilon game

Convergence statements are adversarial. A sceptic names a tolerance $\epsilon$, however small, and you must produce a threshold N beyond which every term of the sequence sits within $\epsilon$ of the limit. You win if you can answer every challenge; the sceptic wins if some $\epsilon$ defeats you. Writing the definition as a game makes both the quantifier order and the proof strategy obvious: $\epsilon$ comes first because the sceptic moves first, and N may depend on $\epsilon$ because you answer afterwards.

Step 4

The detail

Suprema and the completeness axiom

Supremum
An upper bound of $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ is any M with $s \le M$ for all $s \in S$. The supremum $\sup S$ is the least such bound: it is an upper bound, and no smaller number is one. The infimum is the mirror image. The supremum need not belong to S: $\sup\{x : x < 1\} = 1$, which is not in the set.

The completeness axiom states that every non-empty subset of $\mathbb{R}$ that is bounded above has a supremum in $\mathbb{R}$. This is an axiom rather than a theorem: it is part of what defines the reals, and it is exactly what the rationals fail. The working characterisation, used in nearly every proof below, is that $M = \sup S$ if and only if M is an upper bound and, for every $\epsilon > 0$, some element of S exceeds $M - \epsilon$. That second clause is the useful one, because it produces an element of the set close to the supremum on demand.

Completeness at work: the square root of 2 exists. Let S = { x in R : x > 0 and x^2 < 2 }. S is non-empty (1 is in it) and bounded above (by 2, since x > 2 gives x^2 > 4). By completeness, let c = sup S. Claim: c^2 = 2. Rule out both alternatives. Suppose c^2 < 2. Let d = 2 - c^2 > 0. For small h in (0,1): (c+h)^2 = c^2 + 2ch + h^2 <= c^2 + h(2c + 1). Choosing h = min(1/2, d / (2c+1)) makes the right side at most c^2 + d = 2, so (c+h)^2 < 2 and c+h is in S. But c+h > c = sup S, a contradiction. Suppose c^2 > 2. Let d = c^2 - 2 > 0 and take h = d / (2c) > 0. Then (c-h)^2 = c^2 - 2ch + h^2 > c^2 - 2ch = c^2 - d = 2, so c-h is an upper bound for S (anything larger has square above 2), and c - h < c contradicts c being the LEAST upper bound. Both alternatives fail, so c^2 = 2. QED Note what was used: only the axiom. No decimal expansions, no algorithm, no appeal to a calculator. The number exists because the axiom says the supremum does, and this is the template for every existence proof below.

Two consequences follow immediately. The Archimedean property says that for any real x there is a natural number exceeding it; if not, $\mathbb{N}$ would be bounded above, its supremum M would admit some $n > M - 1$, and then $n+1 > M$ would contradict the bound. And the rationals are dense: between any two distinct reals lies a rational, found by choosing a denominator large enough that steps of size $1/q$ cannot straddle the gap, which is the Archimedean property again.

Sequences

$a_n \to L$ means: for every $\epsilon > 0$ there is N such that $|a_n - L| < \epsilon$ for all $n > N$. Limits are unique, since two distinct limits could be separated by taking $\epsilon$ less than half their distance. Limits respect arithmetic: sums, products, and quotients of convergent sequences converge to the corresponding combinations, provided denominators do not vanish in the limit. Convergent sequences are bounded, since all but finitely many terms sit in one band and finitely many terms have a maximum.

The epsilon-N game, played twice. Claim 1: 1/n -> 0. Given eps > 0, the Archimedean property supplies N with N > 1/eps. For n > N: |1/n - 0| = 1/n < 1/N < eps. Done. Note where completeness entered: the Archimedean property, which came from the supremum axiom. Claim 2: (3n^2 + 1)/(n^2 + n) -> 3. Compute the gap: (3n^2 + 1)/(n^2 + n) - 3 = [3n^2 + 1 - 3n^2 - 3n] / (n^2 + n) = (1 - 3n) / (n^2 + n) Bound its size, for n >= 1: |1 - 3n| = 3n - 1 < 3n, and n^2 + n > n^2, so the gap is less than 3n / n^2 = 3/n. Given eps > 0, choose N > 3/eps. For n > N the gap is below 3/N < eps. Done. The structure is identical both times: bound the gap by something obviously shrinking, then invert to find N. Elaborate algebra is almost never needed, because a crude upper bound is enough to win the game.

Three convergence theorems, in dependency order. The monotone convergence theorem says a bounded increasing sequence converges, and the proof is immediate from completeness: the limit is the supremum of the terms, and the characterisation of the supremum produces a term within $\epsilon$ of it, after which monotonicity keeps all later terms in the band.

Bolzano-Weierstrass by bisection: every bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence. Let (a_n) lie in [A, B]. Cut the interval in half. At least one half contains infinitely many terms of the sequence (if both halves held only finitely many, the whole interval would too). Pick such a half and call it I_1. Repeat forever, producing nested intervals [A,B] contains I_1 contains I_2 contains ... with length(I_k) = (B - A) / 2^k, each containing infinitely many terms. Build a subsequence: pick n_1 with a_{n_1} in I_1, then n_2 > n_1 with a_{n_2} in I_2 (possible, since I_2 holds infinitely many terms), and so on. The left endpoints of the intervals form an increasing bounded sequence, so by monotone convergence they converge to some c, and the right endpoints converge to the same c because the lengths tend to zero. Since a_{n_k} lies in I_k, and I_k shrinks to c, we get a_{n_k} -> c. QED Every step used completeness once, through monotone convergence. Over the rationals the nested intervals could close on a gap and the theorem fails.

A sequence is Cauchy when its terms eventually get close to each other: for every $\epsilon$ there is N with $|a_n - a_m| < \epsilon$ for all $n, m > N$. In $\mathbb{R}$, Cauchy and convergent are equivalent. Convergent implies Cauchy by the triangle inequality. The converse needs work: a Cauchy sequence is bounded, so Bolzano-Weierstrass gives a convergent subsequence, and the Cauchy condition then drags the whole sequence to the same limit. The practical value is enormous, because the Cauchy criterion tests convergence without naming the limit, which is what lets you prove that a limit exists before knowing what it is.

Series

A series $\sum a_n$ is the sequence of its partial sums, so everything above applies. The tests are the standard ones, and the table records which hypothesis each needs.

A series converges absolutely when $\sum |a_n|$ converges, and conditionally when it converges but $\sum|a_n|$ does not. The alternating harmonic series $\sum (-1)^{n+1}/n$ is the standard conditional example, converging to $\ln 2$ while its absolute version diverges. The distinction is not cosmetic.

Riemann's rearrangement theorem, and why absolute convergence matters. Theorem. If a series converges conditionally, then for ANY target value L (including plus or minus infinity) some rearrangement of its terms converges to L. Why it works. Conditional convergence means the positive terms alone sum to +infinity and the negative terms alone sum to -infinity, while individual terms tend to zero. So: add positive terms until you first exceed L, then add negative terms until you first fall below L, then positives again, and so on. Each switch overshoots by at most the size of the last term used, and those sizes tend to zero, so the partial sums converge to L exactly. Illustration with the alternating harmonic series (true sum ln 2 = 0.6931): 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + 1/5 - ... = 0.6931... Rearranged as two positives per negative: 1 + 1/3 - 1/2 + 1/5 + 1/7 - 1/4 + ... = (3/2) ln 2 = 1.0397... Same terms, every one used exactly once, different sum. Consequence for practice. A sum whose positive and negative parts are each unbounded has no order-independent value, so any number reported from it is an artefact of the summation order. This is why absolute integrability is the hypothesis of Fubini's theorem, why an expectation E[X] is defined only when E|X| is finite, and why a "sum over scenarios" that relies on cancellation between large offsetting terms should be treated with suspicion rather than rounded and published.

Power series $\sum c_n (x-a)^n$ converge on an interval centred at a, whose half-width R is the radius of convergence, computable by the ratio or root test. Inside the radius, convergence is absolute and the series may be differentiated and integrated term by term; at the two endpoints anything can happen and each must be checked separately. This is the precise sense in which a Taylor series represents its function: only within the radius, and only when the remainder tends to zero, which are two different conditions.

Continuity, and the two existence theorems

$f$ is continuous at a when for every $\epsilon > 0$ there is $\delta > 0$ with $|f(x) - f(a)| < \epsilon$ whenever $|x - a| < \delta$. Equivalently, and often more usefully, $f(x_n) \to f(a)$ for every sequence $x_n \to a$, which converts continuity questions into sequence questions and lets Bolzano-Weierstrass be applied.

The two great existence theorems on a closed bounded interval. INTERMEDIATE VALUE THEOREM. If f is continuous on [a,b] and y lies between f(a) and f(b), then f(c) = y for some c in [a,b]. Proof (suppose f(a) < y < f(b)). Let S = { x in [a,b] : f(x) < y }. S is non-empty (a is in it) and bounded above by b, so c = sup S exists. If f(c) < y: continuity gives an interval around c where f stays below y, so points slightly beyond c are in S, contradicting c = sup S. If f(c) > y: continuity gives an interval around c where f stays above y, so c - delta is already an upper bound for S, contradicting leastness. Hence f(c) = y. QED EXTREME VALUE THEOREM. A continuous f on [a,b] is bounded and attains a maximum and a minimum. Proof (boundedness). If f were unbounded, pick x_n with |f(x_n)| > n. By Bolzano-Weierstrass a subsequence x_{n_k} converges to some c in [a,b] (closedness of the interval is what keeps the limit inside). Continuity gives f(x_{n_k}) -> f(c), so that subsequence is bounded, contradicting |f(x_{n_k})| > n_k -> infinity. Proof (attainment). Let M = sup of f on [a,b], finite by the above. Choose x_n with f(x_n) > M - 1/n. Bolzano-Weierstrass gives x_{n_k} -> c, and continuity gives f(c) = lim f(x_{n_k}) = M. So the supremum is attained. QED Both hypotheses are needed. On the open interval (0,1), f(x) = 1/x is continuous and unbounded. On [0, infinity), f(x) = x is continuous and attains no maximum. Closed and bounded together are what work, and the general name for that combination is compactness, which lecture 5 takes up.

A function is uniformly continuous when the same $\delta$ works for every point, that is, $\delta$ depends on $\epsilon$ alone and not on the location. The order of quantifiers is the only difference from ordinary continuity, and it is decisive: $f(x) = 1/x$ is continuous on $(0,1)$ but not uniformly so, because near zero the function steepens without limit and any fixed $\delta$ eventually fails. The theorem that rescues the situation is that a continuous function on a closed bounded interval is automatically uniformly continuous, proved by Bolzano-Weierstrass in the same style as above, and it is exactly what the integrability proof below needs.

The Riemann integral

Partition $[a,b]$ into subintervals. On each, take the supremum and the infimum of f, multiply by the width, and sum, giving the upper sum $U(f,P)$ and lower sum $L(f,P)$. Refining a partition lowers U and raises L, so every lower sum is below every upper sum, and completeness gives the upper integral $\inf_P U(f,P)$ and lower integral $\sup_P L(f,P)$. The function is Riemann integrable when these agree.

Riemann criterion
$f$ is integrable on $[a,b]$ if and only if for every $\epsilon > 0$ some partition P satisfies $U(f,P) - L(f,P) < \epsilon$. This turns an existence question about two suprema into a single computable inequality, and it is the form used in every integrability proof.
Continuous implies integrable, and where uniform continuity is used. Let f be continuous on [a,b], hence uniformly continuous. Given eps > 0, uniform continuity supplies delta > 0 such that |x - y| < delta implies |f(x) - f(y)| < eps / (b - a). Take any partition P with all subintervals shorter than delta. On the i-th subinterval, f attains its max M_i and min m_i (extreme value theorem, since each closed subinterval is closed and bounded), at points less than delta apart, so M_i - m_i < eps / (b - a). Then U(f,P) - L(f,P) = sum_i (M_i - m_i) * width_i < [eps / (b - a)] * sum_i width_i = [eps / (b - a)] * (b - a) = eps. By the Riemann criterion, f is integrable. QED Notice the chain: completeness -> Bolzano-Weierstrass -> extreme value and uniform continuity -> integrability. The innocuous claim "continuous functions can be integrated" sits on top of the entire lecture, and this is why the proof appears last rather than first. Where Riemann fails. Let f be 1 on the rationals and 0 on the irrationals, on [0,1]. Every subinterval contains both, so every upper sum is 1 and every lower sum is 0, and the two never meet: f is not Riemann integrable. Yet "almost every" point is irrational in a sense that measure theory makes precise, so the answer ought to be 0. Repairing this is what lecture 7 does.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Six theorems, one axiom, all equivalent

The results in this lecture are not merely consequences of completeness; over an ordered field satisfying the Archimedean property, they are equivalent to it and to each other. The supremum property, the monotone convergence theorem, the nested interval property, Bolzano-Weierstrass, the Cauchy criterion, and the Heine-Borel property are six statements that stand or fall together, and any one may be taken as the axiom with the other five following. This is worth knowing for two reasons. It explains why every proof in this lecture eventually bottoms out in the same place, and it means that when a new setting is introduced, checking any one of the six settles whether the whole analytic toolkit is available there. That is precisely the check performed on metric spaces in lecture 5, where completeness becomes a property a space may or may not have rather than an axiom, and where the spaces that have it, the Banach and Hilbert spaces, become the ones worth studying.

Pointwise limits destroy properties that uniform limits preserve

Take $f_n(x) = x^n$ on $[0,1]$. Each function is continuous, and at every point the sequence converges: to 0 for $x < 1$, and to 1 at $x = 1$. The limit function is discontinuous. Continuity was not preserved by taking a pointwise limit, and the reason is that the convergence is not uniform: near $x = 1$, the index needed to get within $\epsilon$ blows up. The distinction is the same quantifier swap as continuity against uniform continuity, and it recurs at every level of analysis. Uniform convergence preserves continuity, permits term-by-term integration, and makes limits of integrals equal integrals of limits; pointwise convergence guarantees none of these. Lecture 5 develops this properly, and lecture 7 supplies the theorems, dominated and monotone convergence, that recover interchange under much weaker hypotheses by changing the integral rather than strengthening the convergence.

Why this lecture is where the track turns

Everything before this point could be done by computation, and everything after it requires proof. The change is not one of difficulty but of what counts as an answer. A calculus question asks for a number, and an analysis question asks whether the number exists and what it is a limit of. The three courses that follow are all consequences of taking that question seriously. Topology strips the metric away and asks which arguments survive; measure theory replaces the integral so that limits behave; functional analysis applies the whole apparatus to spaces of functions. A reader who is comfortable with the epsilon game and with the completeness chain can read all three. A reader who is not will find each of them a wall, and the correct response to hitting that wall is to return here rather than to push on.

Watch out Three specific errors. Assuming the supremum belongs to the set: it need not, and the difference between $\sup$ and $\max$ is exactly whether it does, which is why the extreme value theorem is a theorem and not a definition. Letting $\delta$ depend on the point when the argument needs uniformity: that error silently invalidates the integrability proof above and most interchange-of-limit arguments. And treating a Taylor series as equal to its function outside the radius of convergence, or inside it without checking that the remainder vanishes; the standard counterexample is $e^{-1/x^2}$ extended by zero at the origin, whose derivatives all vanish there, so its Taylor series is identically zero while the function is not.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. State the completeness axiom, then give the epsilon characterisation of a supremum and prove the two are consistent by showing the characterisation identifies the least upper bound uniquely.
  2. Prove that $\sqrt{3}$ exists as a real number, following the supremum argument used for $\sqrt{2}$ and adjusting the two inequalities.
  3. Prove the Archimedean property from completeness, and use it to show that between any two reals there is a rational.
  4. Prove from the definition that $(2n+3)/(n+1) \to 2$, exhibiting N as an explicit function of $\epsilon$.
  5. Prove the monotone convergence theorem, identifying precisely where completeness is used.
  6. Prove Bolzano-Weierstrass by bisection, then use it to prove that a Cauchy sequence of reals converges.
  7. Determine whether each converges, and whether absolutely: $\sum 1/n^{3/2}$, $\sum (-1)^n / \sqrt{n}$, $\sum n!/n^n$, $\sum 1/(n \ln n)$.
  8. Explain Riemann's rearrangement theorem in your own words, then state the condition under which a sum is order independent and name two places elsewhere in this track where that condition appears as a hypothesis.
  9. Prove the intermediate value theorem using a supremum, and give a function on the rationals showing the theorem fails there.
  10. Show that $f(x) = 1/x$ is continuous but not uniformly continuous on $(0,1)$, then prove that continuity on a closed bounded interval implies uniform continuity, and say why the integrability proof needs the stronger version.

Where this is used later

  • Statistics: consistency, asymptotic normality, and every "converges in probability" statement rest on the definition of a limit given here; the delta method is a Taylor expansion with a remainder argument.
  • Machine learning: convergence proofs for iterative optimisers are monotone-and-bounded or Cauchy arguments, and non-convergence diagnoses come down to which hypothesis fails.
  • Financial mathematics: the geometric series result is the annuity formula, and perpetuity values are exactly the convergence condition $|r| < 1$ in financial dress.
