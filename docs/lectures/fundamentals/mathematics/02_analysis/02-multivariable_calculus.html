Multivariable calculus

Everything from the previous lecture happens again with more directions available, and two things change as a result. The derivative stops being a number and becomes a vector, the gradient, which points the way uphill and whose length says how steep the hill is. And optimisation acquires constraints, because in more than one dimension you can be forced to stay on a curve or a surface while searching for a maximum. Lagrange multipliers handle that, and they turn out to price the constraint as well as satisfy it.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A partial derivative differentiates with respect to one variable while holding the others fixed. Collect them into a vector and you have the gradient $\nabla f$, which has two properties worth memorising: it points in the direction of steepest increase, and it is perpendicular to the level set through the point. The directional derivative in any unit direction u is then $\nabla f \cdot u$, which is largest when u aligns with the gradient, and that single dot product explains why gradient descent works.

Optimisation needs a second derivative. The Hessian collects all second partials into a symmetric matrix, and its definiteness at a stationary point classifies that point: positive definite gives a minimum, negative definite a maximum, indefinite a saddle. With a constraint $g = c$ in the way, the condition changes to $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$, meaning the two gradients are parallel, and the multiplier $\lambda$ measures how much the optimum would improve if the constraint were relaxed. Integration extends by iterating one dimension at a time, with a Jacobian factor whenever the coordinates change.

Feynman test Stand on a hillside in fog. You cannot see the summit, but you can feel the slope under your feet in every direction you might step. Face north and the ground rises steeply; face east and it barely rises at all; face south and it falls away. The gradient is the arrow pointing in the single steepest uphill direction, and its length is how steep that direction is. Two consequences follow without any algebra. If you want to climb fastest, walk along the arrow. And if you want to stay at the same altitude, walk at right angles to it, which is why contour lines on a map always cross the steepest-ascent direction perpendicularly. Gradient descent is this, with the sign flipped and the fog never lifting.
What's beyond a first course A first course computes partials, applies the second derivative test, and drills Lagrange multipliers as a recipe. This lecture adds the interpretation of the multiplier as a shadow price, which is what makes the technique useful rather than mechanical; the Gaussian integral evaluated by the polar coordinates trick, because it is the reason the normal density has the constant it has; and an honest treatment of what can go wrong, including the fact that continuity of all partial derivatives is needed for differentiability, that mixed partials commute only under a hypothesis, and that swapping the order of integration requires a theorem rather than an assumption.
Step 2

Why this matters

Every model with more than one parameter is fitted by multivariable optimisation, so this lecture is the machinery underneath model estimation generally. A log-likelihood with p parameters is maximised by setting a p-vector of partial derivatives to zero (the score) and checking a $p \times p$ matrix of second partials (the observed information). The statistics course calls those objects the score and the Fisher information, and they are the gradient and the negative expected Hessian of this lecture, wearing statistical names. Newton-Raphson and iteratively reweighted least squares, which fit every generalised linear model including the logistic regressions used for PD scorecards, are gradient and Hessian iterations.

Constrained optimisation is equally direct. Mean-variance portfolio construction minimises a quadratic subject to a budget constraint and possibly a return target, which is a Lagrange problem with a closed-form solution. Regularised regression is an unconstrained problem that is equivalent to a constrained one, and the equivalence is exactly the Lagrangian correspondence, with the penalty parameter playing the multiplier's role. Wherever a capital allocation, a risk limit, or a budget appears in an optimisation, the multiplier attached to it is the marginal value of loosening it, which is the quantity a committee actually wants to know.

Multiple integrals carry the probability. A joint density integrates over a region to give a probability, marginals are integrals over the nuisance variables, and any transformation of random variables introduces a Jacobian factor. The change-of-variables formula in this lecture is the same formula that appears in the density transformation theorem, and the polar-coordinates evaluation of the Gaussian integral below is where the normalising constant of the normal distribution comes from.

Step 4

The detail

Partial derivatives, the gradient, and a warning

For $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$, the partial derivative $\partial f / \partial x_i$ differentiates in the i-th coordinate direction with the others held fixed, and it is computed by treating the other variables as constants. The gradient is the vector of partials, $\nabla f = (\partial f/\partial x_1, \ldots, \partial f/\partial x_n)$.

Differentiability in several variables
$f$ is differentiable at a when $f(a + h) = f(a) + \nabla f(a) \cdot h + o(\|h\|)$, that is, when the tangent plane approximates f with error vanishing faster than the step length. Existence of all partial derivatives is not sufficient for this. A sufficient condition, and the one to check in practice, is that all partials exist and are continuous near a.

The gap between "partials exist" and "differentiable" is real. The function $f(x,y) = xy/(x^2+y^2)$ with $f(0,0) = 0$ has both partials equal to zero at the origin, since it vanishes identically along each axis, yet it takes the value $1/2$ along the line $y = x$ arbitrarily close to the origin, so it is not even continuous there. Partial derivatives look along the axes only, and a function can misbehave in a diagonal direction while both axes report nothing wrong.

The directional derivative in a unit direction u is $D_u f = \nabla f \cdot u = \|\nabla f\| \cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between u and the gradient. This is maximised at $\theta = 0$, giving steepest ascent along the gradient, and it is zero at $\theta = \pi/2$, which is the perpendicularity to level sets drawn in the first figure. Gradient descent takes a step of size $\eta$ against the gradient, $x_{k+1} = x_k - \eta \nabla f(x_k)$, and the learning rate $\eta$ is the step length whose tuning consumes so much practitioner time.

The chain rule generalises to a sum over paths of dependence. If $z = f(x,y)$ with $x = x(t)$ and $y = y(t)$, then $\frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}$. In matrix form, the derivative of a composition is the product of Jacobian matrices, which is the statement that backpropagation implements.

The Hessian and the second derivative test

The Hessian is the matrix of second partials, $H_{ij} = \partial^2 f / \partial x_i \partial x_j$. Under Clairaut's theorem, if the second partials are continuous then mixed partials commute, $\partial^2 f/\partial x \partial y = \partial^2 f/\partial y \partial x$, and the Hessian is symmetric. Symmetry matters because it guarantees real eigenvalues and an orthogonal eigenbasis, by the spectral theorem from linear algebra.

The test agrees with the direct inspection, which is the sanity check worth doing the first few times.

Lagrange multipliers, and what the multiplier means

To optimise $f$ subject to $g = c$, form the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}(x,\lambda) = f(x) - \lambda(g(x) - c)$ and set all its partial derivatives to zero. The x-derivatives give $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$, the tangency condition drawn above, and the $\lambda$-derivative gives back the constraint $g = c$. That is $n+1$ equations in $n+1$ unknowns.

The multiplier as a shadow price
At the optimum, $\lambda = \dfrac{\partial f^*}{\partial c}$: the multiplier is the rate at which the optimal objective value improves as the constraint level is relaxed. This is why the same object is called a shadow price in economics, a marginal cost of capital in a capital-allocation problem, and a regularisation parameter in a penalised regression. Computing $\lambda$ and discarding it, which is what a first course encourages, throws away the more useful half of the answer.
Minimum variance portfolio with two assets, by Lagrange. Two assets with variances s1^2 = 0.04 and s2^2 = 0.09 (20% and 30% vol) and correlation rho = 0.3, so covariance s12 = 0.3 * 0.2 * 0.3 = 0.018. Minimise V(w1,w2) = w1^2 (0.04) + w2^2 (0.09) + 2 w1 w2 (0.018) subject to w1 + w2 = 1. Lagrangian: L = V - lambda (w1 + w2 - 1) dL/dw1: 0.08 w1 + 0.036 w2 - lambda = 0 dL/dw2: 0.18 w2 + 0.036 w1 - lambda = 0 dL/dlambda: w1 + w2 = 1 Subtract the first two equations: 0.08 w1 + 0.036 w2 - 0.036 w1 - 0.18 w2 = 0 0.044 w1 = 0.144 w2 w1 = 3.2727 w2 With w1 + w2 = 1: 4.2727 w2 = 1, so w2 = 0.2340 and w1 = 0.7660. Minimum variance: V = 0.7660^2 (0.04) + 0.2340^2 (0.09) + 2(0.7660)(0.2340)(0.018) = 0.023473 + 0.004928 + 0.006453 = 0.034854, so portfolio vol = 18.67%. Lower than either asset alone (20% and 30%), which is diversification. The multiplier: lambda = 0.08(0.7660) + 0.036(0.2340) = 0.069706. Interpretation: relaxing the budget constraint by one unit (allowing total weight 1 + dc) would change minimum variance at rate 0.0697 per unit. The general closed form, w1 = (s2^2 - s12)/(s1^2 + s2^2 - 2 s12), gives (0.09 - 0.018)/(0.04 + 0.09 - 0.036) = 0.072/0.094 = 0.7660, confirming the arithmetic.

Multiple integrals and Fubini

A double integral over a rectangle is computed by iterating: integrate in y holding x fixed, then integrate the result in x. Fubini's theorem is what licenses the swap of order, and its hypothesis is that the integral of the absolute value is finite. Over a bounded region with a continuous integrand this always holds, so a first course can ignore it; over unbounded regions or with sign-changing integrands it can fail, and the counterexamples are not exotic. The careful statement belongs to lecture 7, and the working rule until then is that swapping order is safe when the integrand is non-negative or absolutely integrable.

For non-rectangular regions, the inner limits depend on the outer variable, and setting them up correctly is most of the work. Sketch the region, choose an order, and for each fixed value of the outer variable read off the range of the inner one.

Change of variables and the Jacobian

Under a smooth invertible change of coordinates $(x,y) = T(u,v)$, the area element transforms by the absolute determinant of the Jacobian matrix:

$$ \iint_R f(x,y)\,dx\,dy = \iint_{T^{-1}(R)} f(T(u,v)) \left| \det \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} \right| du\,dv. $$

The determinant is there because a linear map scales area by its determinant, and locally every smooth map is linear, which is the derivative idea from the previous lecture reappearing. For polar coordinates $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$ the Jacobian determinant is r, giving the familiar $dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$; for spherical coordinates it is $\rho^2 \sin\phi$.

The Gaussian integral, and where the normal constant comes from. Goal: I = integral over all reals of exp(-x^2/2) dx. No elementary antiderivative exists, so square it and go to polar. I^2 = [ int exp(-x^2/2) dx ] * [ int exp(-y^2/2) dy ] = double integral over the whole plane of exp(-(x^2+y^2)/2) dx dy Switch to polar: x^2 + y^2 = r^2, and dx dy = r dr dtheta, with r from 0 to infinity and theta from 0 to 2 pi. I^2 = int_0^{2pi} int_0^inf exp(-r^2/2) r dr dtheta Inner integral, substituting s = r^2/2 so ds = r dr: int_0^inf exp(-s) ds = 1. So I^2 = int_0^{2pi} 1 dtheta = 2 pi, giving I = sqrt(2 pi). Therefore the function (1 / sqrt(2 pi)) exp(-x^2/2) integrates to 1, which is the standard normal density. The constant that looks arbitrary in every statistics text is the square root of 2 pi precisely because the polar Jacobian supplies the factor of r that makes the radial integral elementary. The trick is worth remembering as a technique, not just a result: a hard one-dimensional integral became easy in two dimensions because the extra symmetry allowed a change of coordinates.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The Hessian's condition number is why optimisation is slow

Near a minimum, a smooth function looks like a quadratic form determined by its Hessian, whose level sets are ellipsoids with axes along the eigenvectors and lengths set by the eigenvalues. When the eigenvalues are similar, the ellipsoids are nearly spherical, the gradient points almost at the minimum, and gradient descent converges quickly. When they differ by orders of magnitude, the ellipsoids are long thin valleys, the gradient points almost perpendicular to the valley floor, and descent zigzags across it making little progress along it. The relevant number is the condition number, the ratio of largest to smallest eigenvalue, and the convergence rate of gradient descent degrades directly with it.

Everything practitioners do about slow training is a response to this geometry. Feature standardisation reduces the condition number by putting the axes on comparable scales. Momentum accumulates progress along the valley while cancelling the oscillation across it. Newton's method multiplies by the inverse Hessian, which transforms the ellipsoids back into spheres and converges in one step for an exact quadratic, at the cost of forming and inverting a $p \times p$ matrix. Adam and its relatives approximate that rescaling cheaply, per coordinate. Seeing the eigenvalue picture once explains the whole family of methods as variations on making the level sets rounder.

Multiple integration does not scale, which is why Monte Carlo exists

A deterministic quadrature rule with m points per dimension needs $m^n$ points in n dimensions, so a modest 10 points per axis needs $10^{20}$ evaluations in 20 dimensions, which is impossible. This is the curse of dimensionality for integration, and its consequence is that no amount of engineering rescues grid-based methods for the high-dimensional integrals that portfolio loss distributions, Bayesian posteriors, and derivative valuations require. Monte Carlo integration has error of order $1/\sqrt{N}$ regardless of dimension, because it depends on the variance of the integrand rather than on the geometry of a grid. That dimension independence, not accuracy, is why simulation dominates: at 20 dimensions a slowly converging method that ignores dimension beats a fast one that cannot be run at all. The probability lecture supplies the law of large numbers that makes the $1/\sqrt{N}$ rate a theorem.

Constrained and penalised problems are the same problem

Minimising $\|y - X\beta\|^2$ subject to $\|\beta\|^2 \le t$ and minimising $\|y - X\beta\|^2 + \lambda\|\beta\|^2$ without a constraint give the same solution path as t and $\lambda$ vary, with $\lambda$ the multiplier attached to the constraint. Ridge regression is therefore a constrained least-squares problem, and lasso is the same with the constraint region changed from a ball to a diamond, whose corners on the axes are exactly why lasso sets coefficients to zero and ridge does not. This correspondence is the reason a regularisation parameter has a dual reading as a budget, and it is worth carrying into machine learning, where the penalised form is standard and the geometric explanation lives on the constrained side.

Watch out Three traps. Existence of partial derivatives does not give differentiability, and the standard counterexample is not contrived, so check continuity of the partials before trusting a tangent-plane approximation. A stationary point with a singular Hessian is genuinely unclassified by the second derivative test, and reaching for a higher-order expansion is the correct response rather than guessing. And the Lagrange condition is necessary, not sufficient: it locates candidates, including maxima, minima, and saddle points on the constraint, and it fails entirely at points where $\nabla g = 0$, so the candidate list must always be evaluated rather than assumed.

  2. Show that $f(x,y) = xy/(x^2+y^2)$ with $f(0,0)=0$ has both partials at the origin but is not continuous there. State what this proves about the relationship between partials and differentiability.
  3. Prove that the gradient is perpendicular to the level set through a point, using the chain rule along a curve lying in the level set.
  4. Find and classify all stationary points of $f(x,y) = x^4 + y^4 - 4xy$.
  5. State Clairaut's theorem and explain why symmetry of the Hessian matters for the eigenvalue-based classification.
  6. Maximise $f(x,y) = xy$ subject to $x + y = 10$ by Lagrange multipliers. Interpret the multiplier by computing the optimal value as a function of the constraint level and differentiating.
  7. Repeat the two-asset minimum variance calculation with correlation $\rho = -0.5$ instead of $0.3$. Report the weights, the portfolio volatility, and explain the direction of the change.
  8. Evaluate $\iint_R e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dA$ over the whole plane by switching to polar coordinates, showing where the Jacobian factor enters.
  9. Derive the normalising constant of the standard normal density from the Gaussian integral, and say in one sentence why the two-dimensional trick was necessary.
