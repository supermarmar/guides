Single-variable calculus

Calculus is one idea applied twice. The idea is that a curve, examined closely enough, is indistinguishable from a straight line. Differentiation extracts the slope of that line and gives you rates of change; integration accumulates the line segments back into area, and the fundamental theorem says these two operations undo each other. Everything else in this lecture is technique for computing them and for knowing when the computation is legitimate.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The derivative of f at a is the limit of the average rate of change over shrinking intervals, $f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} [f(a+h) - f(a)]/h$, and it is the slope of the best straight-line approximation to f near a. The integral is the limit of sums of thin rectangles under the curve. The fundamental theorem of calculus says that accumulating a rate of change recovers the total change, so integration and differentiation are inverse operations.

Three results turn those definitions into a working subject. The mean value theorem says that over any interval, some interior point has instantaneous slope equal to the average slope, which is what converts local derivative information into global statements about the function. Taylor's theorem extends the straight-line approximation to a polynomial of any degree, with an explicit error term, which is where the practical accuracy comes from. And L'Hopital's rule resolves indeterminate limits by comparing derivatives, provided its hypotheses are actually checked.

Feynman test Take a curve, any curve without corners, and zoom in on one point. Zoom again. Zoom again. At every step the piece of curve in the window looks straighter than it did before, and if you keep going it becomes visually indistinguishable from a straight line. The derivative is nothing more than the slope of that line. This is why the derivative predicts what happens nearby and says nothing reliable about what happens far away: it is the slope of the line you would draw after zooming in, and the moment you zoom back out, the curve peels away from it. Everything in this lecture is either computing that slope, bounding how fast the curve peels away (Taylor's remainder), or reversing the process to get back from slopes to the curve.
What's beyond a first course A first course computes derivatives and integrals fluently and states the theorems. This lecture adds three things. The mean value theorem is proved from Rolle's theorem rather than asserted, because almost every later inequality in analysis is an application of it. Taylor's theorem is stated with the Lagrange remainder and then used quantitatively, to bound an approximation error rather than to write a series and stop. And the convergence of improper integrals is treated seriously, because whether an integral converges is exactly the question of whether a heavy-tailed distribution has a mean, which is a live question in risk modelling rather than an exercise.
Step 2

Why this matters

Calculus is the most reused mathematics in this repository, and the uses are specific rather than atmospheric. Maximum likelihood estimation in the statistics course sets a derivative to zero and checks a second derivative, so every estimator you fit is a stationary point. Gradient descent in machine learning is the derivative used as a direction of travel, iterated. The force of interest in financial mathematics is defined as the logarithmic derivative of an accumulation function, which is why continuous compounding produces exponentials rather than powers. Bond duration is a first derivative and convexity is a second derivative, so the standard price-change approximation used on every fixed income desk is a two-term Taylor expansion, error term included whether or not anyone mentions it.

Integration carries the probabilistic half. An expectation is an integral against a density, a distribution function is an integral of a density, and the moment generating function is an integral of an exponential weighting. When those integrals diverge, the corresponding quantity does not exist, which is not a technicality: a Pareto severity with tail index below one has no finite mean, so the loss-distribution approach used in operational risk capital produces an infinite expected loss unless the tail is truncated. Knowing which improper integrals converge is knowing which models are usable.

Step 3

The mental model

Two pictures. The first is local linearity, which is the derivative. The second is the area-so-far function, which is the fundamental theorem in one image.

Picture one: zoom in and the curve straightens

Picture two: the area-so-far function

Fix a function f and define $A(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt$, the signed area accumulated from a up to x. Push x a little further right by h, and the extra area is a thin strip of width h and height roughly $f(x)$, so $A(x+h) - A(x) \approx f(x)h$. Divide by h and let it shrink: $A'(x) = f(x)$. The rate at which accumulated area grows is the height of the curve, which is the fundamental theorem, and the picture is the proof with the epsilons removed.

Step 4

The detail

Limits and continuity, stated but not yet proved

Write $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ to mean that $f(x)$ can be forced arbitrarily close to L by taking x close enough to a, without requiring anything at a itself. The formal version, with $\epsilon$ and $\delta$, is deferred to lecture 4, where it is proved rather than assumed. Here the limit is used as a computational device, which is how the subject developed historically and how it is best learned.

f is continuous at a when $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$, which packs three requirements into one equation: the limit exists, the function is defined at a, and the two agree. Polynomials, exponentials, sines, and cosines are continuous everywhere; rational functions are continuous away from zeros of the denominator. Continuity is weaker than differentiability, and the gap between them is larger than intuition suggests, as the grandmaster section discusses.

The derivative and the rules

Derivative
$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$, when the limit exists. Equivalently, $f'(a)$ is the unique number m for which $f(a+h) - f(a) - mh$ shrinks faster than h. Notation varies by discipline: $f'$, $df/dx$, and $\dot{f}$ all mean the same object, and $D f$ appears in the operator notation used later.
Differentiating from first principles, twice. f(x) = x^2: [f(x+h) - f(x)] / h = [(x+h)^2 - x^2] / h = [x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - x^2] / h = (2xh + h^2) / h = 2x + h -> 2x as h -> 0. f(x) = 1/x (x nonzero): [f(x+h) - f(x)] / h = [1/(x+h) - 1/x] / h = [x - (x+h)] / [h x (x+h)] = -h / [h x (x+h)] = -1 / [x(x+h)] -> -1/x^2 as h -> 0. Both cases cancel the h before taking the limit. That cancellation is the entire technique: the difference quotient is 0/0 until the algebra removes the factor of h, and then the limit is just evaluation.

The chain rule deserves emphasis beyond its formula. Written as $\frac{dz}{dx} = \frac{dz}{dy}\frac{dy}{dx}$, it says that rates of change multiply along a chain of dependence, and that single fact is the whole of reverse-mode automatic differentiation. A neural network is a composition of simple functions, backpropagation applies the chain rule from the output backwards, and every gradient in machine learning is computed this way.

The mean value theorem, and why it carries the subject

Two results, the second following from the first, and between them they convert local derivative information into global conclusions.

Rolle's theorem. If f is continuous on [a,b], differentiable on (a,b), and f(a) = f(b), then f'(c) = 0 for some c in (a,b). Proof. f is continuous on a closed bounded interval, so it attains a maximum and a minimum there (the extreme value theorem, proved in lecture 4). If both occur at the endpoints, then since f(a) = f(b) the max and min are equal, so f is constant and f' = 0 everywhere inside. Otherwise an extremum occurs at an interior point c. At an interior maximum the difference quotient is <= 0 from the right and >= 0 from the left, and both one-sided limits equal f'(c), so f'(c) = 0. Same argument for a minimum. QED Mean value theorem. If f is continuous on [a,b] and differentiable on (a,b), then there is c in (a,b) with f'(c) = [f(b) - f(a)] / (b - a). Proof. Subtract off the chord. Define g(x) = f(x) - f(a) - [f(b) - f(a)]/(b - a) * (x - a). Then g is continuous on [a,b], differentiable on (a,b), and g(a) = g(b) = 0. Rolle gives c with g'(c) = 0, and g'(x) = f'(x) - [f(b)-f(a)]/(b-a), so f'(c) = [f(b) - f(a)]/(b - a). QED Read it geometrically: somewhere in the interval, the instantaneous slope equals the average slope over the whole interval.

Three consequences follow immediately and are used constantly. If $f' = 0$ throughout an interval then f is constant there, which is why antiderivatives are unique up to an additive constant. If $f' > 0$ throughout, f is strictly increasing, which is the standard monotonicity test. And if $|f'| \le M$ throughout, then $|f(b) - f(a)| \le M|b-a|$, a Lipschitz bound that turns a derivative bound into an error bound, which is the shape of most estimates in analysis and numerical work.

Taylor's theorem, used quantitatively

The tangent line is the degree-one approximation. Taylor's theorem supplies every higher degree, together with an explicit remainder:

$$ f(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k \; + \; \frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1} $$

for some $\xi$ between a and x. The remainder has the form of the next term with the derivative evaluated at an unknown intermediate point, which is exactly the mean value theorem generalised, and it is what turns Taylor from a formal series into a usable bound: if the $(n+1)$-th derivative is bounded by M on the interval, the error is at most $M|x-a|^{n+1}/(n+1)!$.

Taylor in practice: the duration and convexity approximation. A bond price P is a function of yield y. Expand around the current yield y0: P(y0 + dy) = P(y0) + P'(y0) dy + (1/2) P''(y0) dy^2 + remainder Divide by P(y0) and name the standard quantities: modified duration D = -P'(y0) / P(y0) convexity C = P''(y0) / P(y0) dP / P = -D dy + (1/2) C dy^2 + ... Numbers. Take P(y0) = 100, D = 6.5 years, C = 70, and a yield rise of 100bp (dy = 0.01): first order only: dP/P = -6.5 * 0.01 = -6.500% -> 93.50 with convexity: dP/P = -0.065 + 0.5*70*1e-4 = -6.150% -> 93.85 For a 25bp move (dy = 0.0025): first order only: -1.625% with convexity: -1.625% + 0.5*70*6.25e-6 = -1.603% Note the scaling. The convexity correction is quadratic in dy, so cutting the yield move by a factor of 4 cuts the correction by a factor of 16: from 35bp of price to 2bp. That is Taylor's remainder behaving exactly as the theorem says it must, and it is why first-order duration is adequate for small moves and dangerous for large ones.

L'Hopital's rule, with its hypotheses

If $f(a) = g(a) = 0$ (or both tend to infinity), f and g are differentiable near a, $g' \ne 0$ near a, and $\lim f'/g'$ exists, then $\lim f/g$ equals it. The hypotheses are not decoration. The rule applies only to the indeterminate forms $0/0$ and $\infty/\infty$; applying it to $1/2$-type limits produces nonsense. And it requires the limit of the ratio of derivatives to exist: for $f(x) = x + \sin x$ and $g(x) = x$ as $x \to \infty$, the ratio of derivatives oscillates forever while the original ratio converges to 1, so the rule says nothing and the correct answer must be found another way.

Integration: definition, theorem, and technique

The Riemann integral $\int_a^b f$ is the common limit of upper and lower sums over partitions as the partitions refine, when that common limit exists. Continuous functions on closed bounded intervals are always integrable, which is the practical statement; the careful treatment, including functions that are not integrable, waits for lecture 4 and, in a much more powerful form, for measure theory in lecture 7.

Fundamental theorem of calculus
Part one: if f is continuous and $A(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt$, then A is differentiable with $A' = f$. Part two, the evaluation form: if F is any antiderivative of a continuous f, then $\int_a^b f(t)\,dt = F(b) - F(a)$. Part one says accumulation and differentiation are inverse; part two turns that into a computational method, since finding one antiderivative settles every definite integral of that function.

Part two follows from part one and the mean value theorem in two lines. Both A and F are antiderivatives of f, so $(A - F)' = 0$, so $A - F$ is constant. Evaluate at a, where $A(a) = 0$, to identify the constant as $-F(a)$, then evaluate at b: $\int_a^b f = A(b) = F(b) - F(a)$.

Three techniques cover most integrals that can be done in closed form. Substitution reverses the chain rule: $\int f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx = \int f(u)\,du$ with $u = g(x)$. Integration by parts reverses the product rule: $\int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du$. Partial fractions splits a rational function into pieces with linear or irreducible quadratic denominators, each of which integrates to a logarithm or an arctangent.

Integration by parts, computing the mean of an exponential. X has density f(x) = lambda * exp(-lambda x) for x >= 0, lambda > 0. E[X] = integral from 0 to infinity of x * lambda * exp(-lambda x) dx Take u = x, dv = lambda exp(-lambda x) dx, so du = dx and v = -exp(-lambda x): E[X] = [ -x exp(-lambda x) ]_0^inf + integral_0^inf exp(-lambda x) dx First term: at 0 it is 0; as x -> inf, x exp(-lambda x) -> 0 because the exponential beats the linear factor. So the boundary term vanishes. E[X] = [ -exp(-lambda x) / lambda ]_0^inf = 0 - (-1/lambda) = 1/lambda. Sanity check: larger lambda means faster decay means shorter expected lifetime, and 1/lambda decreases in lambda. Correct. The same integration by parts with u = x^2 gives E[X^2] = 2/lambda^2, hence Var(X) = 2/lambda^2 - 1/lambda^2 = 1/lambda^2, so the exponential's standard deviation equals its mean. That single fact is why the exponential is the reference point for whether a loss distribution is heavy-tailed.

Improper integrals and when a mean fails to exist

An integral is improper when the interval is unbounded or the integrand blows up inside it, and its value is defined as a limit of proper integrals. The limit may fail to exist, and in probability that failure has a name: the quantity does not exist.

Which tails have a mean? The Pareto family. Density: f(x) = alpha * x_m^alpha / x^(alpha+1), for x >= x_m > 0, alpha > 0. E[X] = integral from x_m to infinity of x * f(x) dx = alpha * x_m^alpha * integral_{x_m}^inf x^(-alpha) dx The integral of x^(-alpha) from x_m to T is: alpha > 1: [x^(1-alpha) / (1-alpha)] evaluated -> converges, since 1 - alpha < 0 makes T^(1-alpha) -> 0. E[X] = alpha * x_m / (alpha - 1), finite. alpha = 1: the integral is log(T) - log(x_m) -> infinity. No mean. alpha < 1: T^(1-alpha) -> infinity. No mean. Second moments need alpha > 2 by the same computation with x^2. Consequence for practice. Operational risk severities are often fitted with Pareto or lognormal tails. A fitted alpha below 1 means the model has an infinite expected loss, so any reported "expected loss" figure is an artefact of truncating the integral at the largest observed loss rather than a property of the fitted model. The convergence question is the model validation question.

Two convergence tests handle most cases. Comparison: if $0 \le f \le g$ and $\int g$ converges, so does $\int f$. Limit comparison: if $f(x)/g(x)$ tends to a finite positive limit, the two integrals converge or diverge together, which reduces most tail questions to comparison with a power $x^{-p}$, convergent at infinity exactly when $p > 1$.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Continuity does not imply differentiability, and the gap is enormous

Differentiability implies continuity, by a one-line argument: if the difference quotient converges then the numerator must go to zero. The converse fails at any corner, which is unsurprising, but the scale of the failure is not. Weierstrass constructed a function continuous everywhere and differentiable nowhere, built as an infinite sum of increasingly rapid oscillations, and in a precise sense such functions are typical rather than pathological. The sample paths of Brownian motion are continuous and nowhere differentiable with probability one, which is exactly why stochastic calculus exists: you cannot differentiate a price path, so the entire apparatus of Ito integration is built to do what ordinary calculus cannot. Meeting the fact here, in one sentence, makes that later course less mysterious.

The exponential is the fixed point of differentiation

Ask which function is its own derivative and the answer is $Ce^x$ and nothing else. That single property explains the ubiquity of the exponential across every applied track. Continuous compounding gives $e^{rT}$ because the force of interest is defined as the rate of proportional growth, and the equation "growth rate proportional to current level" has exponential solutions and no others. Exponential decay of a survival function corresponds to a constant hazard rate for the same reason. The normal density is $e^{-x^2/2}$ because it is the distribution maximising entropy for a given variance, and the maximisation is solved by an exponential of the constraint. Whenever an exponential shows up unexpectedly, look for a differential equation saying that a rate is proportional to a level, because it will be there.

Analytic and numerical derivatives disagree, and the error has two halves

Given a black-box function, the obvious numerical derivative is the forward difference $[f(x+h) - f(x)]/h$. Taylor's theorem says its truncation error is proportional to h, so smaller steps are better. Floating-point arithmetic says the opposite: subtracting two nearly equal numbers loses precision, and the rounding error is proportional to $\epsilon/h$ where $\epsilon$ is machine epsilon. Adding the two error terms and minimising gives an optimal step size of order $\sqrt{\epsilon}$, roughly $10^{-8}$ in double precision, and using a much smaller h makes the answer worse. The central difference $[f(x+h) - f(x-h)]/2h$ has truncation error of order $h^2$ and an optimal step of order $\epsilon^{1/3}$. This is why finite-difference Greeks are noisy near expiry, and why automatic differentiation, which applies the chain rule exactly rather than approximating it, is preferred wherever the source code is available.

Watch out Two habits worth breaking early. The first is treating a Taylor expansion as an equality rather than an approximation with a remainder: the duration-and-convexity formula is exact only in the limit, and the error grows cubically in the yield move, which is precisely when a large move makes the answer matter. The second is applying L'Hopital's rule without checking that the form is genuinely indeterminate, or iterating it until something looks clean. Each application requires the hypotheses to hold afresh, and a ratio that stops being $0/0$ after one round must be evaluated directly rather than differentiated again.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Differentiate $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ from first principles, showing the algebraic step that removes the h from the denominator.
  2. Prove that differentiability at a implies continuity at a, then give a function continuous but not differentiable at a point and say precisely which limit fails.
  3. State and prove Rolle's theorem, then derive the mean value theorem from it by subtracting the chord.
  4. Use the mean value theorem to prove that if $f' = 0$ on an interval then f is constant there. Explain why this is what makes "the" antiderivative well defined up to a constant.
  5. Write the second-order Taylor expansion of $\ln(1+x)$ about 0 with the Lagrange remainder, and bound the error at $x = 0.1$.
  6. A bond has price 100, modified duration 4.2, and convexity 32. Estimate the price after a 150bp yield rise, with and without the convexity term, and state the ratio of the two corrections at 150bp versus 50bp.
  7. Evaluate $\lim_{x \to 0} (\sin x - x)/x^3$ twice: once by L'Hopital, once by Taylor expansion. Confirm the answers agree and say which method you trust more, and why.
  8. Prove the evaluation form of the fundamental theorem from the accumulation form, stating where you use the mean value theorem.
  9. Compute $\int_0^\infty x^2 \lambda e^{-\lambda x}\,dx$ by parts and hence find the variance of an exponential random variable.
  10. For which $\alpha$ does a Pareto distribution with tail index $\alpha$ have a finite mean, and for which a finite variance? Explain what a fitted $\alpha$ of 0.8 implies about a reported expected-loss figure from that model.
