Complex analysis

Requiring a derivative to exist in the complex plane looks like a small change from the real case and is not. Because the limit must give the same answer from every direction at once, complex differentiability at every point of a region forces the function to be infinitely differentiable, equal to its Taylor series, determined everywhere by its values on any small arc, and completely pinned down by its behaviour on the boundary of a region. Nothing in real analysis is remotely this rigid, and the rigidity is what makes the subject useful: it turns hard real integrals into simple algebra at a handful of points.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A function is holomorphic on a region when the limit $\lim_{h \to 0} [f(z+h) - f(z)]/h$ exists there, with h complex, so the limit must agree along every direction of approach. Writing $f = u + iv$, that requirement is equivalent to the Cauchy-Riemann equations $u_x = v_y$ and $u_y = -v_x$, a pair of partial differential equations linking the real and imaginary parts.

Three theorems follow, each stronger than anything true of real functions. Cauchy's theorem: the integral of a holomorphic function round any closed contour in a simply connected region is zero. Cauchy's integral formula: the value at any interior point equals a contour integral of the boundary values, so boundary data determines the interior completely. And every holomorphic function is analytic, meaning it equals its own Taylor series on any disc inside the region. Where a function misbehaves at isolated points, the Laurent series adds negative powers, the coefficient of $1/(z-a)$ is the residue, and the residue theorem says a contour integral is just $2\pi i$ times the sum of the residues enclosed.

Feynman test A real differentiable function has one direction to worry about: the slope from the left must match the slope from the right. A complex differentiable function has infinitely many directions, and the same complex number must serve as the derivative along all of them simultaneously. That is a colossal constraint. It forces the function, locally, to act as multiplication by a fixed complex number, which geometrically means a rotation together with a uniform scaling, the same in every direction. So an infinitesimal square must map to an infinitesimal square, turned and resized but never sheared. Almost no function can do this, and the ones that can are so tightly constrained that knowing them on a tiny arc determines them everywhere.
What's beyond a first course A first course derives the Cauchy-Riemann equations, states Cauchy's theorem, and computes residues. This lecture adds the payoff calculations that justify the machinery for anyone in this repository: the characteristic function of the Cauchy distribution computed by residues, which explains in one line why that distribution has no mean and why averaging its samples achieves nothing; and the connection to transform methods in option pricing, where a price is recovered from a characteristic function by contour integration. It also states the identity theorem and analytic continuation explicitly, because the rigidity they express has no analogue in real analysis and is the deepest structural fact in the lecture.
Step 2

Why this matters

Transform methods are the reason. The characteristic function of a random variable, $\varphi(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{itX}]$, is a complex-valued object, and the statistics course uses it because it converts convolution into multiplication: the characteristic function of a sum of independent variables is the product of theirs. Recovering the distribution from the transform is an inversion, and inversion formulas are contour integrals evaluated by the residue theorem. The proof of the central limit theorem via characteristic functions rests on complex analytic facts about those functions near the origin.

The same machinery is operational in derivative pricing. For models where the characteristic function of the log price is known in closed form while the density is not, which includes the Heston stochastic volatility model and the whole family of exponential Levy models, the standard pricing route is to write the option value as a Fourier integral of the characteristic function and evaluate it numerically. The Carr-Madan formulation used across financial engineering is exactly this, and its damping parameter exists to move a contour into a strip where the integral converges, which is a complex-analytic manoeuvre and not a numerical trick. In actuarial work, transforms of aggregate loss distributions play the same role, and the inversion has the same shape.

Beyond transforms, the residue theorem evaluates real integrals that resist every real method, which is worth having when a moment or a normalising constant is needed in closed form. And the rigidity results have a modelling consequence worth internalising: an analytic function is determined by its values on any set with a limit point, so two analytic models agreeing on a small region agree everywhere. Extrapolation, which is unsafe for general functions, is forced for analytic ones.

Step 4

The detail

Complex differentiability and the Cauchy-Riemann equations

Write $z = x + iy$ and $f(z) = u(x,y) + i\,v(x,y)$. Requiring that $\lim_{h\to 0}[f(z+h)-f(z)]/h$ be the same for every route to zero, and comparing the two easiest routes, produces the equations.

Deriving Cauchy-Riemann from two directions of approach. Approach along the real axis, h = t real: f'(z) = lim [u(x+t,y) + i v(x+t,y) - u(x,y) - i v(x,y)] / t = u_x + i v_x. Approach along the imaginary axis, h = i s with s real: f'(z) = lim [u(x,y+s) + i v(x,y+s) - u(x,y) - i v(x,y)] / (i s) = (1/i)(u_y + i v_y) = v_y - i u_y. For the derivative to exist, these must agree: u_x = v_y and v_x = -u_y. (Cauchy-Riemann) Consequence: differentiate the first in x and the second in y and add: u_xx + u_yy = 0, and similarly v_xx + v_yy = 0. Both parts are harmonic. Holomorphic functions are pairs of harmonic functions locked together, which is why complex analysis solves plane potential problems. CHECKS. f(z) = z^2 = (x^2 - y^2) + i(2xy): u_x = 2x, v_y = 2x agree. u_y = -2y, v_x = 2y so v_x = -u_y. Holomorphic everywhere. f(z) = conjugate of z = x - iy: u_x = 1, v_y = -1 disagree. Nowhere holomorphic, despite being as smooth as any real map of the plane could be. f(z) = |z|^2 = x^2 + y^2, so u = x^2 + y^2, v = 0: u_x = 2x, v_y = 0 require x = 0; u_y = 2y, -v_x = 0 require y = 0. Differentiable at the origin only, and holomorphic nowhere, since holomorphy needs differentiability on an open set.

Contour integration and Cauchy's theorem

A contour integral $\int_\gamma f(z)\,dz$ is defined by parametrising the curve and integrating $f(\gamma(t))\gamma'(t)$, exactly as for line integrals in lecture 3. The ML inequality bounds it: $\left|\int_\gamma f\right| \le M L$ where M bounds $|f|$ on the contour and L is its length. Crude as it looks, this is the workhorse estimate for showing that arcs of large circles contribute nothing in the limit, which is how real integrals are evaluated later.

Cauchy's theorem
If $f$ is holomorphic on a simply connected region, then $\oint_\gamma f(z)\,dz = 0$ for every closed contour $\gamma$ in it. The hypothesis of simple connectivity is doing real work, exactly as it did in vector calculus: the region must have no holes for the contour to be contractible.

The single most important computation in the subject is the integral of $1/z$ round the unit circle. Parametrise $z = e^{i\theta}$, so $dz = ie^{i\theta}d\theta$ and $1/z = e^{-i\theta}$, giving $\int_0^{2\pi} i\,d\theta = 2\pi i$. This is non-zero, and it must be, because $1/z$ fails to be holomorphic at the origin, which sits inside the loop. Every residue calculation is ultimately this one integral, moved around and rescaled.

The integral formula and its consequences

If $f$ is holomorphic inside and on a simple closed contour $\gamma$, and a lies inside, then

$$ f(a) = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_\gamma \frac{f(z)}{z-a}\,dz, \qquad f^{(n)}(a) = \frac{n!}{2\pi i} \oint_\gamma \frac{f(z)}{(z-a)^{n+1}}\,dz. $$

The first formula says boundary values determine interior values. The second says the same for every derivative, and it is where the rigidity comes from: since the right side makes sense for all n, a function differentiable once is automatically differentiable infinitely often, which is flatly false in real analysis where $f(x) = x|x|$ is differentiable once and not twice.

Laurent series, singularities, and residues

Around an isolated singularity a, a function has a Laurent series $\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_n (z-a)^n$, valid in an annulus. The negative-power part, called the principal part, classifies the singularity. If there are no negative terms the singularity is removable. If there are finitely many, ending at $(z-a)^{-m}$, it is a pole of order m. If there are infinitely many it is essential, and the function's behaviour there is wild: near an essential singularity, a holomorphic function comes arbitrarily close to every complex value in any neighbourhood, which is Picard's theorem.

Residue
$\operatorname{Res}(f,a) = c_{-1}$, the coefficient of $(z-a)^{-1}$ in the Laurent series. For a simple pole, $\operatorname{Res}(f,a) = \lim_{z\to a}(z-a)f(z)$; for a pole of order m, differentiate $(z-a)^m f(z)$ exactly $m-1$ times and divide by $(m-1)!$ before taking the limit. When $f = p/q$ with a simple zero of $q$ at a, the residue is $p(a)/q'(a)$, which is the fastest route in practice.

The residue theorem then states that $\oint_\gamma f = 2\pi i \sum \operatorname{Res}(f, a_k)$, summing over singularities enclosed by $\gamma$. Its proof is the deformation picture: shrink the contour onto small circles round each singularity, and on each circle only the $c_{-1}$ term survives, contributing $2\pi i c_{-1}$, since every other power integrates to zero round a closed loop.

Residues at work, twice. (1) A real integral with no elementary route. I = integral over all reals of 1 / (1 + x^2) dx. Consider f(z) = 1/(1+z^2) = 1/((z-i)(z+i)) on a contour made of the segment [-R, R] and the upper semicircle of radius R. Poles: z = i (inside, for R > 1) and z = -i (outside, below the axis). Residue at i: lim (z - i) f(z) = 1/(i + i) = 1/(2i). Residue theorem: contour integral = 2 pi i * 1/(2i) = pi. The arc contributes nothing as R grows: on it |1 + z^2| >= R^2 - 1, so by the ML inequality the arc integral is at most (pi R)/(R^2 - 1) -> 0. So the real integral is pi. (Check: the antiderivative is arctan, and arctan(inf) - arctan(-inf) = pi/2 + pi/2 = pi. Agreed.) (2) The characteristic function of the Cauchy distribution. Density: f(x) = 1 / (pi (1 + x^2)). Characteristic function, for t > 0: phi(t) = integral over reals of exp(itx) / (pi (1 + x^2)) dx. Close the contour in the UPPER half plane, where |exp(itz)| = exp(-t*Im z) decays for t > 0, so the arc again vanishes (Jordan's lemma is the careful version of this step). Only the pole at z = i is enclosed. Residue of exp(itz)/(pi(1+z^2)) at z = i = exp(it * i) / (pi * 2i) = exp(-t) / (2 pi i). phi(t) = 2 pi i * exp(-t)/(2 pi i) = exp(-t). By symmetry for t < 0, phi(t) = exp(-|t|). WHY THIS MATTERS. phi(t) = exp(-|t|) is not differentiable at t = 0, and the first derivative of the characteristic function at 0 is what supplies the mean. No derivative, no mean, which is the analytic explanation of a fact usually stated as an oddity. Worse, phi is multiplicative over independent sums, so the average of n independent Cauchy variables has characteristic function [exp(-|t|/n)]^n = exp(-|t|), the same as a single observation. Averaging Cauchy data achieves precisely nothing: the sample mean of a thousand observations is distributed exactly like one observation. The law of large numbers fails, and this three-line contour integral is the cleanest available proof of it.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Rigidity, and why extrapolation is safe here and nowhere else

The identity theorem says that if two functions holomorphic on a connected region agree on any set with a limit point, a tiny arc or even a convergent sequence of points, they agree on the whole region. There is no real analogue: smooth real functions can agree on an interval and diverge outside it, as bump functions demonstrate. The practical reading is that an analytic function has no freedom left once a small amount of it is known, which is what makes analytic continuation possible: a function defined by a series on a small disc can be extended, uniquely, to the largest region where an extension exists. This is how the Riemann zeta function acquires values at points where its defining series diverges, and how transform methods in pricing move a contour into a strip where an integral converges while representing the same function. When a Fourier pricing formula introduces a damping parameter, it is exercising this freedom, and the answer does not depend on the choice precisely because the continuation is unique.

Branch cuts are the price of multivaluedness

The complex logarithm cannot be defined continuously on the whole punctured plane: going once round the origin increases the argument by $2\pi$, so $\log$ returns to a different value than it started with. The standard remedy is a branch cut, a curve removed from the domain to prevent encircling the singularity, which restores single-valuedness at the cost of a line of discontinuity. This is the same $2\pi$ discovered in the punctured-plane example of vector calculus, and it is not an artefact of notation: it is the topology of the domain, showing up in whatever language is being used. The proper resolution, a Riemann surface on which the function genuinely is single valued, belongs to a later course, and the practical warning belongs here: numerical implementations of complex logarithms and powers have discontinuities across their cuts, and characteristic-function pricing implementations are a known place where crossing a cut silently produces wrong prices.

What complex analysis contributes to the rest of this repository

Three things, in decreasing order of directness. Transform inversion, as set out in step 2, which is the operational core of Fourier-based option pricing and of characteristic-function methods in statistics and actuarial aggregate-loss work. Asymptotic methods, where the saddle point technique evaluates integrals with a large parameter by deforming the contour to pass through a stationary point of the exponent, and which underlies Laplace approximations to posteriors and large-deviation estimates of tail probabilities. And the structural results, which explain facts otherwise memorised: why the Cauchy distribution defeats averaging, why moment generating functions may fail to exist while characteristic functions always do (the latter integrate a bounded function, the former need not converge), and why a distribution is determined by its characteristic function at all, which is an inversion theorem in disguise.

Watch out Two errors specific to this subject. The first is applying Cauchy's theorem without checking that the function is holomorphic throughout the enclosed region rather than merely on the contour; a single pole inside changes the answer from zero to $2\pi i$ times a residue, and the arithmetic gives no warning. The second is closing a contour in the wrong half plane. The choice is dictated by where the integrand decays: for $e^{itz}$ with $t > 0$ the decay is in the upper half plane and for $t < 0$ in the lower, so the sign of t determines which poles are captured. Getting this backwards produces an answer with the wrong sign or the wrong poles, and it is the commonest mistake in characteristic-function computations.

Test yourself

  1. Derive the Cauchy-Riemann equations by comparing the derivative along the real and imaginary directions, then use them to show that $u$ and $v$ are both harmonic.
  2. Determine where each of $f(z) = z^3$, $f(z) = \bar{z}$, $f(z) = e^z$, and $f(z) = |z|^2$ is holomorphic, justifying each answer with the equations.
  3. Compute $\oint 1/z\,dz$ round the unit circle directly from the parametrisation, and explain why the answer is not zero without contradicting Cauchy's theorem.
  4. State the Cauchy integral formula for derivatives and use it to prove that a holomorphic function is infinitely differentiable. Contrast with a real function differentiable exactly once.
  5. Prove Liouville's theorem from the derivative formula and the ML inequality, then deduce the fundamental theorem of algebra.
  6. Classify the singularities of $\dfrac{\sin z}{z}$, $\dfrac{1}{(z-2)^3}$, and $e^{1/z}$, and give the residue where one is defined.
  7. Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{dx}{1+x^4}$ by residues, listing which poles lie in the upper half plane and showing the arc contribution vanishes.
  8. Compute the characteristic function of the Cauchy distribution by contour integration, taking care over the choice of half plane for each sign of t.
  9. Using that characteristic function, show that the mean of n independent Cauchy variables has the same distribution as one of them, and state which hypothesis of the law of large numbers fails.
  10. Explain what a branch cut is, why the complex logarithm needs one, and how this relates to the value $2\pi$ obtained from the punctured-plane field in the vector calculus lecture.
