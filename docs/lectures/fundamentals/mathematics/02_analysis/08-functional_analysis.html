Functional analysis

Take linear algebra, let the vector spaces be infinite dimensional, and add enough topology that limits make sense. What emerges is the natural home for spaces whose points are functions, and the surprise is how much of the finite-dimensional intuition survives: there is still an inner product, still orthogonality, still projection onto a subspace, still a spectral decomposition. Three things that look unrelated turn out to be the same projection: least squares regression, conditional expectation, and the best approximation of a function by a truncated Fourier series.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A normed space is a vector space with a length function; a Banach space is one that is complete, so limits of Cauchy sequences stay inside; a Hilbert space is a Banach space whose norm comes from an inner product, so angles and orthogonality exist. The hierarchy matters because each level buys specific theorems, and $L^2$ sits at the top.

The central result is the projection theorem: in a Hilbert space, every point has a unique nearest point in any closed subspace, and the error is orthogonal to that subspace. Everything else in the lecture is either an application of that theorem or a statement about operators. Riesz representation identifies every continuous linear functional with an inner product against a fixed vector. The spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators generalises diagonalisation, giving an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors with eigenvalues tending to zero, which is principal component analysis in infinite dimensions. Four structural theorems (Hahn-Banach, uniform boundedness, open mapping, closed graph) hold the subject together.

Feynman test You are standing off a flat floor and want the nearest point on it. Drop a perpendicular: the foot of that perpendicular is the answer, and the line from you to it makes a right angle with every direction you could move within the floor. That is the entire projection theorem, and it does not care that the floor is a plane in three dimensions. Replace the floor by the set of all functions of a certain kind, and replace "distance" by the square-integral distance, and the same perpendicular argument gives the best least-squares fit, the conditional expectation, and the optimal truncation of a Fourier series. Three different-sounding problems become one: drop a perpendicular onto a closed subspace.
What's beyond a first course Scope is set, as in the previous lecture, by what other tracks consume. The projection theorem is developed in full and then applied three times, to least squares, to conditional expectation, and to Fourier truncation, because seeing them as one construction is the main thing this lecture has to offer a modelling audience. The dual representation of coherent risk measures is included because it explains what expected shortfall actually is, and reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces are named because kernel methods rest on them. Unbounded operators, distributions, and most of Banach space geometry are omitted.
Step 2

Why this matters

Conditional expectation is a projection, and this is the single most useful identification in the lecture. In $L^2$, $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ is the orthogonal projection of X onto the subspace of $\mathcal{G}$-measurable square-integrable variables, so it is the best mean-square predictor of X given the information in $\mathcal{G}$. Every property that looks like a rule to memorise becomes geometry: the tower property is the statement that projecting twice onto nested subspaces equals projecting once onto the smaller, and taking out what is known is the statement that the projection is linear over the subspace. Linear regression is the same projection with $\mathcal{G}$ replaced by the span of the regressors, which is why the normal equations say the residual is orthogonal to every predictor.

Principal component analysis is the spectral theorem. A covariance operator is self-adjoint and positive, so it has an orthonormal eigenbasis with non-negative eigenvalues; the leading eigenvectors are the principal components and the eigenvalues are the variances explained. In finite dimensions this is a symmetric matrix, and in function space it is the Karhunen-Loeve expansion, which is how a whole yield curve is reduced to level, slope, and curvature factors. The same decomposition underlies the factor models used in credit portfolio work.

Risk measures have a dual representation, and it is the most direct application of this lecture to banking. Artzner and coauthors showed that a risk measure satisfying four natural axioms can be written as a supremum of expected losses over a set of probability measures, a statement in the dual space. Expected shortfall is exactly this with the set being all measures whose density is bounded by $1/\alpha$, which is why expected shortfall is coherent and value at risk is not. That result is a theorem about duality, and it is the reason the Basel framework moved from value at risk to expected shortfall for market risk capital.

Step 4

The detail

Normed and Banach spaces

A norm satisfies $\|x\| = 0$ only for $x = 0$, $\|\lambda x\| = |\lambda|\|x\|$, and the triangle inequality. It induces the metric $d(x,y) = \|x-y\|$, so all of lecture 5 applies. In finite dimensions all norms are equivalent, meaning each is bounded by a constant multiple of any other, so convergence does not depend on the choice; in infinite dimensions this fails, and the choice of norm is a modelling decision that changes which sequences converge.

A linear map between normed spaces is continuous if and only if it is bounded, meaning $\|Tx\| \le C\|x\|$ for some constant, and the least such constant is the operator norm $\|T\|$. The equivalence of continuity and boundedness is special to linear maps and is proved in three lines; it means that checking a single inequality settles a topological question. The dual space $X^*$ is the space of bounded linear functionals $X \to \mathbb{R}$, itself a Banach space under the operator norm.

TheoremStatementTypical use
Hahn-BanachA bounded functional on a subspace extends to the whole space with the same normGuarantees the dual is rich enough to separate points; underlies duality arguments including risk measure representation
Uniform boundednessA family of operators bounded pointwise is bounded in normConverts pointwise control into uniform control, for example in proving convergence of quadrature or approximation schemes
Open mappingA surjective bounded operator between Banach spaces maps open sets to open setsGives the bounded inverse theorem: a bijective bounded operator has bounded inverse, so well-posedness follows from solvability
Closed graphA linear map between Banach spaces with closed graph is boundedThe practical route to proving continuity: verify a limit condition rather than an inequality

All four require completeness, and three of them rest on the Baire category theorem. That dependence is the reason Banach spaces, rather than merely normed spaces, are the working setting.

Hilbert spaces

An inner product $\langle x, y\rangle$ is linear in the first argument, symmetric, and positive definite, and it induces the norm $\|x\| = \sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle}$. Cauchy-Schwarz, $|\langle x,y\rangle| \le \|x\|\|y\|$, is what makes the induced norm satisfy the triangle inequality, and in probability it is the statement that a correlation lies between $-1$ and 1. A norm arises from an inner product exactly when it satisfies the parallelogram law $\|x+y\|^2 + \|x-y\|^2 = 2\|x\|^2 + 2\|y\|^2$, which is how $L^2$ is distinguished from the other $L^p$ spaces.

Projection theorem
Let M be a closed subspace of a Hilbert space H and $x \in H$. There is a unique $Px \in M$ minimising $\|x - m\|$ over $m \in M$, and it is characterised by orthogonality: $\langle x - Px, m\rangle = 0$ for every $m \in M$. Moreover $H = M \oplus M^\perp$, every vector splitting uniquely into a component in M and one orthogonal to it.

Closedness is essential. The polynomials sit inside $L^2[0,1]$ as a subspace that is not closed, and there is no nearest polynomial to a general function, only a sequence of polynomials approaching it. Completeness of the ambient space, supplied by Riesz-Fischer in lecture 7, is what makes the minimising sequence converge to an actual minimiser.

The projection theorem, applied three times. (1) LEAST SQUARES. H = R^n with the usual inner product. M = column space of the design matrix X, a closed subspace since it is finite dimensional. Project the response y: orthogonality: <y - X b, X c> = 0 for every coefficient vector c (X' (y - X b)) . c = 0 for every c X'(y - X b) = 0 X'X b = X'y. The normal equations. The residual is orthogonal to every column of X, which is why residuals are uncorrelated with fitted values, and why adding a regressor cannot increase the residual sum of squares: enlarging M can only move Px closer to x. (2) CONDITIONAL EXPECTATION. H = L^2(Omega, F, P). M = L^2(Omega, G, P) for a sub-sigma-algebra G, a closed subspace. The projection of X onto M is E[X | G]: orthogonality: E[ (X - E[X|G]) Z ] = 0 for every G-measurable Z in L^2, which, taking Z the indicator of a set A in G, is exactly the defining property integral over A of X = integral over A of E[X|G]. Geometry now explains the algebra: - Tower property, E[ E[X|G] | H ] = E[X|H] for H inside G: projecting onto a subspace and then onto a smaller one equals projecting onto the smaller one directly. - E[X|G] minimises E[(X - Z)^2] over G-measurable Z: conditional expectation IS the best mean-square predictor, by definition of projection rather than by a separate calculation. - Var(X) = Var(E[X|G]) + E[Var(X|G)]: this is Pythagoras. The total squared length splits into the projection and the residual, which is the variance decomposition used in every ANOVA table and in credit portfolio systematic-versus-idiosyncratic risk splits. (3) FOURIER TRUNCATION. H = L^2[-pi, pi]. M = span of the first 2N+1 trigonometric functions. The projection is the truncated Fourier series, and its coefficients c_k = <f, e_k> / <e_k, e_k> are exactly the classical Fourier coefficient formulas. So the truncated series is not merely A approximation, it is THE best mean-square approximation of that order, which is the content of the statement that the trigonometric system is an orthonormal basis. Bessel's inequality: sum |<f, e_k>|^2 <= ||f||^2 for any orthonormal set. Parseval's identity: equality holds exactly when the set is a basis. Both are Pythagoras again, with countably many terms.

Riesz representation and duality

The Riesz representation theorem states that every bounded linear functional $\varphi$ on a Hilbert space is $\varphi(x) = \langle x, y\rangle$ for a unique y, with $\|\varphi\| = \|y\|$. A Hilbert space is therefore isomorphic to its own dual, which is why Hilbert space arguments feel like linear algebra: there is no distinction to keep track of between vectors and functionals. In general Banach spaces the dual is a genuinely different object, and the $L^p$ duality $\left(L^p\right)^* = L^q$ with $1/p + 1/q = 1$ is the standard example, failing only at $p = \infty$.

Duality in banking: why expected shortfall is coherent. A risk measure rho assigns a number to a loss random variable. Artzner, Delbaen, Eber and Heath call it COHERENT if it satisfies four axioms: monotonicity X <= Y implies rho(X) <= rho(Y) translation rho(X + c) = rho(X) + c positive homog. rho(t X) = t rho(X) for t >= 0 subadditivity rho(X + Y) <= rho(X) + rho(Y) (diversification helps) REPRESENTATION THEOREM. On a suitable space, rho is coherent if and only if there is a set Q of probability measures with rho(X) = sup over Q in Q of E_Q[X]. The risk measure is a supremum of expectations over a family of scenarios, which is a statement in the DUAL space: each measure Q acts as a linear functional X -> E_Q[X], and rho is the upper envelope of a set of them. Hahn-Banach is what supplies the separating functionals in the proof. EXPECTED SHORTFALL. Take Q to be all measures absolutely continuous with respect to P whose density satisfies dQ/dP <= 1/alpha. Then sup over that set of E_Q[X] = ES_alpha(X), the average loss in the worst alpha tail. So expected shortfall is coherent by construction, and the family of measures is exactly the set of ways of reweighting probability that put at most 1/alpha times the original mass on any outcome. VALUE AT RISK. VaR is not subadditive: two loans each with a 3% default probability can each have a 95% VaR of zero while the portfolio of both has a positive 95% VaR, because the probability that at least one defaults exceeds 5%. VaR therefore admits no such representation, and the failure is not a technicality: it means VaR can penalise diversification. This is the argument behind the Basel move from VaR to expected shortfall in the market risk framework, and it is a theorem in functional analysis.

Operators and the spectral theorem

The adjoint $T^*$ satisfies $\langle Tx, y\rangle = \langle x, T^*y\rangle$; an operator is self-adjoint when $T = T^*$, the infinite-dimensional version of a symmetric matrix. An operator is compact when it maps bounded sets to sets with compact closure, which is the substitute for finite rank and the repair to the failure of compactness noted in lecture 5.

Spectral theorem, compact self-adjoint case
A compact self-adjoint operator on a Hilbert space has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, with real eigenvalues that can be ordered by decreasing modulus and tend to zero. Hence $T x = \sum_k \lambda_k \langle x, e_k\rangle e_k$: the operator acts by decomposing along the basis, scaling each coordinate, and reassembling. This is diagonalisation, with a countable basis.

The applications are immediate. A covariance operator is self-adjoint, positive, and compact under mild conditions, so it decomposes exactly this way: the eigenvectors are the principal components, the eigenvalues are the variances along them, and truncating after k terms gives the best rank-k approximation in mean square, which is the projection theorem again. For a stochastic process this is the Karhunen-Loeve expansion, which represents a random function as a series of deterministic eigenfunctions with uncorrelated random coefficients, and which is how a yield curve is decomposed into level, slope, and curvature. The decay of the eigenvalues is the reason three factors typically suffice.

A reproducing kernel Hilbert space is a Hilbert space of functions in which pointwise evaluation is a bounded functional, so by Riesz each evaluation is an inner product against some function $k_x$, and $k(x,y) = \langle k_x, k_y\rangle$ is the kernel. The representer theorem then says that the solution of a regularised fitting problem over such a space is a finite linear combination of kernels at the observed data points, which is why kernel ridge regression, support vector machines, and Gaussian process regression reduce to finite computations despite optimising over an infinite-dimensional space. That is the mathematical content of the kernel trick used throughout machine learning.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Weak convergence recovers the compactness that was lost

Lecture 5 showed the closed unit ball of an infinite-dimensional space is not compact, which threatens every existence proof that works by extracting a convergent subsequence. The repair is to weaken the notion of convergence: $x_n \to x$ weakly when $\varphi(x_n) \to \varphi(x)$ for every bounded linear functional $\varphi$. Under this weaker notion, the Banach-Alaoglu theorem says the closed unit ball of a dual space is compact, and in a Hilbert space every bounded sequence has a weakly convergent subsequence. Existence proofs then run as before: take a minimising sequence, extract a weakly convergent subsequence, and show the limit is a minimiser, which needs the objective to be lower semicontinuous rather than continuous. This pattern, the direct method in the calculus of variations, is how existence is proved for optimal controls, optimal stopping problems, and variational formulations of partial differential equations. Weak convergence in this sense is also the same object as convergence in distribution in probability, which is why the statistics course calls that mode weak convergence.

Why squared error dominates, and what is lost by it

Squared error is not chosen because errors are genuinely quadratic in cost. It is chosen because $L^2$ is the only $L^p$ space with an inner product, and therefore the only one where the projection theorem gives a unique nearest point characterised by orthogonality, computable by solving linear equations. Least squares, conditional expectation, principal components, Kalman filtering, and Fourier analysis all live in $L^2$ for this reason. The cost is a known one: squared error weights large deviations heavily, so estimates are sensitive to outliers and to tail assumptions. Absolute-error methods live in $L^1$, which has no inner product, so quantile regression and median estimation have no closed-form projection and require linear programming instead. Recognising that the choice of norm is a modelling decision with a computational price attached, rather than a default, is the practical lesson of the hierarchy in the first figure.

Where this lecture sits relative to the rest of the repository

Three specific handovers. To lecture 9, where conditional expectation is defined for integrable rather than square-integrable variables, using Radon-Nikodym instead of projection, with the $L^2$ picture developed here as the geometric intuition behind the general definition. To financial engineering, where the space of attainable payoffs is a subspace, market completeness is the statement that it is the whole space, and pricing an unattainable claim by mean-square hedging is a projection onto it. And to machine learning, through kernels, principal components, and the general fact that regularised fitting is a projection onto a constrained set, which is the constrained-versus-penalised correspondence from lecture 2 in its infinite-dimensional form.

Watch out Two errors. Projecting onto a subspace that is not closed: the polynomials in $L^2$ are the standard example, and there is no nearest element, only an infimum that is not attained. The fix is either to close the subspace or to accept an approximating sequence. And assuming a self-adjoint operator has eigenvalues at all: without compactness it may have purely continuous spectrum, as the multiplication operator on $L^2[0,1]$ does, so the spectral theorem in the form stated here does not apply and the general version requires spectral measures.

  1. Prove that a linear map between normed spaces is continuous if and only if it is bounded, and give the operator norm as an explicit supremum.
  2. Verify that the supremum norm on $C[0,1]$ fails the parallelogram law, and conclude that $C[0,1]$ is Banach but not Hilbert.
  3. State and prove the projection theorem's orthogonality characterisation, then derive the Pythagoras identity showing the projection is the unique minimiser.
  4. Derive the normal equations $X'X\beta = X'y$ as the orthogonality condition of a projection, and explain geometrically why adding a regressor cannot increase the residual sum of squares.
  5. Show that $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ is the $L^2$ projection onto $\mathcal{G}$-measurable variables, and deduce the tower property and the variance decomposition from the geometry.
  6. State Bessel's inequality and Parseval's identity, and explain what distinguishes an orthonormal set from an orthonormal basis.
  7. State the Riesz representation theorem, and use it to explain why a Hilbert space can be identified with its dual while a general Banach space cannot.
  8. State the four axioms of a coherent risk measure and construct a two-asset example showing that value at risk violates subadditivity.
  9. Write expected shortfall as a supremum of expectations over a set of measures, describing that set explicitly in terms of Radon-Nikodym derivatives.
  10. State the spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators and explain how principal component analysis is an instance of it, identifying the operator, the eigenvectors, and the eigenvalues.
