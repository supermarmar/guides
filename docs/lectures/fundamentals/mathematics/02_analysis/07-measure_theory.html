Measure theory and Lebesgue integration

The Riemann integral has one fatal defect: limits do not pass through it. Sequences of integrable functions converge to non-integrable ones, and integrals of limits differ from limits of integrals, so the operation analysis most wants to perform is exactly the one that fails. Lebesgue's repair is to slice the range instead of the domain, which requires knowing the size of the resulting sets, which requires a theory of measure. What comes back is an integral under which limits behave, three convergence theorems that make interchange routine, and, as a by-product, the correct foundation for probability.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A sigma-algebra is a collection of subsets closed under complement and countable union: the sets whose size we are willing to talk about. A measure assigns a non-negative size to each of them, additively over countable disjoint unions. Lebesgue measure on the line is the one giving each interval its length, and it cannot be extended to every subset, which is why the sigma-algebra is there at all.

The integral is then built in three steps: define it for indicator functions as the measure of the set, extend by linearity to simple functions, and define it for a non-negative measurable function as the supremum over simple functions below it. Three theorems justify the whole enterprise. Monotone convergence: for an increasing sequence, limit and integral commute unconditionally. Fatou's lemma: an inequality that always holds. Dominated convergence: if the sequence is bounded by one integrable function, limit and integral commute. Two more results supply the machinery used downstream: Fubini-Tonelli for iterated integrals, and Radon-Nikodym, which says one measure has a density with respect to another exactly when it is absolutely continuous, and which is what change of measure means.

Feynman test You are counting a heap of coins. Riemann's method is to work along the table left to right, picking up whatever is in each strip and adding as you go. Lebesgue's method is to sort first: find all the pound coins and count them, all the fifty-pence pieces and count them, and so on, then multiply each denomination by its count. Both give the same total for an ordinary heap. But suppose the coins are scattered in a pathological way, dense in some places and absent in others, so that no strip has a well-defined content. Sorting by denomination still works, provided you can say how many coins are in each class. That is exactly the trade Lebesgue makes: harder question about the sizes of sets, in exchange for an integral that survives the limits Riemann's cannot.
What's beyond a first course This entire lecture is beyond the UP curriculum, and it is scoped deliberately: everything here is included because a later lecture or another track consumes it. Radon-Nikodym gets full treatment with a worked change of measure, because that is Girsanov's theorem in finite dimensions and the risk-neutral pricing measure in financial engineering is nothing else. Dominated convergence is worked as a licence to differentiate under an integral sign, because that is how score functions are computed. Abstract measure theory pursued for its own sake, including most of the construction of Lebesgue measure, is stated rather than proved.
Step 2

Why this matters

Probability is measure theory. Kolmogorov's 1933 axioms define a probability space as a set of outcomes, a sigma-algebra of events, and a measure of total mass one; a random variable is a measurable function; an expectation is an integral. Everything in the statistics course that is stated informally becomes precise here, and several things that look like conventions turn out to be theorems. Why is an event required to belong to a sigma-algebra rather than being any subset? Because Lebesgue measure cannot be defined on all subsets. Why does a density exist for some distributions and not others? Because Radon-Nikodym applies exactly when the distribution is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure, and a discrete distribution is not. Why is $\mathbb{E}[X]$ undefined for a Cauchy variable rather than infinite? Because the integral of the positive part and of the negative part are both infinite, and the definition requires at least one to be finite.

Change of measure is the operational payoff. Risk-neutral pricing replaces the real-world probability measure by an equivalent one under which discounted prices are martingales, and the object that performs the replacement is a Radon-Nikodym derivative. Girsanov's theorem, which the financial engineering course uses to remove a drift, is the statement that a specific density does this for Brownian motion; the finite-dimensional core of it is the Gaussian calculation worked below, and seeing that calculation once makes the continuous-time version a matter of technique rather than magic. Importance sampling in Monte Carlo is the same identity used in the other direction, sampling from a convenient measure and reweighting by the density.

The convergence theorems are used constantly without acknowledgement. Differentiating a likelihood under the integral sign to obtain a score, interchanging expectation and infinite sum in an actuarial aggregate-loss calculation, and justifying that a Monte Carlo average converges to the quantity of interest are all applications of dominated convergence or monotone convergence. When such an interchange is invalid, the resulting number is wrong in a way no amount of computation reveals, and these theorems are the check.

Step 4

The detail

Sigma-algebras and measures

Sigma-algebra
A collection $\mathcal{F}$ of subsets of $\Omega$ containing $\Omega$, closed under complementation, and closed under countable unions (hence countable intersections). The pair $(\Omega, \mathcal{F})$ is a measurable space. The Borel sigma-algebra on $\mathbb{R}$ is the smallest one containing all open intervals, and it contains every set that arises in practice without containing every set.
Measure
A function $\mu : \mathcal{F} \to [0,\infty]$ with $\mu(\emptyset) = 0$ that is countably additive: $\mu\left(\bigcup_n A_n\right) = \sum_n \mu(A_n)$ for pairwise disjoint $A_n$. If $\mu(\Omega) = 1$ it is a probability measure. Countable additivity, rather than finite additivity, is the axiom that makes limits work, and it is the direct descendant of the addition rule from combinatorics.

Countable additivity yields two continuity properties used constantly: if $A_n$ increases to A then $\mu(A_n) \to \mu(A)$, and if $A_n$ decreases to A and $\mu(A_1)$ is finite then again $\mu(A_n) \to \mu(A)$. The finiteness caveat is real: the intervals $[n,\infty)$ decrease to the empty set while each has infinite measure.

Lebesgue measure is constructed by defining an outer measure as the infimum of total lengths of interval covers, then restricting to the sets that split every other set additively, which is Caratheodory's criterion. The construction is omitted here; what matters is the outcome. Lebesgue measure exists on a sigma-algebra containing the Borel sets, assigns each interval its length, is translation invariant, and cannot be extended to all subsets of $\mathbb{R}$, by the Vitali construction met in foundations lecture 4. A set of measure zero is null, a property holding off a null set holds almost everywhere, and the countable sets, including the rationals, are all null.

Measurable functions, and the property Riemann lacked

$f$ is measurable when the preimage of every Borel set is in $\mathcal{F}$, which for real functions reduces to checking that $\{f > a\}$ is measurable for every a. Sums, products, and compositions with continuous functions preserve measurability, and so, crucially, do limits: if each $f_n$ is measurable then so are $\sup f_n$, $\inf f_n$, $\limsup f_n$, and any pointwise limit. That closure under limits is precisely what the Riemann-integrable functions fail to have, and it is the structural reason the Lebesgue theory is better behaved.

The integral, in three steps

For an indicator, $\int \mathbf{1}_A \,d\mu = \mu(A)$. For a simple function $s = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \mathbf{1}_{A_i}$ with $c_i \ge 0$, define $\int s\,d\mu = \sum_i c_i \mu(A_i)$. For a general non-negative measurable f, define $\int f\,d\mu = \sup\{\int s\,d\mu : s \text{ simple}, 0 \le s \le f\}$. For a general f, split into positive and negative parts, $f = f^+ - f^-$, and define the integral as the difference provided at least one part is finite; f is integrable when both are finite, equivalently when $\int|f| < \infty$.

The function that broke Riemann, integrated. f = indicator of the rationals on [0,1]. Riemann (from lecture 4): every subinterval contains rationals and irrationals, so every upper sum is 1 and every lower sum is 0. Not integrable. Lebesgue: f is already simple, f = 1 * indicator(Q n [0,1]). integral = 1 * measure(Q n [0,1]). The rationals are countable, so cover the k-th rational by an interval of length eps/2^k. Total length at most eps, for every eps > 0, so the measure is 0. integral of f = 1 * 0 = 0. The answer matches the intuition that "almost every" point of [0,1] is irrational, and f agrees with the zero function almost everywhere. RELATIONSHIP TO RIEMANN, in general: - If f is Riemann integrable on [a,b], it is Lebesgue integrable and the two integrals agree. Nothing is lost. - A bounded f is Riemann integrable exactly when its set of discontinuities is null. That is Lebesgue's criterion, and it explains the earlier failure in one line: the indicator of the rationals is discontinuous everywhere. - Improper Riemann integrals are a partial exception: an integral that converges only conditionally, such as the integral of sin(x)/x over the positive reals, exists as an improper Riemann integral but not as a Lebesgue integral, because the absolute value has infinite integral. This is the same absolute-versus-conditional distinction from lecture 4.

The three convergence theorems

Monotone convergence. If $0 \le f_1 \le f_2 \le \cdots$ pointwise with limit f, then $\int f_n \to \int f$. No domination is required, and the limit may be infinite on both sides. This theorem is what allows an integral to be defined by approximation from below, and it makes term-by-term integration of series with non-negative terms automatic.

Fatou's lemma. For non-negative measurable $f_n$, $\int \liminf f_n \le \liminf \int f_n$. It always holds, with no hypotheses beyond non-negativity, and the inequality can be strict: the sliding bump has $\liminf f_n = 0$ on the left and $\liminf \int f_n = 1$ on the right. Mass can be lost in the limit but never gained.

Dominated convergence. If $f_n \to f$ pointwise almost everywhere and $|f_n| \le g$ for a single integrable g, then f is integrable and $\int f_n \to \int f$, indeed $\int |f_n - f| \to 0$. The dominating function must not depend on n, and finding it is the entire practical difficulty.

Dominated convergence as a licence to differentiate under the integral. Setting: a likelihood or transform of the form M(t) = integral over x of exp(t x) f(x) dx and the question of whether M'(t) = integral of x exp(t x) f(x) dx. Write the difference quotient: [M(t+h) - M(t)] / h = integral of f(x) * [exp((t+h)x) - exp(tx)] / h dx. Pointwise in x, the bracket tends to x exp(tx) as h -> 0. To pass the limit inside we need a dominating function valid for all small h. By the mean value theorem, for each x there is s between t and t+h with [exp((t+h)x) - exp(tx)] / h = x exp(s x). For |h| <= d, s lies in [t-d, t+d], so | x exp(sx) | <= |x| ( exp((t+d)x) + exp((t-d)x) ). So IF that bound is integrable against f, that is, if the moment generating function is finite on a neighbourhood of t, dominated convergence applies and differentiation under the integral is valid: M'(t) = integral of x exp(tx) f(x) dx, and at t = 0, M'(0) = E[X]. WHERE IT FAILS. For a lognormal density, the integral defining M(t) diverges for every t > 0: no neighbourhood exists, no dominating function exists, and the moment generating function does not exist even though every moment does. This is why characteristic functions, which integrate the BOUNDED function exp(itx) and therefore always admit the constant dominating function 1, are the safer transform, and it is the analytic reason the complex analysis lecture worked with them rather than with MGFs. The same argument justifies swapping the derivative and the expectation when computing a score function in maximum likelihood, which is done routinely and is legitimate exactly when a dominating function exists.

Product measures and Fubini-Tonelli

Tonelli: for non-negative measurable functions on a product of sigma-finite measure spaces, the double integral equals either iterated integral, with no further hypothesis. Fubini: for general measurable f, the same conclusion holds provided $\iint |f| < \infty$. The practical procedure follows immediately: apply Tonelli to $|f|$ to check finiteness, then apply Fubini to f to compute. The hypothesis is not decorative, and a standard counterexample on the unit square with $f(x,y) = (x^2-y^2)/(x^2+y^2)^2$ gives $\pi/4$ in one order and $-\pi/4$ in the other, which is the two-dimensional face of the rearrangement phenomenon from lecture 4.

Absolute continuity and Radon-Nikodym

Absolute continuity and the Radon-Nikodym derivative
$\nu$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $\mu$, written $\nu \ll \mu$, when every $\mu$-null set is $\nu$-null. The Radon-Nikodym theorem states that for sigma-finite measures, $\nu \ll \mu$ if and only if there is a non-negative measurable f with $\nu(A) = \int_A f\,d\mu$ for all measurable A. That f, unique up to null sets, is written $d\nu/d\mu$. Two measures are equivalent when each is absolutely continuous with respect to the other, so they agree on which events are impossible while disagreeing on probabilities.

This definition unifies things that look unrelated. A probability density function is $dP/d\lambda$, the derivative of the distribution with respect to Lebesgue measure, which is why discrete distributions have no density: they charge single points, which are Lebesgue-null. A probability mass function is $dP/d\kappa$ with respect to counting measure. A likelihood ratio is $dP_1/dP_0$. And the risk-neutral pricing measure is an equivalent measure whose derivative reweights the real-world one.

Change of measure on a Gaussian: the core of Girsanov. Let P make X standard normal, and let Q make X normal with mean theta and the same variance 1. Both have densities against Lebesgue measure, so both are absolutely continuous with respect to each other, and the derivative is the ratio of densities: dQ/dP (x) = [ (2pi)^(-1/2) exp( -(x - theta)^2 / 2 ) ] / [ (2pi)^(-1/2) exp( -x^2 / 2 ) ] = exp( -(x^2 - 2 theta x + theta^2)/2 + x^2/2 ) = exp( theta x - theta^2 / 2 ). Call this Z(x). Two checks. (1) Z has P-expectation 1, as any density must: E_P[Z] = E_P[ exp(theta X - theta^2/2) ] = exp(theta^2/2) * exp(-theta^2/2) = 1, using the standard normal MGF E[exp(tX)] = exp(t^2/2). (2) Q really does shift the mean: E_Q[X] = E_P[X Z] = E_P[ X exp(theta X - theta^2 / 2) ]. Differentiate the MGF identity E_P[exp(tX)] = exp(t^2/2) with respect to t (legitimate by the dominated convergence argument above) to get E_P[X exp(tX)] = t exp(t^2/2). At t = theta: E_Q[X] = theta exp(theta^2/2) * exp(-theta^2/2) = theta. Correct. WHAT THIS IS. The density exp(theta x - theta^2/2) is exactly the Girsanov factor, with x a Brownian increment and theta the drift being removed. In continuous time the derivative becomes exp( - integral of theta dW - (1/2) integral of theta^2 dt ), which is the same expression with sums replaced by stochastic integrals. The finite-dimensional case above contains all of the algebra and none of the technical difficulty, and it is worth working through by hand before meeting the continuous version. WHY EQUIVALENCE MATTERS FOR PRICING. P and Q agree about which events have probability zero, so they agree about what is impossible, and therefore about what constitutes an arbitrage. They disagree about probabilities, which is why an expected value under Q is a price rather than a forecast. That is the measure-theoretic content of the risk-neutral valuation formula.

The $L^p$ spaces

For $p \ge 1$, $L^p(\mu)$ consists of measurable f with $\int |f|^p < \infty$, normed by $\|f\|_p = (\int |f|^p)^{1/p}$. Elements are equivalence classes of functions agreeing almost everywhere, since otherwise a non-zero function with zero norm would exist and the norm axioms would fail. Holder's inequality $\|fg\|_1 \le \|f\|_p \|g\|_q$ with $1/p + 1/q = 1$ generalises Cauchy-Schwarz, and Minkowski's inequality is the triangle inequality for these norms. The Riesz-Fischer theorem says each $L^p$ is complete, and that completeness is exactly the completion promised in lecture 5: $L^2$ is what you get by completing the continuous functions under the square-integral metric, and it is the space the next lecture works in throughout.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Almost everywhere is the right equivalence relation

Measure theory systematically ignores null sets, and this is not carelessness but the correct level of resolution. Two functions differing on a null set have the same integral against every set, so no measurement distinguishes them, and treating them as different would attach meaning to something unobservable. The formal move is the quotient construction from foundations lecture 3: define $f \sim g$ when they agree almost everywhere, and let $L^p$ consist of the classes. In probability the same convention appears as "almost surely", and the same reasoning applies: an event of probability zero cannot be excluded logically, only ignored measure-theoretically, which is why convergence theorems are stated almost everywhere and why a continuous distribution assigns probability zero to every individual outcome without those outcomes being impossible.

Which integral to use, and when the distinction bites

For any function encountered in practice the Riemann and Lebesgue integrals agree, so the choice is not about answers to specific computations. It is about which theorems are available. Riemann's integral supports term-by-term integration under uniform convergence; Lebesgue's supports it under pointwise convergence plus domination, which is enormously weaker and is the hypothesis that actually holds in applications. Riemann's integrable functions do not form a complete space; Lebesgue's do, which is what allows Hilbert space methods. Riemann's theory does not extend to abstract spaces; Lebesgue's is defined for any measure, which is what allows probability on function spaces, and therefore stochastic processes. The distinction bites whenever a limit, an infinite sum, or an abstract sample space appears, which in this repository is nearly always.

Kolmogorov's move, and what it bought

Before 1933 probability was a collection of techniques with contested foundations, and questions such as "what is the probability of a random continuous function having a given property" could not be posed precisely. Kolmogorov's identification of probability with normalised measure resolved this at a stroke, and the specific gains are worth listing. Conditional expectation acquires a definition that works when the conditioning event has probability zero, via Radon-Nikodym, which is what makes continuous-time filtrations usable. Infinite sequences of random variables acquire a joint distribution, via the extension theorem, which is what makes stochastic processes objects rather than metaphors. The strong law of large numbers becomes a statement about almost-sure convergence, provable rather than assumed. And the modes of convergence separate cleanly into almost sure, in probability, in $L^p$, and in distribution, each with its own theorems, which is the hierarchy the statistics course uses. Every one of those depends on having a measure rather than a heuristic.

Watch out Three traps. Dominated convergence needs a single dominating function that works for all n; a bound $|f_n| \le g_n$ with each $g_n$ integrable is worthless. Fubini needs absolute integrability checked first, by Tonelli on $|f|$, and skipping that check is how sign-changing double integrals produce two different answers. And absolute continuity is directional: a discrete measure is not absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure, so no density exists, which is the precise reason a mixed distribution with an atom cannot be described by a density function alone.

Test yourself

  1. Verify that the Borel sets form a sigma-algebra, then show that countable additivity implies continuity from below, and give the example showing continuity from above needs a finiteness hypothesis.
  2. Prove that the rationals have Lebesgue measure zero using a covering with total length $\epsilon$, and deduce the integral of their indicator.
  3. State Lebesgue's criterion for Riemann integrability and use it to decide whether the indicator of the Cantor set is Riemann integrable.
  4. Give the sliding-bump sequence explicitly, compute $\int f_n$ and $\int \lim f_n$, and verify Fatou's inequality is strict for it. Explain why no dominating function exists.
  5. State the dominated convergence theorem and use it to justify differentiating $M(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]$ under the integral sign, naming the dominating function.
  6. Explain why a lognormal random variable has all moments finite but no moment generating function, and why its characteristic function nonetheless exists.
  7. State Tonelli and Fubini, and describe the two-step procedure for using them safely on a sign-changing integrand.
  8. Define absolute continuity and compute $dQ/dP$ when P makes X standard normal and Q makes it $N(\theta, 1)$. Verify the derivative has P-expectation 1.
  9. Using that derivative, compute $\mathbb{E}_Q[X]$ as $\mathbb{E}_P[XZ]$ and confirm it equals $\theta$. Say what the same object is called in the stochastic calculus setting.
  10. Explain why elements of $L^2$ are equivalence classes rather than functions, and what would go wrong with the norm axioms if they were not.
