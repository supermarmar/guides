Measure-theoretic probability

This is measure theory with total mass one and a change of vocabulary, and the change of vocabulary is worth taking seriously because it is what makes stochastic processes possible. A random variable is a measurable function, an expectation is an integral, independence is a product measure, and information is a sigma-algebra. That last identification is the one that pays: once information is a mathematical object, a filtration can model information arriving over time, conditional expectation becomes a projection onto what is known, and a martingale becomes the precise statement that a game is fair.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ is a measure space with $\mathbb{P}(\Omega) = 1$. A random variable is a measurable function $X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$, its law is the pushforward measure $\mathbb{P} \circ X^{-1}$ on the line, and its expectation is $\int_\Omega X \,d\mathbb{P}$. Independence of random variables is the statement that their joint law is the product of their marginals.

Four modes of convergence then organise the limit theorems: almost sure, in probability, in $L^p$, and in distribution, in decreasing order of strength. The weak law of large numbers is convergence in probability, the strong law is almost sure, and the central limit theorem is convergence in distribution. The second half of the lecture builds the machinery for processes: a filtration is an increasing family of sigma-algebras representing information over time, conditional expectation $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ is defined by Radon-Nikodym and coincides with the $L^2$ projection when X is square integrable, and a martingale is a process whose conditional expectation of the future equals its present value. The optional stopping theorem says you cannot beat a martingale by choosing when to quit, subject to hypotheses that are exactly where the interesting failures live.

Feynman test Consider the doubling strategy at a fair casino: bet one pound on a coin flip, and after every loss double the stake, stopping the moment you win. You will certainly win eventually, and when you do, your net position is exactly plus one pound. A guaranteed profit from a fair game. The catch is not in the arithmetic, which is correct, but in what the strategy requires: unbounded capital and unbounded time. The optional stopping theorem states precisely which hypotheses rule this out, and the doubling strategy fails every one of them. That is why the theorem's hypotheses deserve attention rather than being skipped: they are the difference between "no strategy beats a fair game" being true and being false.
What's beyond a first course A first probability course defines conditional expectation by a formula that only works when the conditioning event has positive probability, and then uses it in continuous settings where that condition fails. This lecture gives the general definition and shows what it repairs, including the Borel-Kolmogorov paradox, where conditioning on a measure-zero event gives different answers depending on how the event is approached. It also states the fundamental theorem of asset pricing in its measure-theoretic form, because that statement is what the entire risk-neutral apparatus of the financial engineering track reduces to.
Step 2

Why this matters

Stochastic calculus is unreadable without this lecture. A stochastic integral is defined as an $L^2$ limit of sums of predictable integrands against martingale increments, so every word in that description comes from here or from the previous two lectures. Ito's lemma is a statement about processes adapted to a filtration. Girsanov's theorem is a change of measure, worked in finite dimensions in lecture 7. And the risk-neutral valuation formula, which the financial engineering course uses on every page, is the assertion that discounted prices are martingales under an equivalent measure, which is the fundamental theorem of asset pricing stated below.

Conditional expectation is the other operational payoff. Filtering, prediction, and every model that updates as information arrives is built on it, and the general definition matters because the elementary one breaks in exactly the settings that arise: conditioning on the value of a continuous variable is conditioning on a probability-zero event. The statistics course uses conditional expectation throughout without needing this machinery, and the machinery becomes necessary the moment a continuous-time process appears.

The convergence modes matter for reading asymptotic claims correctly. Consistency of an estimator is convergence in probability; the strong law gives almost-sure convergence, which is a genuinely stronger statement about the whole sample path rather than about each fixed sample size; and asymptotic normality is convergence in distribution, which says nothing about any particular realisation. Monte Carlo convergence is the strong law, and its error bars come from the central limit theorem, so a simulation's guarantees rest on two different modes at once.

Two consequences of the second row are worth drawing out. Because a random variable is a function on an unspecified $\Omega$, the space itself is never described in practice; only the law matters for distributional questions, which is why textbooks say "let X be normal" without ever naming $\Omega$. But when several variables must be related, they must live on a common space, and constructing that space is exactly what the Kolmogorov extension theorem does for infinite sequences and continuous-time processes.

Borel-Cantelli and almost sure statements

The two Borel-Cantelli lemmas are the standard route to almost-sure conclusions. If $\sum \mathbb{P}(A_n) < \infty$ then almost surely only finitely many $A_n$ occur. Conversely, if the $A_n$ are independent and $\sum \mathbb{P}(A_n) = \infty$, then almost surely infinitely many occur. The first needs no independence and is the one used constantly.

Borel-Cantelli in use: convergence rates that hold almost surely. Let X_1, X_2, ... be independent standard normals. How large does the largest of the first n get? For any c > 0, P(|X_n| > c sqrt(log n)) is approximately 2 * (1 - Phi(c sqrt(log n))) ~ n^(-c^2/2) (up to log factors), using the standard normal tail bound 1 - Phi(x) <= exp(-x^2/2)/(x sqrt(2pi)). Take c = 2: the probabilities behave like n^(-2), and sum n^(-2) converges. First Borel-Cantelli: almost surely, only finitely many n have |X_n| > 2 sqrt(log n). So beyond some random index N(omega), |X_n| <= 2 sqrt(log n) for all n. Take c = 1: the probabilities behave like n^(-1/2), which sums to infinity, and the events are independent. Second Borel-Cantelli: almost surely infinitely many n have |X_n| > sqrt(log n). Conclusion: the maximum of n independent standard normals grows like sqrt(2 log n), neither faster nor slower. This is why extreme value theory in the statistics course normalises maxima by exactly that factor, and why a "record" observation in a large normal sample is not evidence of a heavy tail: sqrt(2 log n) is what a normal sample does by construction.

The limit theorems

The weak law says the sample mean of independent identically distributed variables with finite mean converges to that mean in probability. The strong law, due to Kolmogorov, upgrades this to almost sure convergence under the same hypothesis of a finite mean, and it is a genuinely stronger statement: the weak law allows the sample mean to wander outside any band infinitely often provided it does so with vanishing probability at each fixed n, and the strong law forbids it. Monte Carlo estimation relies on the strong law, which is why a single long simulation run is meaningful rather than only a sequence of independent short ones.

The central limit theorem says that standardised sums converge in distribution to a standard normal, provided the variance is finite. The clean proof uses characteristic functions from lecture 6: expand $\varphi(t/\sqrt{n})$ to second order, raise to the n-th power, and take the limit to get $e^{-t^2/2}$, then apply Levy's continuity theorem to conclude convergence of the laws. Both hypotheses matter. Without a finite variance the limit is a stable law rather than a normal, and without a finite mean, as for the Cauchy, the sample mean does not converge at all, which was computed by residues in the complex analysis lecture.

Conditional expectation, defined properly

Conditional expectation
For integrable X and a sub-sigma-algebra $\mathcal{G}$, $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ is the almost surely unique $\mathcal{G}$-measurable integrable random variable Y satisfying $\int_A Y \,d\mathbb{P} = \int_A X\,d\mathbb{P}$ for every $A \in \mathcal{G}$. Existence follows from Radon-Nikodym applied to the measure $A \mapsto \int_A X\,d\mathbb{P}$, which is absolutely continuous with respect to $\mathbb{P}$ restricted to $\mathcal{G}$.

Note what the definition does not require: nothing has positive probability, no event is conditioned on individually, and X need only be integrable rather than square integrable. When X is in $L^2$ this object coincides with the orthogonal projection of lecture 8, which supplies the geometry, but the general definition covers the $L^1$ case where no projection exists. The defining property is an averaging identity over sets, which is exactly the "average within each cell" picture drawn above.

Why the elementary definition is not enough: the Borel-Kolmogorov paradox. Take a point uniformly distributed on the surface of a sphere. Condition on it lying on a particular great circle. What is the conditional distribution along that circle? Answer one. Use lines of longitude as the conditioning family. Each meridian is a great circle through the poles, and the conditional distribution along it turns out to be proportional to cos(latitude), concentrating near the equator. Answer two. Use the equator and its rotations. Conditioning on the equator gives the uniform distribution along it. Both great circles have probability zero, and the two answers differ. There is no contradiction, because "condition on this circle" is not a well-defined instruction: the answer depends on which family of probability-zero events the circle is regarded as a member of, since the conditional distribution is only determined up to null sets. WHAT THE MEASURE-THEORETIC DEFINITION DOES. It conditions on a sigma-algebra, not on an event. Specifying the sigma-algebra specifies the family, which removes the ambiguity. The elementary formula P(A|B) = P(A and B)/P(B) simply does not apply when P(B) = 0, and the general definition is not a technical refinement of it but a different construction that agrees with it whenever the elementary one is defined. WHY A PRACTITIONER SHOULD CARE. Conditioning on a continuous variable taking an exact value is conditioning on a null event, which is done constantly: "given that the factor equals x". It is legitimate because a version of the conditional expectation is specified implicitly by the model's construction, and it becomes illegitimate when two constructions that look equivalent imply different families. Sensitivity of a conditional result to the parametrisation of the conditioning variable is the practical symptom.

Filtrations, martingales, and optional stopping

A filtration is an increasing family $\mathcal{F}_0 \subseteq \mathcal{F}_1 \subseteq \cdots$ of sub-sigma-algebras. A process is adapted when $X_t$ is $\mathcal{F}_t$-measurable, meaning its value is known by time t. An adapted integrable process is a martingale when $\mathbb{E}[X_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = X_t$, a submartingale when the left side is at least the right, and a supermartingale when it is at most. A stopping time is a random time $\tau$ with $\{\tau \le t\} \in \mathcal{F}_t$: whether you have stopped by now is decidable from what you know now, which rules out rules that depend on the future.

Optional stopping, and the doubling strategy that appears to defeat it. OPTIONAL STOPPING THEOREM. If (X_t) is a martingale and tau is a stopping time, then E[X_tau] = E[X_0] provided ONE of: (a) tau is bounded; or (b) tau is almost surely finite and X is uniformly bounded; or (c) E[tau] is finite and the increments are bounded. THE DOUBLING STRATEGY. Fair coin, bet 1, double after each loss, stop at the first win. Let X_t be the gambler's net position, a martingale. P(win on flip k) = 2^(-k), so tau is finite almost surely. Stake on flip k is 2^(k-1); losses before it total 2^(k-1) - 1. Net at stopping: 2^(k-1) - (2^(k-1) - 1) = +1, always. So E[X_tau] = 1, while E[X_0] = 0. The theorem's conclusion fails, so every hypothesis must fail. Check each: (a) tau unbounded: yes, it can take any value. (b) X not uniformly bounded: before winning, the position reaches -(2^(k-1) - 1), unbounded below. (c) increments unbounded: stake doubles without limit. All three fail, so no contradiction. And the failure is economically exact: the strategy requires unbounded credit and unbounded time, and with any finite bankroll B the expected value returns to zero, because the small probability of the losing run that exhausts B carries a loss large enough to cancel the many small wins. With bankroll B = 2^m - 1 (enough for m doublings): P(win eventually) = 1 - 2^(-m), gain +1 P(lose all m) = 2^(-m), loss -(2^m - 1) E = (1 - 2^(-m))(1) + 2^(-m)(-(2^m - 1)) = 1 - 2^(-m) - 1 + 2^(-m) = 0. The martingale property is restored exactly when the hypothesis is. GAMBLER'S RUIN, the same theorem used positively. A fair random walk starts at a, absorbed at 0 or at N. Here tau is finite with finite expectation and increments are bounded, so hypothesis (c) holds: E[X_tau] = a. But X_tau is 0 or N, so 0 * P(ruin) + N * (1 - P(ruin)) = a, giving P(reaching N before 0) = a / N. One line, from the theorem, with no difference equations.

The fundamental theorem of asset pricing

Everything above assembles into one statement. Consider a market of traded assets over a filtered probability space, with prices discounted by the money market account. The first fundamental theorem says the market admits no arbitrage if and only if there exists a probability measure $\mathbb{Q}$, equivalent to the real-world measure $\mathbb{P}$, under which every discounted price process is a martingale. The second says the market is complete, meaning every contingent claim is replicable, if and only if that measure is unique.

Each ingredient has now been built. Equivalence of measures, from lecture 7, is what guarantees $\mathbb{P}$ and $\mathbb{Q}$ agree about which events are impossible and therefore about what constitutes an arbitrage. The martingale property is the formal content of "the discounted price is a fair game". The Radon-Nikodym derivative $d\mathbb{Q}/d\mathbb{P}$ is the object that performs the change, and in the Brownian case it is the Girsanov factor computed in that lecture. The price of a claim is then $\mathbb{E}_\mathbb{Q}[\text{discounted payoff}]$, an expectation under a measure chosen to make the arithmetic of no-arbitrage work, and emphatically not a forecast. The single-period binomial version of this, worked concretely in the financial engineering course, is the same theorem with $\Omega$ containing two points.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

A martingale is the mathematical form of "no free lunch"

The martingale property says the best prediction of tomorrow's value, given everything known today, is today's value. Three apparently different statements are the same statement. In gambling, no strategy of choosing when to stop improves the expected outcome of a fair game, subject to the optional stopping hypotheses. In finance, if discounted prices are martingales under some equivalent measure, no self-financing strategy produces something from nothing, which is the first fundamental theorem. In statistics, the likelihood ratio between two hypotheses is a martingale under the null, which is what makes sequential testing procedures valid and gives their error bounds. Recognising the shared structure means results transfer: the optional stopping hypotheses that kill the doubling strategy are the same conditions that make a sequential test honest, and they fail in the same way, through unbounded stakes or unbounded time.

Continuous time is where the technicalities earn their keep

In discrete time much of this lecture can be done with elementary tools, and the measure-theoretic apparatus looks like overhead. In continuous time it is unavoidable. There are uncountably many time points, so a statement holding at each fixed time almost surely need not hold at all times almost surely, and the gap requires path regularity conditions on the filtration. Stochastic integrals cannot be defined pathwise, because Brownian paths have unbounded variation, so they are defined as $L^2$ limits, which needs the completeness from lecture 7. And a stopping time must be decidable from current information, which is only expressible with sigma-algebras. Every one of the "usual conditions" imposed at the start of a stochastic calculus text is repairing a specific failure of this kind, and knowing which failure makes those conditions readable rather than ritual.

What this closes, and what it opens

This lecture completes the analysis course, and the arc is worth naming. It began with calculus computed, moved to calculus proved, found that the proofs needed only completeness and compactness, generalised those to metric spaces, discovered the Riemann integral could not survive limits, replaced it, and finally used the replacement to build a rigorous theory of randomness. Each step was forced by a defect in the previous one rather than chosen for abstraction's sake. What it opens is the whole of continuous-time finance and modern statistics, both of which are applications of this machinery, and neither of which can be read carefully without it.

Watch out Three errors. Treating convergence in distribution as though it says something about realisations: it constrains only the law, so a sequence can converge in distribution to X while never being close to X on any sample path. Applying optional stopping without checking a hypothesis: the doubling strategy is the standing reminder, and in practice the hypothesis that fails is usually the unbounded one. And conditioning on a null event without specifying the family: the Borel-Kolmogorov paradox shows the answer genuinely depends on it, so "given that the continuous variable equals x" is meaningful only once the model fixes how that limit is taken.

Test yourself

  1. Define a random variable's law as a pushforward measure and explain why two variables on different probability spaces can share a law.
  2. State both Borel-Cantelli lemmas, noting which needs independence, and use them to bound the growth of the maximum of independent standard normals.
  3. Give the four modes of convergence, draw the implication diagram, and supply a counterexample showing that convergence in probability does not imply almost sure convergence.
  4. State the weak and strong laws of large numbers and explain precisely what the strong law forbids that the weak law permits.
  5. Sketch the characteristic function proof of the central limit theorem, and say what the limit becomes when the variance is infinite.
  6. Give the measure-theoretic definition of conditional expectation and verify it agrees with the elementary formula when the conditioning event has positive probability.
  7. Explain the Borel-Kolmogorov paradox in your own words and state what the general definition conditions on instead of an event.
  8. Define a martingale and a stopping time, and prove the gambler's ruin probability $a/N$ by optional stopping, naming the hypothesis you rely on.
  9. Show that the doubling strategy has $\mathbb{E}[X_\tau] = 1$ while $\mathbb{E}[X_0] = 0$, and identify which optional stopping hypothesis fails. Then compute the expectation under a finite bankroll and confirm it is zero.
  10. State both fundamental theorems of asset pricing and identify, for each ingredient, which earlier lecture supplied it.
