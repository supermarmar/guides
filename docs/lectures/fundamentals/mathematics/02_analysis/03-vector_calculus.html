Vector calculus

Integration moves off the interval and onto curves and surfaces, and one theorem appears three times in different clothing. Green, Gauss, and Stokes all say the same thing: integrating a derivative over a region gives the same answer as evaluating the original object on the boundary of that region. The fundamental theorem of calculus was the one-dimensional case, where the boundary of an interval is its two endpoints. Everything here is that statement in higher dimensions, and the differential geometry lecture later collapses all four into a single line.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A vector field attaches an arrow to every point of space: a force, a velocity, a flow. Two integrals measure what a field does. The line integral $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ adds up the component of the field along a curve, and it measures work done or circulation. The surface integral $\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S}$ adds up the component crossing a surface, and it measures flux.

Two derivatives measure what a field does locally. The divergence $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}$ is the net outflow per unit volume, so it detects sources and sinks. The curl $\nabla \times \mathbf{F}$ is the circulation per unit area, so it detects rotation. The three big theorems then connect the local to the global: Green relates circulation round a plane curve to curl inside it, Stokes does the same for a surface in space, and Gauss relates flux through a closed surface to divergence inside it. A field with zero curl on a well-behaved domain is conservative, meaning it is the gradient of a potential and its line integrals depend only on endpoints.

Feynman test Stand a paddle wheel in a flowing river. If it spins, the flow has curl at that point, and the spin rate measures it. Now imagine a small imaginary box in the water and count what flows in against what flows out. If more leaves than enters, there is a source inside the box, and that imbalance per unit volume is the divergence. Neither quantity requires you to know anything about the river far away; both are local. The three theorems of this lecture are the claim that if you know these local quantities everywhere inside a region, you know the total circulation round its edge and the total flow through its skin, without measuring the edge or the skin at all. Interior information determines boundary behaviour, which is exactly what the fundamental theorem of calculus said about intervals.
What's beyond a first course A first course computes line and surface integrals and applies the three theorems to physics problems. This lecture adds the unifying reading, that all three plus the fundamental theorem of calculus are instances of one statement about derivatives and boundaries, which becomes literal in the differential geometry lecture. It also treats the distinction between closed and exact fields honestly: zero curl gives path independence only on a simply connected domain, and the standard counterexample on the punctured plane is worked in full, because it is the point where topology enters analysis and it is usually skipped.
Step 2

Why this matters

This is the lecture in the analysis course with the least direct application to risk modelling, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. Its return comes in three specific places, each real.

The first is the continuity equation. Divergence measures net outflow, so "nothing is created or destroyed" becomes $\partial \rho / \partial t + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{v}) = 0$, a conservation law. When the flow is a diffusion rather than a current, the same bookkeeping produces the Fokker-Planck equation, which describes how the probability density of a diffusing quantity evolves in time. That is the density-level counterpart of the stochastic differential equations in financial engineering, and the Black-Scholes partial differential equation is a close relative reached by the same conservation reasoning.

The second is path independence. A conservative field is one whose line integral depends only on its endpoints, which is equivalent to the existence of a potential function. That structure appears whenever a quantity can be reconstructed from local increments without reference to the route taken, and its failure is equally informative: a non-conservative field is one where a closed loop returns a non-zero total, which in a market setting is the shape of an arbitrage. The formal parallel is worth holding loosely rather than pressing hard, but the mathematical content, that closed loops integrate to zero exactly when a potential exists, is the same in both settings.

The third is preparation. The generalised Stokes theorem on manifolds, which is the destination of the differential geometry lecture, cannot be understood without the classical three first. That lecture is where the language of differential forms makes all of this one equation, and this lecture is the concrete ground it abstracts from.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture one: the boundary principle

Picture two: why interior edges cancel

The proof of Green's theorem is a cancellation argument, and the picture is more convincing than the algebra. Chop the region into many small cells. Each interior edge is shared by exactly two cells, which traverse it in opposite directions, so their contributions cancel exactly. Only the outer edges, belonging to one cell each, survive. Adding the small circulations therefore leaves the circulation round the outer boundary, and shrinking the cells turns the sum of small circulations into the integral of curl.

Step 4

The detail

Curves, parametrisation, and arc length

A curve in space is given by a vector function $\mathbf{r}(t) = (x(t), y(t), z(t))$ for t in an interval. Its derivative $\mathbf{r}'(t)$ is the velocity, tangent to the curve, and its magnitude $\|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|$ is the speed. Arc length is the integral of speed, $L = \int_a^b \|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|\,dt$, which is the statement that distance is accumulated speed with the direction discarded. A curve is smooth when $\mathbf{r}'$ is continuous and never zero, which rules out cusps where the parametrisation stalls.

Parametrisation is a choice, and every integral below must be independent of that choice or it would not be a property of the curve. That independence holds for arc length and for line integrals, and the proof is the substitution rule from lecture 1 applied to a reparametrising function. Orientation is the one thing that is not free: reversing the direction of travel flips the sign of a vector line integral, and leaves a scalar one unchanged.

Line integrals

Line integral of a vector field
$\displaystyle \int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_a^b \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t)\,dt$. Only the component of the field along the direction of travel contributes, because the dot product discards the perpendicular part. Read as work when F is a force, and as circulation when the curve is closed.

And indeed A = grad(xy), so its integral is just xy evaluated at the endpoints: 1*1 - 0*0 = 1, matching both paths without any integration.

Conservative fields, potentials, and the gradient theorem

The gradient theorem is the fundamental theorem of calculus for line integrals: if $\mathbf{F} = \nabla \varphi$, then $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \varphi(\text{end}) - \varphi(\text{start})$. The proof is one application of the chain rule followed by the one-dimensional fundamental theorem. Three conditions turn out to be equivalent on a connected open domain: the field is a gradient, its line integrals are path independent, and its integral round every closed loop is zero. Each implication is short, and together they say that a potential is exactly a record of accumulated increments that does not depend on the route.

The practical test is the curl. If $\mathbf{F} = \nabla \varphi$ with continuous second partials, then $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = 0$, because mixed partials commute (Clairaut, from lecture 2). The converse needs a hypothesis about the shape of the domain, and this is where topology enters.

Zero curl is not enough: the punctured plane. Field: F = ( -y / (x^2 + y^2), x / (x^2 + y^2) ), defined off the origin. Curl (scalar, in 2D): P = -y/(x^2+y^2), Q = x/(x^2+y^2) dQ/dx = [ (x^2+y^2) - x(2x) ] / (x^2+y^2)^2 = (y^2 - x^2)/(x^2+y^2)^2 dP/dy = [ -(x^2+y^2) + y(2y) ] / (x^2+y^2)^2 = (y^2 - x^2)/(x^2+y^2)^2 curl = dQ/dx - dP/dy = 0 everywhere the field is defined. Now integrate round the unit circle, r(t) = (cos t, sin t), t in [0, 2pi]: x^2 + y^2 = 1, so F(r(t)) = (-sin t, cos t), r'(t) = (-sin t, cos t) F . r' = sin^2 t + cos^2 t = 1 integral = int_0^{2pi} 1 dt = 2 pi, NOT zero. So the field has zero curl everywhere yet a non-zero loop integral, and it therefore has no global potential. The resolution is the domain: the plane minus the origin is not simply connected, because the loop encircles a hole and cannot be shrunk to a point without leaving the domain. On any simply connected subdomain (say, the plane minus the whole negative x-axis) a potential does exist, namely the polar angle theta, and the 2 pi is exactly the jump theta makes on going once round. Terminology: zero curl is "closed", being a gradient is "exact". Closed implies exact only on simply connected domains, and the gap between the two is measured by the topology of the domain, which is the subject the algebraic topology lecture takes up.

Divergence and curl

For $\mathbf{F} = (P, Q, R)$, the divergence is the scalar $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = P_x + Q_y + R_z$, and the curl is the vector $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = (R_y - Q_z,\; P_z - R_x,\; Q_x - P_y)$. Both have limit definitions that explain the names better than the formulas do: divergence at a point is the limit of flux out of a small closed surface divided by the volume enclosed, and the curl component in a direction is the limit of circulation round a small loop perpendicular to that direction divided by the area enclosed. The coordinate formulas are what those limits evaluate to.

Two identities are worth knowing because they are used constantly and both say "boundaries have no boundary". The curl of a gradient is zero, $\nabla \times \nabla \varphi = 0$, and the divergence of a curl is zero, $\nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) = 0$. Both follow from equality of mixed partials, and both reappear in differential geometry as the single statement $d^2 = 0$.

Surfaces and flux

A surface is parametrised by two variables, $\mathbf{r}(u,v)$, and the vector $\mathbf{r}_u \times \mathbf{r}_v$ is normal to it with length equal to the area scaling factor, so $d\mathbf{S} = (\mathbf{r}_u \times \mathbf{r}_v)\,du\,dv$. The flux of a field through the surface is $\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S}$, the total amount crossing per unit time. Orientation must be chosen, and for a closed surface the convention is outward. Not every surface admits a consistent choice: the Mobius band is one sided, and no orientation exists, which is why the theorems below require orientable surfaces.

The three theorems

TheoremStatementHypotheses that matter
Green$\oint_{\partial D} P\,dx + Q\,dy = \iint_D (Q_x - P_y)\,dA$D a plane region with piecewise smooth boundary, traversed anticlockwise; field continuously differentiable on all of D, including any holes
Stokes$\oint_{\partial S} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot d\mathbf{S}$S an oriented piecewise smooth surface with the boundary orientation induced by the surface normal (right-hand rule)
Gauss$\iint_{\partial V} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iiint_V (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F})\,dV$V a solid region with closed piecewise smooth boundary, outward orientation, field differentiable throughout V

Green is the special case of Stokes in which the surface is flat and lies in the plane, so there are really two theorems here, and the differential geometry lecture reduces them to one. The hypothesis to watch in all three is that the field must be well behaved throughout the region, not merely on the boundary: the punctured-plane counterexample above fails Green's theorem precisely because the field is undefined at the origin, which sits inside the loop.

Two quick applications. (1) Area by Green's theorem. Take P = -y/2, Q = x/2, so Q_x - P_y = 1/2 + 1/2 = 1. Then Area(D) = double integral of 1 dA = (1/2) * loop integral of (x dy - y dx). For the ellipse x = a cos t, y = b sin t, t in [0, 2pi]: x dy - y dx = (a cos t)(b cos t dt) - (b sin t)(-a sin t dt) = ab (cos^2 t + sin^2 t) dt = ab dt Area = (1/2) int_0^{2pi} ab dt = pi a b. Correct. A planimeter, the mechanical device that measures the area of a shape by tracing its outline, is this identity built in brass. (2) Divergence theorem check on a sphere. Field F = (x, y, z), so div F = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3. V = ball of radius a, so the volume integral is 3 * (4/3) pi a^3 = 4 pi a^3. Direct flux computation: on the sphere the outward unit normal is n = r/a, so F . n = (x^2 + y^2 + z^2)/a = a^2/a = a, constant. Surface area is 4 pi a^2, so the flux is a * 4 pi a^2 = 4 pi a^3. Matches. The volume integral needed no surface geometry and the surface integral needed no interior information, which is the trade the theorem offers.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

All four theorems are one theorem

Written in the language of differential forms, the fundamental theorem of calculus, Green, Stokes, and Gauss become the single statement

$$ \int_M d\omega = \int_{\partial M} \omega, $$

where $\omega$ is a differential form, d is the exterior derivative, and $\partial M$ is the boundary of the region. Choosing the dimension of M and the degree of $\omega$ recovers each classical theorem in turn: a zero-form on an interval gives the fundamental theorem, a one-form on a plane region gives Green, a one-form on a surface gives Stokes, a two-form on a solid gives Gauss. The identities "curl of a gradient is zero" and "divergence of a curl is zero" both become $d \circ d = 0$, and the closed-versus-exact distinction from the punctured-plane example becomes the statement that $d\omega = 0$ does not force $\omega = d\eta$. The differential geometry lecture builds this machinery properly; knowing the destination while learning the classical cases makes them feel like instances rather than coincidences.

The gap between closed and exact is a topological invariant

On the punctured plane, closed one-forms that are not exact exist, and the space of them modulo the exact ones turns out to be one dimensional, reflecting the single hole. That quotient is called a de Rham cohomology group, and the general theorem is that it depends only on the topology of the domain, not on any analysis done there. This is the cleanest place in elementary mathematics where a calculus question ("does this field have a potential?") has a purely topological answer ("does the domain have a hole in the way?"). The algebraic topology lecture computes such invariants by other means and finds the same answers, which is the content of de Rham's theorem.

Conservation laws are divergence statements

Let $\rho$ be a density and $\mathbf{J}$ the corresponding flow. Apply the divergence theorem to a fixed region: the rate of decrease of the total inside equals the flux out through the boundary. Shrinking the region gives the local form $\partial \rho/\partial t + \nabla \cdot \mathbf{J} = 0$. Every conservation law in physics has this shape, and so does the evolution of a probability density: for a diffusion with drift b and diffusion coefficient $\sigma$, the flux has a drift part and a gradient part, and the resulting equation is Fokker-Planck. That is the density-level description of the same process the financial engineering course describes path by path with stochastic differential equations. Two descriptions, one process, and the bridge between them is the divergence theorem applied to probability instead of to fluid.

Watch out The commonest error in applying these theorems is ignoring where the field is undefined. Green, Stokes, and Gauss all require the field to be continuously differentiable throughout the region, and a single singular point inside the loop invalidates the conclusion, as the punctured-plane example shows numerically rather than abstractly. The second commonest is orientation: the sign of every vector line integral and every flux depends on a direction convention, and the theorems pair a specific boundary orientation with a specific surface normal. If a computed answer differs from the expected one only by a sign, check the orientation before checking the algebra.

Test yourself

  1. Compute the arc length of the helix $\mathbf{r}(t) = (\cos t, \sin t, t)$ for t from 0 to $2\pi$, and confirm the answer is independent of reparametrising by $t = 2s$.
  2. Evaluate $\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}$ for $\mathbf{F} = (2xy, x^2)$ along two different paths from $(0,0)$ to $(2,3)$. Then find a potential and recompute using the gradient theorem.
  3. State the three equivalent characterisations of a conservative field on a connected open domain, and prove that path independence implies zero circulation round every closed loop.
  4. Verify that the field $(-y, x)/(x^2+y^2)$ has zero curl, then compute its integral round the circle of radius 3 centred at the origin. Explain why the two facts are compatible.
  5. Explain why the same field integrates to zero round a small circle centred at $(5,5)$, and state the property of the domain that makes the difference.
  6. Prove that $\nabla \times \nabla \varphi = 0$ and $\nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) = 0$, naming the hypothesis both proofs need.
  7. Use Green's theorem to compute the area enclosed by the astroid $x = \cos^3 t$, $y = \sin^3 t$.
  8. Verify the divergence theorem for $\mathbf{F} = (x^2, y^2, z^2)$ over the unit cube $[0,1]^3$, computing both sides in full.
  9. State Stokes' theorem and explain why Green's theorem is the special case of it, identifying what the surface and its normal are in that case.
  10. Derive the continuity equation $\partial\rho/\partial t + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = 0$ from the divergence theorem applied to a fixed region, stating the physical assumption at each step.
