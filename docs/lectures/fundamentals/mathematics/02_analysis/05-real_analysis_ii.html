Real analysis II

The previous lecture proved everything on the real line. This one asks which of those proofs used the line and which merely used distance, and the answer is that almost none used the line. Replace the interval by a set with a distance function and the arguments survive intact, provided two properties hold: completeness, so limits exist, and compactness, so extraction of convergent subsequences is possible. The payoff is that the same theorems then apply to spaces of functions, which is where the rest of analysis happens.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A metric space is a set with a distance function satisfying three axioms: distance is zero exactly between identical points, it is symmetric, and it obeys the triangle inequality. That is enough to define open sets, limits, and continuity, and therefore enough to redo the previous lecture. Nothing about the real line was ever needed except its distance.

Two properties then decide how much survives. Completeness means every Cauchy sequence converges, so limits you can prove ought to exist actually do; it is what makes fixed-point arguments work and what allows constructions by successive approximation. Compactness means every open cover has a finite subcover, equivalently in metric spaces that every sequence has a convergent subsequence, and it is what guarantees maxima are attained and continuity is uniform. In $\mathbb{R}^n$, compactness is exactly closed and bounded, which is the Heine-Borel theorem. In infinite dimensions that equivalence fails, and repairing it is the business of functional analysis.

The last third of the lecture concerns sequences of functions, where a distance between functions is needed before "convergence" means anything. Pointwise convergence preserves almost nothing; uniform convergence, which is convergence in the supremum distance, preserves continuity and permits term-by-term integration.

Feynman test Suppose you must guarantee that a search finds the highest point in a region. On a closed bounded patch of ground the guarantee holds: the peak exists and is attained somewhere. Remove the boundary, so the region is open, and the ground can rise forever as you approach the missing edge, with no highest point anywhere inside. Keep the boundary but let the region run off to infinity, and again the ground can climb without limit. The two failures are different, and compactness is precisely the condition that rules out both at once. Every existence theorem in optimisation is this observation dressed formally, which is why the word compact appears in the hypotheses of results that seem to be about something else entirely.
What's beyond a first course A first course defines metric spaces and states Heine-Borel. This lecture adds three things that pay for themselves later. The Banach fixed point theorem is proved and then applied numerically, because it is the single most useful theorem in the lecture and it is what guarantees that iterative algorithms converge. The failure of Heine-Borel in infinite dimensions is demonstrated with the standard orthonormal-sequence argument rather than asserted, since that failure is the reason functional analysis needs weaker notions of compactness. And uniform convergence is treated with counterexamples showing exactly which conclusions fail without it.
Step 2

Why this matters

Compactness is why optimisation problems have solutions. Every claim that a maximum likelihood estimate exists, that a minimum-variance portfolio exists, or that an optimal policy exists is an application of the extreme value theorem on a compact set, and the standard technical fix when existence fails, restricting the parameter space to a closed bounded region, is an appeal to exactly this lecture. When an optimiser diverges or the likelihood is maximised only in a limit, the diagnosis is that the parameter space was not compact and the supremum was not attained.

Completeness is why iterative algorithms converge. The Banach fixed point theorem says a contraction on a complete space has exactly one fixed point, reached from any starting value, with a geometric error bound that says in advance how many iterations are needed. That result covers Newton-Raphson near a root, the fixed-point iterations behind implicit numerical schemes, policy iteration in dynamic programming, and the existence and uniqueness theorem for ordinary differential equations. In each case the algorithm converges because the space is complete and the map contracts, not because it happens to work in practice.

Uniform convergence is the licence to interchange limits with integrals. Approximating a function by a series and then integrating term by term is legitimate only under a hypothesis, and the hypothesis is uniform convergence or something stronger. Every expansion used to price an instrument or compute a moment relies on this, and the counterexamples in this lecture show that the conclusion genuinely fails when the hypothesis does. The measure theory lecture later replaces this hypothesis with a much weaker one by changing the integral, which is the main practical reason to prefer the Lebesgue integral.

The fourth row deserves attention. The same set of continuous functions is complete under the supremum distance and incomplete under the integral distance, because a sequence of continuous functions can converge in average to a discontinuous step while staying far apart in supremum. Completeness is a property of the pair, never of the set alone, and choosing the metric is therefore a modelling decision with consequences.

An open ball is $B(x,r) = \{y : d(x,y) < r\}$. A set is open when every point of it has a ball around it inside the set, and closed when its complement is open, equivalently when it contains all its limit points. Continuity has an equivalent formulation with no epsilons at all: $f$ is continuous exactly when the preimage of every open set is open. That version makes no reference to distance, which is why it becomes the definition in the topology lecture.

Completeness and the Banach fixed point theorem

A space is complete when every Cauchy sequence converges in it. The reals are complete by lecture 4, and $\mathbb{R}^n$ inherits it coordinatewise. The theorem below is the main reason to care.

BANACH FIXED POINT THEOREM. Let (X,d) be complete and non-empty, and let T : X -> X satisfy d(Tx, Ty) <= k d(x,y) for some fixed k < 1 (a contraction). Then T has exactly one fixed point, and iterating T from any starting point converges to it. Proof. Existence. Pick any x0 and set x_{n+1} = T x_n. By contraction, d(x_{n+1}, x_n) <= k d(x_n, x_{n-1}) <= ... <= k^n d(x_1, x_0). For m > n, the triangle inequality and the geometric series give d(x_m, x_n) <= sum_{j=n}^{m-1} k^j d(x_1,x_0) <= k^n /(1-k) * d(x_1,x_0). Since k < 1 this tends to 0, so the sequence is Cauchy, and completeness supplies a limit x*. T is continuous (contractions are), so taking limits in x_{n+1} = T x_n gives x* = T x*. Uniqueness. If x* and y* are both fixed, then d(x*, y*) = d(Tx*, Ty*) <= k d(x*, y*), and since k < 1 this forces d(x*,y*) = 0. Error bound (the practically useful part): d(x_n, x*) <= k^n / (1 - k) * d(x_1, x_0). The number of iterations needed for a given accuracy is known in advance. QED NUMERICAL ILLUSTRATION. Solve x = cos(x) on [0,1]. T(x) = cos x maps [0,1] into [0.54, 1], and |T'(x)| = |sin x| <= sin(1) = 0.841, so T is a contraction with k = 0.841 on that interval. x0 = 1.000000 x1 = 0.540302 x2 = 0.857553 x3 = 0.654290 x4 = 0.793480 x5 = 0.701369 x6 = 0.763960 x7 = 0.722102 x8 = 0.750418 x9 = 0.731404 ... x20 = 0.739184 x30 = 0.739087 x40 = 0.739085 Fixed point: 0.7390851332... The convergence is geometric at rate 0.841, which is slow because k is close to 1; Newton's method on the same problem contracts far harder near the root and converges in four iterations. Both are instances of this theorem, differing only in k.

The theorem's reach is wide. Picard's existence and uniqueness theorem for ordinary differential equations recasts the equation as a fixed point of an integral operator on $C[a,b]$ with the supremum metric, and completeness of that space is exactly what the proof needs. Value iteration in dynamic programming contracts because future rewards are discounted, with k the discount factor, which is why discounting is not merely economically sensible but mathematically necessary for the algorithm to converge. Implicit numerical schemes, and the fixed-point solvers inside them, are the same result again.

Compactness

An open cover of K is a family of open sets whose union contains K. K is compact when every open cover has a finite subcover. In a metric space this is equivalent to sequential compactness, that every sequence in K has a subsequence converging to a point of K, and also to being complete and totally bounded. The Heine-Borel theorem says that in $\mathbb{R}^n$, and only in finite dimensions, compactness coincides with being closed and bounded.

Two consequences, both generalising lecture 4. (1) The continuous image of a compact set is compact. Let f be continuous on compact K, and take an open cover of f(K). The preimages of the covering sets are open (continuity) and cover K, so finitely many suffice, and their images cover f(K). QED Corollary (extreme value theorem, general form): a continuous real function on a compact set is bounded and attains its bounds, because f(K) is a compact subset of R, hence closed and bounded, hence contains its supremum. This is the version that guarantees estimators and optimal portfolios exist. The parameter space must be compact; closed and bounded suffices only because it is finite dimensional. (2) Continuity on a compact set is automatically uniform. Suppose not: some eps > 0 admits points x_n, y_n with d(x_n,y_n) < 1/n but |f(x_n) - f(y_n)| >= eps. By sequential compactness pass to a subsequence with x_{n_k} -> c; then y_{n_k} -> c too, since the two sequences close on each other. Continuity at c forces both f(x_{n_k}) and f(y_{n_k}) to approach f(c), contradicting the gap of eps. QED This is the ingredient the integrability proof in lecture 4 needed, now established in general rather than only on a closed interval.

The failure in infinite dimensions, drawn in the first figure, is worth stating carefully because it drives so much later theory. In $\ell^2$, the sequence $e_1, e_2, \ldots$ of standard basis vectors lies in the closed unit ball, and any two of them are at distance $\sqrt{2}$, so no subsequence is Cauchy and none converges. The ball is closed and bounded and not compact. Since compactness is what delivers existence of optima, its loss is serious, and the repair in functional analysis is to weaken the notion of convergence until the ball becomes compact again, which is what weak compactness achieves.

Connectedness

A space is disconnected when it splits into two non-empty disjoint open sets, and connected otherwise. The continuous image of a connected set is connected, by the same preimage argument as for compactness, and the connected subsets of $\mathbb{R}$ are exactly the intervals. Put those three facts together and the intermediate value theorem falls out immediately: a continuous function on an interval has connected image, which is an interval, which therefore contains every value between any two it attains. The lecture 4 proof by suprema and this proof by connectedness are the same theorem reached from opposite directions, and the topological one generalises to any connected domain.

Sequences and series of functions

$f_n \to f$ pointwise when $f_n(x) \to f(x)$ for each fixed x, and uniformly when $\sup_x |f_n(x) - f(x)| \to 0$. Uniform convergence is convergence in the metric of $C[a,b]$, so it is ordinary convergence in a metric space, which is why it behaves well.

WEIERSTRASS M-TEST. If |u_n(x)| <= M_n for all x and sum M_n converges, then sum u_n converges uniformly. Example: sum sin(nx)/n^2 has |terms| <= 1/n^2, and sum 1/n^2 converges, so the series converges uniformly on all of R, and its sum is continuous and may be integrated term by term over any bounded interval.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Compactness is finiteness for infinite sets

Finite sets have properties that infinite sets generally lack: a finite set of numbers has a maximum, a finite collection of positive quantities has a positive minimum, and finitely many statements can be checked one at a time. Compactness is the property that recovers all of these for infinite sets, by guaranteeing that any covering argument can be reduced to a finite one. This is why the same hypothesis appears in results that appear unrelated: attainment of maxima, uniform continuity, the equivalence of norms in finite dimensions, and the existence of solutions to variational problems. When a theorem requires compactness, the underlying reason is nearly always that its proof needs to pass from infinitely many local statements to one global statement, and only finiteness permits that.

Completion is a construction, not just a property

Any incomplete metric space can be completed by adding the missing limits, formally by taking equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences under "difference tends to zero", which is the quotient construction from foundations lecture 3 applied again. Completing $\mathbb{Q}$ produces $\mathbb{R}$, which is one of the two standard constructions of the reals. Completing the continuous functions under the integral metric produces the Lebesgue space $L^1$, and under the square-integral metric produces $L^2$. This is the honest answer to why measure theory is needed: the spaces analysis actually wants to work in are completions of familiar spaces, and describing their elements requires an integral more general than Riemann's. The measure theory lecture supplies it, and functional analysis then works in the completed spaces throughout.

The right notion of convergence depends on what must be preserved

This lecture presents two modes, pointwise and uniform, and shows that uniform is strong enough to preserve continuity and permit interchange with integrals. The full picture is a hierarchy. Uniform convergence implies convergence in $L^p$ on bounded domains, which implies convergence in measure, which implies a subsequence converging almost everywhere; and pointwise convergence sits off to the side, implied by none of them in general. Each mode preserves different properties and is the natural one for different purposes: uniform for approximation theory, $L^2$ for Fourier analysis and projections, almost everywhere for probability, and weak convergence for distributional limits, which is what convergence in distribution means in the statistics course. Learning which mode a theorem requires is most of the skill of applying analysis correctly, and the reason so many results carry hypotheses that look decorative until a counterexample arrives.

Watch out Two errors recur. The first is assuming closed and bounded means compact in a space of functions or sequences, where it does not, and where the failure is not a technicality but the reason a natural optimisation problem may have no solution. The second is interchanging a limit with an integral or a derivative without checking a hypothesis. Pointwise convergence licenses neither, uniform convergence licenses the integral swap but not the derivative swap, and the derivative swap needs uniform convergence of the derivatives themselves. When a numerical result disagrees with an analytic one, an unjustified interchange is among the first things to check.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Verify that the discrete metric satisfies the three axioms, then describe its open balls and explain why every subset is both open and closed.
  2. Show that $C[0,1]$ is incomplete under the metric $\int_0^1 |f-g|$ by exhibiting a Cauchy sequence of continuous functions whose limit is a step function.
  3. Prove that a contraction is continuous, then prove the Banach fixed point theorem in full, including the error bound.
  4. Apply the fixed point theorem to $x = e^{-x}$ on $[0,1]$: verify the contraction constant, iterate to four decimal places, and compare the observed convergence rate to the predicted $k^n$.
  5. Prove that the continuous image of a compact set is compact, and deduce the extreme value theorem from it.
  6. Show that the closed unit ball of $\ell^2$ is closed and bounded but not compact, computing the distance between two distinct basis vectors.
  7. Prove that a continuous function on a compact metric space is uniformly continuous, and identify where sequential compactness is used.
  8. Prove the intermediate value theorem using connectedness, and say how this proof differs in reach from the supremum proof of lecture 4.
  9. For $f_n(x) = nx e^{-nx^2}$ on $[0,1]$, find the pointwise limit, compute $\sup_x |f_n|$, decide whether convergence is uniform, and compare $\lim \int f_n$ with $\int \lim f_n$.
  10. Use the Weierstrass M-test to prove that $\sum \cos(nx)/n^2$ converges uniformly on $\mathbb{R}$, and state one conclusion about the sum that the test licenses and one it does not.
