Lectures / Mathematics / Geometry and topology

Point-set topology

Real analysis II showed that almost every argument about limits used the distance function only through which sets were open. This lecture takes the hint and throws the distance away, keeping the open sets as the primitive notion. What survives is everything about continuity, compactness, and connectedness; what is lost is any notion of how far apart two points are. The trade is worth making because it identifies the minimum structure limits require, and because some spaces that matter carry no useful metric at all.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A topology on a set is a collection of subsets, called open, containing the empty set and the whole space, closed under arbitrary unions and under finite intersections. That asymmetry between unions and intersections is the one design decision in the definition, and it is forced: infinite intersections of open intervals can shrink to a single point, which should not be open.

Everything is then defined by open sets. A map is continuous when preimages of open sets are open, which drops the epsilons entirely. A space is compact when every open cover has a finite subcover, connected when it does not split into two disjoint non-empty open pieces, and Hausdorff when distinct points have disjoint neighbourhoods. Compactness is preserved by continuous maps, which gives the extreme value theorem; connectedness likewise, which gives the intermediate value theorem; and Hausdorff is exactly the condition making limits unique.

Feynman test Suppose all you can do is ask, of any point and any region, whether the region contains a bit of space all around that point. You cannot measure distances and you cannot compare them. Can you still say what it means for a function to be continuous? Yes: it means that whenever the output lands well inside a region, every nearby input also lands inside it. Can you say what a limit is? Yes: eventually the sequence enters every region surrounding the limit. What you cannot say is that one point is twice as far from a third as another. Topology is exactly the set of statements that survive when the ruler is taken away, and it turns out that all the important theorems about limits do.
What's beyond a first course Scope is set by what the analysis course needed and could not fully justify. Tychonoff's theorem is stated because infinite products are how stochastic processes on infinite index sets are constructed, and because it is equivalent to the axiom of choice met in foundations lecture 4. Weak topologies are treated because they are how functional analysis recovers the compactness that fails in infinite dimensions. The topologist's sine curve is worked out because it is the standard demonstration that connected and path-connected genuinely differ.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first return is clarity about the analysis already done. Compactness in real analysis II had three descriptions that agreed in metric spaces and one, the covering definition, that looked strange. Here it is the definition, and the reason becomes visible: it is the formulation that says "finitely many local facts suffice", which is what every compactness proof actually uses. Similarly, continuity by preimages explains why continuous images of compact and connected sets inherit those properties, in one line each, where the metric proofs needed sequences.

The second is that some spaces analysis needs have no useful metric. The weak topology on an infinite-dimensional space is defined by declaring which functionals must stay continuous, not by a distance, and Banach-Alaoglu's compactness of the dual unit ball holds in that topology and fails in the norm topology. Distributions, used for generalised functions and for the delta function that appears throughout applied probability, live in a space topologised by seminorms rather than a metric. Without the language of this lecture, those objects cannot be defined at all.

The third is infinite products. Kolmogorov's construction of a stochastic process on an uncountable index set builds a measure on a product of infinitely many copies of the line, and the product topology is what makes that product a space rather than a set. Tychonoff's theorem, that arbitrary products of compact spaces are compact, is what keeps such constructions tractable. This is the point at which topology stops being an abstraction of analysis and starts being a prerequisite for it.

Step 4

The detail

Topologies and bases

Topological space
A set X with a collection $\tau$ of subsets such that $\emptyset$ and X lie in $\tau$, arbitrary unions of members of $\tau$ lie in $\tau$, and intersections of finitely many members lie in $\tau$. Members of $\tau$ are the open sets; their complements are closed. A set may be both, or neither.

Specifying every open set is impractical, so topologies are usually given by a basis: a collection of sets such that every open set is a union of them. The standard topology on the line has the open intervals as a basis; the standard topology on the plane has open discs. Two bases generate the same topology when each member of one is a union of members of the other, which is how one proves that open rectangles and open discs give the same topology on the plane, so nothing depends on that choice.

Four constructions produce new spaces from old. The subspace topology on $A \subseteq X$ takes intersections of open sets of X with A. The product topology has as basis the products of open sets, with all but finitely many factors equal to the whole space. The quotient topology on a set of equivalence classes declares a set open when its preimage is open, which is the quotient construction from foundations lecture 3 appearing for the fifth time. And the weak topology generated by a family of maps is the coarsest making them all continuous.

The finiteness clause in the product topology looks arbitrary and is not. The alternative, the box topology, takes all products of open sets, and under it the projection maps are still continuous but Tychonoff's theorem fails and the diagonal map into an infinite product stops being continuous. The product topology is defined as it is precisely so that a map into a product is continuous exactly when each of its components is, which is the universal property of a product in the sense of category theory.

Continuity without distance

The two definitions of continuity agree. TOPOLOGICAL: f : X -> Y is continuous when the preimage of every open set is open. METRIC (epsilon-delta): for every x and every eps > 0 there is delta > 0 with d(x,x') < delta implying d(f(x), f(x')) < eps. (=>) Assume the topological version. Fix x and eps. The ball B(f(x), eps) is open in Y, so its preimage is open in X and contains x, so it contains a ball B(x, delta). Any x' within delta then has f(x') within eps. (<=) Assume the metric version and let V be open in Y. Take x in the preimage of V. Since V is open it contains a ball B(f(x), eps), and the metric condition supplies delta with f(B(x,delta)) inside B(f(x),eps), hence inside V. So B(x,delta) lies in the preimage, which is therefore open. QED WHY THE TOPOLOGICAL FORM IS BETTER. Three one-line proofs that the metric version makes into paragraphs: - A composition of continuous maps is continuous: the preimage of an open set under g o f is the preimage under f of the preimage under g, and both steps preserve openness. - The continuous image of a compact set is compact: pull an open cover of the image back to an open cover of the domain, take a finite subcover, push it forward. - The continuous image of a connected set is connected: a splitting of the image pulls back to a splitting of the domain. Those three lines contain the extreme value theorem and the intermediate value theorem as corollaries, which took most of real analysis I to prove with sequences.

A homeomorphism is a continuous bijection with continuous inverse, and it is the notion of sameness in topology. Properties preserved by homeomorphisms are topological invariants: compactness, connectedness, the Hausdorff condition, and the number of connected components are all invariant, while boundedness, completeness, and distance are not, since they are metric rather than topological. The open interval and the whole line are homeomorphic, which shows immediately that boundedness is not topological.

Compactness

Compact and non-compact, from the definition. [0,1] IS COMPACT. Let U be an open cover, and let S = { x in [0,1] : [0,x] is covered by finitely many members of U }. S contains 0 and is bounded, so c = sup S exists (completeness, from real analysis I). Some member of U contains c, and being open it contains an interval around c, so a finite subcover of [0, c - e] plus that one set covers [0, c + e] for small e. If c < 1 this puts points above c into S, contradicting the supremum; so c = 1 and 1 is in S. QED (0,1) IS NOT COMPACT, by exhibiting a cover with no finite subcover: U_n = (1/n, 1) for n = 2, 3, 4, ... Every point of (0,1) lies in some U_n, so this is a cover. Any finite subcollection has a largest n, and points below 1/n are then uncovered. No finite subcover exists. The failure is exactly at the missing endpoint: the cover creeps towards 0 without ever reaching it, and there is no point there to be caught. TYCHONOFF'S THEOREM. An arbitrary product of compact spaces is compact in the product topology. For finitely many factors this is elementary. For infinitely many it is equivalent to the axiom of choice, which is a striking fact: a statement about compactness turns out to be exactly as strong as a statement about simultaneous selection. It is also why the product topology's finiteness clause matters, since the theorem is false for the box topology. Where it is used: constructing probability measures on infinite products, which is how a stochastic process indexed by an uncountable time set is built, and proving Banach-Alaoglu in functional analysis.

Connectedness

A space is connected when it cannot be written as a union of two disjoint non-empty open sets, and path-connected when any two points are joined by a continuous path. Path-connected implies connected; the converse fails, and the standard counterexample is worth seeing because it shows how much room the definitions leave.

The topologist's sine curve: connected but not path-connected. Take the set S = { (x, sin(1/x)) : 0 < x <= 1 } union { (0, y) : -1 <= y <= 1 }. The curve oscillates faster and faster as x approaches 0, and the vertical segment is glued on at the left. CONNECTED. The curve part is the continuous image of an interval, hence connected. The vertical segment lies in its closure, since every neighbourhood of a point (0,y) meets the oscillating curve, the oscillation passing through every height between -1 and 1 infinitely often. Adding limit points to a connected set preserves connectedness, so S is connected. NOT PATH-CONNECTED. Suppose a continuous path runs from (0,0) to a point on the curve. Near the start the path must stay within a small ball, by continuity. But to reach any point with x > 0 it must traverse the oscillation, which forces the vertical coordinate to swing between -1 and 1 within any interval of x approaching 0, so the path cannot remain in a small ball. No such path exists. WHY IT MATTERS. Connectedness is about the space having no separation; path-connectedness is about being able to travel. They coincide for open subsets of Euclidean space, which is why the distinction is invisible in elementary settings, and they diverge exactly when a limit set is approached by infinite oscillation. The intermediate value theorem needs only connectedness, which is why it holds in the weaker setting.

Separation axioms

AxiomConditionWhat it buys
$T_1$Points are closedFinite sets are closed; the cofinite topology satisfies this and no more
$T_2$ (Hausdorff)Distinct points have disjoint neighbourhoodsLimits are unique; compact subsets are closed. Every metric space is Hausdorff.
$T_3$ (regular)A point and a closed set not containing it can be separatedNeeded for metrisation results
$T_4$ (normal)Two disjoint closed sets can be separatedUrysohn's lemma: continuous functions exist separating them

Hausdorff is the one that matters in practice, and its role is exactly to make limits well behaved: in a non-Hausdorff space a sequence can converge to several points at once, as the indiscrete topology in the second figure shows, where every sequence converges to everything. Every metric space is Hausdorff, so the condition is invisible until non-metric topologies appear, at which point it becomes the first thing to check. Urysohn's metrisation theorem closes the circle: a second-countable regular Hausdorff space is metrisable, so under mild hypotheses the abstraction collapses back to a metric, and the extra generality is only needed for spaces failing those hypotheses, which is precisely where the interesting cases live.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Weakening the topology to buy compactness

Real analysis II showed the closed unit ball of an infinite-dimensional space is not compact, which threatens every existence proof by subsequence extraction. This lecture supplies the repair mechanism, and it is now visible as a deliberate move on the dial from the second figure. Take the weak-star topology on a dual space, defined as the coarsest topology making all evaluation maps continuous. Coarser means fewer open sets, which means covers are harder to refine, which makes compactness easier, and Banach-Alaoglu states that the closed unit ball is compact in it. The price is that convergence becomes weaker, so a weakly convergent sequence need not converge in norm and continuous functionals must be replaced by weakly continuous ones. Existence proofs in the calculus of variations, optimal control, and optimal stopping all follow this pattern: weaken the topology until compactness returns, extract a limit, then show the limit solves the problem. Recognising it as a topology choice rather than a technical trick is what this lecture adds.

The one genuine data application

Topological data analysis uses these ideas directly. Given a point cloud, build a simplicial complex by joining points within distance $\epsilon$, and watch how the topology of that complex changes as $\epsilon$ grows: features that persist across a wide range of scales are taken as real structure, and features that appear and vanish quickly as noise. The output, a persistence diagram, is a topological summary of the data invariant under continuous deformation and robust to small perturbations. It has been applied to sensor networks, to the shape of gene expression data, and to detecting structure in high-dimensional point clouds where distance-based clustering struggles. The invariants being computed are homology groups, which the algebraic topology lecture defines. This is a genuine and current application, and it is the only one in this lecture, which is a fair reflection of the field.

What the abstraction is for

A reasonable objection is that Urysohn's theorem returns most spaces to metric ones, so why abandon the metric at all. Three answers. The spaces that resist metrisation are the ones analysis needs most, including weak topologies and spaces of distributions. Even in metric spaces, the topological proofs are shorter and reveal which hypothesis is doing the work, as the three one-line arguments in step 4 show. And the topological framing is what makes invariance meaningful: asking which properties survive continuous deformation is only a sensible question once continuity is defined without reference to a metric that the deformation would destroy. That question is the whole subject of the remaining two lectures.

Watch out Two errors. Assuming sequences characterise a topology: in metric spaces closure and continuity can be tested with sequences, but in general spaces they cannot, and nets or filters are needed instead. This bites in weak topologies, where sequential arguments quietly fail. And assuming compact implies closed and bounded: closed and bounded is a metric statement, and in a general topological space compact subsets need not even be closed unless the space is Hausdorff.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Verify the topology axioms for the cofinite topology on an infinite set, and determine whether it is Hausdorff.
  2. Give an example showing that an infinite intersection of open sets need not be open, and explain why the axioms are stated with finite intersections.
  3. Prove that the topological and metric definitions of continuity agree, in both directions.
  4. Prove in one line each that continuous images of compact sets are compact and of connected sets are connected, and deduce the extreme and intermediate value theorems.
  5. Show that $(0,1)$ is not compact by exhibiting an explicit open cover with no finite subcover, and identify where the corresponding argument for $[0,1]$ uses completeness.
  6. Show that the open interval and the real line are homeomorphic, and say which of boundedness, completeness, and compactness are therefore not topological invariants.
  7. Describe the topologist's sine curve and prove it is connected but not path-connected.
  8. State the difference between the product and box topologies on an infinite product, and give one theorem that holds for the first and fails for the second.
  9. Prove that limits are unique in a Hausdorff space, and give a space where a sequence converges to two different points.
  10. Explain how weakening a topology can make a set compact, and name the theorem that does this for the unit ball of a dual space.
