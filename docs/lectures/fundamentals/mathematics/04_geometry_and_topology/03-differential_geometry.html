Lectures / Mathematics / Geometry and topology

Differential geometry

Topology kept only nearness; this lecture puts smoothness back, so calculus can be done on a curved space. The central discovery is Gauss's: a surface has a curvature that its inhabitants can measure without ever leaving it, by comparing circumferences to radii or angles to 180 degrees. Curvature is intrinsic. That single theorem is why no flat map of the Earth can be accurate, why general relativity can describe gravity as geometry, and why a statistical model family has a shape.

Step 1

The 30-second version

For a curve, curvature measures how fast the direction of travel turns per unit length, and torsion measures how fast the curve twists out of its own plane. For a surface, the same idea splits: at each point there are two principal curvatures, the extreme rates of bending in different directions, and their product is the Gaussian curvature K, positive on a sphere, zero on a plane or cylinder, negative on a saddle.

Gauss's theorema egregium states that K is intrinsic: it can be computed from measurements made within the surface, with no reference to any surrounding space. Two consequences dominate the lecture. Surfaces with different curvature cannot be bent onto one another without stretching, so a sphere admits no flat map. And the Gauss-Bonnet theorem ties total curvature to topology, $\int K \, dA = 2\pi\chi$, so a purely geometric integral yields a number that no deformation can change. The lecture ends with manifolds and differential forms, where the generalised Stokes theorem absorbs all four theorems of vector calculus.

Feynman test You are a two-dimensional creature living in a surface, with no notion of an outside. Can you tell whether your world is curved? Yes, and here is how. Walk out a distance r in every direction and measure the circumference of the circle you have traced. On a flat world you get $2\pi r$. On a sphere you get less, because the directions converge. On a saddle you get more, because they splay apart. Every measurement is made inside the surface with a tape measure, and yet the answer distinguishes the three cases. That is Gauss's theorem, and it is why gravity can be curvature: nobody needs to step outside the universe to detect it.
What's beyond a first course A first course computes curvature for curves and surfaces and states Gauss-Bonnet. This lecture adds the two payoffs this repository can use. The theorema egregium is applied to map projections, giving a precise statement of why every world map lies and what each kind of projection chooses to preserve. And information geometry is developed far enough to explain the natural gradient: a statistical model family is a manifold, the Fisher information is a Riemannian metric on it, and steepest descent with respect to that metric is invariant to how the parameters happen to be written.
Step 2

Why this matters

Information geometry is the application with real traction. A parametric family of distributions is a manifold whose points are distributions and whose coordinates are the parameters, and the Fisher information matrix is a Riemannian metric on it. Two facts follow that are otherwise mysterious. Kullback-Leibler divergence between nearby distributions equals half the squared Fisher distance to second order, so the information metric is the local geometry of statistical distinguishability rather than of parameter values. And ordinary gradient descent is not parametrisation invariant, since rewriting a model in terms of different but equivalent parameters changes the path it takes, whereas the natural gradient, which premultiplies by the inverse Fisher matrix, is invariant. That is the geometric justification for natural gradient methods and for the Adam-style adaptive scalings that approximate them cheaply.

Optimisation on manifolds is the second. Some parameters are constrained to curved sets by their meaning: correlation matrices must be positive definite with unit diagonal, rotation matrices must be orthogonal, subspace estimates live on a Grassmannian. Treating these as constrained problems in the ambient space fights the constraint at every step; treating the constraint set as a manifold and moving along geodesics respects it automatically. Manifold optimisation libraries do exactly this, and covariance estimation under structure is one of the standard applications.

The third is the unification promised in vector calculus. Green, Gauss, classical Stokes, and the fundamental theorem of calculus become one statement about differential forms, which is worth having if only because it removes four separate results from memory and replaces them with one and a dictionary.

Step 4

The detail

Curves

Parametrise a curve by arc length, so the velocity $T$ is a unit vector. Then curvature is $\kappa = \|dT/ds\|$, the rate at which direction turns per unit distance travelled. The unit vector in the direction of $dT/ds$ is the normal N, and $B = T \times N$ is the binormal; torsion $\tau$ measures the rate at which B turns, meaning how fast the curve escapes the plane spanned by T and N. The Frenet-Serret formulas package this: $T' = \kappa N$, $N' = -\kappa T + \tau B$, $B' = -\tau N$. Curvature and torsion as functions of arc length determine a space curve completely up to rigid motion, which is the fundamental theorem of curves.

Curvature computed twice. CIRCLE of radius a: r(t) = (a cos t, a sin t). Speed |r'| = a, so arc length s = at and t = s/a. Unit tangent T = (-sin(s/a), cos(s/a)). dT/ds = (-cos(s/a)/a, -sin(s/a)/a), magnitude 1/a. So kappa = 1/a: small circles are highly curved, large ones nearly straight, and the "radius of curvature" 1/kappa recovers a exactly. HELIX: r(t) = (a cos t, a sin t, bt). |r'| = sqrt(a^2 + b^2) =: c, constant. Working through the derivatives gives kappa = a / c^2 = a / (a^2 + b^2), tau = b / c^2 = b / (a^2 + b^2). Both constant, which characterises the helix: it is the only curve with constant non-zero curvature and constant non-zero torsion. Sanity checks: b = 0 collapses the helix to a circle of radius a, and the formulas give kappa = 1/a and tau = 0, correct. Letting a -> 0 with b fixed gives a straight vertical line, and both kappa and tau tend to 0.

Surfaces and the two fundamental forms

A parametrised surface has at each point a tangent plane. The first fundamental form is the restriction of the dot product to that plane, written in coordinates as a symmetric $2\times2$ matrix; it is the metric, and it determines lengths of curves, angles, and areas within the surface. Everything computable from the first fundamental form alone is intrinsic.

The second fundamental form measures how the surface bends away from its tangent plane, by differentiating the unit normal. Its eigenvalues are the principal curvatures $\kappa_1, \kappa_2$, the extreme normal curvatures over all tangent directions, and its eigenvectors are the principal directions. Two combinations matter: the Gaussian curvature $K = \kappa_1\kappa_2$, which is the determinant of the shape operator, and the mean curvature $H = (\kappa_1+\kappa_2)/2$, which is half its trace. Mean curvature is extrinsic and governs soap films, which minimise area and therefore have $H = 0$.

THEOREMA EGREGIUM and its consequence for maps. Gauss's remarkable theorem: K can be computed from the first fundamental form alone, so it is intrinsic, even though its definition used the normal vector pointing out of the surface. CHECK ON THE CYLINDER. A cylinder of radius a has principal curvatures 1/a (around) and 0 (along), so K = 0. And indeed a cylinder can be cut and unrolled flat with no stretching: its intrinsic geometry IS the plane's. Circles drawn on it have circumference exactly 2 pi r, and triangles sum to 180 degrees. Extrinsic bending without intrinsic curvature. CHECK ON THE SPHERE. A sphere of radius a has both principal curvatures 1/a, so K = 1/a^2 > 0 everywhere. By the theorem this is intrinsic, so no isometry to the plane exists. CONSEQUENCE: EVERY WORLD MAP LIES. Since K = 1/a^2 for the Earth and K = 0 for paper, no map can preserve all distances; some distortion is mandatory, and cartographic projections differ only in what they choose to sacrifice. Mercator preserves angles (conformal), so shapes are locally right and areas are catastrophically wrong: Greenland appears comparable to Africa while being about a fourteenth of its area. Gall-Peters preserves area, at the cost of shape: landmasses are visibly stretched. Azimuthal equidistant preserves distances from one chosen centre only. There is no projection preserving both area and angle, and this is not an engineering shortfall: it is the theorema egregium. The same statement explains why an orange peel cannot be flattened without tearing and why paper can be rolled into a cylinder or cone but never wrapped smoothly onto a ball.

Geodesics

A geodesic is a curve whose acceleration has no component within the surface: it is as straight as the surface permits, and it locally minimises length. On a sphere geodesics are great circles, which is why flight paths between distant cities arc towards the poles on a Mercator map; on a cylinder they are helices, straight lines, and circles, which is exactly what straight lines on the unrolled plane become when rolled back up. Geodesics are the surface's substitute for straight lines, and in general relativity free-falling bodies follow the geodesics of curved spacetime, which is how gravity becomes geometry rather than force.

Gauss-Bonnet

Gauss-Bonnet theorem
For a compact surface without boundary, $\int_S K \, dA = 2\pi\chi(S)$, where $\chi$ is the Euler characteristic, computable as vertices minus edges plus faces for any triangulation. The local version, for a geodesic triangle, states that the integral of K over the triangle equals the angle excess, the sum of its angles minus $\pi$.
Gauss-Bonnet checked twice, and the link to lecture 1. SPHERE of radius a. K = 1/a^2 everywhere, and the area is 4 pi a^2. integral of K dA = (1/a^2)(4 pi a^2) = 4 pi. Euler characteristic: triangulate as a tetrahedron inflated onto the sphere, giving V = 4, E = 6, F = 4, so chi = 4 - 6 + 4 = 2. 2 pi chi = 4 pi. Agrees. TORUS. Euler characteristic from a triangulation, or from the standard square gluing: chi = 0. So the theorem predicts total curvature zero. Directly: the outer half of the tube has K > 0, the inner half has K < 0, and integrating around shows they cancel exactly. Agrees. THE LOCAL VERSION AND LECTURE 1. For a geodesic triangle, integral of K over the triangle = (sum of angles) - pi. On a sphere (K > 0) the angle sum exceeds pi; on a hyperbolic surface (K < 0) it falls short, and the deficit is proportional to area because K is constant. That is exactly the "defect proportional to area" result asserted in the axiomatic geometry lecture, now derived rather than stated, with the constant identified as -1/K. The axiomatic trichotomy of lecture 1 and the curvature sign of this lecture are the same classification reached from two directions, and curvature is the stronger tool because it allows K to vary from point to point, which no system of axioms about parallels can express.

Manifolds and differential forms

A smooth manifold is a topological space, in the sense of lecture 2, that looks locally like $\mathbb{R}^n$ via charts whose overlaps are smooth. This is the setting in which calculus works without any surrounding space, and each point carries a tangent space, a vector space of directions of travel. A Riemannian metric is a smoothly varying inner product on those tangent spaces, which is the first fundamental form generalised, and it supplies lengths, angles, geodesics, and curvature.

Differential forms are the objects that integrate over submanifolds: a 1-form integrates along curves, a 2-form over surfaces, an n-form over n-dimensional regions. The exterior derivative d raises degree by one and satisfies $d^2 = 0$, which is the single statement behind "curl of a gradient is zero" and "divergence of a curl is zero" from vector calculus. The generalised Stokes theorem then reads

$$ \int_M d\omega = \int_{\partial M} \omega. $$

Chooseand $\int_M d\omega = \int_{\partial M}\omega$ becomes
$M$ an interval, $\omega$ a 0-form (a function)The fundamental theorem of calculus, $\int_a^b f' = f(b)-f(a)$
$M$ a plane region, $\omega$ a 1-formGreen's theorem
$M$ a surface in space, $\omega$ a 1-formThe classical Stokes theorem
$M$ a solid region, $\omega$ a 2-formThe divergence theorem of Gauss

Four theorems, one equation, one table. The promise made in the vector calculus lecture is discharged here, and the reason the classical statements looked like a family is that they were four coordinate expressions of a single fact about derivatives and boundaries.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Information geometry, and why the natural gradient is natural

Take a parametric family $p(x \mid \theta)$ and treat the parameter space as a manifold whose points are distributions. The right metric on it is not the Euclidean one on $\theta$, because the parametrisation is arbitrary: a normal family parametrised by variance and by log variance describe the same distributions with different coordinates, and no geometric conclusion should depend on which was written down. The Fisher information matrix supplies the invariant answer, and it behaves exactly as a Riemannian metric.

Three facts that follow from the geometric reading. (1) KL DIVERGENCE IS THE SQUARED FISHER DISTANCE, LOCALLY. Expanding the Kullback-Leibler divergence between nearby members of the family gives KL( p(theta) || p(theta + d) ) = (1/2) d' I(theta) d + O(|d|^3), with I the Fisher information matrix. So KL, which is not symmetric and is not a distance, is nonetheless generated to second order by a genuine Riemannian metric. That is why KL behaves like a squared distance in small-step arguments and fails to be one globally. (2) ORDINARY GRADIENT DESCENT IS NOT PARAMETRISATION INVARIANT. The gradient of a loss with respect to theta depends on the coordinates chosen: reparametrise and the steepest-descent direction changes, so two statistically identical models train differently. This is a real effect, not a technicality; it is why scaling of inputs and parameters matters so much in practice. (3) THE NATURAL GRADIENT IS INVARIANT. Define the step direction as I(theta)^(-1) grad L instead of grad L. This is the direction of steepest descent when distance is measured by the Fisher metric rather than by the Euclidean metric on coordinates, and it is unchanged by any smooth reparametrisation. Amari's natural gradient is exactly this, and its cost is forming and inverting I, which is why practical methods approximate it: diagonal approximations, Kronecker-factored approximations, and to some extent the per-coordinate scalings inside adaptive optimisers are all cheap stand-ins for the inverse Fisher matrix. The connection back to lecture 2 of the analysis course: the condition number of the Hessian governed gradient descent's convergence there, and the natural gradient is the statement that the right preconditioner is not an arbitrary rescaling but the metric the problem already carries.

Intrinsic versus extrinsic is the lecture's deepest idea

The theorema egregium separates two kinds of property: those an inhabitant can measure and those requiring a view from outside. Mean curvature is extrinsic; Gaussian curvature is intrinsic. The distinction generalises far beyond surfaces. General relativity is possible because spacetime curvature is intrinsic, so no embedding of the universe in a higher-dimensional space is needed to make sense of gravity. In statistics, the same distinction separates properties of a model family from artefacts of its parametrisation, which is the content of the natural gradient discussion above. In each case the discipline is to ask which quantities survive a change of description, and it is the same invariance question posed by similarity in linear algebra II and by the Erlangen programme in lecture 1.

Where the course's two halves meet

Gauss-Bonnet is the first result in this track where a geometric integral produces a topological invariant, and it is the template for a large body of mathematics. Curvature can be redistributed freely by deforming the surface, yet its total is pinned by the Euler characteristic, which no deformation changes. The general phenomenon, that certain analytic integrals compute topological quantities, culminates in the Atiyah-Singer index theorem, which relates solutions of differential equations on a manifold to its topology and is one of the major results of the twentieth century. For this course, Gauss-Bonnet is the handover: the final lecture computes Euler characteristics and their relatives directly, by algebraic means, and this lecture has just shown that geometry can compute them too.

Watch out Two errors. Confusing extrinsic with intrinsic curvature: a cylinder is visibly bent and intrinsically flat, so "looks curved" is not a criterion, and the test is whether circles have circumference $2\pi r$. And treating the Fisher information as merely a variance bound: it is that, via the Cramer-Rao inequality, but the geometric reading is what explains parametrisation invariance, and the two facts are the same object seen from different sides.
