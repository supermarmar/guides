Geometry and topology

Four lectures on space and what survives deforming it: axiomatic geometry from Euclid's postulates through the hyperbolic models that show the parallel postulate is independent, then point-set topology, differential geometry, and algebraic topology. Only the first lecture comes from the UP degree (WTW 389). The other three go beyond it and complete the branch, because a geometry course that stops at the hyperbolic plane leaves the modern subject untouched.

What this course is

Geometry starts as the study of figures under rigid motion and becomes the study of what remains true as the rules are weakened. Euclid fixed five postulates; the first lecture shows that the fifth is independent of the others, so there are consistent geometries in which parallels are not unique, and the intuition that space must be flat quietly dies. Topology weakens the rules much further, discarding distance entirely and keeping only the notion of nearness, and then asks which properties survive. Differential geometry reinstates smoothness, so calculus can be done on a curved surface. Algebraic topology attaches a group to a space and turns questions about shape into questions about algebra, which are decidable.

This is the course with the weakest direct claim on a risk modeller's time, and it is fair to say so plainly. Its return is indirect: the topology lecture is what makes the compactness and convergence arguments of analysis feel inevitable rather than technical, the differential geometry lecture is where the classical Green, Gauss, and Stokes theorems turn out to be one theorem, and the manifold language it establishes is the language in which information geometry and modern optimisation on constrained spaces are written.

Prerequisites

This is the most demanding course in the track in terms of what it assumes, and every prerequisite is genuinely used.

  • Analysis, lecture 5: metric spaces, open and closed sets, compactness, connectedness. Lecture 2 here is that material with the metric removed, so reading it first is not optional.
  • Analysis, lecture 3: vector calculus and the classical Green, Gauss, and Stokes theorems. Lecture 3 here generalises them.
  • Algebra, lecture 3: groups, homomorphisms, and quotients. Lecture 4 here is an extended application of them.

How to read this

Lecture 1 is independent of the rest and can be read on its own; it is the historically motivated one and the most enjoyable. Lectures 2, 3, and 4 are sequential and each is a serious course in its own right, so treat the single-lecture framing as a map rather than a substitute for a textbook. A reader whose interest is the analysis track should read lecture 2 and stop. Course sequence

Part 1 · Geometry from axioms (lecture 1)

What happens when a two-thousand-year-old assumption is dropped.

  • builtLecture 1: axiomatic geometry. Neutral geometry from Euclid's postulates. The parallel postulate and the proof of its independence. Euclidean geometry. Hyperbolic geometry and its models, including the Poincare disc and the upper half-plane. Isometries and transformation groups. (WTW 389)

Part 2 · Topology and smoothness (lectures 2 to 3)

Continuity without distance, then calculus on curved spaces.

  • builtLecture 2: point-set topology. Topological spaces, bases, subspaces. Continuity defined by preimages rather than epsilons. Product and quotient topologies. Separation axioms and the Hausdorff condition. Compactness and Tychonoff's theorem. Connectedness and path-connectedness. Metrisation. (extension, generalising analysis 05)
  • builtLecture 3: differential geometry. Curves, curvature, and torsion. Surfaces in R^3, the first and second fundamental forms. The Gauss map, Gaussian curvature, and the theorema egregium. Geodesics and the Gauss-Bonnet theorem. Smooth manifolds, tangent spaces, differential forms, and the generalised Stokes theorem. (extension, generalising analysis 03)

Part 3 · Algebra applied to shape (lecture 4)

Turning an undecidable geometric question into a computable algebraic one.

  • builtLecture 4: algebraic topology. Homotopy and homotopy equivalence. The fundamental group, covering spaces, and the Seifert-van Kampen theorem. Simplicial and singular homology. The classification of compact surfaces. Euler characteristic, and the Brouwer fixed-point theorem as a corollary. (extension)

Where this course is used later

Less than the other three courses, and the honest summary is that this one is read for understanding rather than for use. The concrete exceptions are worth naming. Compactness from lecture 2 is the hypothesis under which optimisation problems are guaranteed a solution, which sits underneath every existence claim in estimation. The manifold and tangent-space language of lecture 3 is what information geometry uses to treat a parametric model family as a curved surface, which is the natural setting for the Fisher information metric that statistics introduces flat. The Brouwer fixed-point theorem from lecture 4 is the tool that proves general equilibria and Nash equilibria exist, which connects to the economics track. Beyond those, the return on this course is conceptual, and the track landing page says so rather than pretending otherwise.
