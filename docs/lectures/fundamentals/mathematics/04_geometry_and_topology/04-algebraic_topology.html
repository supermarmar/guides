Algebraic topology

Proving two spaces are homeomorphic means exhibiting a map. Proving they are not means ruling out every possible map, which is hopeless directly. The method of this lecture is to attach an algebraic object to each space in a way that respects continuous maps, so that non-isomorphic algebra proves non-homeomorphic spaces. Questions about shape, which have no obvious method, become questions about groups, which are computable. The last lecture of the track, and the one where the two halves of the course finally meet.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Two loops in a space are homotopic when one can be continuously deformed into the other. Fixing a base point and taking loops up to homotopy gives a group, the fundamental group $\pi_1$, with concatenation as the operation. For a disc it is trivial, since every loop contracts to a point; for a circle or an annulus it is $\mathbb{Z}$, counting how many times a loop winds round the hole.

The construction is a functor in the sense of category theory: a continuous map of spaces induces a homomorphism of groups, and a homeomorphism induces an isomorphism. That is the whole method. If two spaces have non-isomorphic fundamental groups, no homeomorphism between them exists, and a hopeless search is replaced by a finite computation. Homology extends this to higher dimensions, counting holes of each dimension, and the alternating sum of its ranks is the Euler characteristic that Gauss-Bonnet computed geometrically.

Feynman test Take a rubber band and lay it on a table. You can always shrink it to a point without lifting it. Now lay it on a table with a pole sticking out, and loop it once around the pole. No amount of sliding will shrink it to a point: the pole is in the way. Loop it twice and you have a genuinely different situation from looping it once, and looping it clockwise then anticlockwise cancels back to nothing. Winding numbers add, they have inverses, and doing nothing is one of them: the loops around the pole form a group, and that group is the integers. The pole is a hole, and the group counted it without anyone ever measuring anything.
What's beyond a first course A first course defines the fundamental group and computes a few examples. This lecture presses on the one result with genuine reach outside mathematics: Brouwer's fixed point theorem, proved here in full from $\pi_1(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$ and functoriality in about ten lines, and then connected to the existence of Nash and general equilibria. It also sets the covering-space correspondence beside the Galois correspondence of algebra lecture 4, since the two are the same dictionary, as that lecture promised.
Step 2

Why this matters

Brouwer's fixed point theorem is the application with real consequences elsewhere. It states that every continuous map from a closed ball to itself has a fixed point, and it is the engine behind the major existence results in economics. Nash's theorem, that every finite game has an equilibrium in mixed strategies, is proved by applying a fixed point theorem to the best-response correspondence. General equilibrium theory, in the Arrow-Debreu formulation, proves that a price vector clearing all markets exists by the same route. Neither result constructs the equilibrium; both assert existence, and both rest on a topological fact about discs having no holes. The economics course uses these results, and this lecture is where the fixed point theorem is actually proved.

The second application is persistent homology, introduced in lecture 2 and made computable here. Given a point cloud, build simplicial complexes at a range of scales and compute the homology of each; features surviving across many scales are recorded as real structure. The invariants being tracked are exactly the homology groups defined below, and the practical output is a summary of shape that is robust to noise and independent of any coordinate system. It has been applied to sensor coverage, to genomic data, and to detecting structure in high-dimensional point clouds.

The third return is methodological, and it is the one this track has been building towards. Converting a hard problem into a tractable one by a structure-preserving map is the same manoeuvre as taking logarithms, applying a Fourier transform, or moving to the risk-neutral measure. Here it is at its purest: the transformation is a functor, what is preserved is exactly the algebra, and what is lost is exactly the geometry that was making the question hard.

Step 4

The detail

Homotopy and homotopy equivalence

Two continuous maps are homotopic when a continuous family of maps interpolates between them. Two spaces are homotopy equivalent when maps exist in both directions whose composites are homotopic to the identities, which is weaker than homeomorphism and is the right notion for this lecture: every invariant below depends only on homotopy type. A deformation retract is the clearest case. The annulus deformation retracts onto its central circle by sliding radially, so the two have the same fundamental group; $\mathbb{R}^n$ retracts to a point and is contractible; a solid disc likewise. Homotopy equivalence forgets dimension, which is why it is coarser than homeomorphism and why it is computable.

The fundamental group

Fundamental group
Fix a base point $x_0$. Loops based there, taken up to homotopy fixing the endpoints, form a group $\pi_1(X, x_0)$ under concatenation, with the constant loop as identity and the reversed loop as inverse. On a path-connected space the choice of base point does not matter up to isomorphism. A space with trivial fundamental group is simply connected.
The fundamental group of the circle is the integers. Setup. Let the circle be the unit complex numbers, base point 1. Consider the map p : R -> S^1, p(t) = exp(2 pi i t), which wraps the line round the circle infinitely often. This is the universal COVERING space. Path lifting. Every path in the circle starting at 1 lifts uniquely to a path in R starting at 0. Intuitively: as you walk round the circle, keep a running total of the angle you have turned through, without resetting it at each full turn. That running total is the lift. The winding number. A LOOP in the circle lifts to a path in R starting at 0 and ending at some integer n, since the endpoint must sit above the base point. That integer is the winding number. Well defined on homotopy classes. Homotopic loops lift to homotopic paths with the same endpoints, so n depends only on the homotopy class. Homomorphism. Concatenating loops adds winding numbers: walk twice round, then three times, and the total is five. Injective. If a loop has winding number 0 its lift is a loop in R, and R is contractible, so the lift contracts; pushing that contraction down gives a contraction of the original loop. Surjective. The loop t -> exp(2 pi i n t) has winding number n. So pi_1(S^1) = Z. QED CONSEQUENCES, immediately. - The disc and the circle are not homotopy equivalent (trivial group versus Z), hence not homeomorphic. - The plane and the punctured plane are not homeomorphic, which is the topological content of the punctured-plane example from vector calculus: the loop integral that failed to vanish was detecting this generator. - The annulus, the punctured plane, the cylinder, and the Mobius band all have fundamental group Z, since all deformation retract to a circle.

Functoriality, and Brouwer

A continuous map $f : X \to Y$ sending base point to base point induces a homomorphism $f_* : \pi_1(X) \to \pi_1(Y)$, by composing loops with f. Composition of maps induces composition of homomorphisms and the identity induces the identity, so $\pi_1$ is a functor. Two consequences do all the work: homeomorphic spaces have isomorphic fundamental groups, and a retraction induces a surjection. The second gives Brouwer.

BROUWER'S FIXED POINT THEOREM (two dimensions). Every continuous map f from the closed disc D to itself has a point with f(x) = x. Proof by contradiction. Suppose f(x) is never equal to x. Then for each x the two points x and f(x) are distinct, so define r(x) as follows: draw the ray starting at f(x) and passing through x, and let r(x) be the point where that ray meets the boundary circle. Since x and f(x) never coincide, the ray is always defined, and r depends continuously on x. Two properties of r: (i) r maps D into the boundary circle S^1. (ii) If x is already on the boundary, the ray from f(x) through x exits exactly at x, so r(x) = x. So r is a RETRACTION of the disc onto its boundary circle. Now apply the fundamental group. Let i : S^1 -> D be the inclusion. Property (ii) says r o i is the identity on S^1. Functoriality turns this into r_* o i_* = identity on pi_1(S^1) = Z. But i_* is a homomorphism from pi_1(S^1) = Z into pi_1(D) = 0, the trivial group, so i_* sends everything to zero, and therefore r_* o i_* sends everything to zero. It cannot be the identity on Z, since Z is not trivial. Contradiction. So no fixed-point-free f exists. QED WHAT MADE IT WORK. Only two inputs: pi_1 of the circle is Z, pi_1 of the disc is trivial, and the functor turns a geometric statement (a retraction exists) into an algebraic one (Z surjects onto a subgroup of the trivial group) that is visibly false. The n-dimensional case is identical with homology in place of the fundamental group. WHERE IT IS USED. Nash's theorem applies a fixed point theorem to the best-response map on the compact convex set of mixed strategy profiles; Arrow and Debreu apply one to an excess-demand map on the price simplex. Both prove equilibria EXIST and neither computes one, which is exactly the character of the topological input: it is an existence argument with no construction inside it.

Covering spaces, and the Galois dictionary

A covering space is a map $p : \tilde{X} \to X$ that is locally a stack of disjoint copies, as $\mathbb{R} \to S^1$ was above. The classification theorem states that for a reasonable space, connected covering spaces correspond to subgroups of $\pi_1(X)$, with the universal cover, simply connected, corresponding to the trivial subgroup, and normal subgroups corresponding to regular coverings whose deck transformation group is the quotient.

This is the correspondence Galois lecture promised would reappear, and the table is not an analogy: Grothendieck's reformulation makes both instances of one theorem, in which fields and spaces are objects of categories with a common notion of connectedness. The circle illustrates it concretely: $\pi_1 = \mathbb{Z}$, whose subgroups are $n\mathbb{Z}$, and the corresponding coverings are the circle wrapping onto itself n times, plus the line as the universal cover corresponding to the trivial subgroup.

Homology

The fundamental group detects one-dimensional holes and is often non-abelian and hard to compute. Homology trades some information for computability. Build the space from simplices, points, edges, triangles, tetrahedra, and let $C_n$ be the free abelian group on the n-simplices. The boundary operator $\partial_n : C_n \to C_{n-1}$ sends a simplex to the alternating sum of its faces, and the key identity is $\partial \circ \partial = 0$: the boundary of a boundary is empty. Define cycles as $\ker \partial_n$ and boundaries as $\operatorname{im}\partial_{n+1}$; the identity guarantees boundaries sit inside cycles, and the homology group is the quotient $H_n = \ker\partial_n / \operatorname{im}\partial_{n+1}$, which is the quotient construction appearing for the last time in this track.

The interpretation is direct: $H_n$ counts n-dimensional holes, meaning cycles that are not filled in. $H_0$ has rank the number of connected components; $H_1$ counts loops that do not bound a disc; $H_2$ counts enclosed voids. The ranks $b_n$ are the Betti numbers. The identity $\partial^2 = 0$ is the same statement as $d^2 = 0$ for differential forms in lecture 3, and de Rham's theorem says the two theories compute the same invariants.

Betti numbers, and the Euler characteristic two ways. SPHERE. b0 = 1 (connected), b1 = 0 (every loop contracts), b2 = 1 (it encloses a void). chi = b0 - b1 + b2 = 1 - 0 + 1 = 2. TORUS. b0 = 1, b1 = 2 (the loop through the hole and the loop round the tube, independent), b2 = 1. chi = 1 - 2 + 1 = 0. GENUS 2. b0 = 1, b1 = 4, b2 = 1. chi = 1 - 4 + 1 = -2. In general for genus g: b1 = 2g and chi = 2 - 2g, matching the figure. CROSS-CHECK AGAINST THE OTHER TWO METHODS. - Triangulation: chi = V - E + F. The tetrahedron gives 4 - 6 + 4 = 2 for the sphere, agreeing. - Gauss-Bonnet, from lecture 3: integral of K dA = 2 pi chi, which gave 4 pi for the sphere (chi = 2) and 0 for the torus (chi = 0), agreeing. So the same integer is produced by counting simplices, by taking an alternating sum of algebraic ranks, and by integrating curvature over the surface. Three methods from three different courses converging on one number is the strongest evidence available that the invariant is real rather than an artefact of any one technique. DISTINGUISHING SPACES. The sphere and the torus have different b1 (0 versus 2), so they are not homeomorphic, and indeed not even homotopy equivalent. The fundamental group settles this too, but homology also handles higher dimensions, where pi_1 sees nothing: the 2-sphere and the 3-sphere are both simply connected, and only their higher homology tells them apart.

Seifert-van Kampen computes the fundamental group of a space glued from two pieces, in terms of the groups of the pieces and of their intersection, which is how $\pi_1$ of a genus-g surface is computed. For homology the corresponding tool is the Mayer-Vietoris sequence. The classification of surfaces follows: every compact connected surface without boundary is a sphere with g handles if orientable, or a sphere with k cross-caps if not, and genus or cross-cap number is a complete invariant. This is one of the few classification theorems in mathematics that is both complete and easy to state.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The functorial method, stated once

The pattern of this lecture is worth extracting because it is the pattern of a great deal of mathematics. Faced with a category of hard objects and hard maps, construct a functor to a category of computable objects. Then any statement about the algebraic side that fails immediately rules out the corresponding topological configuration, because the functor would have carried it across. Brouwer's proof is the pattern at minimum size: a retraction would induce a surjection from a trivial group onto $\mathbb{Z}$, which is impossible, so no retraction exists, so no fixed-point-free map exists.

The same shape recurs throughout the track. Taking a logarithm converts multiplication into addition and hard problems about products into easy ones about sums. The Fourier transform converts convolution into multiplication, which is why characteristic functions in complex analysis made sums of independent random variables tractable. Change of measure converts a drifting process into a martingale. In each case a structure-preserving map moves the problem to a setting where the obstruction is visible. Recognising the move as a single technique, rather than four unrelated tricks, is a large part of what this track was for.

The limits of the method

Homotopy invariants are computable in low dimensions and become desperately hard beyond. The homotopy groups of spheres, $\pi_n(S^k)$, are the natural next question after this lecture, and they are unknown in general despite seventy years of concentrated effort; even $\pi_n(S^2)$ is not fully determined. Homology is computable precisely because it discards the information that makes homotopy hard, which is the standard trade: coarser invariants are calculable and distinguish fewer spaces. Whether a given invariant is fine enough to separate the spaces at hand, and coarse enough to compute, is the working judgement of the subject, and it has the same character as choosing a summary statistic.

What this track built

Twenty-three lectures, and the shape of them is worth stating now that they are finished. Foundations supplied the language and the quotient construction, which then appeared in modular arithmetic, in factor groups, in $L^p$ spaces, in the quotient topology, and finally in homology as $\ker/\operatorname{im}$: one construction, six appearances. Analysis began by computing, discovered that its proofs needed only completeness and compactness, generalised those, found the Riemann integral could not survive limits, replaced it, and built probability on the replacement. Algebra began with solving linear systems and ended by explaining why the same theorem had been proved four times. Geometry began with a two-thousand-year-old postulate and ended computing the same integer by three unrelated methods. The connections drawn across courses were not decoration: the Galois correspondence really is the covering space correspondence, $d^2 = 0$ really is $\partial^2 = 0$, and the Euler characteristic really is computed by both curvature and algebra.

Watch out Two errors. Isomorphic invariants do not prove spaces are homeomorphic: the invariants can only prove a negative, and two non-homeomorphic spaces can share every invariant you compute. And homotopy equivalence is not homeomorphism: a solid ball and a point are homotopy equivalent while being obviously different spaces, so a fundamental group computation says nothing about dimension, which is exactly why homology is needed in higher dimensions.

Test yourself

  1. Define homotopy of paths and explain why the annulus and the circle are homotopy equivalent but not homeomorphic.
  2. Verify that loops up to homotopy form a group, identifying the identity and the inverse.
  3. Prove that $\pi_1(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$ using path lifting, stating where the contractibility of the line is used.
  4. Explain what functoriality of $\pi_1$ means and why it implies homeomorphic spaces have isomorphic fundamental groups.
  5. Prove Brouwer's fixed point theorem in two dimensions from $\pi_1(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$, constructing the retraction explicitly.
  6. State how Nash's theorem uses a fixed point theorem, and say what the topological input does and does not provide.
  7. Set out the covering space correspondence for the circle, listing the coverings matching the subgroups $n\mathbb{Z}$ and identifying the universal cover.
  8. Define the boundary operator, verify $\partial^2 = 0$ on a triangle, and explain why this makes homology well defined.
  9. Compute the Betti numbers and Euler characteristic of the torus, and check the answer against a triangulation and against Gauss-Bonnet.
  10. Give two spaces distinguished by homology but not by the fundamental group, and explain why $\pi_1$ fails there.
