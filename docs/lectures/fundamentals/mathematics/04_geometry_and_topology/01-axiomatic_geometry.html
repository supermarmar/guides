Axiomatic geometry

For two thousand years Euclid's fifth postulate looked like a theorem waiting for a proof. It is not. Consistent geometries exist in which parallels through a point are not unique, and the way this was finally established is more important than the fact: build a model of the strange geometry inside the familiar one, and any contradiction in the strange one becomes a contradiction in the familiar one. That single manoeuvre is how every independence result since has been proved, including the continuum hypothesis.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Euclid's first four postulates are unobjectionable statements about drawing lines and circles. The fifth, equivalent to the assertion that through a point not on a line there passes exactly one parallel, is different in character: longer, less self-evident, and used sparingly in the Elements. Everything provable from the first four alone is neutral geometry, and it includes a surprising amount, but not the angle sum of a triangle.

Assume exactly one parallel and you get Euclidean geometry, with angle sums of 180 degrees, similar triangles, and rectangles. Assume infinitely many and you get hyperbolic geometry, which is consistent, has angle sums below 180 degrees by an amount proportional to area, and has no similar triangles at all: in hyperbolic geometry, equal angles force equal size. Consistency is proved by exhibiting a model, such as the Poincaré disc, in which hyperbolic axioms hold and which is built entirely from Euclidean materials. That construction is what proves the fifth postulate independent of the other four.

Feynman test Draw a triangle on an orange with three right angles: start at the north pole, go down to the equator, along a quarter of the equator, and back up. Three ninety-degree corners, sum 270. Nothing was cheated; the sides are the straightest possible paths on that surface, which is what a line means when the surface is all there is. Now imagine the opposite kind of surface, curving away from itself everywhere like an infinite saddle, where triangles are pinched and their angles sum to less than 180. Both are consistent geometries, and neither is a distortion of the "real" one. The parallel postulate is not a fact about space, it is a choice about which space, and physics later took the other option.
What's beyond a first course A first course states the postulates and describes the models. This lecture presses two points harder. The independence argument is spelled out as a relative consistency proof, and connected explicitly to the independence of the continuum hypothesis met in foundations lecture 4, since they are the same manoeuvre. And the Erlangen programme is stated in full: a geometry is the invariants of a transformation group, which folds this lecture into the group theory of the algebra course and explains why the isometries of the Poincaré disc turn out to be Möbius transformations from complex analysis.
Step 2

Why this matters

The geometry course opens by admitting that its return is mostly conceptual, and this lecture is where that admission is most true and least regrettable. Three things it supplies are worth having.

The first is the method of independence proofs. To show a statement cannot be derived from a set of axioms, build a model where the axioms hold and the statement fails. Beltrami, Klein, and Poincaré did this for the parallel postulate in the 1860s and 1870s; Gödel and Cohen did the same for the continuum hypothesis in 1940 and 1963, constructing models of set theory where it holds and where it fails. The technique is identical and the conclusion has the same shape: the question was not unanswerable through lack of effort, it was underdetermined by the axioms. Anyone who has met the continuum hypothesis as a curiosity will find it demystified by seeing the geometric case first, where the models can be drawn.

The second is that non-Euclidean geometry turned out to be the geometry of the physical world. General relativity models spacetime as a curved manifold whose geometry is determined by mass and energy, and the deviation of angle sums from 180 degrees is exactly the curvature that gravitation is. A mathematical possibility explored for its own sake became, sixty years later, the correct description of gravity, which is the standard example of pure mathematics anticipating physics rather than following it.

The third is the Erlangen programme, and it connects this lecture to the rest of the track. Klein's insight was that each geometry is characterised by a group of transformations and the properties invariant under it: Euclidean geometry studies what survives rigid motions, affine geometry what survives affine maps, projective geometry what survives projectivities, and hyperbolic geometry what survives the isometries of the disc. Geometry becomes a branch of group theory, and the classification of geometries becomes a classification of groups.

Step 4

The detail

The postulates, and the gap in Euclid

Euclid's five: a line may be drawn between any two points; a finite line may be extended indefinitely; a circle may be drawn with any centre and radius; all right angles are equal; and if a line crossing two others makes interior angles on one side summing to less than two right angles, those two lines meet on that side. The fifth is stated here in Euclid's own cumbersome form to make the point that it does not read like the others. Playfair's axiom, that through a point not on a line there is exactly one parallel, is the familiar equivalent.

Euclid's reasoning also has gaps he did not notice, and they are not about the fifth postulate. His very first proposition constructs an equilateral triangle by intersecting two circles, and nothing in the postulates guarantees the circles intersect: that requires a continuity assumption. Similarly, no postulate governs betweenness, so arguments that a point lies inside a triangle are unsupported. Hilbert's 1899 axiomatisation repaired all of this with twenty axioms in five groups, including explicit axioms of order and continuity, and it is Hilbert's system rather than Euclid's that modern treatments mean by axiomatic geometry.

Neutral geometry, and equivalents of the fifth

Neutral geometry assumes the first four postulates and takes no position on the fifth. It proves more than one might expect: the exterior angle theorem, the triangle inequality, the isosceles triangle theorem, and the side-angle-side congruence criterion all hold. It also proves the Saccheri-Legendre theorem, that the angle sum of a triangle is at most 180 degrees, which shows the spherical case is already excluded by the first four. What neutral geometry cannot prove is that the sum equals 180.

Each of these looks like an independent fact about the plane, and each is secretly the fifth postulate. The third row is the most surprising: in neutral geometry, if a single triangle has angle sum exactly 180, then every triangle does. This is why the failed proofs of the fifth postulate failed in the way they did. Saccheri, in 1733, assumed its negation and derived consequence after consequence hoping for a contradiction; he produced a substantial body of correct hyperbolic geometry and rejected it as "repugnant to the nature of the straight line". He had proved the theorems and refused the conclusion.

Hyperbolic geometry

Replace the fifth postulate with its hyperbolic alternative, that through a point not on a line pass at least two parallels, and a coherent geometry follows in which infinitely many do. Its distinctive results:

Four theorems of hyperbolic geometry, and one non-theorem. (1) ANGLE SUM IS ALWAYS BELOW 180. Define the DEFECT of a triangle as 180 minus its angle sum, in degrees. Every triangle has positive defect. (2) DEFECT IS PROPORTIONAL TO AREA. area = k x defect, for a constant k depending on the curvature. Two consequences follow immediately and are both alien to Euclid: - There is a MAXIMUM possible area for a triangle, since the defect cannot exceed 180. In the standard normalisation that maximum is pi, achieved only in the limit by an "ideal" triangle whose vertices sit on the boundary circle and whose angles are all zero. - Area is determined by angles alone, with no lengths involved. (3) NO SIMILAR TRIANGLES: AAA IS A CONGRUENCE CRITERION. If two triangles have equal angles, they have equal defect, hence equal area, and in fact they are congruent. So it is impossible to scale a figure up while preserving its shape. Photographic enlargement does not exist in hyperbolic geometry, and there is an absolute unit of length determined by the geometry itself, which Euclidean geometry lacks. (4) PARALLELS COME IN TWO KINDS. Among the infinitely many lines through P missing a given line, two are limiting cases, approaching the line asymptotically without meeting it; the rest diverge in both directions. Euclidean geometry has only one parallel and no such distinction. THE NON-THEOREM. Pythagoras' theorem is false. Its hyperbolic replacement uses hyperbolic cosines: cosh(c) = cosh(a) cosh(b) for a right triangle with legs a, b and hypotenuse c. Expanding each cosh as 1 + x^2/2 + ... and discarding terms beyond second order recovers 1 + c^2/2 = (1 + a^2/2)(1 + b^2/2) = 1 + a^2/2 + b^2/2 + ... c^2 = a^2 + b^2, so Euclidean geometry is the small-scale limit of hyperbolic geometry. This is why the curvature of physical space is not evident at human scale: over short distances every smooth geometry looks flat.

Models, and what they prove

A model interprets the undefined terms of an axiom system as specific objects, so that each axiom becomes a provable statement about them. Three standard models of hyperbolic geometry exist, all equivalent.

The relative consistency argument, stated carefully. Claim: if Euclidean geometry is consistent, then hyperbolic geometry is consistent, and therefore the parallel postulate cannot be proved from the other four. Argument. 1. The Poincare disc is constructed entirely from Euclidean objects: a circle, its interior, arcs orthogonal to it, and a distance formula written in Euclidean coordinates. 2. Under the dictionary (point = interior point, line = orthogonal arc, and so on), each hyperbolic axiom translates into a statement about these Euclidean objects, and each translated statement is a Euclidean theorem. In particular the first four postulates hold, and through a point off a given arc there are infinitely many arcs missing it. 3. Suppose hyperbolic geometry were inconsistent. Then some finite chain of deductions from its axioms reaches a contradiction. Translating that entire chain gives a finite chain of Euclidean deductions reaching a contradiction. 4. So Euclidean geometry would be inconsistent too. Contrapositively, if Euclidean geometry is consistent, so is hyperbolic geometry. 5. If the fifth postulate followed from the first four, it would hold in every model of those four, including this one, where it fails. So it does not follow. QED WHAT THE ARGUMENT DOES NOT DO. It does not prove either geometry consistent outright. Consistency is relative: it is transferred from Euclidean geometry, which was itself later given a model in the real numbers by Descartes' coordinates, transferring the question to the consistency of arithmetic, which by Godel's second theorem cannot be proved from within arithmetic. The chain of relative consistency results terminates in an assumption rather than a proof, which is exactly the situation described in foundations lecture 4. THE SAME MANOEUVRE, EIGHTY YEARS LATER. Godel built a model of set theory (the constructible universe) in which the continuum hypothesis holds, so it cannot be disproved; Cohen built models (by forcing) in which it fails, so it cannot be proved. Two models, one statement true in each, independence established. Identical in structure to the argument above, with discs replaced by universes of sets.

Isometries and the Erlangen programme

The distance-preserving maps of a geometry form a group under composition, and in the upper half-plane model the orientation-preserving isometries are exactly the Möbius transformations $z \mapsto (az+b)/(cz+d)$ with real coefficients and positive determinant. These are the maps met in complex analysis, and their appearance here is not a coincidence: they are the conformal automorphisms of the half-plane, and hyperbolic geometry is exactly the geometry whose angle measure they preserve.

Klein's Erlangen programme of 1872 generalises this into a definition. A geometry is a space together with a group of transformations, and its content is the collection of properties invariant under that group. Euclidean geometry is the invariant theory of rigid motions, so lengths and angles are geometric and position is not. Affine geometry uses a larger group, so parallelism and ratios along a line survive while lengths and angles do not. Projective geometry uses a larger group still, so only incidence and the cross-ratio survive. Hyperbolic geometry uses the Möbius group of the disc. Larger group, fewer invariants, weaker geometry: the whole subject is organised by a lattice of groups, which is the group theory of algebra lecture 3 supplying the classification.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Independence is the most transferable idea in this lecture

Three times in this track a statement has turned out to be undecidable from the axioms offered: the parallel postulate here, the continuum hypothesis and the axiom of choice in foundations lecture 4. Each time the response that turned out to be right was not to work harder on a proof but to build two models. And each time the intellectual cost was the same, namely giving up the belief that the axioms described a unique intended object. Euclidean geometry was thought to be the geometry; set theory was thought to describe the universe of sets. In both cases the axioms turned out to describe a family, and the mathematics improved once that was accepted rather than resisted. Saccheri's refusal to accept his own theorems is the cautionary case, and it took a further century for Gauss, Bolyai, and Lobachevsky to say plainly what he had already derived.

Curvature is the modern language

This lecture separates three geometries by an axiom. The modern treatment separates them by a number: the Gaussian curvature, positive for the sphere, zero for the plane, negative for the hyperbolic plane. That reformulation is more powerful because curvature can vary from point to point, so it describes surfaces that are not homogeneous, and because it is intrinsic, measurable by an inhabitant of the surface without reference to any surrounding space. Gauss's theorema egregium, that curvature is intrinsic, is what makes this possible, and it is the subject of the differential geometry lecture. General relativity then works because spacetime curvature varies with the distribution of mass, which no axiomatic treatment could express. The axioms answered the question they were posed; curvature answered the question that turned out to matter.

What this lecture does not earn

It would be easy to claim applications for hyperbolic geometry in this repository and the claim would be thin. Hyperbolic embeddings do appear in machine learning, where hierarchical data such as taxonomies embeds with lower distortion in hyperbolic space than in Euclidean space of the same dimension, because the exponential growth of hyperbolic area matches the exponential growth of a tree's node count with depth. That is a genuine and current application, and it is one application. Beyond it, the material here earns its place through the independence method, the physics, and the Erlangen organisation of geometry by groups, all of which are conceptual returns. The course landing page says the geometry course is read for understanding rather than use, and this lecture is the clearest instance.

Watch out Two confusions. A model is not a proof of consistency; it transfers consistency from one system to another, and the chain must end somewhere in an unproved assumption, as Gödel's second theorem guarantees. And hyperbolic geometry is not a distortion of the real one: the pinched triangles in the disc picture are artefacts of drawing an intrinsically curved geometry on a flat page, and an inhabitant of the hyperbolic plane measuring with hyperbolic rulers would find its lines perfectly straight and its triangles unremarkable.

Test yourself

  1. State Euclid's five postulates and explain in one sentence what makes the fifth different in character from the others.
  2. Identify the gap in Euclid's construction of an equilateral triangle, and name the kind of axiom Hilbert added to repair it.
  3. State the Saccheri-Legendre theorem and explain why it rules out spherical geometry as a model of the first four postulates.
  4. Give three statements equivalent to the parallel postulate, and explain why the existence of one triangle with angle sum 180 forces all of them to have it.
  5. In hyperbolic geometry, state the relationship between a triangle's defect and its area, and deduce that triangles have a maximum possible area.
  6. Prove that AAA is a congruence criterion in hyperbolic geometry, and say what this implies about scaling a figure.
  7. Give the dictionary for the Poincaré disc model: what are points, lines, and angles, and why is the boundary infinitely far away?
  8. Set out the relative consistency argument in full, and state precisely what it does and does not prove.
  9. Explain how the independence of the parallel postulate and the independence of the continuum hypothesis are established by the same method.
  10. State the Erlangen programme and identify the transformation group for Euclidean, affine, and hyperbolic geometry, saying what each preserves.
