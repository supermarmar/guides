Relations and graphs

A relation is nothing more than a set of ordered pairs, which sounds too thin to matter and turns out to carry two of the most reused structures in mathematics. Impose reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity and you get an equivalence relation, which chops a set into classes and licenses every quotient construction in the track. Impose reflexivity, antisymmetry, and transitivity instead and you get a partial order, which is dependency, hierarchy, and scheduling. Draw a relation and you get a graph, at which point the questions become algorithmic and the answers become code.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A relation on a set A is a subset $R \subseteq A \times A$: the pairs that are related, and nothing else. Four properties are worth naming (reflexive, symmetric, antisymmetric, transitive), and two combinations of them dominate everything downstream. Reflexive, symmetric, and transitive gives an equivalence relation, which partitions the set into disjoint classes. Reflexive, antisymmetric, and transitive gives a partial order, which arranges the set into a hierarchy with incomparable elements allowed.

A graph is a relation drawn as dots and lines, and the change of picture changes the questions. Instead of asking which properties hold, you ask whether two vertices are connected, whether a cycle exists, which spanning tree is cheapest, and whether a closed walk can use every edge exactly once. Those questions have algorithms: Kruskal and Prim for the cheapest spanning tree, Fleury for the Euler circuit. Finite state automata close the lecture, being graphs whose edges are labelled by input symbols and whose paths recognise a language.

Feynman test Sort a drawer of loose coins into piles by denomination. You have just built an equivalence relation without writing a symbol: two coins are related when they belong in the same pile. Notice three things you did automatically. Every coin is in the same pile as itself. If this coin belongs with that one, that one belongs with this one. And if the first belongs with the second and the second with the third, the first belongs with the third. Reflexive, symmetric, transitive. Notice also what you got for free: every coin lands in exactly one pile, no pile is empty, and no coin is in two piles. That is the partition theorem, and it says the piles and the relation are the same information written two ways.
What's beyond a first course A first course defines the properties, draws Hasse diagrams, and runs the three named algorithms by hand. This lecture adds the proof that equivalence relations and partitions are in exact correspondence, which is the theorem that makes quotient constructions legitimate throughout algebra and topology; the proof that a tree on n vertices has exactly $n-1$ edges, by induction rather than by inspection; a proof of Euler's circuit theorem in both directions; and an honest account of why the greedy Kruskal algorithm is correct, which is a statement about exchange arguments rather than about graphs.
Step 2

Why this matters

Equivalence relations are the mechanism behind every quotient in the mathematics track, and the quotient is one of the three or four genuinely load-bearing constructions in the subject. Modular arithmetic is the integers quotiented by congruence. A factor group in algebra is a group quotiented by a normal subgroup, where the classes are cosets. The rational numbers are ordered pairs of integers quotiented by cross-multiplication, and the real numbers are Cauchy sequences quotiented by "difference tends to zero", which is how analysis constructs them. Each of these is this lecture's partition theorem applied to a different relation, and a reader who understands the theorem once does not have to relearn it four times.

Partial orders and graphs earn their place through applications rather than through later theory. A dependency graph is a partial order, and topological sorting it is what a build system, a task scheduler, and a spreadsheet recalculation engine all do. Directed acyclic graphs are the notation for causal structure, which the causal inference lecture in the statistics course uses to decide which variables to condition on. Trees are the structure behind decision trees and random forests in machine learning, and the fact that a tree on n vertices has $n-1$ edges is why the number of splits determines the number of leaves. Transition graphs of Markov chains are graphs whose connectivity determines whether a stationary distribution exists, which is the structural half of the rating-migration models in credit risk.

Step 4

The detail

Relations and their properties

A binary relation from A to B is a subset $R \subseteq A \times B$, and we write $a\,R\,b$ for $(a,b) \in R$. A relation on A takes $B = A$. That is the whole definition, and its thinness is deliberate: a function is the special case where each a appears in exactly one pair, so functions are relations with a constraint rather than a separate kind of object. The four properties below are conditions on which pairs must be present.

PropertyConditionHolds forFails for
Reflexive$\forall a: a\,R\,a$$\le$, divisibility, congruence$<$, "is a parent of"
Symmetric$a\,R\,b \Rightarrow b\,R\,a$congruence, "shares a border with"$\le$, divisibility
Antisymmetric$a\,R\,b \land b\,R\,a \Rightarrow a=b$$\le$, $\subseteq$, divisibility on positivescongruence mod n
Transitive$a\,R\,b \land b\,R\,c \Rightarrow a\,R\,c$$\le$, $\subseteq$, congruence, divisibility"is a parent of", "differs by at most 1"

Antisymmetry is the property most often misread. It does not say that $a\,R\,b$ and $b\,R\,a$ cannot both hold; it says that when they do, the two elements were the same element all along. Divisibility on the positive integers is antisymmetric because $a \mid b$ and $b \mid a$ force $a = b$, and it fails on all integers because 2 and $-2$ divide each other without being equal, which is a small warning that the domain is part of the relation.

Equivalence relations and the partition theorem

Given an equivalence relation R on A, the equivalence class of a is $[a] = \{x \in A : x\,R\,a\}$, the set of everything related to a. The theorem below says these classes behave exactly like the piles of coins: they cover the set and never overlap.

Partition theorem
Let R be an equivalence relation on a non-empty set A. Then the equivalence classes form a partition of A: every element lies in exactly one class. Conversely, every partition of A arises from exactly one equivalence relation, namely "lies in the same block". Equivalence relations and partitions are therefore the same information in two notations, and the set of classes is written A/R, the quotient set.
Proof of the partition theorem. (1) Every element is in some class. Reflexivity gives a R a, so a is in [a]. No element is left out, and no class is empty. (2) Two classes are equal or disjoint. Suppose [a] and [b] share an element x, so x R a and x R b. By symmetry, a R x. By transitivity with x R b, we get a R b. Now take any y in [a], so y R a. With a R b and transitivity, y R b, so y is in [b]. Hence [a] is contained in [b], and the same argument with a and b swapped gives [b] contained in [a]. So [a] = [b]. Therefore two classes that meet at all are identical, and otherwise they are disjoint. (1) and (2) together: the classes cover A and do not overlap, which is exactly a partition. Converse. Given a partition, define a R b to mean "a and b lie in the same block". Reflexive: a is in its own block. Symmetric: "same block" does not care about order. Transitive: same block as b, and b same block as c, forces same block as c because blocks are disjoint. The classes of this relation are precisely the blocks. QED

The worked case to keep in mind is congruence modulo n: define $a \equiv b \pmod n$ when $n \mid (a-b)$. It is reflexive since n divides 0, symmetric since n divides $a-b$ exactly when it divides $b-a$, and transitive since a sum of multiples of n is a multiple of n. The classes are the n residue classes, the quotient set is written $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, and arithmetic descends to it because the relation is compatible with addition and multiplication. That compatibility question, whether an operation on representatives gives a well-defined operation on classes, is the standard obligation whenever a quotient appears anywhere in the track.

Partial orders, Hasse diagrams, and topological sorting

A partial order is reflexive, antisymmetric, and transitive, and the pair $(A, \preceq)$ is a poset. Two elements are comparable when one precedes the other, and a partial order in which every pair is comparable is a total order, such as $\le$ on the reals. Divisibility on $\{1,2,3,4,6,12\}$ is partial rather than total, because 4 and 6 are incomparable.

A Hasse diagram draws a finite poset with the redundancy removed: elements are placed with larger ones higher, and an edge is drawn only for a covering pair, meaning $a \prec b$ with nothing strictly between them. Reflexive loops and transitive shortcuts are omitted because they can be reconstructed, which is why the diagram is readable at all. A chain is a set of pairwise comparable elements and an antichain is a set of pairwise incomparable ones; the longest chain in a task poset is the critical path, and the largest antichain is the maximum parallelism available.

Topological sorting extends a partial order to a total order consistent with it, which is what turns a dependency graph into a build sequence. It exists for every finite poset, and the standard proof is the algorithm: repeatedly remove a minimal element and append it to the output. A minimal element always exists in a finite non-empty poset (follow strictly decreasing steps, which must terminate since the set is finite and antisymmetry forbids revisiting), so the procedure never stalls, and it terminates because the set shrinks each round. The same argument, run on a directed graph, shows that a topological order exists precisely when the graph is acyclic.

Graphs: definitions and the two counting lemmas

A simple graph $G = (V, E)$ is a set of vertices with a set of unordered pairs of distinct vertices as edges, which is a symmetric irreflexive relation drawn as a picture. The degree of a vertex is the number of edges meeting it. Two facts about degrees are immediate and constantly useful.

The handshake lemma and its parity corollary. Claim: sum of all vertex degrees = 2 |E|. Proof: count the pairs (v, e) where vertex v is an endpoint of edge e, in two ways. Grouping by vertex gives the sum of degrees. Grouping by edge gives 2 per edge, since every edge has exactly two endpoints. The two counts are of the same set, so they are equal. QED Corollary: the number of odd-degree vertices is even. Proof: split the degree sum into odd-degree and even-degree vertices. The total is even (it is 2|E|), and the even-degree part is even, so the odd-degree part is even. A sum of odd numbers is even only when there is an even number of them. QED This is double counting again, exactly the technique from lecture 2, and it is why no party can have exactly three people who each shook hands with an odd number of others.

A path is a walk with no repeated vertex, a cycle is a closed walk with no repeated vertex except its endpoints, and a graph is connected when a path joins every pair of vertices. Connectivity is itself an equivalence relation on vertices, and its classes are the connected components, which is the partition theorem quietly doing work again.

Trees

Tree
A connected acyclic graph. Equivalently, and these are all the same class of graphs: a graph in which every pair of vertices is joined by exactly one path; a connected graph with $n-1$ edges; an acyclic graph with $n-1$ edges; a maximal acyclic graph (adding any edge creates a cycle); a minimal connected graph (removing any edge disconnects it).
Every tree on n vertices has exactly n - 1 edges. Proof by induction on n. Base, n = 1: a single vertex, no edges. 1 - 1 = 0. Holds. Step: assume every tree on k vertices has k - 1 edges, and let T be a tree on k + 1 vertices. T has a leaf (a vertex of degree 1): follow a longest path in T; its final vertex cannot have a second neighbour, since that neighbour would either extend the path or close a cycle, and T is acyclic. Delete that leaf and its single edge. The result is still connected (the leaf was on no path between other vertices) and still acyclic, so it is a tree on k vertices, with k - 1 edges by hypothesis. Restoring the leaf adds one vertex and one edge, giving (k + 1) - 1 = k edges. QED Consequence used later: a spanning tree of a connected graph on n vertices always has n - 1 edges, whichever spanning tree you pick, so all spanning trees have the same size and differ only in total weight.

Minimum spanning trees: Kruskal and Prim

Given a connected graph with a weight on each edge, a minimum spanning tree is a spanning tree of least total weight. Two greedy algorithms find one. Kruskal sorts the edges by weight and adds each in turn unless it would close a cycle. Prim starts at any vertex and repeatedly adds the cheapest edge leaving the component built so far. Both are greedy, both are correct, and greedy algorithms are usually wrong, so the correctness deserves an argument.

Why Kruskal is correct: the exchange argument. Let T be the tree Kruskal produces and let M be any minimum spanning tree. If T = M, done. Otherwise let e be the first edge Kruskal added that is not in M, chosen in weight order. Adding e to M creates exactly one cycle C (M is a tree, so e's endpoints already have a unique path between them). That cycle contains an edge f not in T: if every edge of C were in T, then T would contain the cycle C, and T is acyclic. Both e and f were available to Kruskal when it chose e, and f does not close a cycle among the edges Kruskal had accepted before e (those are all in M, and M plus f is acyclic on that subset). Kruskal chose e, so weight(e) <= weight(f). Now swap: M' = M - f + e is again a spanning tree, of weight weight(M) - weight(f) + weight(e) <= weight(M). Since M was minimum, weight(M') = weight(M), so M' is also minimum, and it agrees with T on one more edge than M did. Repeat the swap. Each step increases the agreement with T by one edge and preserves minimality, so after finitely many steps M has become T. Hence T is minimum. QED

The argument used nothing about geography and everything about the cycle structure, which is why it generalises: greedy selection is optimal exactly when the independent sets (here, the acyclic edge sets) form a matroid, and the exchange property above is the matroid axiom in disguise. That is the general theorem behind the special case, and it is why greedy works for spanning trees and fails for shortest paths with negative weights.

Euler circuits, and the contrast with Hamilton

An Euler circuit is a closed walk using every edge exactly once. Euler's answer to the Konigsberg bridges question is a complete characterisation, and it is decidable by looking at degrees alone.

Euler's theorem. A connected graph has an Euler circuit if and only if every vertex has even degree. Necessity. Suppose an Euler circuit exists. Each time the walk visits a vertex it enters by one edge and leaves by another, pairing up the edges at that vertex. The start vertex is also left at the beginning and entered at the end, so it pairs too. Every edge is used exactly once, so the edges at each vertex are perfectly paired, and the degree is even. Sufficiency. Suppose every degree is even. Start anywhere and walk without repeating edges. The walk cannot get stuck at a vertex other than the start: arriving at v uses one edge and leaves an odd number used there so far, so an unused edge remains to leave by. So the walk returns to the start, forming a circuit C. If C uses every edge, done. Otherwise, since the graph is connected, some vertex v on C has unused edges; the graph of unused edges still has all degrees even, so the same argument from v produces a second circuit, which can be spliced into C at v. Each splice strictly increases the edges used, so the process ends with an Euler circuit. QED Fleury's algorithm is this proof made procedural: walk anywhere, but never cross a bridge (an edge whose removal disconnects the remaining graph) unless it is the only edge available.

Now change one word. A Hamiltonian cycle visits every vertex exactly once, and there is no known characterisation remotely like Euler's. Deciding whether one exists is NP-complete, so the two questions, which differ only in whether "edge" or "vertex" appears, sit on opposite sides of the tractability boundary. This is the cleanest illustration in elementary mathematics that difficulty is not proportional to how complicated a question sounds.

Finite state automata

A deterministic finite automaton is a labelled directed graph put to work: a finite set of states, an alphabet, a transition function assigning to each state and symbol a next state, one start state, and a set of accepting states. Feed it a string, follow the edges, and accept if you finish in an accepting state. The set of strings accepted is the language recognised by the automaton.

An automaton accepting binary strings divisible by 3. States: r0, r1, r2 (the remainder of the number read so far, mod 3) Start: r0. Accepting: r0. Reading bit b takes the value n to 2n + b, so the remainder updates as r -> (2r + b) mod 3: input 0 input 1 r0 -> r0 r1 r1 -> r2 r0 r2 -> r1 r2 Trace "1100" (the number 12, divisible by 3): r0 --1--> r1 --1--> r0 --0--> r0 --0--> r0 accepted, correctly. Trace "1011" (the number 11, not divisible by 3): r0 --1--> r1 --0--> r2 --1--> r2 --1--> r2 rejected, correctly. Three states suffice to test divisibility for arbitrarily long inputs, because the machine never needs the number, only its remainder.

The reason automata belong in a lecture on relations is that the state set is a quotient. Two input strings are equivalent when no continuation distinguishes them, this is an equivalence relation on strings, and the Myhill-Nerode theorem says the minimal automaton's states are the classes of that relation. The partition theorem from the start of this lecture reappears as the theory of minimal machines, which is a satisfying distance to travel from a drawer of coins.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The quotient is one construction, met four times

The partition theorem is the most reused result in this lecture and it is worth stating in the abstract, because the abstraction is what saves work later. Whenever you want to treat certain objects as identical, you define an equivalence relation, form the classes, and work with the classes as a new set. The obligations are always the same two. First, check the relation really is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, because relations that fail transitivity ("differs by less than one") produce overlapping heaps rather than a partition. Second, if you want to carry an operation across, check it is well defined on classes: the result must not depend on which representative you chose. In algebra this second check is exactly why normal subgroups exist as a concept, since normality is precisely the condition under which multiplication descends to cosets. Meeting the construction here, on coins and remainders, makes the algebraic version a repetition rather than a novelty.

Isomorphism is easy to define and hard to decide

Two graphs are isomorphic when a bijection of their vertex sets carries edges to edges. The definition is a single line, and testing it is famously awkward: no polynomial-time algorithm was known for decades, the problem is not known to be NP-complete either, and the best current algorithm is quasi-polynomial. What practitioners actually do is compute cheap invariants, which are quantities preserved by isomorphism, and use them to prove non-isomorphism quickly: vertex count, edge count, degree sequence, number of triangles, connectivity, the spectrum of the adjacency matrix. Matching invariants never prove isomorphism, since non-isomorphic graphs can share all of them, but a single mismatch settles the question negatively in one line. This asymmetry, cheap refutation and expensive confirmation, is a pattern worth recognising because it recurs across mathematics and testing alike.

Where the adjacency matrix connects this lecture to the rest of the track

Encode a graph as a matrix A with $A_{ij} = 1$ when an edge joins i to j. Then $(A^k)_{ij}$ counts walks of length k from i to j, by induction on k and the definition of matrix multiplication. That single observation converts graph questions into linear algebra questions: connectivity becomes a statement about the sparsity of powers, the largest eigenvalue bounds the growth rate of walk counts, and the eigenvector of that eigenvalue is a centrality measure. Replace the ones with transition probabilities and the same matrix is a Markov chain, where the stationary distribution is an eigenvector and the conditions for its existence are the graph conditions of irreducibility and aperiodicity, which is precisely how rating-migration models in credit risk are analysed. The graph, the matrix, and the chain are three views of one object.

Watch out Two errors are common enough to name. The first is assuming that a relation which is symmetric and transitive must be reflexive, on the grounds that $a\,R\,b$ and $b\,R\,a$ give $a\,R\,a$ by transitivity. That argument only reaches elements that are related to something; an element related to nothing at all breaks it, which is why reflexivity is an independent axiom rather than a consequence. The second is treating a Hasse diagram as a graph and reading paths off it as if all edges were present. The diagram deliberately omits transitive edges, so "reachable upwards in the diagram" means "related", and the missing edges are missing on purpose.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Give a relation that is symmetric and transitive but not reflexive, and explain precisely where the "proof" that symmetry plus transitivity implies reflexivity breaks down.
  2. Prove that congruence modulo n is an equivalence relation, then show that addition is well defined on the classes, that is, if $a \equiv a'$ and $b \equiv b'$ then $a + b \equiv a' + b'$.
  3. Prove both halves of the partition theorem, and state in one sentence why the theorem is what licenses the notation A/R.
  4. Draw the Hasse diagram of the divisors of 36 under divisibility. Identify the longest chain and a largest antichain, and say what each would mean if the poset described task dependencies.
  5. Prove the handshake lemma by double counting, and use it to show that a graph with five vertices each of degree 3 cannot exist.
  6. Prove that a tree on n vertices has $n-1$ edges. Then prove that a connected graph with $n-1$ edges must be a tree, so the two conditions are interchangeable.
  7. Run Kruskal and then Prim on the seven-vertex graph in the second figure, listing the edges in the order each algorithm accepts them. Confirm both reach total weight 21.
  8. State Euler's circuit theorem and prove the necessity direction. Then explain why the same style of argument does not settle the Hamiltonian question.
  9. Build a deterministic finite automaton accepting binary strings with an even number of ones, and one accepting binary strings divisible by 4. State how many states each needs and why.
  10. Let A be the adjacency matrix of a graph. Prove by induction that $(A^k)_{ij}$ counts walks of length k from i to j, and say what $A^2$ having a zero diagonal entry would tell you about the vertex concerned.
