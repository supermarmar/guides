Course companion · Mathematics track

Foundations

Four lectures on the language the rest of mathematics is written in: propositional and predicate logic, proof technique, sets, counting, relations, graphs, and finally the axiomatic set theory and formal logic that put the whole enterprise on a footing. Built from the University of Pretoria discrete structures modules (WTW 115 and WTW 285), with a fourth lecture that goes beyond the degree into ZFC, cardinals, and the incompleteness theorems.

What this course is

Every other course in the mathematics track assumes you can read a quantified statement, follow an induction, and recognise when two objects are "the same" in the sense that matters. This course supplies that, and it supplies it early because the cost of not having it compounds. A student who has never written a contrapositive proof will spend the real analysis course fighting the notation rather than the ideas.

The counting and graph material earns its place for a second reason. Combinatorics is where probability comes from, so the statistics track quietly depends on the second lecture, and graph algorithms are the discrete backbone of anything involving networks, dependency structures, or the tree models in machine learning. The fourth lecture is different in kind: it is the one place in the track where mathematics turns around and examines itself, and it is worth reading once even if nothing later depends on it.

How to read this

Lectures are sequenced so each one depends on the previous. The first two are short and can be read in an evening each. The fourth is genuinely harder than its position suggests, and it can be deferred until after the analysis and algebra courses without any loss, which is how most degrees order it. Course sequence

Part 1 · Logic, proof, and counting (lectures 1 to 2)

The two lectures that every later course uses without acknowledgement.

  • builtLecture 1: logic, sets and proof. Propositional logic, truth tables, logical equivalence and implication, valid arguments. Predicate logic and quantifiers. Direct, contrapositive, and contradiction proofs. Mathematical induction and the well-ordering principle. Naive set theory, set operations, and functions as relations. (WTW 115)
  • builtLecture 2: combinatorics. The multiplication and addition rules, permutations and combinations, the binomial theorem, inclusion and exclusion, pigeonhole arguments, elementary probability as counting, and setting up and solving recurrence relations. (WTW 115, WTW 285)

Part 2 · Structure and self-examination (lectures 3 to 4)

Discrete structures proper, then the axioms underneath everything.

  • builtLecture 3: relations and graphs. Equivalence relations and partitions, partial orders and Hasse diagrams. Graphs, paths, cycles, and trees. Isomorphism. The Kruskal, Prim, and Fleury algorithms. Finite state automata. (WTW 285)
  • builtLecture 4: set theory and formal logic. The ZFC axioms and why naive set theory needed replacing. Ordinals and cardinals, countability, Cantor's theorem and the continuum hypothesis. The axiom of choice and Zorn's lemma. First-order logic, models, the completeness theorem, and Godel's incompleteness theorems. (extension)

Where this course is used later

Zorn's lemma from lecture 4 is what proves every vector space has a basis in algebra, and it is what gives the Hahn-Banach theorem in analysis. Cardinality is what makes the difference between a countable and an uncountable sample space matter in measure theory. Equivalence relations from lecture 3 are the mechanism behind quotient groups, quotient spaces, and the quotient topology, which are three appearances of one idea. The graph material stands slightly apart from the rest of the track and connects outwards instead, to dependency graphs, decision trees, and network models.
