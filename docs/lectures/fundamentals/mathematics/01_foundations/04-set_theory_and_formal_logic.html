Set theory and formal logic

This is the lecture where mathematics turns around and examines itself. Naive set theory breaks, and the repair is a short list of axioms that say which sets may be built rather than asserting that every property defines one. Cardinality replaces counting once the sets are infinite, and the answer is stranger than expected: there are infinities of different sizes, provably. Then the tools are turned on proof itself, and two theorems mark the boundary. Godel's completeness theorem says first-order logic captures everything that follows; his incompleteness theorems say no adequate system captures everything that is true.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Russell's paradox kills the assumption that any property defines a set. The replacement, ZFC, keeps only restricted construction: you may separate a subset out of a set you already have, take power sets, take unions, and pair things, but you may not conjure a set from a bare property. Everything in mathematics, numbers included, is then built as a set inside this hierarchy.

Size for infinite sets is defined by bijection rather than by counting: two sets have the same cardinality when a bijection pairs them off. The integers and the rationals turn out to be the same size as the naturals, which is surprising, and the reals turn out to be strictly larger, which is Cantor's diagonal argument and is the more important surprise. The axiom of choice then permits selections from infinitely many sets at once, and is equivalent to Zorn's lemma, which is what proves that every vector space has a basis. Finally, formal logic makes proof itself a mathematical object, and Godel's two results fix its limits: everything semantically entailed is provable, and yet any consistent system strong enough for arithmetic leaves true statements unproved, including its own consistency.

Feynman test A hotel has infinitely many rooms, numbered 1, 2, 3, and so on, and every one is occupied. A new guest arrives. Ask everyone to move from room n to room $n+1$, and room 1 is free, without evicting anyone. Now a coach arrives carrying infinitely many new guests. Ask everyone to move from room n to room $2n$, and all the odd rooms are free, infinitely many of them. This is not a trick with words: it is the definition of infinite size doing what it should. "Same size" means "can be paired off exactly", and an infinite set can be paired off with a proper part of itself. What the hotel cannot do is accommodate a coach carrying one guest for every real number between 0 and 1, and the proof of that is the one genuinely startling result in this lecture.
What's beyond a first course Almost all of this lecture is beyond the UP curriculum and beyond most applied degrees. It is here because three of its results are load-bearing elsewhere in the track: Zorn's lemma is what gives bases in infinite-dimensional spaces and the Hahn-Banach theorem in functional analysis, the countable-uncountable distinction is what forces measure theory to use sigma-algebras rather than all subsets, and the existence of non-measurable sets (a consequence of choice) is the reason Lebesgue measure cannot be defined on everything. The incompleteness theorems are included because they are widely misquoted, and knowing exactly what they say is cheaper than repeatedly working out what they do not.
Step 2

Why this matters

The honest answer is that most working mathematicians and every working modeller can ignore this material almost all of the time, and the lecture says so rather than pretending otherwise. It matters at three specific points, and those points are worth knowing in advance.

First, measure theory. The analysis course defines a measure on a sigma-algebra rather than on all subsets, and every student asks why the restriction is there. The answer is that a translation-invariant countably additive measure assigning 1 to the unit interval cannot be defined on every subset of the reals, and the standard proof constructs a Vitali set using the axiom of choice. The restriction is forced, not fastidious. Similarly, the reason a probability space carries a sigma-algebra of events, rather than every possible event, traces to the same fact.

Second, infinite-dimensional linear algebra. "Every vector space has a basis" is proved with Zorn's lemma, and in functional analysis the Hahn-Banach theorem, which underwrites duality and therefore most of the subject, is another Zorn argument. A reader who has never seen Zorn's lemma meets it first in a proof that matters, which is the wrong moment.

Third, the limits of formalisation. Incompleteness gets invoked constantly and loosely, usually to argue that some system cannot be understood or that truth is subjective. It says nothing of the kind. Knowing its actual statement, including the hypotheses it needs, means never being persuaded by an argument that leans on the misreading. The related computability results, particularly the undecidability of the halting problem, are the same phenomenon in a form that a software engineer meets directly.

Step 4

The detail

What went wrong, and the shape of the repair

Naive comprehension says that for any property $P(x)$ there is a set $\{x : P(x)\}$. Take $P(x)$ to be $x \notin x$ and the resulting R satisfies $R \in R \iff R \notin R$, which is a contradiction in one line. The repair adopted by ZFC (Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory with choice) is to replace comprehension with separation: given an existing set A, the collection $\{x \in A : P(x)\}$ is a set. Properties may carve subsets out of sets that already exist, and may not create sets from nothing. Russell's argument then proves something harmless instead of something fatal: for any set A, the set $\{x \in A : x \notin x\}$ is not an element of A, so no set contains everything.

Two consequences of this list are worth stating because they are easy to miss. Everything is a set, including the numbers: the ordered pair $(a,b)$ is coded as $\{\{a\},\{a,b\}\}$, a function is a set of ordered pairs, and the natural number n is coded as the set of all smaller naturals, so $0 = \emptyset$, $1 = \{0\}$, $2 = \{0,1\}$. And the axioms are a list of permissions, not a description of a universe: they say which constructions are safe, and everything mathematics does is a finite chain of them.

Cardinality: size by bijection

Same cardinality
Sets A and B have the same cardinality, written $|A| = |B|$, when a bijection exists between them. A set is countable when it is finite or has the same cardinality as $\mathbb{N}$, and uncountable otherwise. This definition, and not any notion of counting, is what "size" means once sets are infinite.

The definition has an immediate consequence that reads as a paradox and is not: an infinite set can have the same cardinality as a proper subset of itself. The even numbers pair with the naturals via $n \mapsto 2n$, so there are exactly as many even numbers as naturals. Dedekind turned this into the definition of infinite, and the hotel in the Feynman test is the same observation dramatised.

Three countability results, in increasing order of surprise. (1) The integers are countable. List them 0, 1, -1, 2, -2, 3, -3, ... Every integer appears exactly once at a finite position, which is a bijection with N. (2) The rationals are countable. Arrange the positive rationals p/q in a grid with p as the row and q as the column, then traverse it along diagonals: 1/1 1/2 1/3 1/4 ... 2/1 2/2 2/3 ... 3/1 3/2 ... 4/1 ... Reading the finite diagonals in turn reaches every cell at a finite position. Skip any fraction not in lowest terms to avoid repeats, and interleave negatives and zero as in (1). Density does not imply uncountability: between any two rationals lies another, and there are still only countably many. (3) A countable union of countable sets is countable. Same diagonal traversal, with row i listing the elements of the i-th set. (This step quietly uses the axiom of choice to pick a listing for each set, which is a good illustration of how invisibly choice enters.) Consequence used in analysis: the algebraic numbers, being roots of polynomials with integer coefficients, form a countable set. Since the reals are uncountable, transcendental numbers not only exist but are overwhelmingly typical, and this was Cantor's proof of their existence before any specific transcendental was easy to exhibit.

Now the other direction. Cantor's diagonal argument, drawn above, shows the set of infinite binary sequences is uncountable. The same argument generalises to any set at all.

Cantor's theorem: |A| < |P(A)| for every set A. There is an injection A -> P(A), namely a -> {a}, so |A| <= |P(A)|. Show no surjection f: A -> P(A) exists. Given any f, define the diagonal set D = { a in A : a is not in f(a) }. D is a subset of A, so D is in P(A). Suppose D = f(d) for some d in A. Ask whether d is in D: if d is in D, then by the definition of D, d is not in f(d) = D; if d is not in D, then d satisfies the defining condition, so d is in D. Both cases contradict. So D is not in the image of f, and f is not onto. Hence |A| < |P(A)| strictly, for every A, finite or infinite. QED Two readings. First, there is no largest cardinal: power sets keep producing bigger ones forever. Second, this is Russell's paradox used constructively rather than destructively; the same self-reference that broke naive set theory becomes a theorem once it is applied inside a set.

Writing $|\mathbb{N}| = \aleph_0$ and $|\mathbb{R}| = 2^{\aleph_0}$, Cantor's theorem gives $\aleph_0 < 2^{\aleph_0}$. Whether any cardinal sits strictly between them is the continuum hypothesis, and the answer is that the question is not settled by the axioms: Godel showed in 1940 that it cannot be disproved from ZFC, and Cohen showed in 1963 that it cannot be proved either. It is independent, in the same sense that the parallel postulate is independent of Euclid's others, which is the connection to the geometry course.

Ordinals and transfinite induction

Cardinals answer "how many". Ordinals answer "in what order", and the distinction only becomes visible in the infinite. An ordinal is a set well ordered by membership, where a well order is a total order in which every non-empty subset has a least element, exactly the well-ordering principle from lecture 1 promoted to a definition. The finite ordinals are the naturals; the first infinite one is $\omega$, the order type of $\mathbb{N}$; then come $\omega + 1$, $\omega + 2$, up to $\omega + \omega$, and onwards.

Ordinal arithmetic is not commutative, which is the cleanest sign that ordinals measure something other than size. $1 + \omega = \omega$, because putting one item before an infinite queue leaves the same order type, while $\omega + 1 > \omega$, because appending an item after all of them creates a last element, and no element of $\omega$ is last. Both have cardinality $\aleph_0$: the same number of elements, arranged differently. Transfinite induction then extends ordinary induction along the ordinals, with a third case for limit ordinals such as $\omega$, which have no immediate predecessor, and it is the standard tool for constructions that must continue past every finite stage.

The axiom of choice and Zorn's lemma

Axiom of choice
For any family of non-empty sets there is a function selecting one element from each. For finitely many sets this is provable without the axiom, by repeated selection. For infinitely many, no rule need exist, and the axiom asserts a simultaneous choice regardless. Russell's illustration: from infinitely many pairs of shoes you can choose without the axiom (take the left one each time), from infinitely many pairs of socks you cannot, because no rule distinguishes them.

Three statements are equivalent to choice, and mathematicians use whichever is convenient. The well-ordering theorem says every set can be well ordered, including the reals, though no explicit well ordering of the reals can be written down. Zorn's lemma says that a partially ordered set in which every chain has an upper bound contains a maximal element. And the product of any family of non-empty sets is non-empty, which is choice restated in the language of products.

Zorn's lemma is the form that actually gets used, because it converts "keep extending until you cannot" into a legitimate step. The proof that every vector space has a basis is the template: consider the poset of linearly independent subsets ordered by inclusion, observe that the union of a chain of independent sets is independent and so is an upper bound, apply Zorn to get a maximal independent set, and check that maximality forces spanning. The same three-step shape proves that every ideal extends to a maximal ideal in algebra, and that bounded functionals extend without increasing norm in functional analysis, which is Hahn-Banach.

The price is non-constructive existence, and occasionally worse. Choice gives non-measurable sets: partition the unit interval by the equivalence relation "differ by a rational", choose one representative from each class, and the resulting Vitali set can be shown to have no consistent Lebesgue measure, since countably many translates of it both cover the interval and would have to share a single measure. This is the concrete reason the analysis course works with sigma-algebras of measurable sets rather than with all subsets. The Banach-Tarski paradox, which decomposes a ball into finitely many pieces that reassemble into two balls of the same size, is the same phenomenon in three dimensions, and the pieces are non-measurable by construction.

First-order logic: syntax, semantics, and the two Godel results

First-order logic makes the language of lecture 1 into a mathematical object. The syntax specifies which strings are formulas and which sequences of formulas are proofs, both checkable mechanically. The semantics specifies a structure, meaning a domain with interpretations for the symbols, and when a formula is true in it. Two relations follow: $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$ says $\varphi$ is provable from the assumptions $\Gamma$, and $\Gamma \models \varphi$ says $\varphi$ is true in every structure making $\Gamma$ true.

ResultStatementWhat it means in practice
Soundness$\Gamma \vdash \varphi$ implies $\Gamma \models \varphi$The proof rules never manufacture falsehoods. Without this, proof would be worthless.
Completeness (Godel, 1929)$\Gamma \models \varphi$ implies $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$If something follows from the axioms in every structure, a finite proof of it exists. Semantic consequence and formal provability coincide.
CompactnessIf every finite subset of $\Gamma$ has a model, so does $\Gamma$Follows from completeness, since proofs are finite. It is what produces non-standard models of arithmetic containing infinite elements.
Incompleteness I (1931)Any consistent, effectively axiomatised system strong enough for arithmetic has a true sentence it cannot proveNo single axiom list settles all arithmetic questions.
Incompleteness II (1931)No such system proves its own consistencyConsistency can only be established from a stronger vantage point, never internally.

The two Godel theorems sound contradictory and are not, because they quantify over different things. Completeness is about a fixed set of axioms and everything that follows from it in all structures: the proof system misses nothing. Incompleteness is about arithmetic truth in the standard model specifically: the axioms fail to pin that model down uniquely, so sentences true of the naturals can fail in other models satisfying the same axioms, and hence cannot be proved. The gap is between "true in every model of the axioms" and "true in the intended model", and completeness only ever addressed the first.

The mechanism of incompleteness is self-reference, made rigorous by arithmetisation. Godel numbering encodes formulas and proofs as numbers, so statements about provability become statements about arithmetic, and a sentence can be constructed that in effect asserts its own unprovability. If the system is consistent, that sentence is unprovable, and therefore true. The parallel with the halting problem is exact in spirit: both diagonalise, both trade on self-reference, and both draw a boundary that no amount of extra effort within the system moves.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

What incompleteness does not say

It does not say that some truths are unknowable, only that no single effectively specified axiom system proves them all; a stronger system proves the previous system's Godel sentence, and then has its own. It does not say mathematics is inconsistent or unreliable. It does not apply to systems too weak to express arithmetic, and some complete decidable theories exist, including Presburger arithmetic (addition without multiplication) and the first-order theory of real closed fields, which is why polynomial inequalities over the reals can be decided by machine. And it says nothing about human minds, consciousness, or the limits of science, despite a long tradition of claiming otherwise. The hypotheses matter: consistent, effectively axiomatised, and strong enough for arithmetic. Drop any one and the theorem does not apply.

Independence is a positive result, not a failure

When the continuum hypothesis was shown independent of ZFC, the reaction in some quarters was that set theory had failed to answer its own question. The better reading is the geometric one. Euclid's parallel postulate is independent of his other axioms, and the consequence was not that geometry collapsed but that there are several consistent geometries, each describing something real, as the axiomatic geometry lecture in the geometry course sets out. Independence marks a genuine fork in the mathematical landscape rather than a hole in it. Set theorists now study models where the continuum hypothesis holds and models where it fails, and the choice of additional axioms is made on grounds of fruitfulness, which is a scientific judgement rather than a logical deduction.

The pragmatic settlement

Working practice across the rest of this track is straightforward and worth stating explicitly. Assume ZFC. Use choice freely, but notice when a proof depends on it, because a proof using Zorn is an existence proof and not a construction, and the difference matters when you want to compute something. Expect non-measurable sets to exist and expect never to meet one, since every set arising from an explicit construction is measurable. Treat the incompleteness theorems as facts about formal systems rather than as facts about mathematics as an activity. On that footing, nothing in this lecture constrains the practical work of the analysis, algebra, and geometry courses, and the three specific dependencies named in step 2 are the whole of the interaction.

Watch out Two traps. The first is treating cardinality as a refined kind of counting, which produces confident errors: infinite sets can match proper subsets, the rationals are dense yet countable, and the algebraic numbers are countable while the reals are not. Bijection is the only definition; intuition about size is unreliable here and should be replaced rather than repaired. The second is invoking incompleteness rhetorically. If a claim about limits of knowledge does not mention consistency, effective axiomatisation, and arithmetic strength, it is not using the theorem, it is borrowing its prestige.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. State Russell's paradox in full, then explain precisely which axiom of ZFC blocks it and why the blocked construction becomes a harmless theorem instead.
  2. Show that the ordered pair coding $(a,b) = \{\{a\},\{a,b\}\}$ satisfies the only property an ordered pair needs, namely $(a,b) = (c,d)$ if and only if $a=c$ and $b=d$.
  3. Exhibit an explicit bijection between $\mathbb{N}$ and $\mathbb{Z}$, and one between $\mathbb{N}$ and $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$. State where the second is used in proving the rationals countable.
  4. Prove that the set of finite binary strings is countable, and then prove that the set of infinite binary strings is not. Identify the exact point where the argument for the first fails for the second.
  5. Prove Cantor's theorem $|A| < |\mathcal{P}(A)|$ in full, and explain how the diagonal set D relates to Russell's R.
  6. Show that the algebraic numbers are countable, and deduce that transcendental numbers exist. Say why this proof exhibits none.
  7. Explain why $1 + \omega \ne \omega + 1$ as ordinals while both have cardinality $\aleph_0$, and state in one sentence what ordinals measure that cardinals do not.
  8. Use Zorn's lemma to prove that every vector space has a basis, naming the poset, the chains, and the upper bounds explicitly. Then say which step would fail without choice.
  9. State the completeness and first incompleteness theorems precisely, and explain in three sentences why they are compatible.
  10. A colleague claims that Godel proved "no system can fully understand itself, so all models are ultimately unreliable". Identify every hypothesis of the theorem their claim ignores, and give the strongest accurate statement you can that stays within what the theorem supports.

Where this is used later

  • Analysis: measure theory restricts to sigma-algebras because non-measurable sets exist, and functional analysis proves Hahn-Banach by Zorn. Countability is also what makes the rationals a valid countable dense set in completeness arguments.
  • Algebra: existence of bases, existence of maximal ideals, and the algebraic closure of a field are all Zorn arguments with the same three-step shape.
  • Geometry and topology: Tychonoff's theorem is equivalent to choice, and the independence of the continuum hypothesis is the exact analogue of the independence of the parallel postulate treated there.
