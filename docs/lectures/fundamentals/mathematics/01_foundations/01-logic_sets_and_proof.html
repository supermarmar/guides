Logic, sets and proof

Mathematics is not a collection of facts, it is a collection of arguments, and every argument in every later lecture is written in one small formal language. This lecture is that language: the five connectives, the two quantifiers, the four proof strategies that between them settle almost everything, and the set notation the objects live in. Most of it will look obvious. The parts that are not obvious, and that quietly ruin later proofs when misread, are implication, the order of quantifiers, and what "for all" means when there is nothing to quantify over.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A mathematical statement is a sentence that is definitely true or definitely false. Connectives (not, and, or, implies, iff) build compound statements out of simple ones, and a truth table settles the truth of any compound statement mechanically. Quantifiers (for all, there exists) let statements range over infinitely many objects, which is where mathematics becomes interesting and where mistakes start. A proof is a finite chain of statements, each following from earlier ones by an accepted rule, ending at the claim.

Four strategies do most of the work. A direct proof assumes the hypothesis and walks to the conclusion. A contrapositive proof proves "not Q implies not P" instead of "P implies Q", which is the same statement wearing a different coat and is often far easier. A proof by contradiction assumes the claim is false and derives an absurdity. Induction proves a statement for every natural number by proving it for the first one and proving that each case forces the next. Everything else, from real analysis to Galois theory, is these four strategies applied to richer objects.

Feynman test Suppose a colleague tells you: "if a borrower misses three consecutive payments, the account is flagged." You look at an account that is not flagged. What do you know? You know it did not miss three consecutive payments, because if it had, it would have been flagged. That is the contrapositive, and you used it without noticing. Now suppose instead you see a flagged account. What do you know about its payment history? Nothing, from that rule alone. The rule says missing three payments is enough to get flagged, not that it is the only way. People conflate those two readings constantly, in code, in policy documents, and in proofs. Logic is the discipline of never conflating them: "P implies Q" and "Q implies P" are different claims, and proving one gives you nothing about the other.
What's beyond a first course A first course lists the connectives, drills truth tables, and practises induction. This lecture adds three things that a first course usually leaves implicit. First, why material implication has the truth table it does, including the vacuous truth case that looks like a bug and is not. Second, the proof that induction and the well-ordering principle are equivalent, so the choice between them is a matter of convenience rather than strength. Third, an honest account of proof by contradiction: it relies on the law of the excluded middle, constructive mathematics rejects it, and knowing where that boundary sits explains why some proofs are called non-constructive and why that criticism has teeth.
Step 2

Why this matters

Every later lecture in the mathematics track is written in the language built here, and the density of that language rises sharply once analysis starts. The definition of a limit is three nested quantifiers with an implication inside, and a reader who cannot negate that sentence mechanically cannot prove a single divergence result. The definition of continuity swaps the order of two of those quantifiers to get uniform continuity, and the entire difference between the two concepts is quantifier order. Nothing about that is deep once the machinery is automatic, and nothing about it is survivable while the machinery is still manual.

The material also earns its place outside pure mathematics, which is worth saying because the notation looks academic. Boolean logic is what a rules engine evaluates, and De Morgan's laws are what let you rewrite NOT (a AND b) as NOT a OR NOT b when refactoring a policy filter or a SQL predicate without changing which rows come back. Quantifier order is what separates "every exposure has a rating" from "there is one rating shared by every exposure", a distinction that model documentation gets wrong often enough to matter. Vacuous truth is why an empty portfolio satisfies every concentration limit you can write, which is correct, occasionally surprising, and a genuine source of silent test passes. The formalism is not decoration; it is the difference between a specification that means one thing and a specification that means whatever the reader assumed.

Step 3

The mental model

Two pictures carry this lecture. The first is the implication square: one conditional statement and its three relatives, only one of which is logically equivalent to it. The second is a decision procedure for choosing a proof strategy, because the hardest part of an early proof is not executing a strategy but picking the right one, and the choice is far more mechanical than it first appears.

Picture one: the implication square

From a conditional $P \Rightarrow Q$ you can form three other statements by swapping and negating. The converse $Q \Rightarrow P$ swaps. The inverse $\lnot P \Rightarrow \lnot Q$ negates. The contrapositive $\lnot Q \Rightarrow \lnot P$ does both. The diagonal pairs are equivalent and the horizontal and vertical pairs are not, which is the single most useful fact in this lecture.

Picture two: choosing a proof strategy

Given a claim to prove, the shape of the claim narrows the strategy almost immediately. The flow below is not a theorem, it is a habit, and it is the habit that separates a reader who stares at a claim from one who starts writing within a minute.

Step 4

The detail

Statements and connectives

A proposition is a declarative sentence with exactly one truth value, true or false. "Seventeen is prime" is a proposition. "Is seventeen prime?" is not, and neither is "x is prime" until x is pinned down, which is what quantifiers will do shortly. Five connectives build compound propositions, and each is defined by what it does to truth values rather than by what it means in English.

ConnectiveSymbolRead asTrue exactly when
Negation$\lnot P$not PP is false
Conjunction$P \land Q$P and Qboth are true
Disjunction$P \lor Q$P or Qat least one is true (inclusive)
Implication$P \Rightarrow Q$if P then QP is false, or Q is true
Biconditional$P \Leftrightarrow Q$P if and only if Qboth have the same truth value

Two of these differ from ordinary speech in ways that cause real trouble. Mathematical or is inclusive: $P \lor Q$ is true when both hold, unlike the "tea or coffee" of a menu. And implication is material, meaning it asserts nothing about causation or relevance. $P \Rightarrow Q$ is defined to be false in exactly one situation, when P is true and Q is false, and true in all three others. That is the whole definition.

Vacuous truth
When P is false, $P \Rightarrow Q$ is true regardless of Q. "Every unicorn in this room is purple" is true, because there are no unicorns here to falsify it. This is not a quirk to be tolerated; it is what makes universal statements behave correctly over empty collections. An empty portfolio satisfies every exposure limit, an empty set is a subset of every set, and a loop body that never executes cannot violate its postcondition.

The reason material implication has to be defined this way becomes clear when you try the alternatives. If $P \Rightarrow Q$ were false whenever P is false, then "if n is divisible by 4 then n is even" would be false for $n = 3$, and a statement everyone regards as a theorem would fail at most integers. If it were undefined when P is false, truth tables would stop being total functions and the whole mechanical apparatus would collapse. The definition given is the only one that keeps universal statements usable.

Truth tables and logical equivalence

Two compound statements are logically equivalent, written $\equiv$, when they have identical truth values under every assignment. Since n propositional variables admit $2^n$ assignments, equivalence is decidable by exhaustion: build the table, compare the columns. The equivalences worth memorising are few.

$$ \lnot(P \land Q) \equiv \lnot P \lor \lnot Q, \qquad \lnot(P \lor Q) \equiv \lnot P \land \lnot Q \qquad \text{(De Morgan)} $$

$$ P \Rightarrow Q \;\equiv\; \lnot P \lor Q \;\equiv\; \lnot Q \Rightarrow \lnot P, \qquad \lnot(P \Rightarrow Q) \equiv P \land \lnot Q $$

$$ P \Leftrightarrow Q \;\equiv\; (P \Rightarrow Q) \land (Q \Rightarrow P) $$

The last of the implication identities is the one to internalise: the negation of an implication is not an implication. It is a conjunction. To deny "if the model is well specified then the residuals are white noise" you do not assert another conditional, you assert that the model is well specified and the residuals are not white noise. Getting this wrong turns a proof by contradiction into nonsense on the first line.

Verifying De Morgan's first law by exhaustion. P Q | P and Q | not(P and Q) | not P | not Q | (not P) or (not Q) ----------+---------+--------------+-------+-------+-------------------- T T | T | F | F | F | F T F | F | T | F | T | T F T | F | T | T | F | T F F | F | T | T | T | T Columns 4 and 7 agree in all four rows, so the two statements are logically equivalent. The same table read differently also settles implication: P Q | P => Q | not P | (not P) or Q ----------+--------+-------+-------------- T T | T | F | T T F | F | F | F <-- the only false row F T | T | T | T F F | T | T | T <-- vacuous truth, twice Only the second row makes the implication false: hypothesis true, conclusion false.

Arguments and validity

An argument is a list of premises followed by a conclusion, and it is valid when the conclusion is true under every assignment that makes all premises true. Validity is about form, not content: a valid argument with a false premise proves nothing, and an invalid argument can stumble onto a true conclusion. Two valid forms and two invalid ones account for most of what appears in practice.

FormPremisesConclusionStatus
Modus ponens$P \Rightarrow Q$, and PQValid
Modus tollens$P \Rightarrow Q$, and $\lnot Q$$\lnot P$Valid
Affirming the consequent$P \Rightarrow Q$, and QPInvalid
Denying the antecedent$P \Rightarrow Q$, and $\lnot P$$\lnot Q$Invalid

The two invalid forms are the converse and inverse errors from the implication square, now dressed as reasoning steps. Modus tollens is the contrapositive in action, and it is the engine of every proof by contradiction: derive something false from an assumption, and the assumption dies.

Quantifiers, and why their order is everything

A predicate $P(x)$ is a statement whose truth depends on a variable ranging over a specified domain. Quantifiers close the variable and turn the predicate back into a proposition: $\forall x \, P(x)$ asserts that every element of the domain satisfies P, and $\exists x \, P(x)$ asserts that at least one does. The domain is part of the statement, and leaving it implicit is the source of half of all quantifier confusion.

Negation passes through quantifiers by flipping them:

$$ \lnot \forall x \, P(x) \;\equiv\; \exists x \, \lnot P(x), \qquad \lnot \exists x \, P(x) \;\equiv\; \forall x \, \lnot P(x). $$

Applied mechanically to a nested statement, this rule is what lets you negate the definition of a limit without thinking, which is exactly the skill real analysis demands. Push the negation inwards one quantifier at a time, flipping each, and when it reaches the innermost implication apply $\lnot(P \Rightarrow Q) \equiv P \land \lnot Q$.

Negating a three-quantifier statement, mechanically. Claim (convergence): for all eps > 0, there exists N, such that for all n > N: |a_n - L| < eps Negation, pushed inwards one quantifier at a time: not [ forall eps > 0, exists N, forall n > N: |a_n - L| < eps ] = exists eps > 0, not [ exists N, forall n > N: |a_n - L| < eps ] = exists eps > 0, forall N, not [ forall n > N: |a_n - L| < eps ] = exists eps > 0, forall N, exists n > N: |a_n - L| >= eps Read back in words: the sequence fails to converge to L when some tolerance eps is never achieved permanently: however far out you go (any N), there is always a later term (some n > N) that misses L by at least eps. No cleverness was used. Each step flipped one quantifier and moved the negation one place right. This is the whole technique.

Order matters, and swapping two adjacent quantifiers of different types generally changes the meaning. Compare over the domain of real numbers:

$$ \forall x \, \exists y \, (y > x) \qquad \text{versus} \qquad \exists y \, \forall x \, (y > x). $$

The first is true: given any x, take $y = x+1$, and note that y may depend on x. The second claims a single y exceeding every real number simultaneously, and is false. The rule of thumb is that a variable bound by $\exists$ can depend on everything quantified to its left, and on nothing quantified to its right. In an applied setting this is the difference between "every exposure has some rating" and "some rating is shared by every exposure", and the two sentences differ by nothing but word order.

Sets: the objects the quantifiers range over

A set is an unordered collection of distinct objects, specified either by listing, $\{2,3,5,7\}$, or by a defining property, $\{n \in \mathbb{N} : n \text{ is prime and } n < 10\}$. The second form, set-builder notation, is a predicate with a domain, which is why sets appear in this lecture rather than a later one: a set is a quantified statement in object form. Membership is written $x \in A$, and containment $A \subseteq B$ means $\forall x \, (x \in A \Rightarrow x \in B)$, which is an implication, which is why the empty set is a subset of everything by vacuous truth.

OperationNotationLogical form
Union$A \cup B$$\{x : x \in A \lor x \in B\}$
Intersection$A \cap B$$\{x : x \in A \land x \in B\}$
Difference$A \setminus B$$\{x : x \in A \land x \notin B\}$
Complement$A^c$$\{x \in U : x \notin A\}$, relative to a universe U
Power set$\mathcal{P}(A)$the set of all subsets of A, of size $2^{|A|}$ when A is finite
Cartesian product$A \times B$$\{(a,b) : a \in A \land b \in B\}$, ordered pairs

The third column is the point of the table. Every set operation is a connective in disguise, so every set identity is a logical equivalence you already know. De Morgan for sets, $(A \cup B)^c = A^c \cap B^c$, is De Morgan for propositions with $\in$ inserted, and the standard proof is exactly that: take an arbitrary element, chase the membership condition through the logical equivalence, conclude both inclusions. Two sets are equal when each contains the other, which turns every set identity into two implications and hands you the proof strategy for free.

The four proof strategies, executed

Strategy is chosen by the shape of the claim, as the second figure set out. Below, each strategy appears once, on a claim small enough that the machinery rather than the mathematics is visible.

DIRECT PROOF. Claim: if n is odd then n^2 is odd. Assume n is odd. Then n = 2k + 1 for some integer k (definition of odd). So n^2 = (2k+1)^2 = 4k^2 + 4k + 1 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1. Since 2k^2 + 2k is an integer, n^2 has the form 2m + 1, so n^2 is odd. QED Unpacking the hypothesis was the whole proof. That is the signature of a claim that wants a direct attack: "n is odd" carries usable structure. CONTRAPOSITIVE. Claim: if n^2 is even then n is even. A direct attack stalls: "n^2 is even" gives n^2 = 2k, and taking a square root leaves nothing usable. So prove the contrapositive instead: if n is odd then n^2 is odd, which is the direct proof above, already finished. Because a conditional and its contrapositive are logically equivalent, the original claim is proved. QED Signature: the conclusion ("n is even") was harder to reach than its negation ("n is odd") was to assume. CONTRADICTION. Claim: sqrt(2) is irrational. Suppose not: sqrt(2) = p/q with p, q integers, q nonzero, and the fraction in lowest terms (no common factor). Then 2 = p^2 / q^2, so p^2 = 2 q^2, so p^2 is even, so p is even (by the contrapositive result above), say p = 2r. Then 4r^2 = 2q^2, so q^2 = 2r^2, so q^2 is even, so q is even. But now p and q are both even, contradicting "lowest terms". The assumption is untenable, so sqrt(2) is irrational. QED Signature: a non-existence claim ("no such fraction exists"). There is nothing to construct, so assume the object exists and break it. INDUCTION. Claim: 1 + 2 + ... + n = n(n+1)/2 for all n >= 1. Base case, n = 1: left side = 1, right side = 1*2/2 = 1. Holds. Inductive step: assume the formula holds for some k >= 1, that is 1 + 2 + ... + k = k(k+1)/2. (hypothesis) Then 1 + ... + k + (k+1) = k(k+1)/2 + (k+1) (by hypothesis) = (k+1) * (k/2 + 1) = (k+1)(k+2)/2, which is the formula with k+1 in place of k. So truth at k forces truth at k+1. With the base case, the formula holds for every n >= 1. QED Signature: a claim indexed by the naturals, with each case reachable from the one before.

Two variants of induction are worth naming now, because later courses use them without comment. Strong induction assumes the claim for all values up to k rather than at k alone, which is what proofs about factorisation need, since the factors of k+1 can be much smaller than k. Structural induction replaces the naturals with any recursively built objects, such as expressions, trees, or the well-formed formulas of this lecture, and it is the standard tool for proving that every syntactically valid object has some property.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Induction and well-ordering are the same axiom

The well-ordering principle states that every non-empty set of natural numbers has a least element. It looks like an observation and is in fact equivalent to induction, which is worth proving because the equivalence explains why induction is an axiom rather than a theorem.

Well-ordering implies induction. Suppose P(1) holds and P(k) implies P(k+1) for every k, yet P fails somewhere. Let S = { n in N : P(n) is false }. Then S is non-empty, so by well-ordering it has a least element m. Since P(1) holds, m > 1, so m-1 is a natural number not in S, meaning P(m-1) is true. But the inductive step applied to m-1 gives P(m), contradicting m in S. So S is empty. QED Induction implies well-ordering. Let S be a set of naturals with no least element; show S is empty. Let P(n) be the statement "no element of S is less than or equal to n". P(1) holds: if 1 were in S it would be least. If P(k) holds then k+1 is not in S either, since it would then be least, so P(k+1) holds. By induction P(n) holds for all n, so S contains nothing. QED

Each principle derives the other, so a formal development of arithmetic may take either as the axiom and prove the other as a theorem. Peano's axioms take induction. The practical consequence is that "take the smallest counterexample" and "induct" are interchangeable proof openings, and picking the one that makes the argument shorter is a matter of taste rather than logical strength.

Proof by contradiction is not free

The step from "assuming $\lnot P$ leads to absurdity" to "therefore P" uses the law of the excluded middle, $P \lor \lnot P$, and its companion the law of double negation, $\lnot \lnot P \equiv P$. Classical logic accepts both. Constructive or intuitionistic logic rejects them, on the view that to assert an existence claim you must exhibit the object, and refuting its non-existence is not the same thing.

This is not philosophical fussiness with no cash value. A constructive existence proof yields an algorithm, and a classical one may not. There are famous non-constructive proofs that some object exists while providing no way to find it, and in computational settings that difference is the difference between a theorem and a program. The rule of thumb for this track: proof by contradiction is entirely legitimate and used throughout, but when a claim asserts that something exists, prefer a construction if one is available, because the construction tells you more than the theorem does.

A distinction worth keeping Proof by contradiction and proof by contrapositive look similar and are not the same. The contrapositive proof of $P \Rightarrow Q$ assumes $\lnot Q$ and derives $\lnot P$, and it is constructively valid. The contradiction proof assumes $P \land \lnot Q$ and derives an absurdity, and it is not. Many written "contradiction" proofs are secretly contrapositive proofs with an unnecessary extra assumption dragged along, and stripping the assumption usually shortens them. If your contradiction never actually used P, you were proving the contrapositive.

Where naive set theory breaks

The set-builder notation used above quietly assumes that any property defines a set. Russell's paradox shows it does not. Let $R = \{x : x \notin x\}$, the set of all sets that are not members of themselves, and ask whether $R \in R$. If it is, then by its defining property it is not; if it is not, then it qualifies for membership, so it is. Both answers are contradictory, so R cannot exist, and unrestricted comprehension has to go.

The repair is axiomatic set theory, where sets are built from existing sets by restricted operations rather than conjured from arbitrary properties. That is the subject of lecture 4, and everything in this lecture survives the repair unchanged, because every set constructed here was carved out of a domain already known to exist. The lesson to carry forward is smaller than the paradox suggests: always specify the domain a predicate ranges over, and the notation stays safe.

Watch out The commonest failure in early proofs is not a wrong deduction, it is proving the converse by accident. You are asked to show $P \Rightarrow Q$, you write "suppose Q", you derive P, and everything you wrote is true and irrelevant. The defence is mechanical: before writing anything, write down what you are assuming and what you are aiming at, and check at the end that the assumption is the hypothesis and the target is the conclusion. The same check catches the second commonest failure, which is assuming the thing you were asked to prove somewhere in the middle of a long chain.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Build the truth table for $P \Rightarrow Q$ and for $\lnot P \lor Q$, and confirm the columns agree. Then state, in one sentence, the only circumstance in which an implication is false.
  2. Write the converse, inverse, and contrapositive of "if a matrix is invertible then its determinant is non-zero". Which of the three is equivalent to the original, and which two are equivalent to each other?
  3. Negate $\lnot(P \Rightarrow Q)$ into a form with no implication symbol, showing the equivalence you used at each step.
  4. Prove De Morgan's second law, $\lnot(P \lor Q) \equiv \lnot P \land \lnot Q$, by truth table, then prove the set version $(A \cup B)^c = A^c \cap B^c$ by double inclusion and note where the two proofs are the same argument.
  5. Over the real numbers, decide the truth of $\forall x \exists y \,(xy = 1)$ and of $\exists y \forall x \,(xy = 0)$. For each, either exhibit the witness or the counterexample.
  6. Negate mechanically: "for every $\epsilon > 0$ there exists $\delta > 0$ such that for all x, if $|x - a| < \delta$ then $|f(x) - f(a)| < \epsilon$". Show each quantifier flip as a separate line.
  7. Prove that if $n^3$ is odd then n is odd. State which strategy you chose and why the other three are worse here.
  8. Prove by induction that $n! > 2^n$ for all $n \ge 4$. Say explicitly why the base case is 4 rather than 1.
  9. Prove that there are infinitely many primes. Identify which of the four strategies you used, and whether your proof is constructive.
  10. Explain why the empty set is a subset of every set, using the definition of $\subseteq$ as an implication. Then explain why an empty portfolio satisfies every concentration limit, and say whether that is a defect of the formalism or a feature.
