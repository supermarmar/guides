Fundamentals

Mathematics

Four pure mathematics courses, twenty-three lectures in total, built from the pure core of the University of Pretoria BSc in Mathematics (programme 02133263) and extended past the degree into the material a quantitative modeller eventually needs: measure theory, functional analysis, topology, Galois theory, and the measure-theoretic construction of probability. Applied and computational modules from the UP curriculum are deliberately left out. Everything here is proof-first, and every lecture states its prerequisites in terms of earlier lectures rather than in terms of a syllabus.

The shape of the subject

Pure mathematics divides cleanly into three branches sitting on one foundation, and the four courses below are exactly those pieces. The foundation is logic, sets, proof, and counting: the machinery every later argument is written in. Analysis is the study of limits and approximation, and it runs from the derivative to the Lebesgue integral. Algebra is the study of structure and symmetry, and it runs from solving linear systems to deciding which polynomials can be solved by radicals. Geometry and topology study shape and continuity, and they run from Euclid's postulates to the classification of surfaces. Almost every advanced result is a hybrid: functional analysis is analysis conducted on algebraic objects, algebraic topology answers topological questions with group theory, and differential geometry does calculus on curved spaces.

Read each course top to bottom rather than reading the page left to right. Every course is a single argument told twice: once concretely, then again in the general setting that explains why the concrete version worked. Real analysis re-derives calculus on the real line and finds that only completeness and the triangle inequality were ever needed, which is what point-set topology then abstracts. Linear algebra proves things about R^n, then discovers in its second lecture that the proofs never used the coordinates, which is what abstract vector spaces then formalise. Measure theory does the same to the Riemann integral, and Galois theory does it to polynomial equations. The pattern is the subject.

How to use this

Foundations comes first and is short. After it, the three branch courses are independent of each other until their closing lectures, where the hybrids appear: functional analysis and measure-theoretic probability in analysis, differential geometry and algebraic topology in geometry. Reading a single course straight through therefore works, and so does reading all four in order. If the goal is to support the statistics and machine learning tracks rather than to study mathematics for its own sake, the shortest useful path is analysis 01 and 02, algebra 01 and 02, then analysis 04 and 07. All twenty-three lectures are built; the course pages carry the detailed sequences and the UP module each lecture is drawn from.

What "pure only" leaves out

The UP BSc in Mathematics mixes pure and applied modules throughout its three years. This track takes the pure core and drops the rest, so the excluded set is worth naming: numerical analysis (WTW 123 and WTW 383), mathematical modelling and programming (WTW 152), dynamical processes and differential equations (WTW 162, WTW 286, WTW 382), partial differential equations (WTW 386), and continuum mechanics (WTW 387). The two financial engineering electives (WTW 354 and WTW 364) are also excluded, though not because they are applied: they are already covered in depth by the financial engineering course. The mathematical statistics modules (the WST stream) belong to the statistics course.

One borderline call went the other way. Vector calculus (WTW 248) is taught as an applied module in most departments, and Green, Gauss, and Stokes are usually motivated by fluid flow and electromagnetism. It is kept here because the generalised Stokes theorem on manifolds is the destination of the differential geometry lecture, and the classical three theorems are the only honest way to reach it.

Source material

The core follows the University of Pretoria BSc in Mathematics (programme 02133263, 2026 yearbook), specifically the pure WTW modules: WTW 114, 115 and 124 in the first year, WTW 211, 218, 220, 221, 248 and 285 in the second, and WTW 310, 320, 381 and 389 in the third. Module content is taken from the yearbook descriptions, so the treatment matches what the degree actually examines rather than a generic syllabus.

The extensions are the standard next courses in a British or American mathematics degree, and the intended references are the usual ones: Rudin for real analysis, Folland or Billingsley for measure and probability, Munkres for topology, Dummit and Foote for algebra, Do Carmo for differential geometry, Hatcher for algebraic topology, and Mac Lane for category theory. Where a lecture serves a downstream track in this repository, the applications are drawn from that track: measure-theoretic probability builds towards the filtrations and change-of-measure arguments used in financial engineering, and the linear algebra lectures build towards the decompositions used in machine learning.
