Lectures / Economics

History of economic thought

The final lecture in the course. Retrace the intellectual arc from Smith and Ricardo through Mill, Marx, the marginalists, Keynes, Friedman, Lucas, and the modern synthesis. Each major thinker is best understood as responding to a specific puzzle their predecessors could not solve. By the end you should be able to place the rest of the course in its chronological context.

Lecture 25 of 25 Reading time, 40 minutes Part IV: field economics
Step 1

The 30-second version

Economics as a self-conscious discipline began with Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations (1776). Smith's themes (the division of labour, the invisible hand, the limits of government) set the agenda for over two centuries. Classical economics (Smith, Ricardo, Malthus, Mill) thought in long-run terms with land, labour, and capital. The marginalist revolution (Jevons, Menger, Walras, around 1870) introduced calculus and the marginal-utility framework that underpins Lectures 1-3. Marshall (1890) consolidated the framework into the supply-and-demand presentation we still use.

Keynes's General Theory (1936) added the short-run failure of aggregate demand and the role for fiscal stabilisation. The post-war neoclassical synthesis combined Keynesian short-run with classical long-run. Friedman, Phelps, and the rational-expectations revolution (Lucas, Sargent, Prescott) challenged the Keynesian synthesis in the 1970s. The "New Keynesian" response of the 1990s integrated rational expectations with nominal rigidities; this is the framework central banks use today. The 2008 crisis and the rise of behavioural and applied microeconomic empirical work shaped the modern field.

Feynman test Each generation of economists asked a question the previous generation could not answer, built a framework to answer it, and then handed their question to the next generation. The arc is cumulative: Keynes's short-run model presupposes Marshall's micro; modern DSGE models presuppose Keynes's macro. Knowing the history is knowing why each piece of the modern toolkit looks the way it does.
What's beyond the syllabus A full history-of-thought course would spend a term on each major figure. This lecture is a guided tour: enough chronology to place the rest of the course, with pointers to deeper reading for each major thinker. It also flags the controversies that the canonical history glosses over (the marginalist revolution's debt to thinkers it sidelined, the gendered erasure of women in the classical canon, the post-Keynesian and Austrian alternatives to the mainstream story).
Step 2

Why this matters

You cannot read modern economic-policy commentary without picking up unstated allegiances to one or another tradition. "Austerity is necessary" usually means an implicit classical or supply-side view; "stimulus is necessary" usually means an implicit Keynesian view; "the central bank should focus on inflation alone" is a monetarist or modern Taylor-rule view. Knowing the history lets you locate any argument in the tradition it draws from and assess what is and is not implicit in it.

Step 3

The mental model

Think of economics as a chain of conversations across centuries. Smith answers Quesnay; Ricardo answers Smith; Marx answers Ricardo; Marshall answers them all; Keynes answers Marshall; Friedman answers Keynes; Lucas answers Friedman; New Keynesians answer Lucas. The questions evolve but each answer carries forward something from the last. The course you have just taken is the latest installment.

Step 4

The detail

Pre-classical and Smith

Eighteenth-century economic writing was dominated by mercantilist thought (trade as zero-sum, gold accumulation as the goal of state policy) and the French Physiocrats (Quesnay's Tableau Economique as the first model of an economy as a circular flow, with agriculture as the only productive sector).

Adam Smith (An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations, 1776) provided the founding text. His themes: the division of labour as the source of productivity gains (the pin-factory example), the invisible hand of competitive markets, the limits of mercantilism, and the proper but circumscribed role of government. Smith's framework was descriptive and qualitative; the mathematics came later.

Classical economics

David Ricardo (Principles of Political Economy and Taxation, 1817) sharpened Smith's themes and introduced comparative advantage (Lecture 20) and the labour theory of value. Thomas Malthus (Essay on the Principle of Population, 1798) introduced the population-vs-subsistence trap that bears his name. John Stuart Mill (Principles of Political Economy, 1848) was the great synthesist of the era, integrating the insights into the standard textbook of the next half-century. The classical economists were preoccupied with long-run questions (the path of wages, profits, rents) and used a labour-theory-of-value framework that the marginalists would later discard.

Karl Marx (Capital, 1867) took Ricardo's labour theory of value to a more radical conclusion: capitalism contains contradictions that lead to its eventual breakdown (falling rate of profit, immiseration of the proletariat, periodic crises). Marx's economics is less influential in the mainstream than his sociology and political theory, but his diagnosis of capitalist crises and the contradiction between capital and labour shaped twentieth-century policy through the European socialist tradition.

The marginalist revolution

Around 1870, three thinkers independently introduced marginal analysis: William Stanley Jevons (UK), Carl Menger (Austria), and Leon Walras (France). The shift was conceptually massive: value is determined not by labour content (the classical view) but by marginal utility (the consumer-theory view of Lecture 2). The mathematics of constrained optimisation arrived in economics for the first time. Walras's general-equilibrium theory (which we touched on in Lecture 4) constructed a system of simultaneous equations for an entire economy.

Alfred Marshall (Principles of Economics, 1890) consolidated marginalism into the supply-and-demand presentation that became standard for the next century. Marshall's compromise between classical long-run reasoning (the supply curve as the marginal cost of production) and marginal-utility-based demand reasoning is essentially the framework of Lecture 1.

The marginalist revolution also marginalised (in the other sense) thinkers like Henry George (single tax on land), the German Historical School, and various continental traditions. The "history of economic thought" as taught is partly a story of selective remembering.

The neoclassical period to Keynes

From Marshall to the 1930s, the discipline became increasingly mathematical and consolidated around the marginalist framework. Vilfredo Pareto introduced indifference curves and Pareto efficiency. Frank Ramsey (whose name appears in Lecture 19) developed dynamic optimisation. Arthur Pigou (Lecture 7) introduced externalities and welfare economics. Knut Wicksell developed the modern theory of capital and the relationship between the natural and market rates of interest, anticipating Keynes.

The 1929-33 Great Depression created a crisis of intuition. Classical economics had no good explanation for why output and employment could fall so far and stay down for so long.

Keynes

John Maynard Keynes (The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money, 1936) proposed that aggregate demand could be persistently deficient, that fiscal policy could be a stabilisation tool, and that the long-run classical analysis did not apply on the time-scales relevant to ordinary economic life. The famous "in the long run we are all dead" was Keynes's swipe at the classical insistence that the long-run equilibrium was the only thing worth modelling.

Keynes's actual book is hard to read. The framework of Lecture 11 (IS-LM, AD-AS) is a 1937 reformulation by John Hicks that distilled the General Theory into a graphical model. The Hicks-Hansen IS-LM dominated macro teaching for the next forty years and is still the standard pedagogical entry point.

The post-war neoclassical synthesis

Paul Samuelson and James Tobin promoted what became the consensus framework from 1945 to 1970: Keynesian short-run with classical long-run, with the Phillips curve providing a usable trade-off between inflation and unemployment. Government active management of demand was the prescription. The era produced the heyday of fiscal stabilisation, the establishment of the Council of Economic Advisers in the US, and the Treasury's central role in UK economic management.

Monetarism and the Friedman counter-revolution

Milton Friedman argued that the Phillips trade-off was an illusion: the long-run Phillips curve is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment. Attempts to push unemployment below the natural rate produce accelerating inflation, not a permanently lower jobless rate. Friedman's 1968 American Economic Association presidential address ("The Role of Monetary Policy") is the canonical statement. Edmund Phelps made the same argument simultaneously and won a separate later Nobel for the work.

The 1970s stagflation (inflation rising alongside unemployment) appeared to vindicate Friedman and discredit the Phillips trade-off. Monetary aggregates became the focus of policy ("monetary targeting" in the UK, US, and Germany in the late 1970s). The experiment was practical only briefly; broad-money targeting proved unreliable because money demand was unstable. But the underlying argument that monetary policy cannot deliver permanent real-side gains was absorbed.

Rational expectations and Lucas critique

Robert Lucas (1970s) and Thomas Sargent took Friedman's argument further. If agents form rational expectations (use all available information including knowledge of the policy regime), they will see through any systematic monetary policy and undo its real effects. Only unanticipated policy can have temporary real effects. The Lucas critique applied even more sharply: any reduced-form macroeconomic model (including IS-LM) breaks down when policy regimes change, because the underlying decision rules of agents adjust.

The rational-expectations revolution swept macroeconomics in the 1980s. Real business cycle theory (Kydland-Prescott, Prescott alone) went further: business cycles are not market failures but optimal responses to technology shocks. The model was clean, microfounded, and entirely contradictory of Keynesian intuition. It was also unable to fit several stylised facts (the labour-supply elasticity required, the cyclical behaviour of real wages).

The New Keynesian response

By the late 1990s a synthesis had emerged: New Keynesian economics integrated rational expectations and microfoundations with nominal rigidities (sticky prices, sticky wages). Models with monopolistic competition (each firm faces a downward-sloping demand and chooses prices subject to adjustment costs) and Calvo pricing (each period a fraction of firms can adjust) produced macro dynamics that looked Keynesian in the short run, classical in the long run, and microfounded throughout.

The workhorse framework today is the New Keynesian DSGE model: three equations (a forward-looking IS, a Phillips curve, a Taylor rule for monetary policy), elaborated to many sectors and frictions. Variants are used at the Bank of England (BEQM/COMPASS), the ECB (NAWM), and the Fed (FRB/US, SIGMA). The framework's failures during 2008-09 (no financial sector, no zero-lower-bound machinery, missing fat tails) spurred the post-crisis literature on financial frictions and macroprudential policy (Lecture 15).

The applied micro renaissance

The 1990s onward saw a parallel revolution in applied microeconomics: the credibility revolution of Card, Angrist, Imbens (Lecture 17). The work shifted economics empirically toward causal identification and away from structural modelling. The RCT movement in development economics (Banerjee, Duflo, Kremer, Lecture 24) was an offshoot. The 2008 crisis and the rise of behavioural economics (Thaler, Lecture 21) further shifted the field's centre of gravity.

The field today

Modern economics is plural. The mainstream uses rational-actor models with friction; behavioural economics treats real-actor models with biases; applied micro emphasises causal identification over structural modelling; macro is divided between DSGE traditions and heterodox alternatives (post-Keynesian, MMT, agent-based models). The applied policy work draws eclectically from all of these.

The big open questions: whether macro models adequately capture financial-sector dynamics; whether behavioural realism can be integrated with formal modelling at scale; how to handle climate and intergenerational welfare in standard frameworks; how to do welfare economics when revealed-preference assumptions fail. These are the questions the next generation of the discipline is working on.

Step 5

Synthesis

The course is complete. You have the analytics of micro choice, the architecture of macro coordination, the empirical toolkit for testing economic claims, and the field applications that put each piece to work. You also have the historical context: each lecture sits inside a tradition of inquiry going back two centuries. From here, the practical next step is reading: the Bank of England's Inflation Report, the IMF's World Economic Outlook, an issue of The Economist, a paper from the most recent NBER working-paper list. The vocabulary is now yours.
