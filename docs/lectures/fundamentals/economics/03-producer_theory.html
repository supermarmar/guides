Producer theory

The symmetric exercise for firms. Start with a technology that turns inputs into output, give the firm input prices and an output price, ask it to maximise profit, and out drops the supply curve. The mathematics is the same Lagrangian machinery as Lecture 2, applied to a different optimisation problem.

Lecture 3 of 25 Reading time, 45 minutes Part I: microeconomics
Step 1

The 30-second version

A firm has a production function that turns inputs (labour, capital, materials) into output. Given input prices, it picks the input combination that produces a target output at minimum cost: this yields the firm's cost function. Given the output price, it then picks the output level that maximises profit: this yields its supply function. Aggregate across firms and you have the market supply curve from Lecture 1.

The single most useful object is the firm's marginal cost curve, which in a price-taking firm is its supply curve above the average-variable-cost minimum. The deeper concept is returns to scale: whether doubling inputs doubles, more than doubles, or less than doubles output. Returns to scale governs whether industries naturally concentrate (increasing returns, leading to monopoly) or fragment (decreasing returns, leading to many small firms).

Feynman test A firm is a recipe: pour in workers, machines, electricity, get out widgets. The recipe is the production function. To make a given number of widgets, there are many input mixes that would work. The firm picks the cheapest one. To decide how many widgets to make, it asks: at the market price, does the next widget bring in more than it cost to make? If yes, make it; if no, stop. Producer theory is just the arithmetic of those two decisions.
What's beyond the syllabus Principles courses introduce the cost curves graphically and stop there. This lecture also writes down the duality between the cost function and the input demand function (Shephard's lemma) and explains why returns to scale, not "barriers to entry", is the deep determinant of industry structure. It also explicitly cross-links to the cost of capital lecture, because the firm's "cost of capital" is exactly the rental rate on the K input in our cost-minimisation problem.
Step 2

Why this matters

Producer theory is the supply side of every market model in the rest of the course. Without it, monopoly pricing (Lecture 5) is just an assertion, the welfare theorems (Lecture 4) have nothing to optimise over, and competition policy (Lecture 22) has no rationale. It is also the bridge from microeconomics to corporate finance: the discount rate that turns up in NPV calculations is the same r that turns up here as the rental rate on capital.

For a credit-risk modeller, producer theory is what justifies treating firm earnings as a function of input costs (a key driver in stressed-scenario PD models) and what makes the difference between a firm with constant returns to scale (linear margin compression under stress) and one with increasing returns (vulnerable to sudden demand drops because fixed costs do not shrink).

Step 3

The mental model

Picture two inputs, labour L and capital K, on a chart. Curves in the plane are isoquants: input combinations that produce the same output. Straight lines are isocost lines: input combinations that cost the same. To make a given output as cheaply as possible, the firm finds the isocost line that just touches the chosen isoquant. The tangency point is the optimal input mix, exactly analogous to the consumer's optimum in Lecture 2.

Step 4

The detail

The production function

For a single-output firm, technology is summarised by a production function Q = F(K, L) giving maximum output from a capital-labour pair. Marginal products are the partial derivatives. The law of diminishing marginal returns says that, holding one input fixed, the marginal product of the variable input eventually falls (it must, because the fixed input becomes a bottleneck). The marginal rate of technical substitution is

$$ MRTS = \frac{MP_L}{MP_K} = \frac{\partial F / \partial L}{\partial F / \partial K} $$

and equals the absolute slope of the isoquant. Diminishing MRTS along an isoquant is the assumption that produces conventionally convex isoquants (the geometric analogue of convex consumer preferences).

Returns to scale

What happens to output if we double both inputs? Three possibilities:

  • Constant returns to scale (CRS): F(λK, λL) = λ F(K, L). Doubling inputs doubles output. The textbook benchmark. Cobb-Douglas with exponents summing to one.
  • Increasing returns to scale (IRS): F(λK, λL) > λ F(K, L) for λ > 1. Doubling inputs more than doubles output. Typical of fixed-cost-heavy industries (software, pharmaceuticals, networks).
  • Decreasing returns to scale (DRS): doubling inputs less than doubles output. Typical when there is an unmodelled fixed factor (talent of the founder, location, regulatory licence).

Returns to scale is the deep determinant of industry structure. CRS supports many small firms. IRS pushes towards natural monopoly. We will see this again in Lectures 5 and 22.

Cost minimisation

The firm's cost-minimisation problem (CMP) is

$$ \min_{K, L \geq 0} wL + rK \quad \text{subject to} \quad F(K, L) \geq Q $$

The Lagrangian is symmetric to the consumer's:

$$ \mathcal{L} = wL + rK + \mu \big( Q - F(K, L) \big) $$

First-order conditions give MRTS = w/r, the geometric tangency we drew. The solutions Lc(w, r, Q) and Kc(w, r, Q) are the conditional input demands. The minimum cost itself is the cost function:

$$ C(w, r, Q) = w L^c + r K^c $$

By Shephard's lemma,

$$ \frac{\partial C}{\partial w} = L^c(w, r, Q) \qquad \frac{\partial C}{\partial r} = K^c(w, r, Q) $$

This is the producer-side mirror of the consumer-side result in Lecture 2 (with the same name). It lets you recover input demands by differentiating the cost function rather than solving the CMP again.

Worked example: Cobb-Douglas production

Let F(K, L) = Kα Lβ. CMP first-order conditions give

$$ \frac{\beta K}{\alpha L} = \frac{w}{r} \quad \Rightarrow \quad K = \frac{\alpha w}{\beta r} L $$

Substitute into the constraint and solve. With α + β = 1 (CRS), the cost function turns out to be linear in Q:

$$ C(w, r, Q) = Q \cdot k \cdot w^\beta r^\alpha $$

where k collects the constants. The marginal cost is constant in Q (which is exactly what CRS predicts: there is no notion of "scale" because every doubling looks like the last one).

Short run vs long run

In the short run, some inputs are fixed (typically capital). The firm faces fixed costs (rent on the fixed capital, regardless of output) plus variable costs (labour, materials). In the long run, all inputs are variable: there are no fixed costs, only the choice of optimal scale.

Short-run total cost: STC(Q) = FC + VC(Q). Short-run average total cost: SATC(Q) = STC(Q) / Q. As Q rises, SATC starts high (fixed cost spread over few units), falls (fixed cost amortised), then rises (diminishing returns to the variable input bite). The result is a U-shape.

The long-run average cost (LRAC) curve is the envelope of all short-run SATC curves. Its shape depends on returns to scale: flat for CRS, falling for IRS, rising for DRS. Some industries combine all three: a U-shaped LRAC with falling IRS at small scale, flat CRS at "minimum efficient scale", and rising DRS beyond.

Profit maximisation

A price-taking firm in a competitive market chooses output Q to maximise profit:

$$ \pi(Q) = P \cdot Q - C(Q) $$

The first-order condition is

$$ P = C'(Q) = MC(Q) $$

The firm produces up to the point where the price of the last unit equals its marginal cost. Below that quantity, the next unit adds more revenue than cost; above it, the next unit costs more than it earns. The second-order condition requires MC to be rising at the chosen Q (otherwise the FOC describes a minimum, not a maximum).

This is the supply curve: a competitive firm's supply curve is its marginal cost curve, above the shutdown point.

The shutdown decision

Even if a firm cannot cover fixed costs, it may still operate in the short run as long as the price covers average variable cost. The logic: fixed costs are paid whether the firm operates or not. Operating just needs to bring in more than its variable costs to be worth doing. So the short-run supply curve is MC above the minimum of AVC. In the long run, all costs are variable, and the shutdown threshold is the minimum of ATC instead.

Below those thresholds, supply is zero. This is why the market supply curve in Lecture 1 has a vertical segment at low prices: enough firms find it cheaper to shut down than to operate, and the quantity supplied collapses to zero.

From firm supply to market supply

Market supply at price P is the horizontal sum of individual firms' supply functions. In the short run, the number of firms is fixed. In the long run, free entry and exit drive economic profit to zero: any firm earning positive profit attracts entry, which expands supply, which lowers price, which erodes profit. The long-run market supply curve in a constant-cost industry is therefore horizontal at the minimum of LRAC: the industry can expand or contract by adjusting the number of firms, each producing at minimum efficient scale.

The link to corporate finance

The rental rate r on capital is exactly the cost of capital from the financial-management course. It includes a risk-free real rate, an inflation expectation, a depreciation allowance, and a risk premium reflecting the firm's leverage and the cyclicality of its earnings. The CMP is therefore not a free-standing micro exercise but the per-period cost side of an NPV calculation. See cost of capital and WACC for the discount-rate side; the operating-cost side comes from here.

Step 5

Synthesis and what's next

You now have both halves of the Lecture-1 chart derived from primitives. Demand comes from consumer optimisation; supply comes from firm optimisation. The next lecture puts them together in a competitive market and proves that the resulting equilibrium maximises total surplus (the First Welfare Theorem). After that, the rest of microeconomics is a tour of how that result breaks: monopoly (Lecture 5), strategic interaction (Lecture 6), externalities (Lecture 7), private information (Lecture 8).
