Time series econometrics

When data are ordered in time, observations are no longer independent: today's GDP is correlated with yesterday's. New problems arise (stationarity, unit roots, spurious regressions) and new techniques are needed (ARMA models, vector autoregressions, cointegration). The tools here underpin macro forecasting, IFRS 9 macroeconomic scenarios, and interest-rate-risk modelling.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A time series is stationary if its mean, variance, and autocovariances do not depend on the time index. Stationary series can be modelled as ARMA (autoregressive moving average) processes: yt as a linear combination of past ys and past shocks. Non-stationary series often have a unit root: shocks have permanent effects, and the variance grows without bound. The Dickey-Fuller test asks whether a unit root is present.

Non-stationary series can still be cointegrated: two random walks can move together in the long run, with deviations from their long-run relationship being themselves stationary. The Granger representation theorem links this to an error correction model. Vector autoregressions (VARs) generalise univariate ARMA to many series and are the workhorse for macro impulse-response analysis and policy simulation.

Feynman test A time series is a movie of one variable. If you average the early frames and the late frames, you get the same answer in a stationary series and different answers in a non-stationary one. Stationary series eventually return to their mean; non-stationary series wander off. Modelling each kind requires different techniques, and confusing them produces "spurious regression": two unrelated random walks that look highly correlated just because they both wander.
What's beyond the syllabus BSc principles courses sometimes skip time series altogether. This lecture covers the standard ARIMA-VAR-cointegration toolkit and explicitly connects to IFRS 9 forward-looking scenarios and IRRBB modelling, the two places banking practice most uses these techniques.
Step 2

Why this matters

Macroeconomic data are time series. Inflation, GDP, interest rates, exchange rates, unemployment: all sampled at fixed intervals, ordered in time, and highly autocorrelated. Every macro forecast is some flavour of time-series model. For banking, IFRS 9 ECL calculations require macro scenarios over the lifetime of the loan, and these scenarios come from time-series models. IRRBB calculations require simulated paths of the yield curve, often via Vasicek-style stochastic processes. Stress-test scenarios are designed conditional on time-series-model-based plausibility.

Time-series identification is also the macro version of Lecture 17's causal-inference question. The Cholesky decomposition of a VAR is one way of recovering structural shocks; sign restrictions and external instruments are modern alternatives.

Step 3

The mental model

A stochastic process generates a sequence of values in time. The simplest model is white noise: independent, identically distributed, zero-mean shocks. The most useful family of models takes those shocks and convolves them with past values to produce realistic patterns of persistence (autoregressive part) and moving-average smoothing.

Step 4

The detail

Stationarity

A time series yt is weakly (covariance) stationary if:

  • E[yt] = μ for all t (constant mean),
  • Var[yt] = σ2 for all t (constant variance),
  • Cov[yt, yt−k] = γ(k) depending only on the lag k, not on t (constant autocovariance structure).

Strict stationarity additionally requires the entire joint distribution to be time-invariant. Weak stationarity suffices for most linear time-series analysis. Many macro series are not stationary in levels but are after differencing (GDP, prices, exchange rates) or after detrending (the cycle around a deterministic trend).

ARMA models

An AR(p) (autoregressive of order p) process is

$$ y_t = c + \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \phi_2 y_{t-2} + \ldots + \phi_p y_{t-p} + \varepsilon_t $$

where εt is white noise. An MA(q) (moving average of order q) is

$$ y_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t + \theta_1 \varepsilon_{t-1} + \ldots + \theta_q \varepsilon_{t-q} $$

ARMA(p, q) combines both. The AR(p) is stationary if the roots of the characteristic polynomial 1 − φ1z − ... − φpzp = 0 all lie outside the unit circle. Estimation is by maximum likelihood or by Yule-Walker equations (which use sample autocorrelations to back out the φs).

Model identification (picking p and q) traditionally uses the Box-Jenkins methodology: examine the autocorrelation function (ACF) and partial autocorrelation function (PACF). AR(p) has PACF cutting off at lag p; MA(q) has ACF cutting off at lag q; ARMA(p, q) has both declining gradually. Modern practice uses information criteria (AIC, BIC) to select p and q.

Unit roots and ARIMA

An AR(1) with φ = 1 is a random walk:

$$ y_t = y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t $$

It is non-stationary: shocks accumulate, variance grows linearly with t, and the autocorrelation does not decay. Many financial series (log share prices, exchange rates) are well approximated by random walks. To make them stationary, take first differences: Δyt = yt − yt−1 = εt, which is white noise.

A series that becomes stationary after d differences is integrated of order d, written I(d). An ARIMA(p, d, q) model fits an ARMA(p, q) to the d-times differenced series.

The Augmented Dickey-Fuller test tests the null of a unit root against the alternative of stationarity. The regression is

$$ \Delta y_t = \alpha + \beta t + \gamma y_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^k \delta_i \Delta y_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t $$

and the test statistic is the t-statistic on γ. The null (γ = 0) corresponds to a unit root. The non-standard distribution of the test statistic under the null requires special critical values (the MacKinnon tables). The KPSS test is the converse, with stationarity as the null.

Spurious regression

Granger and Newbold's 1974 simulation showed that regressing one random walk on another typically gives a high R2, a highly significant t-statistic, and a residual with severe serial correlation, even when the two series are by construction unrelated. This is the spurious regression problem and is why first-differencing (or testing for cointegration) is essential before regressing one non-stationary series on another.

Cointegration and error correction

Two I(1) series can have a linear combination that is I(0). They are then cointegrated. Intuitively, they wander individually but stay anchored to each other in the long run. The classic example: short-term and long-term interest rates are individually I(1) but the spread between them is I(0).

The Engle-Granger two-step procedure tests for cointegration: (i) run OLS in levels, (ii) test the residuals for stationarity. If stationary, the series are cointegrated and the regression coefficient is consistent (despite the spurious-regression worry; cointegrated regressions are an exception). The Johansen procedure generalises to multiple series and multiple cointegrating relationships.

The Granger representation theorem says that any cointegrated system can be written as an error correction model:

$$ \Delta y_t = \alpha (y_{t-1} - \beta x_{t-1}) + \gamma \Delta x_t + \varepsilon_t $$

where the first term is the deviation from long-run equilibrium and α < 0 is the speed of adjustment. The model handles short-run dynamics and long-run equilibrium simultaneously. The Bank of England's macroeconomic models are riddled with such error correction equations.

Vector autoregressions

A VAR(p) generalises AR(p) to multiple variables:

$$ Y_t = c + A_1 Y_{t-1} + \ldots + A_p Y_{t-p} + \varepsilon_t $$

where Yt is a vector and the Ai are coefficient matrices. The reduced-form VAR can be estimated equation-by-equation by OLS. To recover structural impulse responses (the effect of an identified shock), you need additional assumptions:

  • Cholesky decomposition: orders the variables and assumes that variables earlier in the order are not contemporaneously affected by variables later. The classic Sims (1980) approach. Sensitive to ordering.
  • Sign restrictions: restrict the impulse responses to have economically sensible signs. More flexible; identifies a set, not a point.
  • External instruments (proxy SVAR): use an external instrument as a proxy for the structural shock of interest. Avoids ordering, requires a credible instrument.

VARs are the standard tool for impulse-response analysis in macro: trace out the dynamic effect of a one-unit monetary policy shock, a fiscal shock, an oil-price shock, etc. The Smets-Wouters DSGE models, used in many central banks for forecasting, are VAR-like in their reduced-form behaviour.

ARCH / GARCH and volatility

Many financial series have non-constant variance: volatile periods cluster together. Engle's 1982 ARCH(q) model captures this:

$$ \sigma_t^2 = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 \varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \ldots + \alpha_q \varepsilon_{t-q}^2 $$

Bollerslev's GARCH(p, q) extension adds lagged variances. GARCH(1, 1) is the workhorse and fits most equity- and FX-return series adequately. ARCH and GARCH are the time-series tools used in VaR and ES estimation (market-risk capital, Lecture 22 of the banking F107 course).

Forecasting

Once a model is estimated, the conditional forecast h periods ahead is computed by iterating the model forward. Forecast uncertainty grows with the horizon; for a stationary AR(1), the h-step variance converges to the unconditional variance. For a unit-root process, it grows linearly with h.

Out-of-sample evaluation uses metrics like RMSE, MAE, and Diebold-Mariano tests for comparing competing models. The Bank of England, IMF, and major banks routinely produce fan charts (forecast distributions visualised as widening bands) that convey both the central forecast and the uncertainty.

Applications in banking

  • IFRS 9 forward-looking macro scenarios: a base case and one to several upside / downside paths are typically generated from a VAR or factor model, with the lifetime ECL weighted across them.
  • Stress testing: scenarios are designed to be plausibly severe under historical time-series volatility, then propagated through the bank's risk models.
  • IRRBB: yield-curve dynamics are modelled with multifactor short-rate models (Vasicek, CIR, Hull-White), each of which is a continuous-time analogue of an ARMA process.
