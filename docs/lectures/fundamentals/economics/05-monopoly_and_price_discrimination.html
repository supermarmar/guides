Monopoly and price discrimination

Now the seller has market power. They face the whole demand curve and pick the price-quantity combination that maximises profit. The resulting equilibrium produces too little, charges too much, and destroys surplus. Once you let the monopolist charge different prices to different buyers, things become more efficient but more uncomfortable.

Lecture 5 of 25 Reading time, 35 minutes Part I: microeconomics
Step 1

The 30-second version

A monopolist is the only seller in a market. Unlike the competitive firm, it does not take price as given; it picks the price (or equivalently the quantity, since the demand curve maps one to the other). Its profit-maximising rule is marginal revenue equals marginal cost, with marginal revenue strictly less than price because raising output lowers price on all units sold. The result: the monopolist sells less and charges more than the competitive outcome, creating deadweight loss.

The Lerner index compactly captures the markup:

$$ \frac{P - MC}{P} = \frac{1}{|\varepsilon^d|} $$

So markup is large when demand is inelastic and small when demand is elastic. Price discrimination (charging different prices to different buyers or for different units) recovers some or all of the lost surplus, but typically at the cost of giving consumers a worse deal than they would get in competition.

Feynman test A monopolist deciding how much to produce faces a trade-off: every extra unit sold brings in revenue, but to sell it they have to lower the price (which means lower revenue on every previous unit too). They stop expanding output the moment the cost of the last unit equals the net revenue from it (counting the loss on all previous units). The competitive firm does not have to think about previous units because it has no effect on price, so it produces more.
What's beyond the syllabus Principles courses cover single-price monopoly and stop. This lecture also covers the three classical price-discrimination regimes (first, second, third degree), two-part tariffs, and natural-monopoly regulation. The last is the analytical foundation for utility regulation (water, electricity, telecoms), which is the most economically significant application of these ideas in practice.
Step 2

Why this matters

Most real markets are neither perfectly competitive nor pure monopoly, but the two extremes anchor the spectrum. Monopoly is the cleanest place to learn the trade-off between market power and efficiency, and the analytical apparatus translates directly to oligopoly (Lecture 6), monopolistic competition, and the regulation of network industries (Lecture 22). For banking, monopoly analysis underpins competition policy in retail banking, payment networks, and credit-rating agencies, all of which are markets with two-handful counts of participants.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture the same demand curve as in Lecture 1. The monopolist owns the supply side outright. For any quantity Q it picks, the demand curve tells it the maximum price it can charge. As Q rises, price falls. Total revenue is P(Q) · Q. Marginal revenue is the derivative; because raising Q lowers P on inframarginal units, MR is below P.

Step 4

The detail

The monopolist's problem

Pick Q to maximise profit:

$$ \pi(Q) = P(Q) \cdot Q - C(Q) $$

First-order condition: MR(Q) = MC(Q), where

$$ MR(Q) = P(Q) + Q \cdot P'(Q) $$

The second term is negative (P'(Q) < 0), so MR < P. Rearrange and substitute the elasticity definition:

$$ MR = P \left( 1 + \frac{1}{\varepsilon^d} \right) $$

where εd is the (negative) own-price demand elasticity at the optimum. Setting MR = MC gives the Lerner index:

$$ \frac{P - MC}{P} = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon^d} = \frac{1}{|\varepsilon^d|} $$

The markup is the reciprocal of the absolute elasticity. When demand is very elastic, the monopolist has little market power even though it is the only seller (consumers walk away easily). When demand is inelastic, the markup is large (insulin manufacturers have famously high markups). When |εd| < 1, MR is negative; a monopolist never operates on the inelastic part of demand, because cutting output would raise both price and revenue. This is a useful sanity-check question on exams.

Sources of monopoly

Why does a monopoly arise?

  • Natural monopoly: extreme increasing returns to scale make it cheaper for one firm to serve the whole market than many. Examples: water distribution, the National Grid, railway track. (Note: track is a natural monopoly; trains running on it are not.)
  • Legal monopoly: government grants exclusive rights. Patents (time-limited), pharmaceutical regulation, banking licences, taxi medallions.
  • Resource monopoly: control of a unique input. De Beers and diamonds, OPEC and oil (partially), some rare earths.
  • Network effects: the value of the product to a user rises with the number of other users. Operating systems, social networks, payment networks.
  • Strategic entry deterrence: incumbent commits to behaviour that makes entry unprofitable. Returns in Lecture 6.

Welfare costs of monopoly

Compared to the competitive benchmark in Lecture 4:

  • Deadweight loss: the triangle of trades that would have happened at the competitive price but do not at the monopoly price.
  • Surplus transfer: a rectangle of consumer surplus that becomes producer surplus (monopoly profit). This is a redistribution, not a loss, but it has its own equity consequences.
  • Productive inefficiency (X-inefficiency): without competitive pressure, the monopolist may not minimise costs. Empirically real, hard to model cleanly.
  • Rent-seeking: real resources spent acquiring or protecting the monopoly (lobbying, litigation). The triangle is the visible loss; the rectangle of expected monopoly rent can also be partially dissipated, in which case the social cost approaches the entire profit, not just the deadweight loss.

Price discrimination, first degree

If the monopolist could charge each buyer their exact willingness to pay, they would extract all the surplus. Output expands all the way to the competitive level (because the relevant marginal revenue on the last unit is the buyer's WTP, not the average of all buyers' WTPs). Output is efficient; consumer surplus is zero; producer surplus equals the whole triangle from Lecture 1. This is first-degree or perfect price discrimination. It is rarely achievable in practice because it requires the monopolist to identify each buyer's WTP, and because of arbitrage (buyers who pay low prices reselling to those who would pay high prices).

Second degree: non-linear pricing

The monopolist offers a menu of price-quantity bundles and lets buyers self-select. Examples: bulk discounts ("first 10 units at 1 pound each, next 100 units at 50 pence each"), versioning of software (free / standard / premium), two-part tariffs (entry fee plus per-unit price, e.g. mobile-phone contracts, theme-park admission, golf clubs).

The two-part tariff is a classic exam favourite. A monopolist with marginal cost c facing a single buyer with demand curve q(p) can set price p = c (efficient!) and capture the whole consumer surplus as a fixed access fee. Output is efficient; surplus is fully appropriated by the monopolist. With heterogeneous buyers, the optimal two-part tariff trades off the size of the fee against the loss of low-WTP buyers, and yields the kind of "package deal" pricing common in business-to-business contracting.

Third degree: market segmentation

The monopolist splits the market into observable groups (students, retirees, weekdays vs weekends, peak vs off-peak, country A vs country B) and charges each its own optimal monopoly price. Each price obeys the same Lerner formula, with the relevant elasticity being the elasticity of that segment. Higher prices to less elastic segments, lower prices to more elastic ones. Hardback vs paperback, business-class vs economy-class, weekend matinees vs Friday-evening shows.

Welfare consequences are ambiguous. Compared to uniform monopoly pricing, third-degree price discrimination can raise or lower total surplus depending on whether output rises or falls. There is, however, a clean result: price discrimination is welfare-improving relative to single-price monopoly only if it leads to higher total output.

Natural monopoly and regulation

When average cost is declining at the competitive output (the IRS case from Lecture 3), marginal cost lies below average cost. Forcing P = MC produces efficient output but a loss for the firm (P < AC). The textbook responses are:

  • Marginal-cost pricing with subsidy: government covers the loss from tax revenue. Theoretically clean, politically and informationally hard.
  • Average-cost pricing: regulator sets P = AC, firm breaks even, output is below the efficient level but better than monopoly. Common in utility regulation.
  • Two-part tariffs: fixed connection charge plus marginal-cost pricing. The British water sector uses this structure.
  • Price-cap regulation (RPI − X): the regulator caps the firm's revenue growth at inflation minus a productivity factor, encouraging cost reduction. Introduced for BT in 1984 and now near-universal in UK utility regulation.
Step 5

Synthesis and what's next

Monopoly is the cleanest place to learn how market power destroys surplus, and price discrimination is the cleanest place to see why "extracting surplus" and "creating surplus" are not the same thing. Lecture 6 generalises to the realistic case where there are a few sellers, each strategically aware of the others, and demands game theory to handle.
