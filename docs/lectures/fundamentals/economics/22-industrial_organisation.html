Industrial organisation

How firms compete, when competition fails, and what regulators should do about it. The field combines Lecture 5 (monopoly), Lecture 6 (oligopoly), and Lecture 8 (information) into a coherent framework for thinking about market structure, mergers, predatory pricing, two-sided markets, and platform regulation. The applied home of competition policy.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Industrial organisation (IO) studies how firms set prices, choose product variety, invest in R&D, and interact strategically with rivals and entrants. The unifying framework is the structure-conduct-performance triangle: structure (number of firms, barriers to entry) shapes conduct (pricing, advertising, collusion), which shapes performance (profits, welfare, innovation). Modern IO replaces this descriptive frame with structural empirical models of demand and supply that quantify market power and counterfactual policy effects.

The applied home of IO is competition policy: anti-trust rules against cartels and abuse of dominance, merger control, regulation of natural monopolies, and (recently) regulation of digital platforms. The UK's Competition and Markets Authority and the European Commission's DG Competition are the institutional vehicles.

Feynman test Imagine four firms that supply 80% of a market. They could compete fiercely (Bertrand pricing close to cost), accept the implicit equilibrium of Cournot quantities, or quietly coordinate on a near-monopoly outcome. Each is a Nash equilibrium under slightly different assumptions. Competition policy is the institutional design problem of shifting outcomes from the third toward the first. Most of the toolkit (cartel investigations, merger reviews, dominance abuse cases) is about identifying and reversing harmful coordination.
What's beyond the syllabus Standard treatments cover SCP and stop. This lecture covers the modern structural empirical IO methodology (the Berry-Levinsohn-Pakes demand estimation), the economics of two-sided platforms (Tirole's Nobel), and the post-2018 wave of "big tech" regulation (the EU's Digital Markets Act, the UK's Digital Markets Unit). It also addresses the lively debate about whether the recent rise in industry concentration in the US is a story about competition policy or about technology.
Step 2

Why this matters

Competition policy is one of the most consequential and least-glamorous areas of economic regulation. The CMA's decisions on the Microsoft-Activision merger (cleared), the Sainsbury's-Asda merger (blocked), and the Apple App Store investigation each move billions in surplus between consumers, firms, and shareholders. Industrial organisation is also the analytical home for the regulation of network industries (telecoms, energy, transport) and the regulation of two-sided platforms (online marketplaces, payment networks, app stores). For banking, IO underpins competition policy on retail banking, payment-card interchange fees, and credit-rating agencies.

Step 3

The mental model

Three lenses, applied serially.

  1. Structure: how many firms, how concentrated, how easy to enter or exit?
  2. Conduct: do firms price-take, oligopolistically interact, collude, predate, foreclose, or dominate via vertical integration?
  3. Performance: what are the resulting prices, profits, deadweight loss, innovation rates, and consumer welfare?

Competition policy interrogates each lens: structural remedies (block mergers, force divestitures), conduct remedies (cartel fines, MFN-clause bans, behavioural commitments), or performance regulation (price caps, access-pricing rules).

Step 4

The detail

Market structure measurement

The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) is the sum of squared market shares (in percentage points). HHI = 10,000 in a monopoly; HHI = 100 in a market of 100 equal-share firms. Competition authorities use HHI thresholds for screening mergers: in the UK, an increase in HHI of more than 150 points to a level above 1,000 is the rule-of-thumb trigger for closer scrutiny. The threshold is conservative because, in differentiated-product markets, HHI is a noisy measure of effective competition.

Market definition is the practical hard problem. The SSNIP test (small but significant non-transitory increase in price) asks whether a hypothetical monopolist of a proposed market could profitably raise prices by 5-10%. If consumers would switch to outside goods, the market is too narrow. The test is operationally fraught (requires demand elasticities) and has been criticised for circularity, but it remains the standard tool.

Pricing and product strategies

Beyond Lecture 5's price discrimination, modern firms use a wider toolkit.

  • Bundling and tying: selling several products as a package. Welfare effects ambiguous; can be efficient (reduced search costs) or anti-competitive (extending market power from one product to another).
  • Most-favoured-nation (MFN) clauses: requiring a seller not to offer better terms elsewhere. Can soften competition, common in hotel-booking platforms and credit-card networks.
  • Loyalty rebates: discounts conditional on share-of-purchase exceeding a threshold. Can foreclose entrants who cannot match the rebate at small scale.
  • Predatory pricing: pricing below cost to drive out rivals, then raising prices once they exit. Hard to prove in practice (the Brooke Group test requires both below-cost pricing and a plausible "recoupment" story).

Entry, exit, and contestable markets

The contestable markets theory (Baumol-Panzar-Willig 1982): even a monopolist can be disciplined by potential entrants if entry and exit are costless. The threat of "hit-and-run" entry forces incumbents to price near cost. In practice, sunk costs (advertising, R&D, regulatory licences, brand) make most markets non-contestable, and incumbents extract some rents.

Entry deterrence strategies include excess capacity (commit to flooding the market if entry occurs), brand proliferation (fill every product-space niche), exclusive contracts with distributors, and patent thickets. The economics literature is sceptical of most of these as private welfare-enhancing strategies (they often work but waste real resources).

Mergers

Mergers can be horizontal (same industry), vertical (supplier-buyer), or conglomerate (unrelated). The welfare trade-off in horizontal mergers is between unilateral effects (the merged entity gains market power, raises prices) and efficiencies (cost reduction, scale economies, innovation). UK and EU merger review applies a substantial lessening of competition (SLC) test: a merger is blocked if it would substantially lessen competition, even without proof of imminent harm.

Vertical mergers' main concern is foreclosure: the merged entity refuses to deal with a downstream rival, raising its costs. The economics is ambiguous; some vertical integrations are efficient (eliminate double marginalisation), others are anti-competitive. The 2022 Microsoft-Activision review, in which the CMA initially blocked the merger over cloud-gaming concerns, was a high-profile recent case.

Cartels and collusion

Cartel agreements are illegal under competition law and attract heavy fines (in the UK and EU, up to 10% of global turnover). The economic problem from Lecture 6 returns: cartels are sustained by trigger strategies and threatened punishments, but always face the incentive of any individual member to cheat. Modern competition authorities run leniency programmes that grant immunity to the first cartel member to confess, exploiting the prisoner's-dilemma structure to make agreements unstable.

Detecting cartels is a structural-econometric exercise: look for parallel pricing inconsistent with cost movements, look for screening tests (variance ratios, "structural break" analysis after suspected cartel collapses). Banking cartels (the LIBOR rigging, FX-rate fixing) have been prosecuted using these techniques in the 2010s.

Regulation of natural monopoly

From Lecture 5, the textbook responses are MC pricing with subsidy, AC pricing, two-part tariffs, and price-cap (RPI − X) regulation. UK utility regulation since the 1980s has relied predominantly on RPI − X (Stephen Littlechild's invention for BT in 1984), now generalised to water, energy networks, rail track, and airports. The regulator periodically resets the X factor based on benchmarking and productivity assumptions; the regulated firm captures cost reductions exceeding X during the price-control period.

Recent debate has focused on whether RPI − X has under-rewarded long-term investment (the financial structure of regulated utilities); on whether the price-control mechanism distorts investment decisions toward visible projects (Bonbright's "gold plating"); and on whether outcome-based regulation (focused on quality of service) would be superior.

Two-sided markets and platforms

Jean Tirole's 2014 Nobel was partly for the economics of two-sided platforms: markets in which a platform serves two distinct customer groups (advertisers and users; merchants and cardholders; app developers and consumers) and the value to each side depends on the participation of the other (cross-side network effects).

Pricing in two-sided markets often involves charging one side heavily and the other lightly (or even paying them to participate). The price ratio between sides reflects the relative network externality each generates. Standard Lerner-index intuition fails because pricing decisions on one side affect the value of the other.

The competition-policy implications: traditional concentration metrics undercount competition between platforms, MFN-style clauses are pervasive and ambiguous, and self-preferencing (a platform favouring its own services in search results) is hard to characterise. The EU's Digital Markets Act (2022) and the UK's Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act (2024) introduced ex-ante regulatory regimes that target dominant platforms ("gatekeepers" / "strategic market status" firms) with specific behavioural rules, supplementing case-by-case competition enforcement.

Innovation and competition

The Schumpeter-Arrow debate: does monopoly accelerate or retard innovation? Schumpeter argued monopolists are best placed to bear the cost of R&D and capture the rewards. Arrow argued competitive pressure incentivises innovation more strongly. Empirically, the relationship is inverted-U: very little innovation in pure monopoly (no competitive pressure) and pure competition (no rents), most in moderate-competition intermediate settings (Aghion-Bloom-Blundell-Howitt). Patent policy threads the needle: provide rents to motivate innovation, but only for a limited period.

The recent concentration debate

US industry concentration has risen since 1990. Whether this is a problem is contested. The "rising market power" view (De Loecker-Eeckhout, Furman) argues markups have risen, labour share has fallen, and competition policy under-enforced for two decades. The "superstar firms" view (Autor-Dorn-Katz-Patterson-Van Reenen) argues that technology has shifted production toward firms with high productivity, which look concentrated but actually compete fiercely on the productivity margin. The empirical resolution is ongoing.
