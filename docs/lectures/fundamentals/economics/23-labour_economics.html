Lectures / Economics

Labour economics

Wages, unemployment, and the labour market. The classical model has labour demand from firms (the marginal product) and labour supply from workers (the leisure-consumption trade-off). The modern model adds search frictions (jobs and workers cannot find each other instantly), human capital (workers invest in their own productivity), and monopsony (employers have wage-setting power). The combination explains most of what we observe.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The basic labour market has employers demanding labour at the marginal product of labour and workers supplying labour at the marginal disutility of work. The market clears at the equilibrium wage and equilibrium employment. Layers added by modern labour economics: human capital (workers invest in skills that raise their productivity), search frictions (it takes time to match workers with jobs, producing equilibrium unemployment), compensating differentials (worse jobs pay more), and monopsony (large local employers can set wages below the competitive level).

The most-discussed applied debate is the minimum wage. Classical theory predicts that a minimum wage above market-clearing reduces employment. The modern monopsony / search literature (Card-Krueger 1994; Cengiz-Dube-Lindner-Zipperer 2019) finds modest minimum-wage increases have small or no employment effects, suggesting labour markets have non-negligible employer market power. This has fed into the steady increase in the UK National Living Wage to above 60% of median wages.

Feynman test Wages are prices for hours of work. Like other prices, they are set by supply and demand. Unlike other prices, the goods being traded (labour) cannot be separated from the supplier (the worker), so the market is shot through with frictions: people care about who they work for, where the job is, what hours it requires, whether their colleagues are nice. The modern labour economics field treats these frictions as central, not as deviations from a clean baseline.
What's beyond the syllabus Principles courses cover labour demand and supply and the minimum-wage controversy. This lecture also covers the Mortensen-Pissarides search model, Becker's human-capital theory, the Card-Krueger and subsequent minimum-wage evidence, and the recent monopsony literature (Manning, Naidu) that has changed the policy consensus on labour-market regulation.
Step 2

Why this matters

Labour markets account for around 60% of GDP through wages. Every measurable improvement in welfare in industrial societies has run through changes in employment levels, wages, hours, or working conditions. Macro stabilisation policy (Lecture 11) ultimately operates through the labour market, since employment is the most directly affected macro variable.

For credit risk, labour-market dynamics drive household income, the most important variable for retail PD. Unemployment shocks transmit to mortgage defaults; wage stagnation drives credit-card and personal-loan delinquency rates. The IFRS 9 forward-looking macro scenarios almost always centre on a labour-market path.

Step 3

The mental model

Three actors: workers, firms, and the labour market itself (which can be more or less frictional). Workers choose how much to work and how much to invest in their own skills. Firms hire workers up to the marginal product. The market clears at a wage, but the clearing is imperfect because jobs and workers are matched one-by-one with search costs on both sides.

Step 4

The detail

Labour supply

A worker has utility U(C, L) over consumption C and leisure L, with total time endowment T = N + L (work plus leisure). The budget constraint is C = wN + V, where w is the wage and V is non-labour income. Maximising U subject to the budget gives the canonical first-order condition:

$$ \text{MRS}_{L,C} = \frac{U_L}{U_C} = w $$

The marginal rate of substitution between leisure and consumption equals the wage. The Slutsky decomposition of Lecture 2 applies: a wage rise increases the opportunity cost of leisure (substitution effect, work more) and raises income (income effect, work less). The two can offset, producing a backward-bending labour-supply curve at high wages, which empirically shows up in some samples but not others.

The elasticity of labour supply is empirically small for prime-age men (around 0.1) and substantially larger for married women and the elderly. This is why most tax-policy welfare calculations use heterogeneous labour-supply elasticities by demographic group.

Labour demand

Firms set the marginal product of labour equal to the real wage: MPL = w/P. From Lecture 3, labour demand slopes down. Cross-price effects: a fall in the price of capital reduces labour demand for substitutes and raises it for complements; the empirical mix depends on the industry.

The aggregate elasticity of labour demand (the percentage fall in employment from a one-percent wage rise) is the central parameter for assessing minimum wages, payroll taxes, and labour-market reforms. Hamermesh's textbook estimate is around 0.3 to 0.5; recent estimates from monopsony-aware studies are smaller.

Human capital

Gary Becker (1964 Nobel) modelled workers' education and training as investment. The decision rule: invest in human capital if the present value of higher future earnings exceeds the present value of the costs (foregone earnings while studying plus direct costs).

The empirical "Mincer equation" relates earnings to schooling and experience:

$$ \ln w = \alpha + \beta \cdot S + \gamma_1 X + \gamma_2 X^2 + \varepsilon $$

where S is years of schooling and X is years of experience. Estimated β is around 0.08-0.10 in OECD economies: an extra year of schooling raises earnings by 8-10%. The identification challenge from Lecture 16 returns: ability is correlated with both schooling and earnings, biasing OLS upward. The IV strategies of Lecture 17 (compulsory schooling laws, quarter of birth, distance to college) find smaller but still positive returns (5-7%).

The big policy debate is whether the schooling premium reflects causal productivity gains or signalling (Lecture 8). The evidence is mixed; both effects are likely present. For policy, the implication: education subsidies are easier to justify if returns are productive, and harder if they are signalling.

Search and matching

The classical model assumes the labour market clears instantly. In reality, vacancies and unemployed workers coexist (the Beveridge curve: vacancies on one axis, unemployment on the other; the curve slopes down, with the position drifting over time).

The Mortensen-Pissarides search model (2010 Nobel) takes search frictions seriously. Workers search for jobs; firms post vacancies; the meeting technology is a "matching function" m(u, v) of unemployment and vacancies, often Cobb-Douglas. The equilibrium has:

  • A free-entry condition for firms (post a vacancy until the expected value equals zero).
  • A Bellman equation for workers (search until the expected return from waiting equals the wage).
  • A wage-bargaining rule (Nash bargaining splitting the match surplus between worker and firm).

The equilibrium unemployment rate is positive: workers and firms cannot find each other instantly. Policies that shift the match efficiency (job-search assistance), the workers' bargaining power (unemployment benefits, unions), or the cost of vacancies (payroll taxes) shift the equilibrium unemployment level.

Minimum wages

The textbook prediction (perfect competition): a minimum wage above the equilibrium causes unemployment. Workers willing to work at the new wage exceed the firms' demand at that wage. The minimum-wage literature spent decades testing this and largely failing to find the predicted disemployment effect at the levels of minimum wages observed in practice.

Card and Krueger's 1994 DiD study of New Jersey vs Pennsylvania fast-food employment found no negative employment effect of a New Jersey minimum-wage increase. Subsequent literature (Dube, Lester, Reich; Cengiz et al.) has found small or zero employment effects of moderate minimum-wage increases in the US and UK, with substantial spillover into wage compression for low-paid workers.

The leading explanation is monopsony: firms have some wage-setting power because of search frictions or limited outside options for workers. Under monopsony, the firm's MFC (marginal factor cost) exceeds the wage; a minimum wage that does not exceed the MFC actually raises employment, by closing the gap between wages and marginal product. The empirical implication: minimum-wage effects on employment are mild within a range, beyond which they become negative. The UK National Living Wage at 60-66% of median wage is widely thought to be within the safe range; pushing it materially higher is the open empirical question.

Unions and collective bargaining

Unions raise wages of covered workers by 10-20% on average (the union wage premium) and compress wage distributions. The effect on aggregate employment is ambiguous: unions reduce employment in unionised sectors but also raise wages for non-union workers nearby through "threat" effects. UK unionisation has fallen from 70% in the late 1970s to around 23% in the 2020s, accounting for a meaningful share of the rise in wage inequality.

Discrimination

Becker's taste-based discrimination: employers, customers, or co-workers have preferences against certain groups, leading to wage gaps. In competitive markets, taste-based discrimination should erode (non-discriminating firms hire the cheaper workers and outcompete). Empirically, wage gaps persist across decades.

The alternative is statistical discrimination: in the presence of asymmetric information, employers infer productivity from observable characteristics including demographics, leading to wage gaps even with no taste preference. Statistical discrimination can be self-reinforcing: if employers expect lower productivity from a group, members invest less in skills, confirming the original belief.

Identifying the cause of gaps is empirically hard. Audit studies (fictitious-CV experiments) consistently find call-back rates favouring white-sounding names and male-sounding names, even after controlling for stated qualifications. Sales-data studies and field experiments increasingly identify causal discrimination at hiring, promotion, and pay-setting margins.

The aggregate Phillips curve and wage-setting

Lecture 11's Phillips curve has a wage-setting flavour: tight labour markets put upward pressure on wages, which feeds into prices. The post-2009 "Phillips curve flattening" debate is whether the relationship has weakened (perhaps because expectations are well anchored, or because globalisation and technology have changed firms' wage-setting). The 2021-23 inflation surge revived the relationship: tight labour markets and rising wages preceded and accompanied the inflation episode.
