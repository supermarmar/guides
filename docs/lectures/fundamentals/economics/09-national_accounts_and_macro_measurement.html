National accounts and macro measurement

Macroeconomics starts with the question, "what is happening to the economy?" Answering it requires accounting conventions: how output, income, and expenditure relate, how to separate quantity from price, how unemployment is defined, and how the external position is recorded. This lecture is the dictionary every later macro lecture relies on.

Lecture 9 of 25 Reading time, 35 minutes Part II: macroeconomics
Step 1

The 30-second version

Gross domestic product (GDP) is the market value of final goods and services produced inside a country in a given period. It can be measured three ways that all give the same answer: output (sum of value added across firms), income (wages plus profits plus rents plus taxes net of subsidies), and expenditure (consumption plus investment plus government spending plus net exports). These are the three faces of the same coin and the three-way identity is the central organising fact of national accounts.

To make GDP comparable over time, we strip out price changes: real GDP values output at base-year prices. Inflation is the change in a price index (typically CPI for consumer prices, GDP deflator for the whole economy). Unemployment follows the ILO definition: people without work, available for work, actively seeking work. The balance of payments records all transactions between residents and the rest of the world; its sub-accounts (current, capital, financial) must sum to zero by construction.

Feynman test Every pound spent is a pound someone earned. Every pound earned was paid for some output. So total spending, total income, and total output are three different ways of counting the same number. Macroeconomics builds on top of this three-way identity. The rest is bookkeeping conventions, but the conventions matter when you compare countries, decades, or policy regimes.
What's beyond the syllabus Most principles courses present GDP and stop. This lecture explicitly addresses (i) what GDP misses (home production, environmental damage, distribution), (ii) the difference between nominal and real measures and why it matters in cross-country comparisons (PPP), and (iii) the balance-of-payments identity, which sets up the open-economy macroeconomics in Lecture 12.
Step 2

Why this matters

Macro policy debates, "is the economy growing too fast?", "is inflation entrenched?", "is unemployment too high?", "is the deficit sustainable?", are all conducted in the language of the national accounts. Misreading a definitional point (real vs nominal, gross vs net, GDP vs GNI, headline vs core inflation) is the single most common source of bad commentary. This lecture is the vocabulary you need to read the Bank of England Monetary Policy Report, the ONS GDP release, or a budget speech without being fooled.

Step 3

The mental model

The economy is a circular flow. Households sell labour and capital to firms; firms pay wages and rents; firms produce goods and sell them to households, who buy them with their wages. Money goes round one way; goods and services go round the other. Inject government spending, taxes, savings, investment, exports and imports into the loop, and you have the closed system the national accounts record.

Step 4

The detail

GDP: three approaches

GDP can be computed three ways, each yielding the same number.

  1. Output (or value-added) approach: sum the value added by each firm (revenue minus the value of intermediate inputs). This avoids double-counting; only final goods are valued.
  2. Income approach: sum factor incomes (wages, profits, interest, rents, mixed income) plus taxes on production net of subsidies.
  3. Expenditure approach: sum the components of final demand.

The expenditure identity is the workhorse for macro models:

$$ Y = C + I + G + (X - M) $$

where C is private consumption, I is gross fixed-capital formation plus inventory accumulation, G is government consumption and investment, X is exports, M is imports. The bracket (X − M) is net exports, also called the trade balance. The IS-LM model in Lecture 11 is a manipulation of this identity.

Gross, net, domestic, national

  • Gross includes depreciation; net excludes it.
  • Domestic measures output produced within the country regardless of who owns the factors; national measures output accruing to residents regardless of where it was produced. GNI = GDP + net primary income from abroad.
  • At market prices includes indirect taxes net of subsidies; at basic prices excludes them. Eurostat publishes both.

The UK's headline figure is GDP at market prices, real, chain-linked to a fixed reference year.

Real vs nominal

Nominal GDP is the value of output at current prices. Real GDP values output at base-year (or chain-linked) prices, so the change in real GDP reflects quantity changes only. The GDP deflator is the implicit price index:

$$ \text{deflator}_t = \frac{Y_t^{\text{nominal}}}{Y_t^{\text{real}}} \times 100 $$

For consumer-price comparisons, the relevant index is CPI (a Laspeyres-style fixed-basket index, updated periodically) or CPIH (CPI including owner-occupier housing costs, the ONS's preferred headline measure). The CPI / CPIH measure is what the Bank of England targets at 2%.

Useful identities. Approximate decomposition:

$$ \%\Delta Y^{\text{nominal}} \approx \%\Delta Y^{\text{real}} + \%\Delta \text{deflator} $$

Inflation

Inflation is the rate of change of a price index. Three flavours:

  • CPI inflation: percentage change in CPI year-on-year. The Bank of England target.
  • Core inflation: CPI excluding food and energy (volatile components). A cleaner read on the persistent trend.
  • Wage inflation: percentage change in average weekly earnings. Lagged indicator of demand pressure.

The Fisher equation links nominal and real interest rates:

$$ i \approx r + \pi^e $$

where i is the nominal rate, r the real rate, and πe expected inflation. The same equation rewritten gives the famous result that, in the long run, central banks control nominal rates but not real rates (which are determined by saving and investment).

Unemployment

The ILO unemployment rate is the share of the labour force that is out of work, available for work, and actively seeking work in the last four weeks. The labour force is the sum of the employed and the unemployed. The employment rate is the share of the working-age population (16-64 in the UK) in employment. The participation rate is the share of the working-age population in the labour force.

Three types of unemployment by cause:

  • Frictional: short-term, transitional between jobs. Always present.
  • Structural: mismatch of skills and locations to job vacancies. Slow to clear.
  • Cyclical: deficient aggregate demand. Rises in recessions.

The NAIRU (non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment) or natural rate is the sum of frictional and structural; cyclical is the gap. Lecture 11 returns to this.

Balance of payments

The BoP records transactions between residents and non-residents. It has three accounts that, by construction, sum to zero (errors and omissions aside):

  • Current account: trade balance (X − M) plus primary income (investment income, employee compensation) plus secondary income (transfers).
  • Capital account: capital transfers and acquisition / disposal of non-produced non-financial assets.
  • Financial account: net acquisition of financial assets minus net incurrence of liabilities. The big numbers live here (direct investment, portfolio investment, reserves).

Identity: Current + Capital + Financial = 0. A country running a current-account deficit (importing more than exporting) is, by accounting necessity, a net seller of assets to foreigners (financial account surplus). The UK has run a current-account deficit continuously since the early 1980s.

What GDP misses

GDP is a measure of marketed production. It excludes:

  • Home production: unpaid childcare, housework, voluntary work.
  • Black market activity: estimated and added in some accounts, but imperfectly.
  • Environmental degradation: deforestation and pollution count their commercial product positively but their externality negatively only if costs are paid (rarely).
  • Distribution: GDP per capita can rise even if the median is falling.
  • Well-being: leisure, health, security, social cohesion.

Adjusted measures (Net Domestic Product less environmental depletion, the OECD Better Life Index, the Stiglitz-Sen-Fitoussi proposals) try to address these, but none has displaced GDP for high-frequency macro management.

International comparison and PPP

Comparing GDP per capita across countries at market exchange rates understates the true purchasing power of low-income economies, because non-traded goods (haircuts, public transport, housing) are systematically cheaper there. Purchasing power parity (PPP) exchange rates equalise the cost of a representative consumption basket and so give a more realistic measure of real living standards. The IMF publishes both.
