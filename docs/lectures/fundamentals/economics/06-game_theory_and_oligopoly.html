Game theory and oligopoly

When there are a few sellers and each one's decision affects the others, the price-taking and the pure-monopoly tools both fail. We need a framework for strategic interaction, which is game theory. The pay-off is a small toolkit (Nash equilibrium, best-response functions, subgame perfection) that captures everything from a cartel decision to the macro of monetary policy credibility.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A game is a list of players, the actions each can take, and a pay-off for each player as a function of everyone's actions. A Nash equilibrium is a profile of actions such that no player has a unilateral incentive to deviate. The most useful applications in microeconomics are the Cournot model (firms choose quantities), the Bertrand model (firms choose prices), and the Stackelberg model (one firm moves first, the others react).

The Cournot quantity is higher than monopoly and lower than competition; the Bertrand price, with homogeneous goods, collapses to marginal cost (zero profit even with two firms). The two models give wildly different answers and the difference matters: it is why anti-trust policy looks so different across industries.

Feynman test Each player picks their action assuming everyone else is doing the same. Nash equilibrium is the state where these assumptions are mutually consistent. If you and I are both choosing what to charge, and my best response to your price is to charge ten pounds, and your best response to my charging ten pounds is to charge ten pounds, then (10, 10) is a Nash equilibrium. The concept is simply: rationality, mutual knowledge of rationality, and self-fulfilling beliefs.
What's beyond the syllabus A British BSc principles course introduces Nash equilibrium and Cournot. This lecture goes one step further into Bertrand (because it changes the policy conclusion completely) and into sequential games, where the relevant solution concept is subgame perfection. It also briefly previews repeated games and the folk theorem, because cartel sustainability is the deep economic question behind virtually every competition-policy case.
Step 2

Why this matters

Most real industries have somewhere between two and twenty meaningful competitors. Oligopoly, not monopoly or perfect competition, is the typical market structure for cars, supermarkets, airlines, mobile networks, retail banks, payment card schemes, and the auditing industry. Without game theory you cannot model these markets at all, and the welfare consequences (which can be close to competitive or close to monopoly depending on details) cannot be signed.

Game theory is also the language in which the rest of economics formalises strategic interactions: auctions, contract design, monetary policy credibility (central bank vs private sector), international trade negotiations, voting, bargaining. It is the single most-exported tool in modern microeconomics.

Step 3

The mental model

Two firms producing the same good. Each chooses a strategy. Their joint choice determines a market outcome and a pay-off (profit) for each. You write the situation down as a pay-off matrix (for simultaneous games) or as a game tree (for sequential games), and solve it for equilibrium.

The single most useful exercise is the prisoner's dilemma. Two cartel members each choose between "cooperate" (restrict output) and "defect" (cheat and produce more). Each prefers to defect if the other cooperates, but joint cooperation beats joint defection. The unique Nash equilibrium is mutual defection, which is also the worst joint outcome. This single 2x2 game captures why cartels are hard to sustain, why collective action problems exist, why arms races happen, and why public-goods provision is fragile.

Step 4

The detail

Strategic form games

A game in strategic (normal) form is a triple (N, (Si), (ui)): a set of players i ∈ N, a set of strategies Si for each, and a pay-off function ui: Π Sj → ℝ. A strategy profile is a choice of one strategy per player. A profile s* is a Nash equilibrium if no player can profitably deviate unilaterally:

$$ u_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) \geq u_i(s_i, s_{-i}^*) \quad \text{for all } i \text{ and all } s_i \in S_i $$

where s-i denotes "everyone except player i". A dominant strategy for i is one that is best regardless of what others do. A dominant-strategy equilibrium is a profile where each player plays a dominant strategy; it is automatically a Nash equilibrium.

Nash equilibria need not exist in pure strategies, but they always exist in mixed strategies (randomisations over pure strategies) for finite games, by Nash's existence theorem.

The prisoner's dilemma

B cooperatesB defects
A cooperates(3, 3)(0, 5)
A defects(5, 0)(1, 1)

For each player, "defect" is dominant. Mutual defection (1, 1) is the unique Nash equilibrium, even though both players would prefer mutual cooperation (3, 3). This is the formal core of why a cartel that everyone privately wants to abandon is also a cartel that everyone publicly says they support.

Cournot duopoly

Two firms, each with marginal cost c, simultaneously pick quantities q1 and q2. Market price is P(Q) where Q = q1 + q2. Firm 1 maximises π1 = (P(Q) − c) q1, taking q2 as given. First-order condition gives the best-response function:

$$ q_1^*(q_2) = \arg\max_{q_1} (P(q_1 + q_2) - c) q_1 $$

For a linear demand P = a − bQ, the best response is q1*(q2) = (a − c) / (2b) − q2/2. The Nash equilibrium solves both firms' best responses simultaneously, giving

$$ q_1^N = q_2^N = \frac{a - c}{3b} \qquad Q^N = \frac{2(a-c)}{3b} \qquad P^N = \frac{a + 2c}{3} $$

Compare to monopoly (Qm = (a−c)/(2b)) and perfect competition (Qc = (a−c)/b). The Cournot duopoly produces between the two. With n Cournot firms, the equilibrium price approaches marginal cost as n → ∞, recovering perfect competition in the limit. This is one of the most elegant results in oligopoly theory: competition gradually replaces monopoly as you add firms.

Bertrand duopoly

Same setup but firms now choose prices simultaneously, and the lower-priced firm captures the entire market (or they split if equal). Each firm's best response is to undercut the rival by an arbitrarily small amount, as long as price exceeds marginal cost. The unique Nash equilibrium has both firms charging P = c and earning zero profit. Two firms suffice for the competitive outcome, a striking result known as the Bertrand paradox.

The paradox dissolves once you add (a) capacity constraints, (b) product differentiation, or (c) repeated interaction. With product differentiation, Bertrand competition gives intermediate margins (the canonical "Hotelling line" or "logit demand" models). With capacity constraints, equilibrium is in mixed strategies (Edgeworth cycles). With repetition, tacit collusion can be sustained (next subsection).

Stackelberg leadership

Now make the game sequential: firm 1 picks q1 first, observed by firm 2, who then picks q2. The natural solution concept is subgame perfect equilibrium: at every decision node, the player chooses optimally given the continuation game.

Solve by backward induction. Firm 2 best-responds to q1: q2*(q1) = (a−c)/(2b) − q1/2. Firm 1 anticipates this and chooses q1 to maximise its profit knowing how firm 2 will respond. The leader produces more, the follower less; the leader earns more than under Cournot and the follower earns less. This is the first-mover advantage in quantity competition.

Commitment matters here: firm 1's advantage hinges on firm 2 believing q1 is fixed before they act. If firm 1 could revise its quantity after seeing q2, the threat dissolves and we return to Cournot. Strategic commitment is valuable, often counterintuitively: tying your own hands can leave you better off.

Repeated games and the folk theorem

If the prisoner's dilemma is played once, mutual defection is the equilibrium. If played infinitely many times with sufficient patience (discount factor δ close to 1), any individually-rational outcome can be sustained as a subgame perfect equilibrium, including mutual cooperation. This is the (Nash) folk theorem.

The mechanism is a trigger strategy: cooperate as long as the rival cooperated last period; defect forever if they ever defect. The future loss from triggering the punishment, if large enough, deters today's deviation. The smallest patience that sustains cooperation is

$$ \delta \geq \frac{\pi_{\text{def}} - \pi_{\text{coop}}}{\pi_{\text{def}} - \pi_{\text{punish}}} $$

This is the analytical core of why cartels can sometimes survive but often collapse: the moment any party doubts that the others can sustain the punishment, the discounted gain from cheating exceeds the discounted loss, and the cartel unravels. It is also why competition authorities prioritise leniency programmes: by altering the pay-offs of the underlying repeated game, they make cartel agreements harder to sustain.

Sequential games and subgame perfection

Many real interactions are sequential. The solution concept of choice is subgame perfect Nash equilibrium (SPNE): a strategy profile that prescribes a Nash equilibrium in every subgame, including those that would not be reached in equilibrium. SPNE rules out non-credible threats. If a player threatens to do X off-equilibrium but X would not be in their interest if the threat had to be carried out, the threat is incredible and is excluded from the equilibrium.

The classic application is strategic entry deterrence. Incumbent monopolist threatens a price war if a new firm enters. In a one-shot Nash, the threat is empty (after entry, accommodation is best for the incumbent). With a commitment device (irreversible capacity investment, fighting brand, long-term contract), the threat becomes credible and entry is deterred. This is why building "excess capacity" can be profitable: it changes the post-entry game in a way that keeps the entrant out.

Beyond the basics

Game theory extends in three directions that the rest of this course will draw on:

  • Games of incomplete information: players have private information (e.g. costs). The solution concept is Bayesian Nash equilibrium. Foundational for auction theory and contract design.
  • Bargaining: the Nash bargaining solution and the Rubinstein alternating-offers game. Foundational for labour-economics search models (Lecture 23).
  • Mechanism design: how to set up rules so that self-interested agents reveal private information and act in socially desirable ways. Foundational for optimal auctions, voting systems, and public-goods provision (Lecture 19).
