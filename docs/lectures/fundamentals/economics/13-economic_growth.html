Economic growth

The classical model of Lecture 10 was static: Y* sat at one level forever. Real economies grow. Solow's model explains why economies converge to a steady-state level of capital per worker, why developing countries grow faster than rich ones (conditional convergence), and why only technological progress can deliver permanent growth in living standards. Endogenous growth theory then asks where technological progress comes from.

Lecture 13 of 25 Reading time, 40 minutes Part II: macroeconomics
Step 1

The 30-second version

The Solow model has three ingredients: a production function Y = F(K, AL) with constant returns to scale; a saving rate s that determines how much output is reinvested; and a depreciation rate δ. Capital accumulates per the equation K′ = sY − δK. In equilibrium, the capital-to-effective-labour ratio settles at a steady state where the new investment exactly replaces depreciated capital, and output per effective worker is constant. Once you build in exogenous labour-augmenting technical progress at rate g, output per worker grows at g indefinitely.

The model's headline predictions are: (i) the saving rate determines the level of output per worker, not its long-run growth rate; (ii) technology drives the long-run growth of per-capita income; (iii) poor countries converge to rich ones conditional on having similar parameters. Endogenous growth theory then asks where technical progress comes from, attributing it to R&D, education, and institutions.

Feynman test Each year, a fraction s of output is invested in new capital, and a fraction δ of the existing capital wears out. If new investment exceeds wear-and-tear, the capital stock grows; if less, it shrinks. In between, it sits at a steady state. At the steady state, per-worker capital is fixed and so is per-worker output. To get more per-worker output, you need a higher saving rate (a higher steady-state level) or new technology (a higher steady-state path).
What's beyond the syllabus Standard treatments cover Solow and stop. This lecture also covers endogenous growth (Romer's R&D, Aghion-Howitt's creative destruction) and the institutional view (Acemoglu, North): why some countries are rich and others poor when the "fundamentals" do not differ by enough to explain it. The institutional answer is the modern consensus in development economics (Lecture 24).
Step 2

Why this matters

Growth is the single biggest determinant of human welfare over decades. A country growing at 2% per capita doubles incomes in 35 years; at 4%, in 18. The 20th century's biggest economic story is the East Asian growth miracle and its converse, sub-Saharan Africa's stagnation. Without a growth model you cannot have an informed opinion on either.

For banking, growth assumptions sit inside every long-horizon credit-risk model: through-the-cycle PDs, IFRS 9 forward-looking macroeconomic scenarios, climate-transition pathways. The implicit growth model determines how the macro variables feed into expected credit losses.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture an economy whose only state variable is capital per effective worker, k = K / (AL). Investment per effective worker is sf(k), where f(k) = F(K, AL) / (AL). The "break-even" investment (the amount needed to keep k constant as labour grows at rate n, technology at rate g, and capital depreciates at rate δ) is (n + g + δ)k. The steady-state k* is where these two are equal.

Step 4

The detail

The Solow model, formally

Production: Y = F(K, AL) with constant returns to scale, where A is labour-augmenting technology (Harrod-neutral). Per effective worker: y = f(k). The fundamental dynamic equation is

$$ \dot{k} = s f(k) - (n + g + \delta) k $$

where saving rate s, labour-force growth n, technology growth g, and depreciation rate δ are exogenous constants. Steady state: &kdot; = 0, hence

$$ s f(k^*) = (n + g + \delta) k^* $$

The steady-state level of capital per effective worker is increasing in s and decreasing in n + g + δ. Output per effective worker is y* = f(k*), also increasing in s. Output per actual worker Y/L = A · y*, which grows at rate g in the long run.

So the saving rate affects the level of the steady-state path, but not its slope. To grow in per-capita terms indefinitely you need g > 0, which the Solow model treats as exogenous.

The convergence properties

Two countries with the same parameters (s, n, g, δ) will converge to the same per-capita income, regardless of starting point. This is absolute convergence, and it does not hold globally (poor countries are not catching up to rich ones in aggregate). What holds is conditional convergence: countries with similar parameters converge. The OECD looks like absolute convergence within itself. The world looks like a club-convergence pattern: there are convergence clubs that converge internally but diverge from each other.

The Solow model also predicts that growth in y is fast when k is far below k* and slow when close. This explains why South Korea grew at 8% for decades while at low k, then slowed to 3% as it approached k*. It also predicts that very poor countries can grow very fast if they can accumulate capital (which they can, if institutions and access to finance allow).

The golden rule

What saving rate maximises long-run consumption per worker? Differentiate steady-state consumption c* = f(k*) − (n+g+δ)k* with respect to k*, and you get the golden rule condition:

$$ f'(k_{\text{gold}}^*) = n + g + \delta $$

The marginal product of capital should equal the effective depreciation rate. If s is above the golden-rule level, the economy is "dynamically inefficient": it could consume more now and forever by saving less. Modern economies are generally below the golden-rule capital stock, with positive marginal products of capital comfortably above (n+g+δ).

Growth accounting

Taking logs and differentiating the production function gives the growth accounting decomposition:

$$ \frac{\dot{Y}}{Y} = \alpha \frac{\dot{K}}{K} + (1 - \alpha) \frac{\dot{L}}{L} + \frac{\dot{A}}{A} $$

where α is the capital share. Growth in output is the share-weighted sum of growth in capital and labour, plus growth in total factor productivity (TFP), which is the residual. Robert Solow's 1957 paper computed this residual for the US and found that about 80% of growth in per-capita output came from TFP, not from factor accumulation. The "Solow residual" became the catalyst for endogenous growth research: if technology drives growth, what drives technology?

Endogenous growth: AK and R&D models

The simplest AK model assumes constant returns to a broad measure of capital (including human capital). With Y = AK, output per worker grows at sA − (n+δ) indefinitely, with no convergence to a steady state. Saving permanently raises the growth rate, which is a much stronger claim than Solow.

Paul Romer's R&D-based growth models (1990) treat innovations as a deliberate, costly activity by firms. Innovations are non-rival ideas, which means that the stock of knowledge increases over time and contributes to productivity in all sectors. Patent protection and other appropriability institutions are necessary to sustain innovation incentives. Aghion and Howitt's creative destruction model (1992) adds the Schumpeterian element: each new innovation displaces an incumbent, so growth is a series of vertical-quality improvements.

Endogenous growth's central insight is that growth depends on choices: how much to spend on R&D, how much to spend on education, how strong intellectual property rights are, how open the economy is to foreign technology. This is the analytical foundation for industrial policy and the "innovation policy" agenda of modern governments.

Institutions and the deep determinants

Daron Acemoglu, James Robinson, and Douglass North argue that growth's deep determinant is institutions: the rules (property rights, contract enforcement, political constraints) that determine whether productive activities are rewarded or expropriated. Their empirical work (the colonial-origins instrument; the comparison of inclusive vs extractive institutions) suggests that long-run growth differences across countries are primarily institutional, not technological or geographic. This view has shaped post-2000 development policy, including the World Bank's institutional reform agenda. Lecture 24 returns to it.

Demographics and growth

The Solow model treats n as exogenous. In practice, population growth and the age structure are huge growth drivers. The "demographic dividend" (a falling dependency ratio as fertility declines) gave East Asia decades of high growth. The "demographic drag" (an ageing population) is now slowing Japan, Italy, and prospectively China and Germany. The UK's growth potential depends critically on immigration and labour-force participation choices that the Solow model packs into the exogenous n.

Climate and growth

Climate change introduces a fourth state variable: environmental stock. William Nordhaus's DICE model embeds Solow inside a damages-and-emissions structure, with a representative agent choosing carbon taxes and abatement effort optimally. The model produces a "social cost of carbon" that quantifies the externality and informs carbon-pricing policy. Lecture 24 returns to this in a development context; climate-risk modelling in banking (gap area for the user) draws on this literature.

Synthesis and what's next
