Classical macroeconomics: the long run

When prices and wages are flexible, output is determined by supply: the production function, the labour market clearing, and the capital stock. Money is neutral. Saving determines investment via the real interest rate. This is the long-run equilibrium toward which the Keynesian short run eventually returns.

Lecture 10 of 25 Reading time, 35 minutes Part II: macroeconomics
Step 1

The 30-second version

In the classical model, the supply side determines output. Firms hire labour up to where the marginal product equals the real wage; workers supply labour up to where the marginal disutility equals the real wage; the labour market clears and produces full-employment output Y*. The interest rate adjusts to equate desired saving (a function of income and the rate) with desired investment (a function of the rate). The price level is determined by the quantity of money via the quantity theory of money.

Three propositions follow. (i) Classical dichotomy: real variables (Y, employment, the real wage, the real interest rate) are determined by real factors; nominal variables (P, the nominal wage, the nominal interest rate) are determined by monetary factors. (ii) Money is neutral: changing the money supply changes prices proportionally but leaves real variables unchanged. (iii) Say's law: aggregate supply creates its own aggregate demand, so general gluts are impossible in the long run.

Feynman test Imagine an economy that works perfectly, with no sticky prices, no shocks the participants cannot work around, and no unused resources. Output is whatever can be made with the labour and capital available. The interest rate is whatever makes saving equal investment. The price level is set by whatever scales the money supply to the real volume of transactions. Everything has a market and every market clears. This is the long-run benchmark. Keynes did not deny it; he argued that the short run could be away from it for years.
What's beyond the syllabus This lecture is the long-run reference for the rest of macro. It is also the analytical foundation of monetarism, supply-side economics, and (more recently) real-business-cycle theory. Lecture 11 introduces the Keynesian short run; Lecture 13 introduces growth theory which generalises the classical model to a moving target.
Step 2

Why this matters

Every macro debate eventually returns to the question, "what is the long-run equilibrium and is the economy converging to it?" Without a classical anchor, the short-run model floats. The classical model is also the only model in which money is neutral, which is why long-run inflation is always a monetary phenomenon, and why central banks targeting price stability have a defensible job description.

Step 3

The mental model

Four markets, each clearing simultaneously: the labour market, the goods market, the loanable funds market, and the money market. Labour clears at the real wage W/P. Goods clear at the price level. Loanable funds clear at the real interest rate r. Money clears at a price level that scales nominal income to the available money stock. None of the markets directly determines any other market's price, but the values are mutually consistent and the system as a whole determines Y, employment N, W/P, r, P.

Step 4

The detail

The labour market

The aggregate production function Y = F(K, N), where K is fixed in the short run and N is employment, exhibits diminishing returns to labour: FN > 0, FNN < 0. Profit-maximising firms set the marginal product of labour equal to the real wage:

$$ \frac{\partial F}{\partial N} = \frac{W}{P} $$

This is the firm's labour demand. Workers supply labour as an increasing function of the real wage, Ns(W/P). The labour market clears when Nd(W/P) = Ns(W/P), determining equilibrium employment N* and the real wage. Substituting back into the production function yields full-employment output:

$$ Y^* = F(K, N^*) $$

This is the supply-side anchor. The labour market does not care what the price level is, only the ratio W/P; if both nominal wages and the price level double, real labour demand and supply are unchanged. (This is the assumption that breaks down in the Keynesian short run.)

The goods market and loanable funds

In a closed economy without government, the goods market clears when planned investment equals desired saving:

$$ S(Y^*, r) = I(r) $$

Saving is rising in the interest rate (people consume less when saving pays more) and rising in income. Investment is falling in the interest rate (a higher discount rate makes fewer projects pass NPV). The market for loanable funds clears at the real rate r that brings the two together.

With government, add G to demand and T to the saving identity. The classical assumption is Ricardian equivalence: rational households see government borrowing today as future taxation and save more in response, so private saving rises to offset the public dis-saving. The interest rate is unchanged; only government debt and private saving co-move. In practice Ricardian equivalence holds only partially.

The quantity theory of money

The quantity equation, originally due to Irving Fisher, is

$$ M V = P Y $$

where M is the money stock, V is the velocity of circulation (how many times a pound changes hands per period), P is the price level, and Y is real output. If V is stable and Y is at its supply-determined level, then P is proportional to M. Taking logs and differentiating:

$$ \pi \approx g_M - g_Y $$

(assuming ΔV = 0). Inflation equals the excess money-supply growth over real-output growth. This is the most-quoted result in classical macro and the analytical underpinning of monetarist policy advice (most prominently from Milton Friedman): "control M and you control P".

The classical dichotomy and money neutrality

Real variables (Y, N, W/P, r) are determined by the real side: the production function and the labour-market equilibrium for Y and N, the loanable funds market for r, the W/P-clearing labour market for the real wage. Nominal variables (P, W, M·V) are determined by the money side. Once you know real GDP, real wages, and the real interest rate, you know everything real; the price level then scales the rest.

Money neutrality follows immediately: doubling M doubles all nominal prices and wages but leaves all real quantities unchanged. Superneutrality (which adds: changes in the growth rate of M leave real variables unchanged) is a stronger claim that holds in some classical models and fails in others, especially when inflation imposes real costs (shoe-leather, menu costs, distortion of tax bases denominated in nominal terms).

Say's law

"Supply creates its own demand." Each producer, by producing, earns income that finances their own consumption and saving. Saving is channelled to investment by the loanable-funds market. There is no aggregate over-production. Say's law is implicit in the classical model and is the proposition Keynes set out to reject. It can fail (in his view) if saving is hoarded, if interest rates do not adjust, or if expectations of demand turn pessimistic. We return to this in Lecture 11.

Inflation in the classical world

If money grows faster than output, prices rise. There is no real-side cost in the basic classical model (money is neutral), so what is wrong with inflation? Four answers:

  • Shoe-leather costs: people economise on cash holdings as inflation rises, spending real resources on bank trips.
  • Menu costs: firms must revise prices more often.
  • Distortion of nominal tax codes: when tax brackets and capital-gains thresholds are not indexed, inflation creates fiscal drag and double-taxes saving.
  • Redistributive effects: unanticipated inflation transfers wealth from nominal creditors to nominal debtors.

These are real costs, but they are second-order in the basic model. The bigger argument against inflation comes from the Keynesian short run, where inflation expectations become entrenched and disinflation requires costly recessions (Lecture 11, Phillips curve).

The classical labour market and the supply shock

Two useful comparative-statics exercises:

  1. Productivity boom: F shifts up. Labour demand rises, the real wage and equilibrium employment both rise, and Y* rises. The interest rate may rise (more attractive investment) or fall (more saving from richer households), depending on parameters.
  2. Adverse supply shock (oil price): F shifts down. Labour demand falls, real wage falls, employment falls if labour supply is elastic, and Y* falls. Inflation rises if monetary policy accommodates the fall in output. The 1970s stagflation is the canonical real-world example.

Strengths and weaknesses

The classical model gets the long-run right and gets the short run badly wrong. It assumes prices and wages clear instantly, which is true over years but false over quarters. It is the right framework for thinking about average inflation over a decade, the long-run effect of a permanent technology shock, or the long-run effect of fiscal policy on the capital stock. It is the wrong framework for thinking about a recession.

Step 5

Synthesis and what's next

The classical model is the macro long run. Lecture 11 introduces the Keynesian short run, where sticky prices and wages mean the economy can sit below Y* for substantial periods, and aggregate demand temporarily matters. The two views are complements, not rivals: classical for the long run, Keynesian for the short run, with the duration of "short" being an empirical question.
