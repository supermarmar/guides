Information economics

The last assumption of the welfare theorems to relax: full information. When one side of a transaction knows more than the other, markets can collapse (lemons), contracts have to take strange shapes (moral hazard, signalling), and a whole new style of regulation is needed. Most of credit-risk modelling is, secretly, applied information economics.

Lecture 8 of 25 Reading time, 40 minutes Part I: microeconomics
Step 1

The 30-second version

Asymmetric information comes in two flavours. Adverse selection arises when one side knows something about the type of the good or person being traded before the transaction (used-car sellers know whether their car is a lemon; insurance buyers know whether they are high-risk). Moral hazard arises when one side takes a hidden action after the transaction (a worker chooses effort; an insured driver chooses care). Both can prevent welfare-improving trades.

The classical responses are signalling (the informed side credibly conveys information through a costly action, like a degree), screening (the uninformed side offers a menu of contracts that induce types to self-select), and incentive contracts (tie the agent's pay-off to outcomes so that the unobservable action becomes the agent's own private interest). Each is a different mechanism for restoring the missing equality of information.

Feynman test Markets work because the two sides agree on what is being traded. If only one side knows the truth, the other has to guess. A used-car buyer cannot tell if a car is a peach or a lemon, so they bid the average. The lemon sellers happily accept; the peach sellers withdraw. Now the average drops. More peaches leave. Eventually only lemons trade. The market unravels. Adverse selection is the formal name for that unravelling.
What's beyond the syllabus Principles courses usually cover Akerlof's lemons and stop. This lecture walks through all three classical responses (signalling, screening, incentives) and explicitly draws the connection to credit-risk modelling, where the application scorecard is precisely an empirical screening mechanism that exploits observable characteristics to infer unobservable creditworthiness. Step 2

Why this matters

Information failures are why insurance markets exist, why employment contracts pay bonuses, why universities issue degrees, why governments regulate financial markets, why CEO compensation has the form it does, and why banks build credit-scoring systems. Whole industries (insurance, banking, ratings, auditing, regulation) exist primarily to overcome information failures.

For credit-risk modellers, this lecture is the theoretical justification for the entire profession. Probability-of-default modelling is a screening exercise (using observable variables to infer unobservable type); loan covenants are incentive contracts (limiting hidden action); credit ratings are signalling devices (conveying information through a costly process); and the financial-stability framework is explicitly designed to limit the moral hazard that "too big to fail" creates.

Step 3

The mental model

Two parties try to transact. One knows something the other does not. The thing they know might be:

  • The quality of a good (used-car seller knows; buyer does not). This is hidden type, leading to adverse selection.
  • The type of a counterparty (the insured knows their own risk; the insurer does not). Again hidden type.
  • An action they will take after the contract is signed (the insured will or will not drive carefully). This is hidden action, leading to moral hazard.

Hidden type problems arise before the contract; hidden action problems arise after. The fix depends on which problem you face.

Step 4

The detail

Akerlof's lemons

The market for used cars. Sellers know whether their car is a peach (worth 1,000) or a lemon (worth 0). Buyers do not. Fraction q of cars are peaches. If sellers value peaches at vpS < 1000 and value lemons at vlS = 0, and buyers value peaches at 1000 and lemons at 0, the social optimum is for all cars to trade. But at any price P that is below 1000 and above vpS, peach owners pull out (their reservation price exceeds P), only lemons remain, and buyers (knowing this) refuse to pay more than 0. The market collapses entirely. This is adverse selection producing the most extreme possible outcome: a market that should exist, ceases to.

The lemons logic shows up everywhere. Health insurance for the elderly: high-risk types most want it, low-risk types abandon it, premiums spiral. Credit markets: high-risk borrowers most want to borrow at any given rate, the rate that compensates the lender for that risk drives away the low-risk borrowers. The pattern is universal.

Signalling: Spence's education model

Michael Spence proposed a way out. The informed side takes a costly action whose cost depends on type, in a way that only high-type agents find worthwhile. The classic example: education. Suppose high-ability workers find studying easier than low-ability workers do. A firm cannot observe ability but can observe whether the worker has a degree. If high-ability workers are willing to pay the cost of a degree but low-ability workers are not, the degree becomes a credible signal of ability, even if education itself adds nothing to productivity.

Formally, equilibrium requires the cost of the signal to satisfy a single-crossing condition: marginal cost of signal decreases in type. In a separating equilibrium, each type sends a different signal and is correctly identified. In a pooling equilibrium, both types send the same signal and the firm cannot distinguish them. Multiple equilibria typically coexist; refinements (such as Cho and Kreps's "intuitive criterion") pick out the more sensible ones.

The depressing prediction: signalling can be socially wasteful. The cost of the signal is incurred even though the signal itself does not change the worker's productivity. Education spending is partly an investment in human capital and partly a signalling expense, with no obvious way to decompose the two.

Screening: insurance and risk types

The uninformed side can also induce self-selection by offering a menu. Rothschild and Stiglitz studied insurance with two types: low-risk and high-risk. The insurer offers two contracts: full insurance at a high premium, and partial insurance at a low premium. Each type chooses the contract that maximises their own utility. In equilibrium, low-risk types take the partial contract; high-risk types take the full contract; each type pays a premium tailored to their own risk class. The two-contract menu serves as a screening device.

The Rothschild-Stiglitz analysis has the unsettling property that separating equilibrium may not exist. If the proportion of high-risk types is small enough, any candidate separating equilibrium can be broken by a pooling contract that both types prefer. The non-existence of equilibrium is one of the cleanest analytical critiques of unregulated insurance markets and is part of the rationale for mandates (universal coverage, the Affordable Care Act's individual mandate, the UK NHS).

Moral hazard and the principal-agent problem

Now flip to hidden action. A principal hires an agent to perform a task. The agent's effort is unobservable; the outcome is observable but noisy. The principal must design a contract that motivates effort without imposing too much risk on the agent.

Setup: agent picks effort e at private cost c(e). Output is y = e + ε, where ε is noise. The principal observes y and pays the agent w(y). The agent maximises E[w(y)] − c(e); the principal maximises E[y − w(y)] subject to participation (agent prefers the job to the outside option) and incentive compatibility (agent picks the desired effort).

The optimal linear contract is

$$ w(y) = a + b y $$

The slope b balances two forces. High b motivates effort (good) but loads the agent with risk (bad, because risk-averse agents demand a premium). The optimal slope falls when output is noisier (so the signal-to-noise ratio is worse) and rises when the marginal product of effort is higher.

This setup is the analytical core of executive compensation, sharecropping contracts, sales commissions, performance-related pay, malpractice insurance with deductibles, and bonus-malus systems in motor insurance. It is also the rationale for deductibles and co-payments in any insurance contract: full insurance destroys the agent's incentive to take care; partial insurance balances risk-sharing against effort motivation.

Banking applications

The same machinery covers the most important information problems in banking.

  • Adverse selection in credit: high-risk borrowers most want loans at any given rate. Raising the rate to compensate drives out safe borrowers. Result: credit rationing (Stiglitz and Weiss). Banks reach a profit-maximising rate below the market-clearing rate and ration credit at that rate. This is why banks use scorecards rather than just letting the rate clear the market.
  • Moral hazard in lending: once a loan is made, the borrower can take more risk than agreed (asset substitution). Covenants, collateral, and step-up triggers limit this. The whole architecture of bank lending is shaped by hidden-action concerns.
  • Moral hazard from deposit insurance and too-big-to-fail: insured deposits remove depositor discipline; expected bail-outs remove creditor discipline. Result: banks take more risk than they would otherwise. Macroprudential regulation, capital requirements, and resolution regimes (MREL, TLAC) are designed to restore the discipline the implicit guarantee removed.
  • Signalling through ratings and audited accounts: a third party (the rating agency, the auditor) certifies information at a cost, allowing the issuer to convey credibility to the market. The reliability of such certifiers becomes its own concern, as the 2007-08 ratings-agency crisis showed.

Mechanism design (briefly)

The general theory behind signalling, screening, and incentive contracts is mechanism design: given an information environment, how should we set up the rules of the game to elicit truthful behaviour and achieve socially desirable outcomes? The revelation principle says that, without loss of generality, we can focus on direct-revelation mechanisms in which agents simply report their type and the mechanism is designed to make truth-telling a dominant strategy. The Vickrey-Clarke-Groves family achieves this for many problems, including the second-price auction (where bidding your true valuation is dominant) and incentive-compatible public-goods provision.

Mechanism design is the theoretical engine behind modern auction theory (spectrum auctions, electricity markets, online ad markets), matching market design (kidney exchanges, school choice, doctor-hospital matching), and contract theory. It is the modern face of microeconomic theory and won Nobel prizes for Hurwicz, Maskin, Myerson (2007) and Milgrom, Wilson (2020).

Synthesis and what's next
