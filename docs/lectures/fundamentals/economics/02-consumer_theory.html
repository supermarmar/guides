Consumer theory

Where the demand curve comes from. Start with a person, give them preferences over bundles of goods and a budget to spend, ask them to do the best they can, and out drops a function of prices and income that is the individual's demand. Aggregate across people and you have market demand.

Lecture 2 of 25 Reading time, 45 minutes Part I: microeconomics
Step 1

The 30-second version

A consumer ranks bundles of goods according to preferences, which (if they obey four standard axioms) can be represented by a utility function. Facing prices and an income, the consumer picks the affordable bundle that gives the highest utility. The solution to that constrained optimisation problem, as a function of prices and income, is the consumer's Marshallian demand. Summed across all consumers, it is the market demand curve from Lecture 1.

The single most useful result is the Slutsky decomposition: any change in quantity demanded due to a price change can be split into a substitution effect (the consumer rebalances along the same indifference curve) and an income effect (the consumer is now effectively richer or poorer in real terms). The substitution effect is unambiguously negative; the income effect can go either way. That decomposition explains every nuance in the demand curve that Lecture 1 had to take on faith.

Feynman test Imagine a shopping basket and a wallet. The wallet limits what you can put in the basket. Among all the legal baskets, you have favourites, ranked from best to worst. You pick the most-favourite legal basket. Now we secretly drop one price. Two things happen at once: the good that just got cheaper looks more attractive against the others (substitution), and your wallet now buys more of everything (income). Consumer theory is just the bookkeeping that separates those two effects.
What's beyond the syllabus BSc principles courses often stop at indifference curves and budget lines and skip the duality between Marshallian and Hicksian demand. This lecture sets up that duality (expenditure function, indirect utility, Roy's identity, Shephard's lemma) because it pays off in welfare economics (Lecture 4), public economics (Lecture 19), and any time you need to value a price change in pounds rather than in utility units.
Step 2

Why this matters

Consumer theory is the worked-out justification for treating demand as a curve. Without it, you would have no reason to expect demand to slope down, no way to say what a normal vs inferior good is, no way to compute welfare consequences of a price change in pounds, and no defence against the criticism that aggregate behaviour might not look anything like individual behaviour.

It is also the conceptual template for every later optimisation problem in economics: a firm minimising cost, a government maximising welfare, an investor maximising expected utility, a worker choosing labour supply, all use the same Lagrangian machinery. Get fluent here and you get fluent everywhere.

Step 3

The mental model

Two goods, X and Y, on a chart. The straight line is the budget constraint: the set of (X, Y) bundles the consumer can afford given prices and income. The curves are indifference curves: sets of bundles the consumer is equally happy with. The optimum is the affordable bundle on the highest reachable indifference curve, which is where the budget line is tangent to an indifference curve.

Step 4

The detail

Preferences and axioms

A consumer faces a set of bundles x = (x1, ..., xn). Preferences are a binary relation ≻= that says, for any two bundles, whether the first is weakly preferred to the second. To do any economics with this we ask the relation to satisfy four axioms.

  • Completeness: any two bundles can be compared.
  • Transitivity: if A ≻= B and B ≻= C then A ≻= C. Without this, optima may not exist (the consumer goes round in cycles).
  • Continuity: if a sequence of bundles is weakly preferred to a fixed bundle, the limit is too. This excludes lexicographic preferences and makes utility representable.
  • Monotonicity (non-satiation): more is better. Strictly, if x ≥ y and x &neq; y then x ≻ y.

An optional fifth axiom is convexity: averages are preferred to extremes. Convexity yields convex indifference curves, which is what makes the tangency condition a maximum rather than a minimum.

The first three axioms guarantee a utility representation: there exists a continuous function U(x) such that x ≻= y iff U(x) ≥ U(y). Crucially, U is ordinal: any monotonic transformation represents the same preferences. So saying "this bundle gives me 7 utils" is meaningless; saying "this bundle is preferred to that one" is not.

The budget constraint

If the consumer has income I and faces prices p = (p1, ..., pn), the affordable set is

$$ B(p, I) = \{ x \in \mathbb{R}^n_+ : p \cdot x \leq I \} $$

With two goods this is a triangle bounded by the axes and the line px X + py Y = I. The intercepts I/px and I/py tell you the maximum quantity of each good if all income were spent on it. The slope, −px/py, is the relative price.

Utility maximisation

The consumer's problem (UMP) is

$$ \max_{x \geq 0} U(x) \quad \text{subject to} \quad p \cdot x \leq I $$

Form the Lagrangian:

$$ \mathcal{L} = U(x) + \lambda \left( I - p \cdot x \right) $$

and the first-order condition for each interior good is

$$ \frac{\partial U}{\partial x_i} = \lambda \, p_i \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad \frac{\partial U / \partial x_i}{\partial U / \partial x_j} = \frac{p_i}{p_j} $$

The ratio on the left is the marginal rate of substitution of good i for good j: the amount of j the consumer is willing to give up for one more unit of i. The condition says: at the optimum, willingness to substitute equals ability to substitute. The Lagrange multiplier λ has a beautiful interpretation: it is the marginal utility of income, the rate at which utility rises if you give the consumer one more pound.

Solving these first-order conditions together with the budget equation yields Marshallian demand:

$$ x_i = x_i^M(p, I), \quad i = 1, \ldots, n $$

This is the demand we drew on the chart in Lecture 1.

Worked example: Cobb-Douglas utility

Let U(X, Y) = Xα Y1−α with 0 < α < 1. The MRS is

$$ MRS = \frac{\alpha}{1 - \alpha} \cdot \frac{Y}{X} = \frac{p_x}{p_y} $$

Solve together with the budget line and you get

$$ X^M = \frac{\alpha I}{p_x} \qquad Y^M = \frac{(1 - \alpha) I}{p_y} $$

Two things to notice. First, the consumer spends a constant share α of income on X regardless of prices, a characteristic feature of Cobb-Douglas. Second, Marshallian demand is homogeneous of degree zero in (p, I): doubling all prices and income leaves quantities unchanged, as it should.

The indirect utility function and Roy's identity

Plug the Marshallian demands back into the utility function and you get the maximum utility achievable at (p, I):

$$ V(p, I) = U\big(x^M(p, I)\big) $$

This is the indirect utility function. It is decreasing in each price and increasing in income. From V we can recover Marshallian demand without solving the UMP again, via Roy's identity:

$$ x_i^M(p, I) = - \frac{\partial V / \partial p_i}{\partial V / \partial I} $$

Why bother? Because in many problems V is easier to write down than the demand functions themselves, and Roy's identity lets you back into the demands when you need them.

Expenditure minimisation and Hicksian demand

The flip side of the UMP is the expenditure minimisation problem (EMP):

$$ \min_{x \geq 0} p \cdot x \quad \text{subject to} \quad U(x) \geq \bar{u} $$

The consumer is told to hit a target utility ū as cheaply as possible. The solution is the Hicksian (or compensated) demand xH(p, ū), and the minimum expenditure is the expenditure function e(p, ū).

By Shephard's lemma,

$$ x_i^H(p, \bar{u}) = \frac{\partial e(p, \bar{u})}{\partial p_i} $$

The duality between the two problems is what makes consumer theory cleanly tractable: V and e are inverses in the utility argument, and Marshallian and Hicksian demand coincide when evaluated at the right utility level.

Slutsky: the most important equation in microeconomics

Now we can decompose a price change. Suppose pi rises. The total change in Marshallian demand for good i can be written as

$$ \frac{\partial x_i^M}{\partial p_j} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial x_i^H}{\partial p_j}}_{\text{substitution effect}} - \underbrace{x_j^M \, \frac{\partial x_i^M}{\partial I}}_{\text{income effect}} $$

This is the Slutsky equation. The substitution effect is the change in demand that would occur if the consumer were compensated in income to remain on the same indifference curve. It is always weakly negative on the own-price diagonal (i = j): if X gets relatively more expensive, you buy less X, full stop. The income effect captures the loss (or gain) in real purchasing power. Its sign depends on whether the good is normal (positive income elasticity) or inferior (negative).

The two effects together explain everything in Lecture 1's demand curve:

  • Normal good: both effects work in the same direction. Demand slopes down, clearly.
  • Inferior good: substitution effect drags demand down, income effect pushes it up (because the consumer is poorer in real terms and consumes more of the inferior good). Demand still slopes down because the substitution effect usually dominates.
  • Giffen good: the income effect is so strong it reverses the substitution effect. Demand slopes up. Empirically rare but logically possible.

Normal, inferior, luxuries, necessities

These familiar labels are precisely defined by income elasticity of demand:

Income elasticity εILabelExample
> 1Luxury (and normal)Cars, designer clothing, restaurant meals
0 to 1Necessity (and normal)Bread, public transport, electricity
< 0InferiorCheap instant noodles, second-hand clothing, bus travel in places with cars

An Engel curve plots quantity demanded against income (holding prices fixed). It slopes up for normal goods, down for inferior. The shape of the Engel curve is empirically interesting and is one of the few aggregate regularities in consumer behaviour that survives across decades and economies.

Aggregating to market demand

The market demand curve from Lecture 1 is the horizontal sum of individual demand curves: at each price, add up the quantity each consumer demands. This sum inherits the law of demand from each individual (so long as the income effects average out, which they typically do). There is, however, a subtlety known as the Sonnenschein-Mantel-Debreu theorem: aggregate demand can have any shape consistent with budget balance, not just the shape of an individual's demand curve. This is the formal reason representative-agent macro models are an approximation, never a theorem.

Welfare measures of a price change

How much, in pounds, has a consumer gained when a price falls? Three answers:

  • Change in consumer surplus (the area under Marshallian demand): easy to compute, an approximation.
  • Compensating variation (CV): the income reduction that would leave the consumer as well off as before the price fall. CV = e(p1, u0) − I.
  • Equivalent variation (EV): the income increase that would have given the consumer the new utility at the old prices. EV = I − e(p0, u1).

CV and EV bracket the Marshallian consumer surplus change, and coincide exactly when there are no income effects (quasi-linear utility). They are the right welfare measures to use for cost-benefit analysis (returns in Lecture 19).

Revealed preference, briefly

An alternative, less assumption-laden approach is revealed preference: if you saw the consumer buy bundle A when they could have afforded B, you have "revealed" that they prefer A to B. The Weak Axiom of Revealed Preference (WARP) says they should not then turn around and buy B when they could have afforded A. WARP plus a richer Strong Axiom (SARP) is, remarkably, equivalent to the consumer behaving as if maximising a well-behaved utility function. The result is due to Paul Samuelson and is one of the cleanest "as-if" arguments in economics: we do not need consumers to actually have utility functions in their heads, only to behave as if they did.

Step 5

Synthesis and what's next

You can now derive the demand curve from primitives. The recipe is: write down utility, write down the budget, maximise one subject to the other, read off quantities as functions of prices and income. Slutsky decomposition explains why demand normally slopes down; CV and EV are the right pound-denominated welfare measures. Lecture 3 does the symmetric job for sellers, deriving the supply curve from a firm's cost minimisation. Lecture 4 then puts the two together in a competitive market and proves the welfare theorems that Lecture 1 promised.
