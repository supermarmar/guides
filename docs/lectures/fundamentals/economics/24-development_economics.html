Development economics

Why are some countries rich and others poor? The field combines growth theory (Lecture 13) with institutional economics, microeconomic-level evidence on poverty alleviation, and the political economy of development. The recent decades have shifted from grand theories of underdevelopment to randomised-evidence-based assessment of specific interventions.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Cross-country income differences are vast: GDP per capita in the richest countries is around 100 times that in the poorest. Solow's growth model (Lecture 13) gets us part of the way: differences in capital stocks and saving rates account for some. Differences in productivity (the Solow residual) account for most. The modern consensus attributes most of the productivity gap to institutions: the rules, formal and informal, that determine whether productive activity is rewarded or expropriated.

At the microeconomic level, the post-2000 "credibility revolution" has reshaped development economics through randomised controlled trials (RCTs) of specific interventions: conditional cash transfers, microcredit, deworming, school inputs, fertiliser subsidies. Banerjee, Duflo and Kremer shared the 2019 Nobel for this approach. The field has produced a long list of "what works" findings, alongside ongoing debate about external validity and the role of big-picture institutional reform.

Feynman test A country is poor when productive activity does not pay off. The reasons can be physical (no resources, no infrastructure), institutional (property rights are weak, contracts are not enforced), demographic (the workforce is shrinking or unhealthy), or historical (the trajectory was set centuries ago by colonialism or accident). The modern field treats institutions as primary because they explain the most variance and because the other factors are partly downstream of them.
What's beyond the syllabus Standard treatments cover Solow and stop, or wander into "import substitution vs export-led growth" debates that the field has moved past. This lecture covers the modern toolkit: institutions (Acemoglu-Robinson), poverty traps (Banerjee-Duflo), the RCT revolution, and the political-economy frameworks that have replaced classical development theory.
Step 2

Why this matters

The single biggest economic story of the 21st century is the rise of low- and middle-income countries: China's lift of 800 million people out of poverty, India's growth acceleration, sub-Saharan Africa's mixed pattern of high-growth and lagging economies, Latin America's middle-income-trap struggles. None of this makes sense in the closed framework of Lectures 10-13 alone.

For credit risk in emerging markets, the political-economy frame is essential. Sovereign risk premia, currency crisis probabilities, and banking-system fragility track institutional quality much more closely than they track aggregate income.

Step 3

The mental model

Three lenses, applied serially.

  1. Proximate causes: capital, labour, productivity. Solow.
  2. Deep determinants: institutions, culture, geography, history. The "fundamentals" behind the proximate causes.
  3. Micro-level interventions: what specific programmes change household-level welfare. RCT-driven.

The three lenses are complementary, not competitive: macro frameworks set the policy questions; institutional frameworks identify what reforms might shift the proximate causes; micro evidence tells you what specific interventions actually work.

Step 4

The detail

The cross-country income distribution

The ratio of GDP per capita PPP between the richest 10% and poorest 10% of countries is around 30:1. Within the rich club, dispersion is much smaller (the US is around 1.5 times the UK). Within the poor club, dispersion is also smaller. Movement between clubs is rare but happens (East Asian tigers; more recently, parts of Eastern Europe). The pattern of "club convergence" supports the conditional-convergence prediction from Solow but also requires explanations for why some countries' "conditional" steady-state is so much lower than others'.

Institutions as the deep determinant

Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson (Why Nations Fail, 2012) distinguish inclusive from extractive institutions. Inclusive institutions secure property rights, enforce contracts, provide a level playing field, and allow creative destruction. Extractive institutions concentrate political power, allow expropriation, and protect incumbents at the expense of competition.

Their empirical strategy uses colonial settler mortality as an instrument for current institutions. Where Europeans could settle (low mortality), they built inclusive institutions; where they could not (high mortality), they built extractive ones. Settler mortality is plausibly excluded from current GDP except through the institutional channel. The IV estimates show institutions explain a large fraction of cross-country income differences, controlling for other factors.

The deep claim is that institutions are persistent: extractive arrangements set up centuries ago shape current outcomes through path dependence. The Glorious Revolution of 1688 (which constrained the British monarchy) and the Black Death (which reduced labour-supply elasticity in Western Europe) are cited as historical pivots that put some countries on inclusive paths.

Other deep determinants

Competing explanations exist:

  • Geography (Jared Diamond, Jeffrey Sachs): tropical disease burden, soil fertility, distance from trade routes, access to navigable rivers. Some of this empirically matters, but most cross-country variation is not explained by geography directly.
  • Culture (Max Weber, more recently Greif, Tabellini): trust, generalised morality, individualism vs collectivism. Culturally similar regions can diverge institutionally (East and West Germany), suggesting institutions are partly distinct from culture.
  • Religion (the Protestant Ethic hypothesis): early empirical support, contested in modern work.
  • Demography: fertility transitions, age structure, female labour participation. Important especially for the rapid-growth phases of East Asia.

The honest position: institutions, culture, geography, and history are entangled. Disentangling them empirically is the hardest identification problem in economics.

Poverty traps

Why do poor people stay poor? Three families of explanations.

  1. Liquidity constraints: profitable investments exist but the poor cannot afford the upfront cost. Microcredit was designed for this; results have been mixed (positive but small impacts in most RCTs; not the transformative effect originally hoped).
  2. Health-nutrition-productivity loops: low income produces poor nutrition, which lowers productivity, which holds down income. Deworming and supplementation interventions can break this; Kremer-Miguel's deworming work is the seminal RCT.
  3. Information failures: returns to schooling, fertiliser use, or banking are higher than the poor believe; correcting beliefs raises adoption. Information-only interventions can be highly cost-effective.

The RCT revolution

Banerjee, Duflo, and Kremer's 2019 Nobel was for systematically applying RCTs to development questions. The approach: identify a programme, randomly assign treatment, measure outcomes years later. Cumulative findings:

  • Cash transfers: unconditional cash transfers in low-income contexts raise consumption, food security, and modest investment. GiveDirectly's RCTs are influential.
  • Conditional cash transfers: Mexico's Progresa-Oportunidades (later Prospera) raised school attendance and child health.
  • Microcredit: modest impacts on household business outcomes, no transformative effect on poverty in the typical RCT.
  • Deworming: large long-term gains for childhood deworming, though contested in some replications.
  • Mosquito nets: highly cost-effective at the right price; subsidised distribution dominates user fees.
  • School inputs: textbooks alone do little; teacher-pupil ratios matter; pedagogy reforms can deliver large gains; tracking by ability raises both ends.

The critique: RCTs measure local effects of specific interventions and tell you little about the systemic reforms (institutional, trade, macroeconomic) that the big-picture growth literature emphasises. The honest synthesis: micro RCTs improve specific programmes; big-picture growth requires macro and institutional changes that RCTs cannot test.

Trade, growth, and the structural-transformation pattern

The development arc usually involves a shift from agriculture-dominated employment to manufacturing, then to services. China's economic miracle followed this template. East Asian tigers (South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, Hong Kong) used export-led growth to fund rapid industrialisation. India's growth, by contrast, has been services-led, with much smaller manufacturing employment, raising questions about whether the services-led path is replicable elsewhere.

The role of trade in development is contested. The Washington Consensus (Williamson 1989) prescribed openness, privatisation, and macroeconomic stability. Critics argue the East Asian success stories used industrial policy and selective protection, not pure free trade. The empirical literature finds positive trade-growth correlations but identifying the causal channel is hard (the gravity-equation IV studies of Frankel-Romer are influential).

Aid effectiveness

Easterly and Sachs's debate framed the post-2000 aid discussion. Easterly: foreign aid has consistently failed to produce growth; small experiments work but big top-down plans do not. Sachs: targeted health and infrastructure aid can break poverty traps. The cumulative evidence supports a middle ground: well-designed health and direct-transfer programmes work; general budget support's effects are highly conditional on institutional quality. The Millennium Challenge Corporation and Gavi (vaccines) embody the lessons.

Climate and development

Low-income countries are most exposed to climate damages despite contributing least to emissions. Adaptation finance is the main policy lever, with developed-country pledges around 100 billion USD annually but slow disbursement. The "loss and damage" mechanism agreed at COP27 (2022) is a still-evolving instrument. From an analytical perspective, the externality structure (Lecture 7) is inherently cross-border and inherently intergenerational, which makes it the hardest case for the standard policy toolkit.

Behavioural development

Banerjee-Duflo's Poor Economics (2011) is full of behavioural-economics applications: present-bias affecting savings (commitment savings products like SafeSave); status anxiety affecting investment in own children's education; the salience of immediate vs. long-term rewards in health-prevention decisions. The behavioural-economics frame from Lecture 21 has been particularly fruitful in development contexts where the standard rational-actor model fits worst.
