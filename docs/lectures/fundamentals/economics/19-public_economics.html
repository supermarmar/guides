Public economics

The economics of government. What should the state do, how should it raise revenue, and what are the efficiency and equity trade-offs of different tax instruments? The field combines micro tools (demand, supply, surplus) with macro tools (fiscal policy, debt sustainability) and asks how to design tax-and-transfer systems that achieve policy goals at minimum welfare cost.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The textbook role of government is to correct market failures (externalities, public goods, monopoly), redistribute income, and stabilise the macroeconomy. To finance these activities, governments levy taxes that inevitably distort behaviour. Optimal tax theory derives the rules that minimise the welfare cost of raising a given amount of revenue: the Ramsey rule for commodity taxation says tax inelastic goods more; Mirrlees shows that the optimal income tax balances redistribution and labour-supply distortion.

The fiscal multiplier from Lecture 11 quantifies the short-run macro impact of government spending. Debt sustainability requires the primary balance to be consistent with the path of debt and the interest-growth differential. Tax incidence (from Lecture 1) tells you that who pays the tax has nothing to do with who Parliament writes the cheque to.

Feynman test Taxes change behaviour. People work less when wages are taxed, consume less when goods are taxed, save less when returns are taxed. The behavioural change is the deadweight loss of taxation. The best tax is one that raises lots of revenue but causes little behavioural change, which means a tax on something inelastic. The other constraint is fairness: taxing inelastic things often means taxing the poor more heavily (food, fuel), which makes the optimal-tax problem a balance of efficiency and equity.
What's beyond the syllabus Most BSc public-finance texts walk through the same tax instruments. This lecture combines the Ramsey-Mirrlees efficiency frontier with the Atkinson-Stiglitz separability result, plus a UK-specific discussion of the recent income-tax / national-insurance / VAT mix and the political economy of capital taxation.
Step 2

Why this matters

Tax-and-benefit policy is the largest single arm of any government, accounting for around 38% of UK GDP in spending and similar in revenue. Every Budget speech, every Autumn Statement, every welfare-reform debate is a public-economics application. The field is also the analytical foundation for the cost-benefit analysis that justifies (or rejects) every public-spending decision.

For banking, public economics is where you go to understand: (a) why bank profits face surtaxes; (b) the deductibility of debt and the resulting capital-structure distortion (M&M with corporate taxes from the financial-management course); (c) the fiscal implications of bank rescues; (d) the optimal-tax basis for stamp duty on shares, the bank levy, and financial-transaction taxes.

Step 3

The mental model

Two questions. What should the government do? (the welfare rationale: market failures, redistribution, stabilisation). How should it pay for it? (the tax-design problem: minimise the welfare cost of raising revenue, subject to a redistribution target). The two questions interact: more ambitious redistribution requires higher marginal tax rates, which create larger behavioural distortions.

Step 4

The detail

The Musgrave three-branch framework

Richard Musgrave organised public economics around three functions of government.

  • Allocation: correct market failures from Lecture 7 (Pigovian taxes on externalities, provision of public goods).
  • Distribution: redistribute income through progressive taxation and transfers.
  • Stabilisation: smooth the business cycle through fiscal policy (Lecture 11).

The framework is descriptive but useful for orientation. Real-world budgets are messy, mixing the three functions in every line item.

The welfare cost of taxation

A tax raises a wedge between buyer and seller prices, shrinking the quantity traded (Lecture 1). The deadweight loss is approximately

$$ DWL \approx \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{|\varepsilon|}{1 + \varepsilon^s / |\varepsilon^d|} \cdot \tau^2 \cdot R / \tau $$

where τ is the tax rate, R is revenue, and ε is the relevant elasticity. The headline implication is the square root of two intuition: doubling a tax rate quadruples the deadweight loss. This is why broad-base, low-rate taxation generally beats narrow-base, high-rate taxation: spreading the same revenue over a broad base gives a smaller distortion than concentrating it.

The Ramsey rule for commodity taxation

Suppose the government must raise revenue from a set of commodity taxes. To minimise the total deadweight loss, the percentage reduction in the consumption of each taxed good (the "compensated" reduction) should be equal across goods. Equivalently, with constant marginal cost,

$$ \frac{\tau_i}{1 + \tau_i} \approx \frac{k}{|\varepsilon_i^c|} $$

where εci is the compensated own-price elasticity of demand for good i. The inverse-elasticity rule: tax inelastic goods more, tax elastic goods less.

The rule is efficient but distributionally regressive: inelastic goods are often necessities, on which low-income households spend a larger share. The Atkinson-Stiglitz theorem partially resolves the tension: if the income tax is set optimally, commodity taxes that depend on observable consumption add nothing to redistribution and should equal each other. The implication: pursue redistribution through the income tax, leave commodity taxes uniform or close to it. The UK's VAT is broadly uniform at 20%, with notable exceptions (food at zero, fuel at 5%).

The Mirrlees optimal income tax

James Mirrlees (1971 Nobel-winning paper) asked: what is the optimal non-linear income tax schedule, given that ability is unobservable and individuals choose labour supply? The trade-off is redistribution (higher marginal rates redistribute more from high earners to low earners) vs distortion (higher marginal rates discourage work).

The key result: the optimal top marginal rate is

$$ \tau^{\text{top}} = \frac{1}{1 + a \cdot e} $$

where a is the Pareto parameter of the top of the income distribution and e is the elasticity of taxable income with respect to (1 − τ). With a ≈ 2 (typical UK estimate for the top 1%) and e ≈ 0.25 (Saez's preferred estimate), the formula gives an optimal top rate of 67%. Higher elasticity estimates give lower rates. The UK additional rate is 45%; the formula suggests there is fiscal room to raise it, but the empirical magnitudes are highly contested.

The Mirrlees framework also implies that marginal rates should be U-shaped along the income distribution: high at the bottom (because phase-outs of means-tested benefits create high implicit marginal rates), declining in the middle, and rising at the top. The actual UK system is approximately U-shaped, with the trough around median income.

The optimal-tax-mix question

Should we tax capital income? Atkinson-Stiglitz and Chamley-Judt say no, in the long run, in their respective models. The intuition: taxing capital income is taxing future consumption, which compounds the distortion over time. The empirical literature disagrees on whether the Chamley-Judt zero capital-income tax result is robust (Piketty and Saez argue it is not). Most OECD countries tax capital income at lower rates than labour income, with capital gains often taxed even more leniently.

Should we tax wealth? Wealth taxes are rare in practice (only a handful of OECD countries; the UK abolished its inheritance tax above the threshold but kept the structure). The Piketty (2014) argument for a global wealth tax was widely discussed but politically unrealised. The trade-off is the same as Ramsey: wealth is mobile (high elasticity), and large flight responses cap the achievable rate.

Public goods and cost-benefit analysis

For a public-good provision question (build the bridge, fund the vaccine, run the railway), the cost-benefit analysis (CBA) compares the present value of social benefits to the present value of social costs. Benefits include consumer-surplus changes (Lecture 1), externalities (Lecture 7), and use-value plus existence-value for environmental goods. Costs include direct outlays plus the welfare cost of raising the revenue (the marginal cost of public funds, MCPF), typically estimated at 1.2-1.5 in OECD economies.

The UK Treasury's Green Book is the canonical CBA manual. It prescribes a 3.5% real social discount rate (with declining horizon), explicit treatment of distributional weights for projects with strong equity implications, and shadow pricing for externalities (e.g. Â£85 per tonne of CO2 at the time of writing, rising over time).

Fiscal policy and debt

The government's intertemporal budget constraint says that the present value of primary surpluses must equal the current debt:

$$ B_t = \sum_{s=0}^\infty \frac{PS_{t+s}}{(1+r)^{s+1}} $$

where PS is the primary surplus and r is the relevant discount rate. The debt-to-GDP ratio evolves as

$$ \Delta \frac{B}{Y} \approx \frac{r - g}{1+g} \cdot \frac{B}{Y} - \frac{PB}{Y} $$

where PB is the primary balance / GDP and (r − g) is the interest-growth differential. When r < g, debt is sustainable even with persistent primary deficits (Blanchard, 2019); when r > g, persistent surpluses are needed to keep debt stable. The post-2008 environment had r < g for many years; the 2022-23 rate rises moved most advanced economies back to r > g, sharpening the fiscal arithmetic.

Tax avoidance, evasion, and behavioural responses

Behavioural responses to taxation come in three layers (Slemrod's hierarchy):

  1. Real responses: working fewer hours, investing less. These are the deadweight loss in welfare terms.
  2. Avoidance: legal restructuring (incorporation, deferral, jurisdictional shopping). Costly to the tax base but the welfare cost is bounded by the planning expense.
  3. Evasion: illegal under-reporting. Pure tax-base loss, plus the costs of enforcement.

The elasticity of taxable income (ETI) combines all three. Saez-Slemrod-Giertz find ETI in the range 0.1-0.6 depending on the policy environment and the income segment. Lower ETI means higher revenue-maximising tax rates.
