Externalities, public goods, and market failure

Sometimes a market is perfectly competitive in the textbook sense and still produces the wrong outcome. The cause is a gap between the private cost or benefit that drives behaviour and the social cost or benefit that determines welfare. Closing the gap, with Pigovian taxes, Coasian bargaining, or public provision, is the entire purpose of environmental, education, and infrastructure policy.

Lecture 7 of 25 Reading time, 40 minutes Part I: microeconomics
Step 1

The 30-second version

An externality is a cost or benefit borne by a third party not involved in the market transaction. Pollution from a factory imposes costs on its neighbours; a vaccinated person confers benefits on those they would otherwise infect. In each case, private actors making rational decisions face the wrong incentives, because they ignore the part of the cost or benefit that falls on others. The market over-produces goods with negative externalities and under-produces goods with positive externalities.

A public good is a good that is both non-rival (my consumption does not diminish yours) and non-excludable (it is hard to keep non-payers out). Defence, lighthouses, basic research, mosquito eradication, the rule of law: classic examples. Private markets undersupply them because of the free-rider problem. The textbook policy response is public provision, financed from taxation.

Feynman test The price system works because each person's decision affects only themselves. If your decision also imposes a cost on someone else, that cost is invisible to you. You make the right decision for yourself and the wrong one for society. The fix is to make the invisible cost visible: charge a tax equal to it. Once you pay the social cost, your private decision is the social decision.
What's beyond the syllabus Principles courses introduce Pigovian taxes and stop. This lecture also covers the Coase theorem (markets in property rights can solve externalities when transaction costs are zero), Lindahl pricing for public goods, and the open-access "tragedy of the commons", which is the analytical foundation for fisheries policy, congestion pricing, and large parts of environmental regulation.
Step 2

Why this matters

Market failures are the single largest category of justified government intervention. Climate change, pandemics, traffic congestion, antibiotic resistance, financial-sector contagion, biodiversity loss, urban planning: every major policy challenge of the 21st century is, in the formal economist's language, a market failure of some kind. Pre-Pigovian intuition leads to bad answers (ban the bad thing, mandate the good thing). The right answer is almost always to price the externality, then let the market handle the rest.

For banking specifically, the systemic-risk externality is a central organising idea: an individually solvent bank, behaving rationally, can take risks whose social cost (contagion in a crisis) it does not bear, justifying macroprudential regulation. See the economic environment lecture in the banking course.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture a factory producing a good. The supply curve we drew in Lecture 1 reflects the factory's private marginal cost: wages, materials, capital. But each unit of output also produces pollution, which imposes a cost on people downwind. The marginal social cost curve sits above the private marginal cost curve, vertically separated by the marginal external damage.

Step 4

The detail

Externalities, formally

An externality exists when one agent's action enters another agent's utility or production function without being mediated by a market price. Notation: agent A's utility uA(x, y) depends on y, which is chosen by agent B. If A does not compensate B for changing y, the market does not internalise the dependence.

The classification splits by sign and direction:

  • Negative production externality: factory pollution affecting nearby residents.
  • Positive production externality: a beekeeper's hives pollinating a neighbour's orchard.
  • Negative consumption externality: smoking, drinking-driving, loud music.
  • Positive consumption externality: vaccination, education, well-kept gardens.

In every case, the private agent equates marginal private cost with marginal private benefit, but the social optimum equates marginal social cost with marginal social benefit. Where the social and private curves diverge, the market is inefficient.

Pigovian taxes (and subsidies)

Arthur Pigou's solution: impose a per-unit tax equal to the marginal external damage D at the social optimum. After the tax, the firm's effective marginal cost becomes MPC + D = MSC, the private optimum coincides with the social optimum, and the market is efficient. By symmetry, a positive externality should be subsidised at a rate equal to the marginal external benefit (vaccination subsidies, R&D tax credits, electric-vehicle grants).

Pigovian taxation is exquisitely elegant on paper. In practice the regulator needs to know the marginal damage function, which is rarely directly observable. Estimating it (the social cost of carbon, the cost per QALY of pollution-related illness, the congestion cost per car-mile) is one of the central activities of applied environmental and health economics.

Coase, property rights, and bargaining

Ronald Coase pointed out that externalities arise because property rights are missing. If the law clearly assigned the right to clean air (to residents) or the right to pollute (to the factory), and if the affected parties could costlessly negotiate, they would bargain to the efficient outcome regardless of who got the initial right. Only the distribution of surplus would depend on the assignment.

This is the Coase theorem. Two important caveats:

  • Transaction costs: real bargaining is costly, especially with many affected parties. A factory negotiating with one downstream landowner is plausibly Coasian; a coal-fired power station negotiating with the entire global population on the climate externality is not.
  • Wealth effects: in the presence of income effects on willingness to pay or accept, the efficient outcome does depend on who gets the property right.

The Coase theorem is most useful in legal and regulatory design: it tells you that the choice between assigning rights (cap-and-trade) and assigning duties (carbon tax) is often a question of who pays, not of whether the externality gets corrected. Both work in principle. The choice between them is largely political-economy.

Cap-and-trade and emissions markets

Set a binding aggregate quantity (the cap), allocate or auction permits to that total, and let the market price emerge. Firms with low abatement costs sell permits; firms with high abatement costs buy them. The cost of meeting the cap is minimised because abatement happens where it is cheapest, regardless of where the regulator initially placed the permits. This is the Coase theorem put to practical use, and it underpins the EU Emissions Trading System and the carbon-permit market that has functioned since 2005.

The tragedy of the commons

An open-access resource (a common fishing ground, an aquifer, public grazing land, internet bandwidth) is rivalrous (your use diminishes mine) but not excludable (anyone can use it). Each user equates their private marginal benefit to zero (the price of use is zero), ignoring that adding their effort reduces the productivity of others. The market over-exploits the resource, in the limit destroying it entirely. This is Garrett Hardin's tragedy of the commons, formally identical to an unpriced negative externality.

The textbook fix is to assign property rights (privatise the resource), regulate quantity (fishing quotas, water permits), or charge a price (congestion fees). Elinor Ostrom won the 2009 Nobel for documenting that real-world commons often avoid the tragedy through community governance, an institutional middle ground that pure Pigou and pure privatisation both miss.

Public goods

A good is non-rival if my consumption does not reduce the amount available to you (national defence, basic research, GPS signals). It is non-excludable if it is technically or legally infeasible to prevent non-payers from consuming it (lighthouses, mosquito eradication). A public good has both properties; a private good has neither. The full 2x2 matrix:

ExcludableNon-excludable
RivalPrivate good (apple)Common-pool resource (fishery)
Non-rivalClub good (cinema)Pure public good (lighthouse)

Public goods cause the free-rider problem: each potential beneficiary has an incentive to wait for someone else to pay. The market provision falls short. The efficient provision rule, the Samuelson condition, is

$$ \sum_i MRS_i = MRT $$

The sum of all consumers' marginal rates of substitution between the public good and a private good equals the marginal rate of transformation in production. Notice the contrast with private goods: there, each consumer's MRS equals the price ratio individually. For public goods, we add MRSs because everyone consumes the same quantity simultaneously.

Lindahl pricing

Erik Lindahl proposed a mechanism: charge each consumer a personalised price equal to their marginal willingness to pay for the public good. If you value the lighthouse twice as much as I do, you pay twice the per-watt charge. Lindahl equilibrium achieves Samuelson efficiency. Its main practical problem is that consumers have an incentive to misreport their preferences (claim to value the lighthouse less to pay less), which makes Lindahl essentially impossible to implement, except in small communities where preferences are observable. Modern mechanism design tries to design provision rules that elicit truthful reports; the Vickrey-Clarke-Groves family is the cleanest theoretical answer.

Imperfect information as a separate category

Some textbooks lump asymmetric information together with externalities and public goods as "market failures". This lecture treats it as a separate category (Lecture 8) because the analytical tools are different: externalities and public goods are solved by pricing or quantity instruments, but information failures often need contracts, signalling devices, or regulation.

Step 5

Synthesis and what's next

You now have a formal map of why markets sometimes need help. Wherever private cost or benefit diverges from social cost or benefit, the market produces the wrong quantity, and the fix is to close the gap with a price (Pigou), a quantity (cap), or a property right (Coase). Public goods are the limiting case where non-excludability makes private provision collapse, and direct public provision is usually the answer. Lecture 8 closes out the microeconomics part by tackling the last assumption of the welfare theorems: that everyone has the same information.
