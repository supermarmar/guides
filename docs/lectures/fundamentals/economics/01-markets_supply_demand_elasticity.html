Lectures / Economics

Markets, supply, demand, and elasticity

The price system is the single most important social technology economics has ever produced. This lecture builds it from scratch: two curves on a chart, the rule they jointly obey, and the four numbers that say how much each side will move when prices change.

Lecture 1 of 25 Reading time, 40 minutes Part I: microeconomics
Step 1

The 30-second version

A market is any setting in which buyers and sellers exchange a good at a price. Demand says how much buyers want at each price (more cheaply, more wanted). Supply says how much sellers will deliver at each price (more dearly, more delivered). The market clears at the unique price where the two coincide, the equilibrium price, and at that price the quantity bought equals the quantity sold. Everything else in microeconomics, every model, every policy debate, every regulation, is a refinement, an extension, or a deliberate breaking of this picture.

The other half of this lecture is the language of elasticity. Slopes on a chart are easy to read but they depend on the units. Elasticity converts a slope into a percentage response, which is unitless, comparable across goods, and turns out to be what tax authorities, monopolists, and central banks actually care about.

Feynman test A market is a coordination game between strangers. Buyers privately know their willingness to pay; sellers privately know their costs. Neither side can interrogate the other. The price is the single number that lets them coordinate without conversation. When too few sellers show up, the price rises until enough do. When too few buyers want the good, the price falls until enough do. The result is that the good ends up with the buyers who value it most, supplied by the sellers who can produce it most cheaply, without anyone in charge.
What's beyond the syllabus A standard British BSc principles course treats supply and demand as simultaneously given. This lecture follows that pattern but also flags the deeper point that both curves are themselves derivable: demand from consumer theory (Lecture 2), supply from producer theory (Lecture 3). The chart of two curves is therefore a useful starting heuristic, but the curves are not primitive. They are summaries of optimisation problems.
Step 2

Why this matters

Almost every applied debate in economics, from minimum wages to carbon taxes, rents controls to tariffs, comes down to a question about who gets to keep how much of the surplus a market generates, and how much of that surplus disappears when the market is interfered with. To answer those questions you need (a) the two curves, (b) the equilibrium concept that links them, and (c) the elasticity numbers that say how badly each side gets hurt when the price is forced away from clearing.

This lecture is the toolkit. Every later lecture, whether it is on monopoly, taxation, exchange rates, or labour markets, applies it. The lecture is also the foundation for cost-benefit analysis: consumer and producer surplus are the units in which welfare gains are measured, and elasticity is the multiplier that converts a policy change into a surplus change.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture a single market for a single good. The horizontal axis is the quantity bought and sold in a given period; the vertical axis is the price per unit. Two curves live on this chart. The downward-sloping demand curve is the locus of (price, quantity) pairs that buyers would willingly transact at. The upward-sloping supply curve is the same locus for sellers. They cross at one point, which we call the equilibrium.

The economics is in why each curve points the direction it does. Demand slopes down because as the price falls, two things happen at once. Existing buyers buy more (the substitution effect, the good is now relatively cheaper than alternatives) and new buyers enter (the extensive margin, people for whom the good was previously not worth its price now find it is). Supply slopes up because as the price rises, existing sellers find it profitable to produce more (their marginal cost rises with output) and new sellers enter (firms that could not cover costs at the lower price now can). The two motions meet in the middle.

Step 4

The detail

Demand

The law of demand says that, holding everything else constant, the quantity demanded of a good falls as its own price rises. Formally, write quantity demanded as a function of own price and a vector of shifters:

$$ Q^d = D(P; \mathbf{z}) \qquad \text{with} \qquad \frac{\partial D}{\partial P} < 0 $$

The shifters z include income, the prices of substitutes and complements, tastes, expectations about future prices, and the number of buyers. A change in own price moves you along the demand curve; a change in any shifter moves the whole curve. The two are distinct and the language matters: a "change in quantity demanded" is a movement along, a "change in demand" is a shift.

The law of demand has rare exceptions, the most famous being Giffen goods (an inferior good so dominant in a poor household's budget that a price rise pushes consumption towards it). These are textbook curiosities; the empirical record is that virtually every good has a downward-sloping demand curve.

Supply

Symmetric story for sellers:

$$ Q^s = S(P; \mathbf{w}) \qquad \text{with} \qquad \frac{\partial S}{\partial P} > 0 $$

The shifters w include input prices, technology, the number of producers, expectations, and regulation. Again, a change in own price slides you along the curve; a change in a shifter moves the whole curve.

Equilibrium

The market clears at the price P* that solves

$$ D(P^*; \mathbf{z}) = S(P^*; \mathbf{w}) $$

and the resulting quantity is Q* = D(P*) = S(P*). If the price is above P*, sellers want to sell more than buyers want to buy: there is a surplus, inventories pile up, and sellers cut prices. If the price is below P*, buyers want more than sellers will provide: there is a shortage, queues form, and prices are bid up. The equilibrium is the only price at which neither pressure exists.

Disequilibrium is not failure Markets are usually out of equilibrium. The equilibrium price is a long-run attractor, not a description of every Tuesday afternoon. What matters for the model is that there is a force pushing the price towards P* whenever it is away from it. The speed of that adjustment is itself a research question and varies wildly: financial markets clear in seconds; housing markets take years.

Comparative statics

The most useful exercise with this apparatus is asking, "what happens to P* and Q* if a shifter changes?" There are four basic cases. Demand shifts right, demand shifts left, supply shifts right, supply shifts left. The grid below is worth committing to memory: signing the price-and-quantity change correctly under pressure is what first-year economics is testing for.

ShiftEquilibrium price P*Equilibrium quantity Q*Example
Demand right (D ↑)UpUpIncome rises, the good is normal
Demand left (D ↓)DownDownA close substitute becomes cheaper
Supply right (S ↑)DownUpTechnology lowers production cost
Supply left (S ↓)UpDownAn input becomes more expensive

When two shifters move together, the price effect and the quantity effect can go in opposite directions, and you need elasticity to sign the combined outcome. That is one of the reasons we need elasticity at all.

Elasticity, the unit-free version of a slope

The slope of demand has units (pounds per kilogram, say) and so cannot be compared across goods. Elasticity is the slope divided by the ratio of price to quantity at the point of interest, which cancels the units:

$$ \varepsilon^d = \frac{\partial Q^d / Q^d}{\partial P / P} = \frac{\partial Q^d}{\partial P} \cdot \frac{P}{Q^d} $$

Read it as "the percentage change in quantity demanded per one-percent change in price". Because demand slopes down, εd is negative; convention sometimes reports its absolute value. The vocabulary is standard:

  • Elastic demand ( |εd| > 1 ): quantity responds more than proportionally. Luxuries, goods with close substitutes.
  • Inelastic demand ( |εd| < 1 ): quantity responds less than proportionally. Necessities, addictive goods, goods with few substitutes.
  • Unit elastic: |εd| = 1.
  • Perfectly inelastic: |εd| = 0, vertical demand curve. Insulin for a diabetic over a short horizon.
  • Perfectly elastic: |εd| = ∞, horizontal demand curve. The output of a tiny firm in a competitive commodity market.

Supply elasticity is defined identically, with the sign flipped to positive. There are also two cross-elasticities you should be able to name on demand:

$$ \varepsilon^d_{X,Y} = \frac{\partial Q^d_X}{\partial P_Y} \cdot \frac{P_Y}{Q^d_X} \qquad \varepsilon^d_{X,I} = \frac{\partial Q^d_X}{\partial I} \cdot \frac{I}{Q^d_X} $$

The first is the cross-price elasticity of demand for X with respect to the price of Y. Positive means substitutes (a rise in PY raises QX: butter and margarine); negative means complements (cars and petrol). The second is the income elasticity. Positive means a normal good; negative means an inferior good (cheap instant noodles); above one means a luxury (long-haul travel).

What determines elasticity

Four things drive how elastic a demand curve is. Substitutes: the more substitutes a good has, the more elastic the demand (Coca-Cola has more substitutes than "all soft drinks", so Coca-Cola's demand is more elastic). Necessity vs luxury: necessities have fewer substitutes by definition, so they are more inelastic. Time horizon: in the short run, demand and supply are both more inelastic, because adjustment takes time (petrol consumption is highly inelastic to today's price, but very elastic to a five-year forecast). Budget share: goods that take a large fraction of income tend to be more price-sensitive because the household notices the price.

Tax incidence: who pays

Elasticity also answers the most-asked policy question in any intermediate course: when the government puts a tax on a good, who actually pays it? The answer is not "whoever the law makes hand the money over". The answer is "whichever side of the market is more inelastic".

Formally, suppose the government levies a per-unit tax t on sellers. The supply curve shifts up by t at every quantity. The new equilibrium has a higher consumer price Pc and a lower seller-received price Ps = Pc − t. The split of the tax burden is

$$ \frac{\Delta P^c}{t} = \frac{\varepsilon^s}{\varepsilon^s - \varepsilon^d} \qquad \frac{\Delta P^s}{t} = \frac{\varepsilon^d}{\varepsilon^s - \varepsilon^d} $$

(with the elasticities evaluated at the original equilibrium and remembering that εd is negative). The result is a single rule of thumb worth committing to memory: the more inelastic side of the market bears more of the tax. A petrol tax in the short run falls almost entirely on drivers, because petrol demand is short-run inelastic. A tax on land falls entirely on landowners, because the supply of land is perfectly inelastic. The legal incidence (who hands the money to HMRC) is irrelevant to the economic incidence; only elasticities matter.

Worked example: who pays the cigarette tax?

Suppose the demand for cigarettes has elasticity εd = −0.4 (cigarettes are addictive, few substitutes), and the supply elasticity is εs = 2 (factories can ramp up easily). HMRC raises the per-pack tax by 1 pound. How much of that pound is paid by smokers, and how much by tobacco companies?

Apply the formula. Smokers pay

$$ \frac{\Delta P^c}{t} = \frac{2}{2 - (-0.4)} = \frac{2}{2.4} \approx 0.83 $$

or 83 pence of the pound. Tobacco companies absorb the remaining 17 pence as a lower received price. This is why "sin taxes" on inelastic goods are popular with revenue-hungry governments: the producers cannot push the burden back onto consumers because the consumers cannot easily walk away.

Surplus, welfare, and deadweight loss

Consumer surplus is the area between the demand curve and the price, integrated from zero to the equilibrium quantity. Mathematically,

$$ CS = \int_0^{Q^*} \big( D^{-1}(q) - P^* \big) \, dq $$

This is the total amount buyers were willing to pay, minus what they actually paid. Producer surplus is the symmetric area between the price and the supply curve:

$$ PS = \int_0^{Q^*} \big( P^* - S^{-1}(q) \big) \, dq $$

Their sum is total surplus, the welfare measure of the market. Under competitive equilibrium and absent market failure, total surplus is maximised: this is the First Welfare Theorem, formalised in Lecture 4. Any policy that pushes the market away from the competitive quantity (a price floor, a price ceiling, a tax, a quota) loses some of that surplus to deadweight loss, the triangle of trades that would have created value but no longer happen.

The size of the deadweight loss triangle, holding revenue constant, is proportional to the elasticity of both curves. The intuition: if neither side reacts to the price change, no trades are lost, so no surplus is destroyed. The most efficient tax is therefore on the most inelastic activity (a result formalised in the optimal-taxation literature, Lecture 19). This is also why a lump-sum poll tax has the smallest deadweight loss of any tax in theory, and the worst political reception in practice.

Step 5

Synthesis and what's next

You now have the four ingredients of every applied micro argument: a downward-sloping demand curve, an upward-sloping supply curve, the equilibrium concept that links them, and the elasticity numbers that translate any policy change into a quantity response, a surplus change, and a tax incidence. The rest of microeconomics is "what if we relax assumption X?" In Lecture 4 we will prove the welfare optimum claim for the case where neither side has market power; in Lecture 5 we will let one side have all the power (monopoly); in Lecture 6 we will let both sides be strategic (oligopoly and game theory); in Lectures 7 and 8 we will let the two curves silently include things they should not (externalities) or omit things they should (private information).
