Perfect competition and welfare

Put the demand curve from Lecture 2 together with the supply curve from Lecture 3, assume no one has market power, and prove that the resulting equilibrium maximises total surplus. This is the First Welfare Theorem, the formal expression of Adam Smith's invisible hand, and the benchmark every later lecture deviates from.

Lecture 4 of 25 Reading time, 40 minutes Part I: microeconomics
Step 1

The 30-second version

A market is perfectly competitive if (i) there are many buyers and many sellers, none large enough to affect the price; (ii) the product is homogeneous, so buyers do not care which seller they buy from; (iii) there is free entry and exit in the long run; and (iv) buyers and sellers have full information. Under these conditions, the market clears at the price where demand equals supply, every firm earns zero economic profit in the long run, and the resulting allocation is Pareto efficient: no rearrangement could make anyone better off without making someone else worse off.

This is the First Welfare Theorem. The Second Welfare Theorem says the converse: any Pareto-efficient allocation can be achieved as a competitive equilibrium, provided you first redistribute endowments appropriately. Together they make the case for using markets to allocate, and lump-sum transfers to redistribute, separately.

Feynman test Imagine an auctioneer who knows nothing about anybody. They call out prices. At each price, buyers say how much they want, sellers say how much they will deliver. The auctioneer raises the price when supply falls short of demand, lowers it when demand falls short. The process ends when the two match. Magically, when it does, the goods have ended up with the buyers who valued them most and have been made by the sellers who could make them most cheaply. The auctioneer never asked anyone for that information. That is the invisible hand.
What's beyond the syllabus Principles courses often state the welfare theorems verbally. This lecture states them precisely (with the assumptions that make them go through) and uses an Edgeworth box to make Pareto efficiency concrete. It also stresses the often-skipped Second Welfare Theorem, which is the basis for the modern policy position of "use markets for efficiency, taxes for redistribution".
Step 2

Why this matters

Perfect competition is the benchmark against which every other market structure is judged. Whenever an economist says a market is "inefficient" or "distorted", they implicitly mean "worse than the perfectly competitive equivalent". You cannot have an opinion about monopoly without first knowing what competitive equilibrium looks like. You cannot have an opinion about a Pigovian tax without first being able to compute the surplus that pollution destroys relative to a competitive baseline.

For policy: the welfare theorems are the formal justification for market-based allocation. Their failure conditions (market power, externalities, public goods, asymmetric information) are the formal justification for government intervention. That is the entire structure of the public-economics field (Lecture 19) in one sentence.

Step 3

The mental model

Two characters: a buyer and a seller who do not know each other. Each side has many copies of itself. The price is set by the market as a whole, not by any individual; everyone is a "price taker". The market is a coordination device, not a negotiation venue.

To visualise Pareto efficiency, drop two consumers into an Edgeworth box: a rectangle whose dimensions are the total endowment of two goods, one consumer measured from the bottom-left corner, the other from the top-right. Any point in the box is a complete allocation. The contract curve traces out the points where the two consumers' indifference curves are tangent, that is, the Pareto-efficient allocations. A competitive equilibrium is a particular point on the contract curve.

Step 4

The detail

Short-run competitive equilibrium

Fix the number of firms in the industry. Each firm has marginal cost MCi(qi). Market supply is the horizontal sum:

$$ S(P) = \sum_{i} q_i^s(P) \quad \text{where} \quad q_i^s(P) = MC_i^{-1}(P) $$

Market demand is similarly the horizontal sum of Marshallian demands across consumers. Equilibrium price P* solves D(P*) = S(P*). Each firm produces where MCi(qi) = P*. The result: at the optimum, every firm has the same marginal cost (equal to the market price). This equalisation of marginal cost is what makes the allocation production-efficient: no rearrangement of who produces what could reduce total cost.

Long-run equilibrium with free entry

In the long run, firms enter the industry if they can earn positive economic profit and exit if they earn negative. Entry shifts the supply curve right (more firms, more supply at each price); exit shifts it left. Equilibrium is reached when economic profit is zero:

$$ P^* = \min_q ATC(q) $$

The price equals the minimum of average total cost. Each firm operates at minimum efficient scale; the number of firms adjusts to fit market demand. Zero economic profit does not mean zero accounting profit; it means the firm earns just enough to cover the opportunity cost of capital, the wage of the entrepreneur's time, and so on. A firm earning "zero economic profit" is exactly the kind of firm a careful investor would be willing to fund.

Pareto efficiency

An allocation is Pareto efficient if no other feasible allocation makes someone better off without making someone else worse off. Three efficiency conditions characterise it:

  1. Exchange efficiency: the MRS between any two goods is the same for all consumers. (Otherwise two consumers could trade and both gain.)
  2. Production efficiency: the MRTS between any two inputs is the same for all firms. (Otherwise two firms could swap inputs and produce more in total.)
  3. Output mix efficiency: the common MRS equals the marginal rate of transformation in production. (Otherwise the economy should produce more of what consumers value more.)

A competitive equilibrium achieves all three. Consumers face the same prices, so their MRSs are equal (each set to the same px/py). Firms face the same input prices, so their MRTSs are equal (each set to w/r). And the price ratio equals the marginal cost ratio in production, which is the marginal rate of transformation. Three conditions, three equalities, one efficiency.

The First Welfare Theorem

If markets are complete (one price for every good, in every time and state), all agents are price-takers, and preferences are non-satiated, then every competitive equilibrium is Pareto efficient.

The proof is short and worth knowing. Suppose otherwise: there is some feasible allocation that Pareto-dominates the competitive equilibrium. Then in that alternative, every consumer is at least as well off and at least one is strictly better off. But each consumer was already at their optimum given their budget, so the alternative bundle must violate the budget for at least one consumer (otherwise they would have chosen it themselves). When you add up across consumers, the alternative requires more aggregate spending than the equilibrium does. But total spending equals total income, which equals total firm revenue, which equals the value of production at equilibrium prices. So the alternative requires more production value than was produced. Contradiction. Hence no such alternative exists.

The Second Welfare Theorem

If preferences are convex and continuous, every Pareto-efficient allocation can be supported as a competitive equilibrium, for some redistribution of initial endowments. In other words: you can use markets to reach any efficient outcome you like, provided you adjust starting points first.

This is the formal basis for the policy position that efficiency and equity are separable. If you want a more equal outcome, redistribute endowments (with lump-sum transfers, ideally) and then let the market do its job. Do not try to redistribute through the price system itself (price controls, minimum wages, rent caps), because that distorts the efficiency margins.

The practical catch is that lump-sum transfers based on (unchangeable) characteristics are hard to design. In practice, redistribution is done through taxes that do distort behaviour, leading to the efficiency-equity trade-offs explored in Lecture 19.

The competitive benchmark and total surplus

Consumer surplus plus producer surplus is total surplus. The competitive equilibrium maximises it. Any policy that pushes the market away from Q* (a tax, a price ceiling, a price floor, a quota, an import tariff) creates a deadweight loss triangle of size proportional to the wedge between consumer and producer prices and to the elasticities. This is the welfare cost of distortion, and it is the metric every later lecture uses to weigh the cost of intervention.

Where this fails: the road map

The First Welfare Theorem rests on five fragile assumptions. Each one breaks somewhere in the rest of the course:

  • Price-taking behaviour: monopoly (Lecture 5) and oligopoly (Lecture 6) break this.
  • No externalities: pollution, congestion, contagion, knowledge spillovers (Lecture 7).
  • No public goods: defence, lighthouses, basic research (Lecture 7).
  • Complete information: insurance markets, used cars, credit rationing (Lecture 8).
  • Complete markets: missing markets for future and state-contingent goods, especially relevant in macro (Lecture 15).

The remaining seven lectures of Part I take each failure in turn, characterise the resulting inefficiency, and study the policy instruments designed to correct it.

Step 5

Synthesis and what's next

This is the only lecture in the course where everything works out in favour of the market. From here on, every market structure we study breaks at least one welfare-theorem assumption, and the question becomes whether the resulting inefficiency is worth correcting and how. The next four lectures take that question head-on.
