Open economy macroeconomics

Add trade and capital flows to the closed-economy machinery. Two new variables enter (the exchange rate and the current-account balance) and an old result changes drastically: under floating rates, monetary policy is highly effective; under fixed rates, it is not. The Mundell-Fleming model is the half-day toolkit for understanding why.

Lecture 12 of 25 Reading time, 40 minutes Part II: macroeconomics
Step 1

The 30-second version

In an open economy with perfect capital mobility, the domestic interest rate is pinned to the world rate (with adjustments for expected currency depreciation). The exchange rate becomes the variable that adjusts. The Mundell-Fleming trilemma says that a country can choose at most two of: (i) a fixed exchange rate, (ii) free capital mobility, (iii) independent monetary policy. Choose any two, the third is forfeit.

The policy implications fall out immediately. Under floating exchange rates and capital mobility, fiscal policy is partially crowded out via the exchange rate, but monetary policy is highly effective. Under fixed rates, monetary policy is committed to defending the peg and is therefore unavailable for domestic stabilisation; fiscal policy is the only macro tool left. This is the most-applied piece of macro in central-banking and Treasury work.

Feynman test Money flows where the return is highest. If domestic rates are above world rates and the currency can float, capital pours in, bids up the currency, and the trade balance worsens until expected returns are equalised. If the currency is pegged, the same capital inflow forces the central bank to buy foreign currency and sell pounds, expanding the money supply, which pulls rates back down to the world level. Either way, the country cannot keep an interest rate that differs from the world rate. That is the trilemma.
What's beyond the syllabus Most BSc texts cover Mundell-Fleming and stop. This lecture adds the modern perspective from the recent open-economy literature: Helene Rey's "dilemma not trilemma" (capital mobility may pin policy even under floating rates) and the international financial cycle. These show up implicitly in central-bank financial stability reports.
Step 2

Why this matters

The UK is a small open economy with free capital movement and a floating exchange rate. Almost every monetary or fiscal decision is debated in the open-economy frame: sterling fell on Truss's mini-budget; the Bank's QE programme worked partly via exchange-rate depreciation; the post-Brexit pound move changed inflation pass-through. None of this makes sense in the closed-economy model of Lecture 11.

For banking, the open-economy frame underpins (i) cross-currency funding strategies, (ii) NII sensitivity to interest-rate differentials, (iii) FX risk in the trading book, and (iv) macroprudential rules for foreign-currency lending.

Step 3

The mental model

Two new objects join the macro model. The nominal exchange rate e is the price of foreign currency in domestic currency (a rise is a depreciation). The real exchange rate is

$$ q = e \cdot \frac{P^*}{P} $$

where P* is the foreign price level. The real exchange rate measures how cheap a domestic basket is relative to a foreign basket. Exports are rising in q (a real depreciation makes UK goods cheaper to foreigners); imports are falling in q; net exports are rising in q.

The other new ingredient is uncovered interest parity (UIP): the expected return on domestic and foreign bonds must be equal after adjusting for expected currency moves.

$$ i_t = i_t^* + \mathbb{E}_t \big[ \% \Delta e_{t+1} \big] $$

Step 4

The detail

The exchange rate regimes

Three pure regimes and many hybrids.

  • Floating: the rate is determined by market forces with no intervention. UK since 1992, US, eurozone (against non-euro currencies), Japan.
  • Fixed (peg): the rate is set by the central bank, which intervenes to defend it. Hong Kong dollar (against US dollar), Danish krone (against euro).
  • Managed float / crawling peg: somewhere between. Most emerging-market currencies in practice.

The Mundell-Fleming trilemma says three things cannot coexist: capital mobility, an exchange-rate target, and an independent monetary policy. Choose two:

ConfigurationSacrificedExample
Floating rate + capital mobilityExchange-rate targetUK, US, Australia
Fixed rate + capital mobilityIndependent monetary policyHong Kong, the gold standard
Fixed rate + independent monetary policyCapital mobilityBretton Woods, China for much of the 1990s

The eurozone is an interesting case: by sharing a currency, members forfeit independent monetary policy and exchange-rate flexibility but retain free capital movement. Asymmetric shocks then have to be absorbed by fiscal policy and labour mobility (which the EU has less of than, say, US states).

Mundell-Fleming with floating exchange rates

In the standard IS-LM-BP setup, the BP curve (balance of payments equilibrium) is horizontal at the world interest rate i* under perfect capital mobility. The IS-LM intersection must occur at this rate; any deviation triggers capital flows that move the exchange rate.

Monetary expansion (M up). LM shifts right. Rates start to fall below i*. Capital flows out, the exchange rate depreciates, net exports rise, IS shifts right. The new equilibrium has higher Y at the original interest rate. Monetary policy is highly effective under floating rates.

Fiscal expansion (G up). IS shifts right. Rates start to rise above i*. Capital flows in, the exchange rate appreciates, net exports fall, IS shifts left back. Some of the initial expansion is crowded out via the exchange rate. Fiscal policy is partially crowded out under floating rates (the IS-curve shift produces a smaller Y response than in a closed economy).

Mundell-Fleming with fixed exchange rates

Now the central bank commits to defending a peg. Capital flows are sterilised only partially because, to keep the rate pegged, the central bank must absorb the net foreign-currency demand or supply.

Monetary expansion. LM shifts right; rates fall; capital flows out; the central bank must buy domestic currency (sell reserves) to defend the peg. This drains domestic money supply, returning LM to its original position. Monetary policy is ineffective under fixed rates: any expansion is immediately reversed by the foreign-exchange operations needed to defend the peg.

Fiscal expansion. IS shifts right; rates start to rise; capital flows in; the central bank must sell domestic currency (buy reserves) to defend the peg. This expands domestic money supply, shifting LM right. The new equilibrium has substantially higher Y. Fiscal policy works at full power under fixed rates.

The complete reversal of the closed-economy intuition is the Mundell-Fleming lesson. It is also the reason eurozone countries facing asymmetric shocks have only fiscal stabilisation available (and even that is constrained by the Stability and Growth Pact).

Exchange-rate determination

What pins down the level of e?

  • Purchasing power parity (PPP): the long-run anchor. Tradeable goods should cost the same when measured in a common currency. In practice PPP holds only over decades.
  • Covered interest parity (CIP): a no-arbitrage condition involving forward exchange rates. Strongly holds in major currency pairs.
  • Uncovered interest parity (UIP): a no-arbitrage condition involving expected spot rates. Empirically fails over short horizons (the "carry trade" profits from this), holds reasonably well over long horizons.
  • The current account: a chronic deficit puts long-run depreciation pressure on a currency. The UK pound's slow decline against the dollar from 1900 (4.86 USD/GBP) to 2023 (1.25 USD/GBP) is the cumulative consequence of decades of trade deficits and relatively higher UK inflation.

Currency crises

A fixed exchange rate that the market expects will be devalued is unsustainable. Speculators sell the currency in anticipation; the central bank loses reserves defending it; eventually the peg breaks. The 1992 sterling crisis (Black Wednesday) is the textbook UK case. The 1997-98 Asian crisis is the textbook emerging-market case. Modern models (the second-generation Obstfeld model, the third-generation Krugman model) attribute crises to multiple equilibria, contingent on expectations.

The dilemma vs the trilemma

Helene Rey has argued that the trilemma is, in practice, a dilemma: even with floating exchange rates, the global financial cycle (driven by the US monetary stance and global risk appetite) pins domestic financial conditions, leaving little independence for domestic monetary policy. The implication is that capital-flow management ("macroprudential FX rules") may be necessary even for floaters. This is the live policy frontier in emerging-market central banking.

Optimum currency areas

Robert Mundell's other big idea (the one he got the Nobel for) asks: when is it optimal for a group of countries to share a currency? The benefits are reduced transaction costs and lower exchange-rate volatility for trade. The costs are the loss of exchange-rate flexibility for absorbing asymmetric shocks. The criteria for an optimum currency area are (i) high labour mobility between regions, (ii) similar business cycles, (iii) fiscal transfers to cushion regional shocks. By these criteria, the US is closer to an OCA than the eurozone, which has helped some commentators predict the euro's stress in 2010-12 and the structural difficulty of monetary union without fiscal union.
