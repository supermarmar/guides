Monetary economics

What is money, how is it created, how does monetary policy work, and what should it target? This lecture combines the institutional plumbing (central banks, commercial banks, the money multiplier) with the analytical framework (Taylor rule, time-inconsistency, expectations) you need to read a Monetary Policy Committee statement.

Lecture 14 of 25 Reading time, 45 minutes Part II: macroeconomics
Step 1

The 30-second version

Money serves three functions: medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value. The monetary base (cash plus reserves at the central bank) is created by the central bank. Broad money (M2, M4) is mostly created by commercial banks through lending. The ratio of broad money to base is the money multiplier, determined by the public's currency-to-deposits ratio and the banks' reserve-to-deposits ratio.

Modern central banks (Bank of England, Federal Reserve, ECB) target an inflation rate (typically 2%) by adjusting a short-term policy rate. The transmission mechanism runs through borrowing rates, asset prices, the exchange rate, and expectations. The Taylor rule describes how rates respond to inflation and output gaps; the time-inconsistency problem explains why central banks need credible commitments to keep inflation low.

Feynman test Banks make loans, and those loans show up as deposits on the other side of the same banks. New money is created in the act of lending. The central bank does not print most of the broad money; the commercial banks do, on its behalf. The central bank controls the price (interest rate) at which banks fund themselves, which indirectly controls how much lending they want to do, which controls how much money gets created. The whole system runs on the central bank's credibility, not its printing press.
What's beyond the syllabus Principles courses often present a textbook "money multiplier" that does not match how modern central banks actually operate. This lecture distinguishes the textbook story from the post-2009 framework where reserves are abundant and the policy rate is set directly. Step 2

Why this matters

Monetary policy is the most active arm of macro stabilisation in most economies. Every BoE Monetary Policy Committee decision moves bank funding costs, mortgage rates, gilt yields, the exchange rate, and equity prices. For a credit-risk modeller, the monetary regime determines the macroeconomic scenarios that drive ECL: rate-hike cycles change PDs; rate-cut cycles change behaviour scores; unconventional policy (QE, forward guidance) changes the term-premium component of the discount curve used in IRRBB.

This lecture also closes a loop with Lecture 6: the Kydland-Prescott time-inconsistency problem is the most famous applied game-theory result in macro and explains why we have independent central banks.

Step 3

The mental model

Imagine a two-tier banking system. At the top, the central bank, which (a) issues physical currency and reserves, (b) sets a policy rate, (c) provides the ultimate settlement asset (reserves) used by commercial banks to clear payments. Below, commercial banks, which extend loans (creating deposits) and hold reserves at the central bank. The public holds currency plus deposits.

Most macro effects of monetary policy come from changing the price (interest rate) at which the central bank lends or borrows reserves overnight. That price spreads outward to all other interest rates and asset prices through arbitrage, expectations, and the term structure.

Step 4

The detail

What is money

Money is defined functionally, not physically. Anything that serves all three functions is money: gold, paper, central-bank deposits, commercial-bank deposits, mobile money in M-Pesa accounts. Money aggregates from narrowest to broadest:

  • M0 / monetary base / "high-powered money": currency in circulation plus commercial bank reserves at the central bank.
  • M1: currency plus demand deposits (cheque accounts).
  • M2 / M4 (UK): M1 plus savings and time deposits. The "broad money" the public actually transacts with.

Strictly, M0 is created by the central bank and M2/M4 is mostly created by commercial banks through lending. Each new loan increases both the bank's asset (the loan) and its liability (the deposit credited to the borrower), expanding broad money. Loans repaid extinguish money.

The money multiplier and reserves

The classical (textbook) story. Banks hold a reserve ratio r against deposits. The public holds currency-to-deposit ratio c. Starting from a unit increase in monetary base, the eventual increase in broad money is

$$ \frac{M}{B} = \frac{1 + c}{r + c} $$

which is the money multiplier. Increasing the base shifts broad money proportionally.

The modern (post-2009) reality. Most major central banks now operate in an abundant-reserves regime where the policy rate is set directly (by paying interest on reserves at the policy rate). The multiplier is no longer a useful operational concept; the central bank controls the price of reserves, not their quantity. The BoE's policy rate (the Bank Rate) is paid on reserves and is the anchor for SONIA and short-term gilt yields. Asset purchases (QE) expand reserves without operating through the multiplier, instead working through term premia and portfolio rebalancing channels.

The transmission mechanism

A cut in the policy rate spreads outward through several channels.

  • Interest rate channel: bank funding rates fall, loan rates fall, borrowing rises.
  • Asset price channel: discount rates fall, equity and bond prices rise, wealth rises, consumption rises.
  • Exchange rate channel: domestic returns fall, capital flows out, sterling depreciates, net exports rise.
  • Credit channel: bank balance sheets improve, lending standards loosen, credit-constrained borrowers gain access.
  • Expectations channel: forward guidance shifts expected future rates, affecting long rates and expectations.

These channels operate at different speeds. Asset prices respond instantly; exchange rates within days; bank lending over months; CPI inflation over 12-24 months. This is the famous "long and variable lag" that complicates monetary policy.

The Taylor rule

John Taylor proposed in 1993 a simple rule that fitted US Fed behaviour:

$$ i_t = r^* + \pi_t + 0.5 (\pi_t - \pi^*) + 0.5 (Y_t - Y^*) $$

The policy rate equals the neutral real rate plus current inflation, with positive responses to both the inflation gap and the output gap. The crucial coefficient is the one on inflation: it must exceed 1 (the Taylor principle) for the real interest rate to rise when inflation rises, otherwise rising inflation reduces the real rate and the system is destabilising.

The Taylor rule is descriptive rather than prescriptive: central banks do not mechanically follow it, but it provides a useful benchmark against which to evaluate decisions. The neutral real rate r* has declined sharply since 1980 (from around 3% to perhaps 0.5%), which is why nominal rates near zero are no longer obviously "accommodative".

The time-inconsistency problem

Kydland and Prescott (1977 Nobel-winning paper) showed that a central bank that can credibly commit to low inflation achieves both lower inflation and the same level of output, compared to one that cannot. The intuition: ex ante, the central bank announces low inflation; ex post, it has an incentive to surprise the public with a small inflation that raises output along the short-run Phillips curve. The public anticipates this and revises inflation expectations upward, defeating the surprise but locking in higher inflation. The discretionary equilibrium has higher inflation and no real benefit.

The solution is institutional: an independent central bank with a strict mandate, accountable but not subject to political pressure to "surprise". This is the conceptual foundation of the BoE's 1997 operational independence, the ECB's price-stability mandate, and the proliferation of inflation-targeting regimes globally since 1990.

Inflation targeting

The dominant modern regime. The central bank announces a numerical inflation target (2% in the UK, US, and eurozone; sometimes a range elsewhere) and conducts policy to hit it over a medium horizon (typically 1-2 years). The framework's success rests on credibility: if the public believes the target, inflation expectations anchor on it, and the central bank gains substantial room to use the short-run Phillips trade-off for output stabilisation. If credibility erodes, the trade-off collapses.

Variants include average inflation targeting (Fed 2020), price-level targeting (proposed but not adopted), and nominal GDP targeting (academic favourite, no major adoption).

The zero lower bound and unconventional policy

Nominal interest rates cannot go meaningfully below zero (the alternative is to hold cash). When the natural rate of interest falls below zero, conventional monetary policy is exhausted. The post-2008 toolkit added:

  • Quantitative easing (QE): central-bank purchases of long-dated government bonds (and sometimes corporate bonds) to lower term premia and force private investors into riskier assets.
  • Forward guidance: explicit central-bank commitment to keep policy loose for longer than the standard reaction function would suggest.
  • Targeted lending facilities: subsidised lending to banks conditional on lending to specific sectors (e.g. the BoE's Funding for Lending, TFS).
  • Negative interest rates: the ECB, SNB, BoJ, and a few others charged interest on reserves. The UK BoE considered but did not adopt this.

The evidence is mixed: QE clearly lowered yields, but its effect on inflation and real activity is debated. The 2021-23 inflation surge raised serious questions about whether the QE programmes contributed; the consensus view is that supply shocks dominated but QE made unwinding harder.

Quantitative tightening and balance-sheet normalisation

The reverse of QE: gradually reducing the central bank's bond holdings, either by letting bonds mature without reinvestment (passive) or by active sales (active). The BoE began active gilt sales in 2022. The macroeconomic effect is the symmetric counterpart of QE: term premia rise, financial conditions tighten, government financing costs go up. The mechanical impact on broad money is modest in an abundant-reserves regime because the multiplier is broken.
