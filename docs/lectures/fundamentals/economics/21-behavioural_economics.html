Lectures / Economics

Behavioural economics

Lecture 2's rational consumer is a mathematical convenience, not an empirical claim. Real people use heuristics, frame outcomes around reference points, treat losses differently from equivalent gains, and discount the future inconsistently. Behavioural economics integrates the empirical evidence on actual decision-making into otherwise-standard models, sometimes producing dramatically different predictions.

Lecture 21 of 25 Reading time, 40 minutes Part IV: field economics
Step 1

The 30-second version

Behavioural economics replaces the rational-actor assumption with three families of empirically grounded modifications. Prospect theory (Kahneman-Tversky) says people evaluate outcomes relative to a reference point, with losses weighted more than equivalent gains (loss aversion) and small probabilities overweighted. Heuristics and biases describe the rules of thumb people use when full optimisation is too costly (anchoring, availability, representativeness). Self-control problems (hyperbolic discounting) explain why people fail to follow through on long-run plans.

The policy implication is nudge: choice architecture that shapes decisions without removing options. Default enrolments in pensions, simplified disclosures, reminder texts. The deeper implication is that classical welfare economics, which assumes revealed preference is informative about welfare, is on shakier ground than Lecture 2 implied.

Feynman test People are not the rational actors of textbook economics. They feel losses more than gains; they discount tomorrow's pleasure less than the day after's; they use easy mental shortcuts; they care about how a choice is framed. None of this destroys economics, but it changes the predictions. A pension scheme with default enrolment has dramatically higher participation than one with default opt-out. A petrol tax framed as a "carbon levy" is more accepted than one framed as a "tax". The same policy can succeed or fail depending on which behavioural lever is pulled.
What's beyond the syllabus Standard treatments cover prospect theory and stop. This lecture also covers hyperbolic discounting (Laibson), heuristics (Kahneman, Thaler), the rise of nudge as a policy tool (Sunstein-Thaler), and the replication crisis that has chastened parts of the behavioural literature in the 2010s. The honest position is that the field's headline results are robust but many specific applications are not.
Step 2

Why this matters

Behavioural economics has reshaped policy design in pensions (auto-enrolment), savings products (default contribution rates), public health (organ-donor opt-out vs opt-in), tax policy (the framing of letters from HMRC). It also informs the design of disclosure regulation in financial markets (the FCA's "consumer duty"), the structure of credit-card and mortgage warnings, and the regulation of gambling and high-cost short-term credit.

For credit-risk modellers, behavioural economics is the empirical basis for understanding borrower behaviour that does not fit standard rational-default models: voluntary mortgage prepayments that ignore the rate environment, "anchoring" of repayment behaviour on minimum credit-card payments, biased forecasting of future income that leads to over-borrowing.

Step 3

The mental model

Standard economics: people have stable preferences, perfect information, unlimited cognitive capacity, and self-control. Behavioural economics: they have reference-dependent preferences, limited attention, cognitive shortcuts, and time-inconsistent self-control. Add structured deviations from the standard model, keep the optimisation framework otherwise, and you get models that predict observed behaviour better.

Step 4

The detail

Prospect theory

Kahneman and Tversky's 1979 paper documented systematic violations of expected utility theory. Their replacement, prospect theory, has three components.

  1. Reference dependence: outcomes are evaluated as gains or losses relative to a reference point (typically the status quo), not as absolute levels of wealth.
  2. Loss aversion: the value function is steeper for losses than for gains. A 100-pound loss hurts more than a 100-pound gain pleases, by a factor of about 2.
  3. Probability weighting: small probabilities are overweighted; large probabilities are underweighted. This explains why people buy lottery tickets (low-probability large gains) and insurance against unlikely catastrophes (low-probability large losses).

The value function:

$$ v(x) = \begin{cases} x^\alpha & x \geq 0 \\ -\lambda (-x)^\beta & x < 0 \end{cases} $$

with λ ≈ 2.25, α ≈ β ≈ 0.88 in the original Kahneman-Tversky estimates. The probability weighting function is S-shaped.

The endowment effect is loss aversion in another guise: people demand more to give up an object than they would pay to acquire the same object. The classic mug experiment (Kahneman-Knetsch-Thaler 1990) showed the disparity could be 2:1 even for trivial items.

Heuristics and biases

Tversky and Kahneman's program documented systematic deviations from Bayesian probability reasoning.

  • Anchoring: estimates are biased toward initial values, even irrelevant ones (the "10% off" anchor, the "starting bid" in auctions).
  • Availability: probability judgements are influenced by how easily examples come to mind (plane crashes are overestimated; deaths from stomach cancer are underestimated).
  • Representativeness: probability of category membership judged by similarity to a stereotype, ignoring base rates (the "Linda the bank teller / feminist" experiment).
  • Confirmation bias: weighting confirming evidence more than disconfirming.
  • Status-quo bias: preference for the current state, distinct from but related to loss aversion.
  • Overconfidence: subjective probability intervals too narrow.

These heuristics are not "irrational"; they are useful shortcuts that work most of the time. The "biases" are predictable systematic deviations from optimal Bayesian updating. Modern dual-process accounts (Kahneman's Thinking, Fast and Slow) attribute heuristics to System 1 (automatic, fast) and deliberate optimisation to System 2 (slow, controlled).

Time inconsistency and hyperbolic discounting

The standard discounted-utility model assumes exponential discounting: the discount factor between today and a future date is βt. This implies time-consistent preferences: a decision made today about a trade-off between day 30 and day 31 is the same as a decision made on day 29 about the same trade-off.

Empirically, people exhibit hyperbolic discounting: a steeper discount in the short run than the long run. Laibson's quasi-hyperbolic ("beta-delta") model captures this with one extra parameter:

$$ U_t = u_t + \beta \sum_{s=1}^\infty \delta^s u_{t+s} $$

where β < 1 is the "present bias" parameter. The decision-maker over-weights the immediate period relative to all future periods. The result: dynamically inconsistent plans. Today, the agent plans to go to the gym next week. Next week, the agent prefers to stay at home and plans to go the week after. Without a commitment device, the gym is never visited.

Self-aware (sophisticated) hyperbolic discounters value commitment devices: automatic savings contributions, pension auto-enrolment, smoking-cessation deposits. Naive hyperbolic discounters do not anticipate their own future preference reversals and end up worse off.

Bounded rationality and limited attention

Herbert Simon's bounded rationality (1957) acknowledges that decisions are made with limited information, limited time, and limited cognitive capacity. Modern "rational inattention" models (Sims) treat attention as a costly resource: agents choose which information to process and ignore the rest. The result is decisions that look approximately rational on dimensions the agent attends to and systematically biased on dimensions they ignore.

This has direct policy implications: simpler disclosures get read; complex ones get ignored even when they contain decision-relevant information. The FCA's Consumer Duty (effective 2023) is essentially a regulatory bet that simpler, behaviourally informed disclosures lead to better consumer outcomes than detailed-but-ignored ones.

Social preferences

Real people are not pure self-interested optimisers. Ultimatum game experiments (Guth) reliably show that the second player rejects "unfair" offers, leaving both players worse off. Dictator games show that even when nothing prevents pure self-interest, dictators give away significant fractions of the pie. Models with inequality aversion (Fehr-Schmidt), reciprocity (Rabin), and warm-glow giving (Andreoni) integrate these regularities.

Social preferences matter for labour-market behaviour (fair-wage hypotheses), public-goods provision (conditional cooperation), and tax compliance (social norms about paying taxes).

Nudge and choice architecture

Thaler and Sunstein's Nudge (2008) made the policy case: many decisions are reshaped by the structure of the choice environment (defaults, framings, salience), often in ways the chooser would on reflection endorse. Nudges are interventions that change behaviour without restricting choice. Major nudge applications:

  • Pension auto-enrolment (UK from 2012, extended in 2023): default participation raised from around 60% to over 90%. The largest single welfare intervention of behavioural economics.
  • Organ-donor opt-out: opt-out systems (Spain, Belgium) have donor rates 25-30 percentage points higher than opt-in systems with otherwise identical institutions.
  • Tax letters with social-norm framing ("9 out of 10 people in your area pay on time"): HMRC trials in 2009-12 raised collection rates by several percentage points.
  • Energy-bill comparison nudges (Opower in the US): showing households how their consumption compares to neighbours produced 2-3% energy savings.

Nudges are politically attractive: they preserve formal choice, are usually cheap to implement, and have testable effects. The UK Cabinet Office's Behavioural Insights Team (the "Nudge Unit"), founded 2010, has institutionalised the approach.

The replication crisis

Many headline behavioural findings have failed to replicate or have shown smaller effect sizes than originally reported. Power posing (Carney-Cuddy-Yap 2010, retracted by Carney 2016). Ego depletion (Baumeister 1998, largely failed in pre-registered replications). Priming experiments. The behavioural literature now operates with much higher standards: pre-registration, larger samples, replication studies built into research programmes. The robust findings (loss aversion, default effects, time inconsistency) survived; the more speculative claims did not.

Implications for welfare economics

If people make biased decisions, what is their welfare? Revealed-preference logic says we infer welfare from observed choices. Behavioural economics says observed choices may not reflect welfare. The proposed reconciliation, "asymmetric paternalism" (Camerer et al.) or "libertarian paternalism" (Thaler-Sunstein), uses interventions that improve welfare for biased agents while imposing minimal cost on rational ones. The default rules of nudge meet this criterion. The deeper question, "whose welfare", remains contested.
