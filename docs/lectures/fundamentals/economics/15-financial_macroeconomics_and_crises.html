Lectures / Economics

Financial macroeconomics and crises

The earlier macro lectures assumed a smoothly functioning financial system. This one looks at what happens when it fails. Bank runs, fire sales, debt overhang, the financial accelerator, and the lender of last resort: the toolkit for understanding why financial crises hurt the real economy and how policy can either prevent them or mitigate their damage.

Lecture 15 of 25 Reading time, 45 minutes Part II: macroeconomics
Step 1

The 30-second version

A bank is fragile because it transforms maturity (short-dated deposits funding long-dated loans) and liquidity (instantly redeemable claims backed by illiquid assets). If depositors collectively decide to withdraw, even a solvent bank can fail. The Diamond-Dybvig model formalises bank runs as a coordination failure with multiple equilibria. Deposit insurance and the lender-of-last-resort function eliminate the bad equilibrium.

At the macro level, a leveraged financial system amplifies shocks through the financial accelerator: falling collateral values tighten borrowing constraints, which reduce investment and demand, which lower collateral values further. This is the formal expression of the procyclicality the banking-course economic-environment lecture already established. Macroprudential policy (countercyclical capital buffers, LTV caps, liquidity requirements) is designed to dampen the amplification.

Feynman test Banks promise instant cash in exchange for deposits, but the cash is loaned out long-term and cannot be called back instantly. The trick works because not everyone wants their money at once. When everyone does want it at once, the trick fails. A bank run is the collapse of a coordination equilibrium: everyone running is rational if you believe everyone else is running. The Bank of England's job in a panic is to break the coordination by lending against any good collateral at a penalty rate. Bagehot wrote that down in 1873.
What's beyond the syllabus Principles courses sometimes ignore the financial system entirely or treat banks as transparent intermediaries. This lecture connects the dots between Lecture 11 (IS-LM), Lecture 14 (monetary policy), and the F107 banking material: how bank balance sheets matter for the macro outcome, why we have capital and liquidity regulation, and where systemic risk comes from. Step 2

Why this matters

Every major macroeconomic downturn since 1980 has had a financial-sector component: the 1987 stock crash, the early-1990s S&L crisis, the 1997-98 Asian and Russian crises, the 2000 dotcom bust, the 2007-09 Global Financial Crisis, the 2010-12 European sovereign-debt crisis, the 2020 COVID liquidity scare, the 2022-23 banking turmoil (SVB, Credit Suisse, regional US banks). Without a framework for financial-real linkages, the macro story of the last forty years is incomprehensible.

For a credit-risk modeller, this lecture is the macro lens on systemic risk: why a bank's individual PD models, calibrated through-the-cycle, can systematically understate stress losses; why deeply correlated defaults occur exactly in the worst macroeconomic states; why supervisory stress tests apply across-the-board macro shocks.

Step 3

The mental model

Three loops to watch.

  1. Bank-run loop (microeconomic): depositors panic, withdraw, the bank fails, panic spreads. The Diamond-Dybvig coordination failure.
  2. Financial accelerator (macro feedback): falling asset prices → reduced collateral → tighter credit → lower investment → lower output → further falling asset prices.
  3. Sovereign-bank doom loop: stressed banks weigh on the sovereign (through bail-out exposure and tax-revenue loss); a stressed sovereign weakens domestic banks (which hold its bonds). Most visible in the 2010-12 euro crisis.
Step 4

The detail

Diamond-Dybvig and bank runs

The 1983 Diamond-Dybvig model. Depositors have heterogeneous liquidity needs: some will need cash early, others late. A bank pools their deposits and invests in a long-term, illiquid asset that pays a high return if held to maturity but a low return if liquidated early. The bank promises instant convertibility to all depositors.

Two equilibria:

  1. Good equilibrium: only early-type depositors withdraw early; late-type depositors wait; the bank meets all demands; everyone is better off than if there were no bank.
  2. Bad equilibrium (run): late-type depositors withdraw early because they expect others to; the bank liquidates the asset prematurely; not all withdrawal requests can be met; some depositors take losses.

The bad equilibrium is rational given the belief that others will run. The model has no fundamental cause for the run: the bank may be perfectly solvent. The run is pure coordination failure, supported by self-fulfilling expectations.

The fixes:

  • Deposit insurance: government guarantee removes the late-types' incentive to run. The Financial Services Compensation Scheme in the UK covers deposits up to Â£85,000 per depositor per bank.
  • Lender of last resort: central bank lending against good collateral, eliminating the need to liquidate assets at fire-sale prices. Bagehot's rules: lend freely, at a penalty rate, against good collateral.
  • Suspension of convertibility: the bank stops paying out for a defined period. Historically used but reputation-damaging.

Liquidity vs solvency

A bank is illiquid if it cannot meet immediate cash demands but is fundamentally solvent (assets exceed liabilities in present value). It is insolvent if liabilities exceed assets. The classical Bagehot rule is to support illiquid-but-solvent banks and resolve insolvent ones. In practice the distinction is hard to make in real time: SVB's March 2023 collapse looked like a liquidity event but the underlying problem was unrealised losses on held-to-maturity bonds, which had silently rendered it insolvent. The line moves in a crisis.

The financial accelerator (Bernanke-Gertler-Gilchrist)

Borrowing capacity depends on collateral and net worth. When net worth falls, the agency premium (the extra return lenders demand to lend) rises, so external finance becomes more expensive and investment falls. The model has the structure

$$ \text{external finance premium} = \mu \cdot \frac{\text{borrowed amount}}{\text{net worth}} $$

So a fall in net worth (from falling asset prices or bad earnings) tightens the financing constraint, which reduces investment, which reduces aggregate demand, which reduces asset prices further. This procyclicality is the formal expression of the credit cycle and is the macro reason for countercyclical capital buffers in Basel III.

Procyclicality and the credit cycle

Credit growth and asset prices co-move with the business cycle, but with amplifying feedback. In good times, low default rates and rising collateral values relax lending standards, expanding credit, which fuels asset prices and demand. In bad times, the reverse. The cycle is exactly what the banking-course economic-environment lecture covers; this lecture provides its macroeconomic justification.

The Bank of International Settlements measures the credit-to-GDP gap as an early-warning indicator: when credit grows much faster than GDP for an extended period, the probability of a subsequent crisis is materially elevated. The countercyclical capital buffer (CCyB) is calibrated against this gap.

Fire sales and pecuniary externalities

When an individual bank sells assets under stress, it accepts a depressed price. When many banks sell simultaneously, the depressed price affects all other banks holding similar assets, even those not selling. Each bank, in optimising its private trade-off, ignores the effect of its sale on other banks' balance sheets. This is a pecuniary externality and produces over-fire-selling relative to the social optimum. It is the analytical foundation for liquidity regulation (LCR, NSFR) and for the Federal Reserve's repo facilities.

The lender of last resort

Walter Bagehot's Lombard Street (1873) set out the classical rules:

  • Lend freely: do not ration lending to viable institutions.
  • At a penalty rate: discourage moral hazard and ensure that LOLR borrowing is used only in genuine need.
  • Against good collateral: protect the central bank from losses.

Modern LOLR operations have generalised this in two directions. First, by accepting a broader collateral range (the BoE's Sterling Monetary Framework, the Fed's Discount Window). Second, by extending lending beyond banks (the Fed's Primary Dealer Credit Facility and Corporate Bond Purchase Programmes after 2008 and again in 2020). The 2007-09 crisis saw central banks act as lender of last resort to entire markets, not just institutions.

Macroprudential policy

Microprudential regulation ensures each bank is individually safe. Macroprudential policy targets the system: it accepts that an individually safe bank may still contribute to systemic risk, and uses instruments calibrated to system-wide conditions. The main tools:

  • Countercyclical capital buffer (CCyB): raises capital requirements during credit booms, releases them during busts.
  • Capital surcharges for G-SIBs: extra capital for systemically important banks.
  • Loan-to-value (LTV) and debt-to-income (DTI) caps on mortgages: limit household credit excess.
  • Liquidity requirements (LCR, NSFR): ensure banks have enough liquid assets to survive a stress.
  • Bank stress tests: supervisory exercises that test capital adequacy under hypothetical macro scenarios.

The UK's Financial Policy Committee (FPC) sets macroprudential policy alongside the Monetary Policy Committee that sets interest rates. The two committees coordinate but pursue different objectives. The full F107 banking track covers the institutional and regulatory detail; this lecture is the macro justification.

The 2007-09 crisis as a worked example

Conceptually, the GFC ran the full sequence: a credit-and-housing boom (procyclical), driven by securitisation and global savings glut, bid up US house prices well past any plausible fundamentals. When subprime defaults rose in 2006-07, the value of MBS fell, eroding bank capital. Funding markets froze (the run on the shadow banking system in repo and ABCP). The financial accelerator kicked in: tighter credit, falling demand, falling asset prices, more defaults. The Fed cut rates to zero, launched QE, and acted as lender of last resort to dealer banks. Fiscal stimulus filled in the remaining demand gap. The whole episode is a four-step illustration of every concept in this lecture.

Sovereign-bank doom loop

In countries where banks hold large quantities of sovereign debt (typical in the euro periphery), a fall in sovereign creditworthiness damages bank balance sheets, which weakens the banking system, which raises bail-out concerns, which damages sovereign creditworthiness further. The 2010-12 euro crisis exhibited this prominently; the ECB's OMT announcement in 2012 (Draghi's "whatever it takes") broke the loop by credibly capping spread widening. The Banking Union project (single supervisor, single resolution mechanism, eventually deposit insurance) is the structural fix.
