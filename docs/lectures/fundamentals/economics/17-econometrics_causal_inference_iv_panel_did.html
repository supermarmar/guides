Econometrics II: causal inference, IV, panel, DiD, RDD

The modern applied-economics toolkit. When OLS exogeneity fails, four families of techniques can sometimes restore a causal interpretation: randomised trials, instrumental variables, panel-data fixed effects, and difference-in-differences (with regression discontinuity as a useful close cousin). Each works only under specific assumptions, and each can be wrong if those assumptions are wrong.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The potential-outcomes framework (Rubin) defines the causal effect of treatment T on individual i as Yi(1) − Yi(0): the difference between what would happen with and without treatment. Only one of the two outcomes is observed, which is the fundamental problem of causal inference.

Four families of solutions:

  • Randomised controlled trials (RCTs): assign treatment by coin flip. The gold standard, when feasible.
  • Instrumental variables (IV): find a variable that affects T but not Y directly, then use the IV-induced variation in T to identify the causal effect on the compliers.
  • Panel data fixed effects: with repeated observations on the same units, subtract out unobserved time-invariant heterogeneity.
  • Difference-in-differences (DiD): compare changes in outcome across treated and untreated groups, assuming parallel trends.

A fifth, regression discontinuity (RDD), exploits the local randomness of a sharp threshold rule. Each technique gives a clean causal estimate under its assumptions; the art is matching the technique to the setting.

Feynman test To know whether a drug works you want to compare the same patient with and without the drug. You cannot. So you compare a treated patient to an untreated patient who looks otherwise identical. The trouble is that "otherwise identical" is hard to verify; even if observable characteristics match, unobservable ones might not. The four techniques in this lecture are four different ways of making "otherwise identical" defensible.
What's beyond the syllabus Principles courses introduce regression and stop. This lecture covers the four core identification strategies of the post-1990s credibility revolution, the ones that earn Nobel prizes (Card-Angrist-Imbens 2021). Each carries a strong interpretation only under explicit assumptions; the lecture flags the failure modes alongside the techniques.
Step 2

Why this matters

Modern policy debate increasingly turns on identified causal estimates. Minimum-wage effects on employment (Card-Krueger DiD); the returns to education (Angrist-Krueger compulsory schooling IV); the effect of cash transfers on welfare in developing countries (RCTs of conditional cash transfers); the effectiveness of class-size reduction (Project STAR RCT and the RDD on Israeli schools). Without the toolkit you cannot read this literature.

For banking, model validation increasingly demands causal evidence. A scorecard whose variables predict default well in-sample but fail out-of-sample may be picking up correlations that do not survive the relevant policy or population shifts. The discipline of asking "what would survive a counterfactual" is identification thinking.

Step 3

The mental model

You want to know whether T causes Y. The problem is that anything correlated with T might also drive Y. Each strategy below picks a different way of breaking that correlation: random assignment (RCT), find an external nudge that moves T only (IV), look only at within-unit variation (FE), look at changes around an event (DiD), or look at units just on either side of a threshold (RDD).

Step 4

The detail

The potential-outcomes framework

Each unit i has two potential outcomes: Yi(1) if treated, Yi(0) if not. The individual treatment effect is τi = Yi(1) − Yi(0). We observe only Yi = Ti Yi(1) + (1 − Ti) Yi(0). Three averages matter:

  • Average Treatment Effect (ATE): τ = E[Y(1) − Y(0)].
  • Average Treatment effect on the Treated (ATT): τT = E[Y(1) − Y(0) | T = 1].
  • Local Average Treatment Effect (LATE): the average effect on the subgroup whose treatment status is shifted by an instrument.

Different identification strategies recover different averages. RCTs estimate the ATE on the experimental population; IV estimates the LATE on compliers; DiD estimates a particular ATT under parallel-trends; RDD estimates the local effect at the cutoff.

Randomised controlled trials

Treatment is assigned by random number. By construction, T is independent of all unobservables, so

$$ \mathbb{E}[Y_i | T_i = 1] - \mathbb{E}[Y_i | T_i = 0] = \mathbb{E}[Y(1)] - \mathbb{E}[Y(0)] = \tau $$

The difference in means is the ATE. RCTs are the gold standard. Problems: ethical limits (you cannot randomise unemployment), external validity (does the experimental population represent the policy population?), and non-compliance (people assigned to treatment may refuse, breaking the simple identity above and requiring intention-to-treat analysis).

Instrumental variables

A variable Z is a valid instrument for T in the equation Y = T β + ε if (i) relevance: Z affects T (Cov(Z, T) ≠ 0), and (ii) exclusion: Z affects Y only through T (Z is uncorrelated with ε). Under these conditions, the IV estimator is

$$ \hat{\beta}_{IV} = \frac{\text{Cov}(Y, Z)}{\text{Cov}(T, Z)} $$

which is the Wald estimator. The two-stage least squares (2SLS) extension handles multiple instruments and controls.

The interpretation under heterogeneous treatment effects is the LATE: the average treatment effect for the compliers, the subpopulation whose treatment status changes when the instrument changes. The LATE is, in general, neither the ATE nor the ATT. It is the effect on the subgroup that the instrument moves, which is precisely the subgroup the policy can move.

Classic instruments and their interpretation:

  • Compulsory schooling laws as instruments for years of schooling (Angrist-Krueger). The compliers are people who would have left school earlier without the law.
  • Vietnam draft lottery numbers as instruments for military service (Angrist). Compliers are those who would have avoided service but for the draft.
  • Distance to nearest college as an instrument for going to college (Card). Compliers are students near the threshold of college affordability.

The exclusion restriction is untestable and always the most controversial assumption. Many great IV papers have been undone by discovering a back-channel through which the instrument also affects the outcome directly.

Panel data and fixed effects

With observations on the same units over time, the model becomes

$$ Y_{it} = X_{it}'\beta + \alpha_i + \delta_t + \varepsilon_{it} $$

where αi is a unit fixed effect (everything time-invariant about unit i) and δt is a time fixed effect (anything common to all units in period t). The within estimator demeans within units, eliminating αi:

$$ Y_{it} - \bar{Y}_i = (X_{it} - \bar{X}_i)' \beta + \varepsilon_{it} - \bar{\varepsilon}_i $$

OLS on the demeaned data recovers β. The identifying assumption is that the time-varying error is uncorrelated with the time-varying regressors, after removing the time-invariant unit-specific component. Strong if the omitted variable is mostly stable across time within unit (innate ability, firm culture, country institutions). Weak if the omitted variable also moves (a household's risk tolerance changes year to year).

Random effects is an alternative under stronger assumptions (the unit effect uncorrelated with regressors), is more efficient when valid, but is rarely the right choice in causal applications.

Difference-in-differences

An intervention happens at time t*. Group A is treated; group B is not. Compute

$$ \hat{\tau}_{DiD} = \big( \bar{Y}_{A, \text{post}} - \bar{Y}_{A, \text{pre}} \big) - \big( \bar{Y}_{B, \text{post}} - \bar{Y}_{B, \text{pre}} \big) $$

The first bracket is the change in A; the second is the change in B. If B is a good "control" (would have moved the same way as A in the absence of treatment), the second bracket strips out everything that would have happened without the policy. Equivalently, run the regression

$$ Y_{it} = \alpha + \gamma_A \cdot 1[i \in A] + \gamma_T \cdot 1[t \geq t^*] + \tau \cdot 1[i \in A] \cdot 1[t \geq t^*] + \varepsilon_{it} $$

and read τ off the interaction coefficient.

The parallel-trends assumption is the critical one: in the absence of treatment, A and B would have followed parallel paths. Tested informally by checking pre-treatment trends and formally by event-study designs. When the parallel-trends assumption fails (selection into treatment is correlated with pre-treatment trends), DiD is biased.

Classic example: Card-Krueger's 1994 minimum-wage study. New Jersey raised its minimum wage; eastern Pennsylvania did not. The change in fast-food employment in New Jersey minus the change in Pennsylvania was the DiD estimate. They found close to zero (in some specifications positive), challenging the classical labour-demand prediction (Lecture 23 returns to this).

Regression discontinuity

If treatment is assigned based on whether a "running variable" crosses a threshold (test score, income cut-off, vote share), units just above and just below the cutoff are very similar but receive different treatment. Comparing them gives a local causal estimate at the cutoff:

$$ \hat{\tau}_{RDD} = \lim_{x \downarrow c} \mathbb{E}[Y | X = x] - \lim_{x \uparrow c} \mathbb{E}[Y | X = x] $$

The identifying assumption is that all other determinants of Y are continuous in X at the cutoff. Manipulation of X around the cutoff (e.g. test-score retakes targeted at students just below) destroys identification. McCrary density tests check for such manipulation.

Classic examples: the Lee 2008 incumbency advantage paper (US House elections, vote-share threshold of 50%), the Israeli Maimonides-rule study of class size (Angrist-Lavy, class size capped at 40 students), drinking-age effects on mortality at age 21 (Carpenter-Dobkin).

Synthetic controls and event studies

Two recent extensions worth knowing.

  • Synthetic controls (Abadie-Diamond-Hainmueller): for a single treated unit, construct an artificial "synthetic" control as a weighted combination of donor units whose pre-treatment outcomes match the treated unit's. The post-treatment divergence is the treatment effect. Used in studies of regional policies and political shocks (Brexit GDP impact, German reunification).
  • Event studies: trace the path of an outcome around an event, lined up by event time. Pre-period coefficients should be zero (no anticipation); post-period coefficients reveal the dynamic effect. The modern DiD literature on staggered adoption (Goodman-Bacon, Sun-Abraham, Callaway-Sant'Anna) shows that naive two-way fixed-effects regressions with staggered treatment can be badly biased and proposes corrections.

Picking the right strategy

The matchmaking question is, "what is the source of variation in the treatment, and does it look as-if-random conditional on what we can control?". RCTs answer yes by design. IV outsources the as-if-randomness to the instrument. FE relies on within-unit variation. DiD relies on cross-group differences in timing of treatment. RDD relies on local near-randomness at a threshold. The right choice depends on what variation is actually present in the data.

Synthesis and what's next
