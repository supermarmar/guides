Keynesian macroeconomics: the short run

When prices and wages are sticky, the economy can sit below its potential output for years. Aggregate demand drives short-run output; monetary and fiscal policy can change demand. The framework is IS-LM in the goods-and-money plane, AD-AS in the price-and-output plane, and the Phillips curve linking inflation to unemployment.

Lecture 11 of 25 Reading time, 45 minutes Part II: macroeconomics
Step 1

The 30-second version

In the short run, prices and wages are sticky. Firms meet demand at the prevailing price by adjusting quantity, not by raising price. So aggregate demand temporarily determines output. The IS curve traces combinations of the interest rate and output that clear the goods market; the LM curve traces combinations that clear the money market. Their intersection is the short-run equilibrium. Shifts in fiscal policy move the IS curve; shifts in monetary policy move the LM curve.

Translating from the (r, Y) plane to the (P, Y) plane gives aggregate demand and aggregate supply. Short-run AS is upward-sloping (output rises with price because nominal wages adjust slowly); long-run AS is vertical at Y* (the classical full-employment output from Lecture 10). The Phillips curve links unemployment to inflation: when output is above potential, inflation rises; when below, it falls. Expectations augment the relationship: in the long run, the curve is vertical at the NAIRU.

Feynman test Prices and wages do not change every Tuesday. Menus get reprinted twice a year; wages get reset annually; mortgages and rents lock in for years. So in any given quarter, the relevant variable is not price but quantity. If demand falls, firms do not cut prices; they cut shifts. If demand rises, they do not raise prices; they hire. That is why short-run macro is a story about demand and not about supply. Eventually prices adjust, and you are back to Lecture 10. But "eventually" can be measured in years, and in the meantime there is a job for the central bank and the Treasury.
What's beyond the syllabus The IS-LM machinery is a 1937 Hicks reformulation of Keynes. Modern macro (DSGE, New Keynesian) replaces it with three equations (a forward-looking IS, a Phillips curve, a Taylor rule for monetary policy), but the intuition is identical. This lecture sticks with IS-LM and AD-AS for clarity and adds a brief Phillips-curve / expectations discussion that bridges to the modern view.
Step 2

Why this matters

Every recession, fiscal stimulus, monetary loosening, or central-bank rate decision is debated in the language of this lecture. "The output gap is negative", "the multiplier is two", "we are at the zero lower bound", "the Phillips curve has flattened" all come from this framework. Without it you cannot follow any FOMC statement, MPC minutes, or Treasury budget commentary.

For banking, IS-LM is the macro environment in which interest-rate risk, liquidity risk, and credit-risk stress scenarios live. Macro stress tests are essentially "shift the IS curve down and see what happens to the bank's balance sheet".

Step 3

The mental model

The IS-LM diagram lives in the (r, Y) plane: interest rate on the vertical axis, output on the horizontal.

Step 4

The detail

The Keynesian cross and the multiplier

Start in a closed economy with no government. Planned aggregate expenditure is E = C(Y − T) + I + G. Consumption is linear: C = c0 + c(Y − T), where c is the marginal propensity to consume (MPC), 0 < c < 1. Equilibrium sets E = Y:

$$ Y = c_0 + c(Y - T) + I + G $$

$$ Y^* = \frac{1}{1 - c} \big( c_0 - cT + I + G \big) $$

The factor 1/(1−c) is the Keynesian multiplier. A 1 pound increase in government spending raises Y by 1/(1−c) pounds, more than one-for-one. The intuition: the initial spending becomes someone's income, which they partly spend, which becomes someone else's income, and so on. The geometric series sums to 1/(1−c). With MPC = 0.6 the multiplier is 2.5.

Real-world multipliers are smaller. In an open economy, part of extra spending leaks to imports. With distortionary taxes, part flows to the public purse. With rising interest rates, part is crowded out via investment. Modern estimates put fiscal multipliers between 0.5 and 1.5, depending on the state of the economy, the kind of spending, and the monetary-policy regime.

Deriving the IS curve

Now make investment depend on the interest rate: I = I(r), with I'(r) < 0. The Keynesian-cross equilibrium becomes a function of r: higher r reduces I, which reduces planned spending, which reduces Y. Plotting (Y, r) pairs at which the goods market clears gives the downward-sloping IS curve. Algebraically:

$$ Y = \frac{1}{1 - c} \big( c_0 - cT + I(r) + G \big) $$

Shifts in G, T, c0, or any exogenous component of investment shift the IS curve. The slope depends on the interest-sensitivity of investment.

Deriving the LM curve

The money market consists of money supply (set by the central bank, M/P) and money demand. Money demand has a transactions motive (rising in Y, since more output means more transactions) and a speculative motive (falling in r, since higher r raises the opportunity cost of holding cash):

$$ \frac{M}{P} = L(Y, r), \quad L_Y > 0, \quad L_r < 0 $$

For given M/P, higher Y shifts money demand up; the equilibrium r must rise to clear the market. The locus of (Y, r) pairs that clears the money market is the upward-sloping LM curve. Shifts in M, P, or autonomous money demand shift LM.

IS-LM in motion

The short-run equilibrium is the intersection. Comparative statics:

  • Expansionary fiscal policy (G up, T down): IS shifts right. Output and interest rate both rise. Some of the demand expansion is "crowded out" by the rise in r reducing investment.
  • Expansionary monetary policy (M up): LM shifts right (down). Output rises and interest rate falls. Investment is "crowded in".
  • The liquidity trap: at the zero lower bound, LM is flat. Monetary expansion is ineffective (more money is just hoarded), and fiscal policy is unambiguously expansionary because crowding-out is absent. This is the relevant regime for major economies for most of 2009-2022.

From IS-LM to AD

Each price level P implies a different LM curve (because money supply in real terms is M/P), which implies a different IS-LM intersection (Y, r). Plotting (Y, P) gives the aggregate demand curve, downward-sloping for three reasons: the Keynes effect (P falls → M/P rises → r falls → I rises → Y rises), the Pigou effect (P falls → real wealth rises → C rises), and the open-economy effect (P falls → real exchange rate depreciates → net exports rise).

Short-run and long-run aggregate supply

Short-run AS slopes up: with sticky nominal wages, a higher P raises the labour-demand schedule relative to the labour-supply schedule, producing more employment and more output. Long-run AS is vertical at Y* (Lecture 10): once wages and prices have adjusted, output is determined by supply factors only.

AD shifts (from fiscal, monetary, or external shocks) change Y and P in the short run. As nominal wages catch up, short-run AS shifts back, and the economy gradually converges to Y* at a new P. This is the formal expression of "in the long run, Keynes equals Lecture 10".

The Phillips curve and inflation expectations

Bill Phillips's 1958 paper plotted UK wage inflation against unemployment for 1861-1957 and found a stable inverse relationship. By the 1970s, the empirical relationship broke down: stagflation (high inflation alongside high unemployment) violated the simple curve. Friedman and Phelps argued that the long-run Phillips curve is vertical: at any unemployment rate other than the NAIRU, expected inflation eventually catches up and the trade-off vanishes.

The modern expectations-augmented Phillips curve is

$$ \pi_t = \pi_t^e - \beta (u_t - u^*) + \nu_t $$

where u* is the NAIRU and ν is a supply shock. In the short run, holding expectations fixed, there is a trade-off. In the long run, expectations adjust until u = u*. Anchored expectations are essential: if πe is firmly tied to the central bank's target, the short-run trade-off is robust; if expectations drift, even small demand pressures can dislodge them. This is the modern central-bank credo: keep expectations anchored, monetary policy buys you the short-run trade-off.

Open-economy IS-LM, briefly

With trade and capital flows, the IS curve becomes the IS* curve (depending on the real exchange rate) and the system gains a BP (balance of payments) curve. The Mundell-Fleming model is Lecture 12; the result there is that under fixed exchange rates monetary policy is ineffective and fiscal policy works at full power, while under floating exchange rates the reverse is true.
