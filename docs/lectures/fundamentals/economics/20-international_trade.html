International trade

Why do countries trade, what determines what they trade, and who wins and loses from it? The classical answer (Ricardo, Heckscher-Ohlin) is comparative advantage. The modern answer adds increasing returns, product variety, and firm-level heterogeneity. Either way, trade raises total surplus but creates losers as well as winners, which is why trade policy is politically contentious.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The classical theory of trade rests on comparative advantage (Ricardo, 1817): a country gains from specialising in the goods it produces relatively most efficiently and trading for the rest, even if it is absolutely worse at everything. Heckscher-Ohlin generalises this to multi-factor production: capital-abundant countries export capital-intensive goods, labour-abundant countries export labour-intensive goods.

Modern trade theory (Krugman, 1980) explains intra-industry trade and the gains from variety using monopolistic competition with increasing returns. Melitz (2003) introduces firm heterogeneity: trade reallocates production from less to more productive firms within an industry. The empirical "gravity equation" describes trade volumes as proportional to the product of GDPs divided by some power of distance. All of these models predict net gains from trade, but they also predict distributional losers, which is the Stolper-Samuelson result: trade in goods is equivalent to trade in the factors that produce them, so factor owners on the wrong side of comparative advantage lose.

Feynman test Suppose you are the world's best lawyer and also the world's best typist. Should you type your own briefs? No. The hour you spend typing is an hour you cannot spend lawyering, and lawyering pays better. You should hire a typist (whose typing is worse than yours) and use the freed time on legal work. Both of you end up better off. That is comparative advantage. The principle generalises from one person to one country: even if a country is worse at making everything, trade lets it specialise in what it loses least at.
What's beyond the syllabus Principles courses cover Ricardo and stop. This lecture also covers Krugman's new trade theory, Melitz's firm-heterogeneity revolution, the empirical gravity equation, and recent China-shock literature on the labour-market consequences of trade. It also addresses why politically rational trade policy diverges from welfare-optimal trade policy.
Step 2

Why this matters

Trade policy is the most internationally negotiated area of economic policy and arguably the area where economic theory has had the largest direct policy impact (the GATT-WTO system, the EU customs union, NAFTA, the post-Brexit UK trade reset). For credit risk, trade exposure drives sovereign-risk premia, country-specific economic scenarios, and supply-chain credit concentrations.

The China shock (2001-) has rebalanced global manufacturing and triggered the political backlash (Brexit, the Trump-era tariff wars) that has shaped trade policy since 2016. Understanding this requires the modern trade toolkit, not the Ricardian one.

Step 3

The mental model

Two countries, two goods, two factors (capital and labour). Each country has a different mix of factor endowments and a different technology. Free trade reallocates production according to comparative advantage. Goods flow one way; payment flows the other. Both countries consume more after trade than either could in autarky.

Step 4

The detail

Absolute and comparative advantage

Absolute advantage: a country produces a good with less labour than another. Comparative advantage: a country has a lower opportunity cost of producing a good. Ricardo's celebrated example: England and Portugal, cloth and wine. Portugal is absolutely better at both, but the opportunity cost of cloth in Portugal (the wine forgone) is higher than in England. Each country gains by specialising in the good of low opportunity cost and trading.

The gains from trade are unambiguous in aggregate: post-trade, the world produces more of both goods than it could in autarky. The world consumption possibilities expand. The distribution of gains is a separate question.

Heckscher-Ohlin

Generalise Ricardo to two factors (capital, labour) and assume the same technology across countries. The H-O theorem says: a country exports the good that uses its abundant factor intensively. The UK is capital- and skill-abundant; it exports financial services, pharmaceuticals, advanced engineering. Bangladesh is labour-abundant; it exports garments.

The Stolper-Samuelson theorem follows: trade raises the real return to the abundant factor and lowers the real return to the scarce factor. In the UK, free trade with China should raise the relative return to capital and skilled labour, and lower the relative return to unskilled labour. Empirically, the China-shock literature finds local labour markets exposed to Chinese import competition suffered substantial and persistent declines in employment, wages, and intergenerational mobility (Autor, Dorn, Hanson). The aggregate gains from trade are real; so are the distributional losses.

The new trade theory (Krugman)

Why do similar countries (USA and Germany, UK and France) trade similar goods (cars for cars, drugs for drugs)? Ricardo and H-O predict trade between dissimilar countries in dissimilar goods. The data show extensive intra-industry trade between similar countries.

Krugman's 1980 model: assume firms have increasing returns and differentiated products. In autarky, each country produces a limited number of varieties at limited scale; in trade, the world produces more varieties at greater scale. Consumers gain from variety; producers gain from scale. The result is two-way trade between similar countries in differentiated versions of the same product class.

The new trade theory also predicts home-market effects: larger countries are disproportionately the location of increasing-returns industries. This shapes the pattern of FDI and the geographic concentration of finance, tech, and pharma.

Melitz and firm heterogeneity

Within an industry, firms differ in productivity. Marc Melitz (2003) showed that opening to trade reallocates production: the most productive firms expand into exports; the least productive exit; medium-productivity firms shrink. Average industry productivity rises (a within-industry compositional effect), even when no firm individually becomes more productive. This is the modern micro-foundation for the aggregate gains from trade, and explains why exporters are disproportionately the high-productivity firms in any economy.

The gravity equation

Empirically, bilateral trade between countries i and j obeys

$$ X_{ij} \approx A \cdot \frac{Y_i^\alpha Y_j^\beta}{D_{ij}^\delta} $$

where Y is GDP and D is distance. The model fits remarkably well: it explains 70-80% of cross-country variation in bilateral trade. It has been derived from each of the main theoretical traditions (Ricardian, H-O, Krugman, Melitz), suggesting that gravity is a deep structural feature of trade rather than a particular theory's prediction. The implication for the UK: trade with the EU (close, large) was several times larger than trade with the Commonwealth (distant, smaller GDP), independent of preferential trade agreements.

Trade policy

  • Tariffs: taxes on imports. Raise the domestic price of the imported good, protect domestic producers, reduce consumer surplus, generate revenue, create deadweight loss. A small country's optimal tariff is zero (no terms-of-trade effect); a large country can in principle improve its terms of trade with a non-zero "optimum tariff", but this triggers retaliation and dynamic costs.
  • Quotas: quantity restrictions. Same effect on quantity as a tariff but the rents go to importers (or licence holders) instead of to government.
  • Subsidies: pay domestic producers to expand. Encourages production but distorts the cost calculus; WTO-illegal in most sectors.
  • Non-tariff barriers: standards, regulations, customs procedures. Often the binding constraint in modern trade (the Brexit experience).

The infant-industry argument for tariffs: a new industry with learning-by-doing potential needs temporary protection until it matures. Theoretically defensible, empirically rarely well-executed. The standard counter-argument: governments are bad at picking winners, and "temporary" tariffs become permanent.

Trade in services

Around 25% of UK exports are services (financial, business, insurance, transport). Services trade is less easily measured (no customs barrier) and faces different regulatory frictions (mutual recognition, professional qualifications, data flows). Brexit's largest economic cost has likely come through services-trade barriers, not goods tariffs.

Capital and labour mobility

The classical models assume immobile factors. Allowing capital mobility makes the world more like a single economy; H-O predictions about factor returns weaken because factors can move to where they are scarce. Allowing labour mobility does the same. The political resistance to migration is, in part, recognition by losers (the abundant labour in receiving countries) of the Stolper-Samuelson logic in reverse.

The global trading system

The post-1947 GATT and post-1995 WTO system institutionalised multilateral tariff reductions on the principle of non-discrimination (most-favoured-nation status). Average advanced-economy tariffs fell from 25% in 1947 to 3% by 2000. Since 2018 the trend has reversed: US-China tariff wars, Brexit-era frictions, and post-COVID supply-chain concerns have pushed average tariffs back up. The "geoeconomic" turn (CHIPS Act, Inflation Reduction Act, EU CBAM) signals a shift from efficiency to resilience in trade policy thinking.
