Econometrics I: identification and OLS

Statistics tells you whether two variables move together. Econometrics tells you whether one causes the other. The bridge is identification: the conditions under which a regression coefficient can be interpreted as a causal effect, not just a conditional mean. OLS is the workhorse estimator, but its interpretation rests on what you assume about the unobservables.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A linear regression y = x'β + ε estimated by ordinary least squares (OLS) recovers β consistently if and only if the error term is uncorrelated with the regressors: E[ε | x] = 0. This is the exogeneity assumption. When it fails, the OLS estimate is biased and inconsistent, and the regression coefficient is some weighted average of the causal effect and the bias term.

The three classical violations are: omitted variable bias (a relevant variable is correlated with both y and x and is left out), simultaneity (y and x are jointly determined), and measurement error in x. Diagnosing which one is at work and how to fix it is the entire content of applied econometrics. The fixes come in Lecture 17.

Feynman test A regression coefficient is whatever combination of x and the error term that, when you wiggle x, gives you a wiggle in y. If x moves only for reasons that are unrelated to the outcome, the wiggle in y tells you the causal effect. If x also moves for other reasons that themselves affect y, the wiggle confounds the causal effect with those other reasons. Identification is the assumption that lets you separate the two.
What's beyond the syllabus Standard treatments cover the Gauss-Markov theorem first. This lecture inverts that: it leads with identification (the question, what is the coefficient telling me?) and follows with estimation (how do I compute it?). This is the post-credibility-revolution approach, which puts causal interpretation ahead of statistical efficiency. Wooldridge is the textbook reference; Angrist and Pischke's Mostly Harmless Econometrics is the philosophy.
Step 2

Why this matters

Every empirical claim in economics rests on an identification argument, explicit or implicit. "Education raises earnings by X%": which thing identifies the causal effect? Random assignment to schooling? A natural experiment? A clever instrument? Or is it just correlation between schooling and unobserved ability? The answer determines whether the X is a number you can act on or a number you should distrust.

For credit-risk modelling, identification is the unspoken layer underneath every model. PD modelling using past behaviour assumes that the borrower's selection into past stress is exogenous to current default; LGD modelling using realised recoveries assumes that workout strategy is exogenous to the assets recovered. Most model performance issues at boundary cases trace back to identification failure.

Step 3

The mental model

Two variables, X (treatment) and Y (outcome). The causal effect is the change in Y that would follow from a counterfactual change in X, with everything else held fixed. The correlation is the joint movement of X and Y in the data, with everything else letting itself adjust. The two coincide only if X moves independently of all the "everything else".

In a randomised trial, X is assigned by a coin flip, so it must be independent of the unobservables. In observational data, this is almost never true. Identification is the art of finding situations where X (or a piece of it) moves for reasons unrelated to Y.

Step 4

The detail

The classical linear regression model

Population model: yi = xi'β + εi, for i = 1, ..., n. The conditional expectation function is

$$ \mathbb{E}[y_i | x_i] = x_i' \beta \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_i | x_i] = 0 $$

The OLS estimator minimises the sum of squared residuals:

$$ \hat{\beta} = \arg\min_{\beta} \sum_i (y_i - x_i' \beta)^2 = (X'X)^{-1} X'y $$

Under the Gauss-Markov assumptions (linearity, no perfect collinearity, exogeneity, homoskedasticity, no autocorrelation), β-hat is the best (minimum variance) linear unbiased estimator (BLUE). Under the additional assumption of normal errors, it is the maximum likelihood estimator and the basis for exact-finite-sample t-tests and F-tests.

Asymptotically, under exogeneity and standard moment conditions, β-hat is consistent and asymptotically normal, regardless of the error distribution:

$$ \sqrt{n} (\hat{\beta} - \beta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}\left(0, \mathbb{E}[x_i x_i']^{-1} \mathbb{E}[x_i x_i' \varepsilon_i^2] \mathbb{E}[x_i x_i']^{-1}\right) $$

The middle term is what changes when errors are heteroskedastic; the White (heteroskedasticity-consistent or HC) standard errors estimate it without imposing homoskedasticity.

What can go wrong: violations of exogeneity

Exogeneity fails when E[ε | x] ≠ 0. Three canonical causes:

  1. Omitted variable bias (OVB). The true model is y = x'β + z γ + u, with z correlated with x. Omitting z lumps z γ into the error, and the OLS estimate of β is biased by

$$ \text{bias} = \gamma \cdot \frac{\text{Cov}(x, z)}{\text{Var}(x)} $$

The bias has the sign of the product of (i) z's effect on y and (ii) z's correlation with x. The classic example: regressing wages on schooling without ability. Ability raises wages (γ > 0) and is correlated with schooling (cov > 0). Hence the OLS schooling coefficient overstates the causal return to schooling.

  1. Simultaneity (reverse causation). X affects Y but Y also affects X. In a market, quantity and price are jointly determined: regressing quantity on price recovers neither demand nor supply. The classic example: regressing crime rates on police force size: more police reduce crime, but high crime hires more police.
  2. Measurement error in regressors. If x is measured with classical mean-zero noise (x* = x + ν), the OLS estimate is biased toward zero by an "attenuation factor":

$$ \hat{\beta} \xrightarrow{p} \beta \cdot \frac{\text{Var}(x)}{\text{Var}(x) + \text{Var}(\nu)} $$

Education econometrics is plagued by this: years of schooling are recorded approximately, and the resulting attenuation is non-negligible. The fix is to use a noisy instrument (Lecture 17).

Standard errors and inference

Even if β-hat is unbiased, the standard error tells you how confident to be. Three issues to flag:

  • Heteroskedasticity: error variance varies with x. Default fix: HC standard errors (White, MacKinnon-White, HC3).
  • Clustering: observations within natural groups (firms, classrooms, postcodes) have correlated errors. Default fix: cluster-robust standard errors at the appropriate level.
  • Serial correlation: in time series, residuals are correlated across time. Default fix: Newey-West HAC standard errors.

Using OLS standard errors when any of these is present produces severely under-stated uncertainty. The combination of "small estimated standard errors and dubious identification" is the calling card of bad applied work.

The Frisch-Waugh-Lovell theorem

A useful tool for understanding multivariate regression: the coefficient on x1 in the multivariate regression of y on (x1, x2) is identical to the coefficient in the bivariate regression of the residual of y-on-x2 on the residual of x1-on-x2. In other words, multivariate regression "partials out" the other variables before estimating the relationship of interest. This is the analytical justification for the matching and "control for everything" approaches in applied work.

Goodness of fit and what it does not tell you

R2 is the fraction of variance in y explained by the regression. High R2 is consistent with low identification (a good correlational model can be a bad causal model) and with very high identification (a randomised trial typically has low R2 and clean causal interpretation). R2 tells you nothing about whether the regression has identified the causal effect. This is a recurring trap for newcomers.

Diagnostic tests

  • Ramsey RESET: tests for omitted non-linearities by adding powers of fitted values.
  • Breusch-Pagan / White: tests for heteroskedasticity.
  • Durbin-Watson, Breusch-Godfrey: tests for serial correlation.
  • Hausman: tests whether OLS is consistent against an instrumental-variables alternative.

These tests are useful but cannot prove identification. They can only flag certain failure modes. A regression that passes every diagnostic can still be hopelessly biased by an unmeasured confounder.

Logit, probit, and other extensions

OLS estimates linear conditional expectations. For binary outcomes (default / no default, employed / unemployed), the conditional expectation is bounded in [0, 1], and OLS is replaced by logit or probit models that estimate a latent index passed through a CDF. The identification logic is identical: it is the same exogeneity assumption applied to a different functional form. Credit-risk PD models are logits at heart; everything else is feature engineering.

The credibility revolution

Mid-1990s onward, applied econometrics shifted away from "estimate elaborate structural models with assumed exogeneity" toward "find a quasi-random source of variation in X and exploit it". The flagship was David Card's 1995 paper using compulsory schooling laws as an instrument for years of education. The intellectual leaders were Joshua Angrist, Guido Imbens, and David Card, who shared the 2021 Nobel "for their methodological contributions to the analysis of causal relationships". The new generation of identification strategies are the subject of Lecture 17.

Synthesis and what's next

You now know what OLS does, when its coefficients can be read causally, and which classical violations destroy that interpretation. Lecture 17 covers the modern toolkit for restoring causal interpretation when OLS fails: instrumental variables, panel data fixed effects, difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity.
