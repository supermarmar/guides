Multiple decrements

When a life can leave a population for more than one reason, the survival model splits into competing forces of decrement; the trap is that the rate you observe is not the rate that would prevail if the other causes were not there.

Lecture 2 of 8 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: UP / Contingencies (standard CM1 syllabus)
Step 1

The 30-second version

A multiple-decrement model is the multi-state special case with one starting state (active, alive, in force) and several absorbing states (death by cause $A$, death by cause $B$, withdrawal, retirement). Each cause has its own intensity $\mu_x^{(d)}$, and the rate of leaving the starting state is the sum. The data you observe in any real population are dependent rates of decrement, $\,(aq)_x^{(d)}$: the probability of leaving by cause $d$ in the presence of the other causes. The rate you would observe if cause $d$ were the only one operating is the independent rate, $q_x^{*(d)}$. The two are not the same and one of the most common pricing errors in actuarial work is treating them as if they were.

Multiple-decrement modelling is the engine inside pension valuations, withdrawal-loaded life products, accidental-death riders, and any contract whose cashflows distinguish between causes of leaving. The next lecture extends the framework to two lives at once; the lecture on pension funds uses the multi-decrement service table directly.

Feynman test Imagine 10,000 employees aged 30 in a pension scheme. Over the next year, 50 leave the company, 5 die, and 0 retire (too young). The probabilities you observe are $\,(aq)_{30}^{(w)} = 0.005$ for withdrawal and $\,(aq)_{30}^{(d)} = 0.0005$ for death. These are dependent rates: deaths can only happen to people who did not withdraw, and vice versa. If you wanted to know the probability that a 30-year-old would have died in a year if nobody could withdraw, you would need to compute the independent rate $q_{30}^{*(d)}$ — slightly higher than the dependent one, because the withdrawal pool can no longer subtract people from the at-risk population mid-year.
Step 2

Why this matters

Three big classes of actuarial work depend on multi-decrement bookkeeping. Pension fund valuations distinguish between active members who retire, who withdraw, who die in service, and who become disabled; the cost of the scheme depends on which of these happens to each member. Life insurance products with separate riders (accidental death, terminal illness, premium waiver) price each cause separately, then aggregate. Withdrawal-sensitive products like single-premium investment bonds and unit-linked policies cannot be priced without an explicit lapse decrement, because surrender values are paid on a different basis from death claims.

Outside actuarial work the same machinery appears under different names. In epidemiology it is the competing-risks framework: a patient can die from the disease, die from a different cause, or be censored, and the cumulative-incidence function for each cause is the multi-decrement analogue of $\,(aq)_x^{(d)}$. In credit modelling, a bond can default, be called, be put back, or mature; pricing the bond requires a multi-decrement model on the issuer state. In reliability engineering, a component can fail by wear, by overload, by chemical attack; each failure mode is a decrement.

The single critical concept across all four domains is that removing a cause is not free. If you build a model under the assumption that withdrawal has been eliminated, then naively re-fit the mortality rate from a population where withdrawal exists, you will underestimate mortality. The grandmaster's section returns to this in detail.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture a single active state at the top with one arrow flowing out per cause of decrement. Each arrow lands in its own absorbing state. The total rate of leaving the active state is the sum of all the per-cause rates.

The structural identity to internalise: the active population follows a single survival curve at the total rate $\mu_x^{(T)}$. That curve is

$$ {}_t p_x^{(T)} \;=\; \exp\!\left(-\int_0^t \mu_{x+s}^{(T)}\,ds\right) \;=\; \exp\!\left(-\int_0^t \sum_d \mu_{x+s}^{(d)}\,ds\right). $$

The probability of leaving for cause $d$ in particular is the integral of the cause-specific density against this survival curve, derived in the next section.

Step 4

The detail

Forces of decrement and the survival of the active population

Let the active population face $k$ causes of decrement, indexed $d = 1, \dots, k$. The force of decrement for cause $d$ is

$$ \mu_x^{(d)} \;=\; \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{1}{h}\, P(\text{leave by cause } d \text{ in } (x, x+h] \mid \text{active at } x). $$

The total force of decrement is the sum $\mu_x^{(T)} = \sum_d \mu_x^{(d)}$. The probability of remaining active for $t$ years is the standard exponential of negative integrated force, but using the total:

$$ {}_t p_x^{(T)} \;=\; \exp\!\left(-\int_0^t \mu_{x+s}^{(T)}\,ds\right). $$

This is the multi-state occupation probability ${}_t p_x^{\overline{00}}$ specialised to the multi-decrement diagram: there are no return transitions to the active state, so the occupation and the transition probabilities coincide.

Dependent decrement probabilities

$\,(aq)_x^{(d)}$
The dependent rate of decrement for cause $d$: the probability that an active life aged $x$ leaves by cause $d$ before age $x + 1$, in the presence of the other causes. Sometimes written $q_x^{(d)}$, but the prefix $(a)$ (for "all causes operating") is good practice to avoid the trap below.

Express the dependent rate as an integral. To exit by cause $d$ at time $s \in (0, 1]$ the life must (i) remain active for $s$ years and (ii) experience a transition by cause $d$ at the instant $s$:

$$ (aq)_x^{(d)} \;=\; \int_0^1 {}_s p_x^{(T)}\, \mu_{x+s}^{(d)}\, ds. $$

The total dependent rate is $\,(aq)_x^{(T)} = \sum_d (aq)_x^{(d)} = 1 - {}_1 p_x^{(T)}$, as it must be: every active life either stays active or leaves for some specific cause.

Independent decrement probabilities

$q_x^{*(d)}$
The independent rate of decrement for cause $d$: the probability that an active life leaves by cause $d$ before age $x + 1$, in a hypothetical world where no other causes are operating. $q_x^{*(d)} = 1 - \exp\!\left(-\int_0^1 \mu_{x+s}^{(d)}\,ds\right)$.

The independent rate is what you would observe if only cause $d$ were possible. It is the rate the actuary needs to know when, for example, taking a published mortality table (which assumes "deaths from any cause" as the only decrement) and inserting it into a pension model that also has withdrawal and retirement. The mortality rate from the table is a $q^{*(d)}$ in that context; it is not directly the rate of dying observed in the pension population.

Watch out The single most common multi-decrement error is to plug a published independent rate $q_x^{*(d)}$ into a model as if it were $(aq)_x^{(d)}$, or vice versa. The two are equal only in the limit where all other forces are zero. Whenever you reach for a decrement rate from an external source (a national life table, an industry experience study, a published lapse table), pause and ask: was this rate observed in a single-decrement population or in a multi-decrement population? If single, you have $q^{*(d)}$ and need to combine; if multi, you have $(aq)^{(d)}$ and must not combine again with the others as if they were independent.

The relationship between dependent and independent rates

Under the standard assumption of constant forces over the year of age (so $\mu_{x+s}^{(d)} = \mu_x^{(d)}$ for $0 \le s \le 1$), both kinds of rate are available in closed form. The independent rate is $q_x^{*(d)} = 1 - e^{-\mu_x^{(d)}}$. The dependent rate factorises:

$$ (aq)_x^{(d)} \;=\; \frac{\mu_x^{(d)}}{\mu_x^{(T)}}\left(1 - e^{-\mu_x^{(T)}}\right) \;=\; \frac{\mu_x^{(d)}}{\mu_x^{(T)}}\,(aq)_x^{(T)}. $$

The factor $\mu_x^{(d)}/\mu_x^{(T)}$ is the proportion of exits that are of type $d$; the factor $(aq)_x^{(T)}$ is the total probability of an exit. The two combine to give the probability of an exit of type $d$ specifically.

Inverting: given a set of independent rates $\{q_x^{*(d)}\}_{d=1}^k$, recover the forces by $\mu_x^{(d)} = -\ln(1 - q_x^{*(d)})$ and combine. Under the constant-force assumption this lets you take published single-decrement tables (one per cause), invert each to a force, sum the forces, and reconstruct the multi-decrement table.

Constructing a multi-decrement table

A multi-decrement table tabulates, at each integer age, the active population $(al)_x$ and the per-cause exits $(ad)_x^{(d)}$. The recursion is

$$ (al)_{x+1} \;=\; (al)_x \;-\; \sum_d (ad)_x^{(d)}, \qquad (ad)_x^{(d)} \;=\; (al)_x \cdot (aq)_x^{(d)}. $$

The table is built by starting from a chosen radix $(al)_0$ (typically a round number such as 100,000), computing each $(ad)_x^{(d)}$ from the dependent rates, and rolling forward. Pension fund service tables are exactly this kind of table, with causes typically labelled $w$ (withdrawal), $i$ (ill-health retirement), $r$ (normal retirement), $d$ (death in service).

A worked example: pension service table

Consider an employee aged 30 with constant annual independent rates $q_{30}^{*(d)} = 0.001$ (death), $q_{30}^{*(w)} = 0.060$ (withdrawal), $q_{30}^{*(r)} = 0.000$ (retirement, not yet possible). Compute the dependent rates and one year of the service table starting from $(al)_{30} = 100{,}000$.

Step 1: forces. $\mu_{30}^{(d)} = -\ln(1 - 0.001) = 0.001000$, $\mu_{30}^{(w)} = -\ln(1 - 0.060) = 0.061875$, $\mu_{30}^{(r)} = 0$. Total $\mu_{30}^{(T)} = 0.062875$.

Step 2: total dependent rate. $(aq)_{30}^{(T)} = 1 - e^{-0.062875} = 0.060958$.

Step 3: per-cause dependent rates from the factorisation $(aq)_{30}^{(d)} = (\mu^{(d)}/\mu^{(T)}) \cdot (aq)^{(T)}$:

Two observations. First, the dependent death rate 0.000970 is slightly less than the independent rate 0.001000. Reason: some of the people who would have died under the death-only model have withdrawn first, so the at-risk population for death is smaller. Second, the dependent withdrawal rate 0.059988 is also slightly less than its independent 0.060. The total $(aq)^{(T)}$ is less than the simple sum of $q^{*(d)}$ because no life can leave twice; the combination is multiplicative in the survival, not additive in the rate.

The active population at age 31 is therefore $(al)_{31} = 100{,}000 - 97 - 5{,}999 - 0 = 93{,}904$.

Practitioner insight Pension scheme valuations are dominated by withdrawal rates at young ages and retirement rates at older ages. The "independent" mortality assumption you plug into a model is taken from a national life table, which already reflects the population's other ways of dying but not its rate of withdrawing from your scheme. Subtracting your withdrawal force from the table mortality, naively, gives the wrong answer because the table is already "post-withdrawal" from the perspective of the general population's labour-market dropouts. Untangling this in practice usually means working in forces rather than rates: keep $\mu^{(d)}$ as the unobserved quantity to estimate and add or subtract whatever competing forces are appropriate for the modelled population.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The competing-causes assumption

The multi-decrement framework assumes the causes are competing but independent in force: each force $\mu^{(d)}$ is a property of the individual, unaffected by the existence or magnitude of the other forces. This is rarely literally true. A pension scheme's withdrawal rate often falls during a recession because workers fear unemployment; the death rate is unaffected, but their ratio shifts. A scheme's retirement rate spikes at age 65 because of the normal retirement age clause in the rules; the death rate doesn't. The two are not statistically independent in the realised data. The independence assumption is a modelling independence, not an empirical one, and the gap between them is the territory of more sophisticated cause-specific hazard models.

Fractional ages

The constant-force assumption used in the worked example is one of three standard choices for splitting a one-year decrement rate. The other two, the analogues of UDD and CMR from single-life modelling, are: uniform distribution of decrements (UDD) within the year, and the constant rate within the year. Constant force is usually the cleanest because it makes the factorisation $(aq)^{(d)} = (\mu^{(d)}/\mu^{(T)}) \cdot (aq)^{(T)}$ exact; UDD requires care because it produces a different relationship.

Multiple decrement to multiple state and back

A multi-decrement model is a special case of a multi-state model (one initial state, $k$ absorbing states, no recovery). Anything you can compute in the multi-decrement framework can be computed in the multi-state framework by writing out the same diagram and using the multi-state machinery from lecture 1. Going the other way is harder: a generic multi-state model with recovery cannot be collapsed into a multi-decrement model because the active population is not absorbed when it leaves the initial state. So multi-decrement is the right framework for monotone (non-recovering) products, and multi-state is required for products with recovery transitions like income protection.

Forces are the natural parameter

Estimation from data should always work on the forces $\mu^{(d)}$ directly, not on the rates $(aq)^{(d)}$ or $q^{*(d)}$. The forces have a clean maximum-likelihood interpretation: each observed sojourn time contributes the exponential survival under the total force, and each observed exit contributes the relevant single force. The rates are derived quantities. Treating them as primitives leads to inconsistent estimators when the exposure period is not exactly one year, when censoring is present, or when the data are pooled across ages.

Watch out Examiners love a question where they give you a published "mortality rate" and a "withdrawal rate" and ask for a probability of dying or withdrawing or both. The trap is that the published rates may be independent (from a national life table; from an industry lapse study) or dependent (from a pension scheme's experience analysis). Read the question carefully and convert appropriately. If you cannot tell from context, the safe assumption is that single-cause published tables give independent rates and population-experience tables give dependent rates.

Test yourself

Try each prompt in plain English first; only reach for a formula once you have stated the answer in words.

  1. Define $\mu_x^{(d)}$, $(aq)_x^{(d)}$, and $q_x^{*(d)}$. State the relationship between them under the constant-force assumption.
  2. Why is the dependent rate of decrement for cause $d$ always less than or equal to the independent rate? When are they equal?
  3. An active population faces two decrements with constant forces $\mu^{(1)} = 0.02$ and $\mu^{(2)} = 0.08$. Compute (a) $(aq)^{(T)}$, (b) $(aq)^{(1)}$, (c) $(aq)^{(2)}$, (d) $q^{*(1)}$ and $q^{*(2)}$.
  4. Starting from a multi-decrement table radix $(al)_{30} = 50{,}000$ with dependent rates $(aq)^{(d)} = 0.001$, $(aq)^{(w)} = 0.080$, $(aq)^{(r)} = 0.000$, compute $(al)_{31}$ and the expected exits by each cause.
  5. A pension scheme reports its annual death rate as 0.0008 and its withdrawal rate as 0.075. Are these dependent or independent rates? What additional information would let you decide unambiguously?
  6. Show that under constant forces $(aq)_x^{(d)} = \frac{\mu_x^{(d)}}{\mu_x^{(T)}}(aq)_x^{(T)}$. Where does the assumption enter the derivation?
  7. Suppose your published independent mortality $q^{*(d)} = 0.005$ is taken from a national life table, and your pension scheme has a withdrawal rate $(aq)^{(w)} = 0.10$. Estimate the dependent death rate in the scheme.
  8. Explain in two sentences why multi-decrement bookkeeping is essential for pension valuations but only mildly useful for term-assurance pricing.
