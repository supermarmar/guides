Pension funds

A defined-benefit pension is a single contract that bundles every piece of contingency machinery in the course: a service table, salary projection, an annuity at retirement, and reversionary benefits to dependants — each priced against decrement-weighted survival.

Lecture 5 of 8 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: UP / Contingencies (standard CM1 syllabus)
Step 1

The 30-second version

A defined-benefit (DB) pension promises a retirement income equal to (years of service) × (accrual rate) × (pensionable salary). The accrual rate is typically 1/60th or 1/80th per year of service; the pensionable salary is the final salary, the final-average, or a career-average revalued earnings. The actuarial valuation problem is to put a present value on a stream of contingent cashflows whose triggers depend on a four-decrement service table (active, withdrawn, retired, died-in-service), whose magnitudes depend on a salary scale, and whose payment patterns are post-retirement annuities, possibly with dependant reversionary payments. Every piece needed has been built in the preceding four lectures.

The two valuation outputs the actuary produces are the past-service liability (EPV of benefits already earned by service to date) and the future-service contribution rate (level percentage of pensionable salary the sponsor must pay to fund the continuing accrual). Their sum gives the projected liability at any horizon. Defined-contribution (DC) pensions are simpler: the benefit is whatever pot the contributions buy, so the sponsor risk is zero and the valuation is just accumulation, not discounted EPV.

Feynman test A 45-year-old earning 50,000 with 15 years of service in a 1/60th final-salary scheme has already accrued a pension of $15/60 \times 50{,}000 = 12{,}500$ per year, payable from age 65 for life. Picture three things: a probability she stays in the scheme until 65 (decrement-weighted survival), an inflation factor for her salary between now and retirement (salary scale), and an annuity factor that converts the retirement-age cashflow into a present value (post-retirement annuity). Multiply all three by the 12,500 and you have her past-service liability. Repeat for her future 20 years of service and you have the future-service piece.
Step 2

Why this matters

Defined-benefit pension liabilities are among the largest single line items on corporate balance sheets, and on national accounts they are often larger than national debt. UK plc has roughly £1.5 trillion of DB pension liabilities at end-2024. South African employer pension funds total roughly R3 trillion. Valuation gets these numbers right or wrong by tens of percent depending on assumptions about salary growth, retirement rates, and the discount rate. The 2022 UK gilt crisis and the resulting LDI margin calls were a direct consequence of pension valuation assumptions and their sensitivity to long-dated rates.

Beyond the valuation itself, pension funds are the largest single market for long-dated annuities, index-linked gilts, and equity-release mortgages. The asset-side composition of pension funds in turn shapes financial market liquidity at long maturities — when funds derisk by buying gilts the term premium falls, when they re-risk by buying equities the term premium rises. Understanding the pension actuarial framework is therefore necessary for understanding the financial system at long horizons.

The F107 syllabus treats pensions briefly. The deeper coverage in the actuarial exams (CM1, SP6) is built on exactly this machinery: a multi-decrement service table plus a salary scale plus a post-retirement annuity. Mastering the bookkeeping is the entry ticket to either exam track.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture a member's lifetime as a two-phase journey. The service phase runs from joining the scheme to leaving it for any reason (withdrawal, ill-health, normal retirement, death). The post-service phase runs from the date of leaving to death, with the cashflow depending on which exit happened.

The three quantitative ingredients are: (i) the service-table dependent decrement rates $(aq)_x^{(w)}, (aq)_x^{(i)}, (aq)_x^{(r)}, (aq)_x^{(d)}$ from lecture 2; (ii) a salary scale $s_x$ giving the projected pensionable salary at age $x$ relative to the current salary; (iii) post-retirement annuity factors from the standard pensioner mortality tables. Combine.

Step 4

The detail

Salary scales

The salary scale $s_x$ tabulates the salary at age $x$ relative to a reference age (often the joining age). A member earning $S$ at age $x$ today is projected to earn $S \cdot s_{x+t}/s_x$ at future age $x+t$, on the assumption that salary growth follows the table. The scale incorporates both age-related progression (younger workers' salaries grow faster) and any across-the-board inflation.

Two pension-specific salary variants matter. Final pensionable salary is the salary at the date of retirement. Final-average pensionable salary is the average over (typically) the last three years. Career-average revalued earnings (CARE) is the average of each year's salary revalued to retirement-date money by an index. UK public-sector schemes moved from final-salary to CARE in 2015 to control the cost; the actuarial valuation difference between the two can exceed 20% of the past-service liability.

The standard DB benefit formula

The annual pension at retirement is

$$ P \;=\; \frac{n}{60} \cdot S_{\text{final}} $$

where $n$ is years of service (often capped at 40) and $S_{\text{final}}$ is the final pensionable salary. The accrual rate 1/60 is conventional; UK private-sector schemes typically use 1/60 or 1/80, public-sector use 1/49 (with a CARE base), South African schemes use 1/55 or 2%.

The past-service liability

Consider a member currently aged $x$ with $n$ years of accrued service, salary $S$, and $r - x$ years to normal retirement age $r$. Her past-service pension at the future retirement date is

$$ P^{\text{past}} \;=\; \frac{n}{60} \cdot S \cdot \frac{s_r}{s_x}, $$

so the EPV of this future cashflow at the valuation date is

$$ V^{\text{past}} \;=\; \frac{n S}{60} \cdot \frac{s_r}{s_x} \cdot v^{r-x} \cdot {}_{r-x} p_x^{(r)} \cdot \ddot{a}_r, $$

where ${}_{r-x} p_x^{(r)}$ is the probability she remains active and retires normally at $r$ (taken from the service table) and $\ddot{a}_r$ is the post-retirement annuity factor at retirement age (taken from the pensioner mortality table). The decomposition is: probability times salary projection times discount times annuity factor.

Past-service liabilities for the other exit decrements (withdrawal with a deferred pension, ill-health retirement, death-in-service spouse benefit) are added similarly. Each is a separate integral against the corresponding decrement rate.

The future-service contribution rate

The future-service piece is the EPV of pension accruals between today and retirement. For each future year of service the member adds 1/60 of final salary, contingent on her being active at the end of that year. Summed,

$$ V^{\text{future}} \;=\; \frac{S}{60} \cdot \frac{s_r}{s_x} \cdot v^{r-x}\cdot {}_{r-x} p_x^{(r)}\cdot \ddot{a}_r \cdot \sum_{t=0}^{r-x-1}\frac{1}{\text{probability adjusted}}, $$

where the sum collects the accruals from each future year, weighted by the probability of accruing them. The clean way to present this is the standard contribution rate, the level percentage of pensionable salary the sponsor must pay to exactly fund the future accruals:

$$ \pi \;=\; \frac{V^{\text{future}}}{V^{\text{salary}}} \;=\; \frac{\text{EPV of future accruals}}{\text{EPV of future pensionable salary}}. $$

The denominator, the EPV of all future pensionable salary while active, is the salary-weighted analogue of the active-member service annuity. Both are commutation functions in classical pension notation (the $\,^s\!\!N_x$ and $\,^s\!\!D_x$ functions).

Watch out The "standard contribution rate" is the level percentage that funds future accruals on the chosen valuation basis. It is not the same as the funded contribution rate, which adjusts for the existing surplus or deficit. UK trustees often distinguish between the technical provisions contribution rate and the schedule of contributions rate; the latter includes a deficit-repair payment. Exam questions usually ask for the standard rate; practitioner valuations distinguish.

A worked example: simple final-salary valuation

Active member aged 45, currently earning 50,000, 15 years of accrued service in a 1/60 scheme. Normal retirement age 65. Salary scale $s_{65}/s_{45} = 2.50$ (4% per annum compound for 20 years). Probability of remaining active to retirement ${}_{20} p_{45}^{(r)} = 0.50$ (50% withdrawal / death / ill-health attrition over 20 years). Post-retirement annuity factor $\ddot{a}_{65} = 13.5$. Discount rate 4%.

The standard contribution rate for the future-service piece, expressed as a fraction of current salary, is approximately $128{,}340 / V^{\text{salary}}$. With $V^{\text{salary}} \approx 50{,}000 \cdot 12.5 = 625{,}000$ (an illustrative EPV of future salary), the standard contribution rate is roughly $128{,}340 / 625{,}000 = 20.5\%$. This is broadly consistent with funded UK final-salary scheme contribution rates, which historically ran in the 15-25% range.

Sensitivity of the liability to assumptions

Practitioner insight The two assumptions that swamp every other lever in DB valuation are the discount rate and the long-term salary growth. Mortality matters at the 5% level; investment returns matter at the 1-2% level on a year-by-year basis; the discount rate and salary growth move the answer by tens of percent. UK trustees set the discount rate as gilt yield plus a credit spread, then negotiate the spread; the negotiation is the entire valuation. Schemes with stronger sponsor covenants get higher discount rates, smaller liabilities, smaller deficit-repair payments.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Defined contribution shifts every risk to the member

Under DC the sponsor pays a fixed contribution into the member's pot; the pension at retirement is whatever the pot buys. Mortality risk, investment risk, longevity risk, inflation risk are all transferred from the sponsor to the member. This is administratively much simpler (no actuarial valuation needed) but is regressive: members without financial sophistication tend to make suboptimal investment choices, end up with smaller pots, and face the longevity risk personally. The global shift from DB to DC since the 1990s is the single largest transfer of financial risk in modern economic history.

The funding basis vs the accounting basis

UK practice distinguishes three pension valuations of the same scheme. The funding valuation, on a trustee-agreed prudent basis, drives the sponsor's contribution rate. The accounting valuation, under IAS 19 / FRS 102 using AA-bond yields, drives the corporate balance sheet. The solvency valuation, using risk-free rates plus a credit-rating-implied spread, drives PPF levies and would drive any insurance buyout. The three valuations of the same scheme often differ by 30-50% of the liability.

The LDI episode of 2022

UK DB schemes had hedged their interest-rate exposure with long-dated gilts and swaps, often using leverage to free capital for return-seeking assets. When long gilts collapsed in price in September 2022 (yields rose 100bps in days), the leverage triggered margin calls, schemes had to liquidate assets into the falling market, the Bank of England intervened. The actuarial framework above explains why hedging mattered (long-dated salary-linked liabilities have huge duration) but does not by itself flag the leveraged-LDI implementation risk. Subsequent regulatory work has tightened collateral requirements.

South African pension specifics

South African retirement funds are a mix of DB (mostly public-sector and parastatal), DC, and hybrid. The Pension Funds Act 1956 and FSCA rules govern valuation. The relevant local mortality tables are PA(90), PA(92), and SLM2008 for pensioners. The discount rate is typically chosen by reference to the inflation-linked bond curve plus a margin, reflecting the predominance of CPI-linked benefits. Valuation actuaries in SA often report on multiple bases (gilts plus spread, real plus spread) following FSB practice.

Watch out The accrual rate quoted in scheme rules is sometimes the nominal accrual (1/60th of final salary per year) and sometimes the cash-balance accrual (a fixed amount in pounds per year of service). The two have completely different valuation profiles: nominal accrual is salary-linked and grows with salary scale, cash-balance accrual is independent of salary and grows only with the indexation rate. Read the scheme rules before plugging numbers.
Step 6

Test yourself

For each prompt, identify whether the question is about the service-table side, the salary side, or the post-retirement annuity side before reaching for a formula.

  1. Define the salary scale $s_x$ in words and explain how it differs from a wage-inflation index.
  2. Write down the past-service liability for a member aged $x$ with $n$ years of accrued service, salary $S$, scheme retirement age $r$, accrual rate $1/60$, and standard pensioner mortality. Identify each factor.
  3. Distinguish final-salary, final-average, and CARE pension formulas. State the typical accrual rate for each and the magnitude of the valuation difference.
  4. Derive the standard contribution rate $\pi$ as a ratio of EPVs. Why is the denominator the EPV of future pensionable salary rather than the EPV of future scheme membership?
  5. For the worked example, recompute the past-service liability assuming (a) discount rate falls to 3%, (b) salary growth rises to 5%. Verify the two sensitivities are roughly 20% and 25% respectively.
  6. A scheme has a 50% spouse's reversionary benefit on death after retirement. Add this to the EPV computation in the worked example, assuming the member's spouse is two years younger and standard joint-life mortality applies.
  7. What is the difference between the funding, accounting, and solvency valuation bases for a UK DB pension scheme? Why do they typically disagree?
  8. Explain in two sentences why the move from DB to DC schemes since the 1990s was driven by the corporate balance sheet rather than by employee preference.
