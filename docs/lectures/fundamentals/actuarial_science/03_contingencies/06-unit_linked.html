Unit-linked policies

Two parallel accounts run in parallel: the policyholder's unit fund holds the investment, the insurer's non-unit fund holds the cashflows of charges and expenses; pricing happens entirely in the non-unit fund.

Lecture 6 of 8 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: UP / Contingencies (standard CM1 syllabus), Chapter 26 unit-linked
Step 1

The 30-second version

A unit-linked policy is a hybrid investment-and-insurance contract that splits cashflows into two parallel funds. The unit fund holds the policyholder's investment in units of a notional asset pool; the value at any time is units times the unit price. The non-unit fund (also called the sterling fund) holds the insurer's own cashflows: the charges it deducts, the expenses it incurs, the difference between the death benefit paid and the unit-fund payout, and the reserves needed to cover negative future cashflows. The unit fund grows with investment returns and the policyholder gets it at maturity or surrender; the non-unit fund is the insurer's profit engine and is the subject of profit testing in lecture 7.

Three transactional mechanics drive how money flows between the two funds. The allocation rate $\alpha$ is the percentage of each premium that buys units (the rest stays in the non-unit fund as an immediate charge). The bid-offer spread is the difference between the unit price at which premiums buy units (offer price) and the price at which units are redeemed (bid price). The annual management charge (AMC) is a percentage of the unit fund deducted each year, accruing to the non-unit fund. Each of these three is a charge structure choice with implications for the policyholder and for the insurer's profit signature.

Feynman test Imagine paying a 1,000 annual premium into a unit-linked savings policy. Of the 1,000, 950 (allocation rate 95%) goes into your unit fund; the remaining 50 is the insurer's immediate income. Each year, the fund value (initially 950) earns market returns of, say, 6%; the insurer then takes 1% of the year-end fund value as an annual management charge. The policyholder sees the unit fund growing; the insurer sees a 50 upfront fee plus 1% of the running fund balance as ongoing income. Premium pays in, charges flow out, units accumulate. Reserve so the cashflows match.
Step 2

Why this matters

Unit-linked products are the dominant retail savings and protection vehicle in most modern life-insurance markets. They displaced with-profits products in the UK during the 1990s and 2000s, and they are the underlying structure of virtually every modern pension wrapper, retirement annuity, and investment bond. The transparent fee structure is the main selling point versus with-profits: the policyholder can see exactly what they paid, what they have, and what charges have been deducted.

The valuation framework is also the template for every other modern long-term savings product. Variable annuities, equity-indexed annuities, with-profits in modern style, and most personal-pension contracts inherit the unit-fund / non-unit-fund split. Profit testing (lecture 7) and reserve setting (lecture 8) both operate on the non-unit fund's projected cashflows.

For F107 readers, unit-linked is also the natural bridge to bank-style asset-liability management. The unit fund is, from the insurer's perspective, a balance-sheet item that mirrors a policyholder liability; the insurer is neutral to investment returns on the unit fund (the policyholder takes the gain or loss). The non-unit fund's net cashflows have positive duration (charges) and negative duration (expenses), and the insurer manages them like a bank manages its trading book. The same VAR / duration tools apply.

Step 3

The mental model

Two accounts in parallel. The unit fund accrues units worth a market price. The non-unit fund accrues sterling cashflows. Money moves between them via the allocation, the AMC, the bid-offer spread, and the death-benefit-versus-unit-fund difference.

The unit fund is, in actuarial terms, a policyholder asset and insurer liability: it is the insurer's promise to give the policyholder the market value of the units. The non-unit fund is the insurer's actual asset: its profit, less expenses, less reserves, is the insurer's free capital from the contract. Pricing decisions are about non-unit-fund cashflows; valuation is about non-unit-fund reserves.

Step 4

The detail

Charge mechanics and the unit fund recursion

Let $F_t$ be the unit fund value at the end of year $t$, after all year-$t$ cashflows. Let $P_t$ be the premium paid at the start of year $t$, $\alpha$ the allocation rate, $i_f$ the assumed unit-fund investment return, and $\mu_a$ the annual management charge as a fraction of the year-end fund. The recursion is

$$ F_t \;=\; (F_{t-1} + \alpha P_t)(1 + i_f)(1 - \mu_a). $$

The mechanics: at the start of year $t$, the existing fund $F_{t-1}$ is topped up by the year's premium allocation $\alpha P_t$; the combined fund grows at investment return $i_f$ over the year; the AMC of $\mu_a$ is taken at year-end.

A bid-offer spread applies if units are bought at an offer price above the bid price at which they are redeemed. The effective allocation becomes $\alpha (1 - b)$ where $b$ is the bid-offer spread; for typical UK practice $b \in [0.05, 0.07]$.

Non-unit fund cashflows

The insurer's non-unit fund receives, for each unit of premium, the amount $(1 - \alpha) P_t$ at the start of year $t$ (the allocation residual) and $\mu_a (F_{t-1} + \alpha P_t)(1 + i_f)$ at the year end (the AMC). It pays out:

  • Initial expense $E_0$ at policy inception (commission, underwriting, setup).
  • Renewal expenses $E_r$ at the start of each subsequent year.
  • Mortality top-up: if the death benefit on death in year $t$ is the sum assured $S$ but only the unit fund $F_t$ is held in the policyholder's account, the non-unit fund pays out the shortfall $\max(S - F_t, 0)$ on death. Probability-weight by the mortality rate $q_{x+t-1}$.
  • Surrender penalty: at surrender in year $t$, the unit fund $F_t$ is paid net of any surrender charge. The penalty accrues to the non-unit fund.

The net cashflow in year $t$ is the sum of these; profit testing in lecture 7 chains them.

The implied policyholder yield

From the policyholder's perspective, the effective yield on the policy is the internal rate of return that equates the EPV of premiums paid to the unit fund value at maturity. With non-trivial charges this is lower than the assumed gross fund yield $i_f$; the difference is the reduction in yield, a regulatory disclosure quantity in many jurisdictions (UK COBS, SA FAIS).

Watch out Unit-linked profit emerges in three different shapes depending on the charge structure. Front-end loaded (low allocation rate, high allocation in later years) gives early profit and late losses. Annual-management-charge loaded (high allocation, all profit from running AMC) gives steady profit that grows with the fund. Surrender-penalty loaded gives a profit jump at expected surrender ages. The insurer typically combines all three; the combination is chosen for marketability, not for profit shape.

Non-unit fund cashflows for the same policy

Practitioner insight Unit-linked design choices have first-order effects on the insurer's capital strain. A 95% allocation rate means the insurer gets only 50 per 1,000 of premium upfront, but has to pay 100 of initial expense. So year 1 is loss-making, requiring capital to be put up. The AMC stream is the recovery mechanism: at 1% of a growing fund, the AMC compounds the insurer's recovery. Total profit emerges over the policy term, but only after the upfront cost is recovered. Solvency II reserves recognise this by holding a positive reserve in early years (the non-unit fund's expected future net cashflow is negative at the start). Zeroisation of the sterling reserve is covered in lecture 8.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The two funds are the same balance sheet read two ways

From the insurer's view, the unit fund is a liability matched by an investment asset of identical market value; the unit fund's investment risk is neutral to the insurer (gain or loss flows through to the policyholder). The non-unit fund is real capital. From the policyholder's view, the unit fund is an investment, the non-unit fund's existence is invisible. The whole framework is the insurer's bookkeeping superimposed on a savings product. This makes unit-linked products particularly susceptible to mis-selling: the explicit charges in the non-unit fund are usually visible only via the reduction-in-yield disclosure, and a hostile broker can frame the same product to look much cheaper than it really is.

Surrender modelling is the dirty problem

Surrender behaviour is the single largest source of valuation uncertainty in unit-linked. Surrender rates vary with the market value of the fund (high after losses), with the level of interest rates (higher rates make alternative investments more attractive), with policyholder demographics (younger policyholders surrender more often), and with the time since inception (the duration-dependent "honeymoon" effect). The standard valuation assumption is a deterministic surrender table, but Solvency II requires stress testing on dynamic surrender models. The next two lectures show how reserves change when surrender is modelled.

Guaranteed minimum returns make the problem stochastic

A unit-linked policy with a minimum guarantee (e.g., the policyholder gets the maximum of the unit fund value and a return-of-premiums guarantee) is no longer a deterministic problem. The guarantee is a put option on the fund struck at the guaranteed amount. Valuing it requires stochastic models of the fund return, which moves the problem from CM1 territory to CM2 / SP6 territory. The Equitable Life and AXA Sun Life crises of the 2000s and 2010s revolved around the mispricing of such guarantees in early unit-linked policies.

The connection to mutual funds

Strip the insurance wrapper and the unit fund is essentially a mutual fund: it pools investors' contributions in proportion to units owned and accrues investment returns. The differences are the insurance bolt-on (death benefit, tax treatment, surrender penalties) and the non-unit fund's charge stack. Most modern UK pensions are structurally identical to a mutual fund with an insurance wrapper. The Markets in Crypto Assets (MiCA) regulation and FCA work on packaged retail and insurance-based investment products (PRIIPs) is trying to harmonise the disclosure regime across both types.

Watch out A common exam mistake: treating "fund management charge" and "annual management charge" as different things. They are usually the same: a percentage of the year-end fund value, accruing to the insurer. Some older policies separate "fund management fee" (paid to the asset manager) from "policy administration fee" (paid to the insurer); for valuation purposes both flow out of the unit fund and need separate tracking. Check the contract.

Test yourself

For each prompt, identify whether the question is about the unit fund, the non-unit fund, or the relationship between the two before reaching for a formula.

  1. Distinguish the unit fund from the non-unit fund. Which one is the policyholder's asset? Which one drives the insurer's profit signature?
  2. Write down the recursion for the year-end unit fund value $F_t$ in terms of $F_{t-1}$, premium $P_t$, allocation $\alpha$, return $i_f$, and AMC $\mu_a$.
  3. Define the bid-offer spread. How does it modify the effective allocation rate from the policyholder's perspective?
  4. For the worked example, recompute the unit fund value at the end of year 10 if the AMC is 1.5% instead of 1%. What is the reduction-in-yield to the policyholder?
