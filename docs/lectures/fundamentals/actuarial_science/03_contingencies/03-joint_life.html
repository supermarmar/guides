Joint-life and multi-life functions

A contract on two lives has four possible states and four standard "statuses" the contract can be written against; getting the status right is the first thing the exam tests.

Lecture 3 of 8 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: UP / Contingencies (standard CM1 syllabus)
Step 1

The 30-second version

A joint-life status $(xy)$ fails on the first death; a last-survivor status $(\overline{xy})$ fails on the second death. Annuities and assurances on $(xy)$ pay while both lives are alive; on $(\overline{xy})$ pay while at least one is alive. Contingent assurances condition on the order of deaths, paying only if one specific life dies before the other. Reversionary annuities pay one life conditional on the death of the other. All four of these are standard contract forms in pensions and family-protection insurance, and all four are expectations against the same two-life Markov chain.

The single most-used identity in the subject is $A_{xy} + A_{\overline{xy}} = A_x + A_y$ — a one-line statement that on any single trajectory, one death is "first" and one is "second", so the sum of the EPV of paying on the first and the EPV of paying on the second equals the sum of paying on each life individually. The same identity holds for annuities: $\ddot{a}_{xy} + \ddot{a}_{\overline{xy}} = \ddot{a}_x + \ddot{a}_y$. Every joint-life computation either uses one of these identities or sits one step removed from them.

Feynman test A married couple buy a pension. The contract pays a full pension while both are alive, then half-pension to the survivor until the second death. Decompose: a joint-life annuity pays the full pension while both alive (status fails on first death); a last-survivor annuity continues while either is alive. The total is the joint-life annuity plus half of the difference between last-survivor and joint-life. Now write down each piece as a probability times a discount factor times a payment — the whole product prices in two lines.
Step 2

Why this matters

Joint-life arithmetic is the workhorse of family and pension protection. The vast majority of UK and South African retirement annuity contracts are written on $(\overline{xy})$ with a partial reversionary feature: full payment while both alive, two-thirds or one-half to the survivor. Family-protection life insurance is often written on $(xy)$: the sum assured is paid on the first death, so the survivor has financial support during a vulnerable period. Pension trustees value scheme liabilities member by member but must allow for dependants' pensions, which are reversionary annuities of the same form. Estate-planning insurance products are often written on $(\overline{xy})$, with the lump sum payable on the second death to fund inheritance tax.

The framework also matters outside life products. Joint-default modelling in credit risk uses the same two-life machinery: two counterparties with correlated default times, and the price of a basket credit derivative depends on which defaults first. Joint-survival in actuarial science and joint-failure in reliability engineering share the same probability theory; only the application differs.

Step 3

The mental model

Two lives, four states. The chain starts in state 00 (both alive) and absorbs into state 11 (both dead). The two intermediate states (only $x$ alive, only $y$ alive) are entered on the first death and exited on the second.

Two simplifying assumptions make all the standard joint-life formulas tractable. Independence: the future lifetimes $T_x$ and $T_y$ are independent random variables. Constant cause-specific forces: the rate at which $y$ dies is $\mu_{y+t}$ regardless of whether $x$ is alive or not (so state 00 and state 01 transition out at the same rate). Both assumptions can be relaxed but at the cost of considerable extra machinery — see the grandmaster's section.

Step 4

The detail

Joint and last-survivor survival probabilities

${}_t p_{xy}$
Joint-life survival probability: probability that both $x$ and $y$ are alive at time $t$. Under independence, ${}_t p_{xy} = {}_t p_x \cdot {}_t p_y$.
${}_t p_{\overline{xy}}$
Last-survivor survival probability: probability that at least one of $x$, $y$ is alive at time $t$. From the inclusion-exclusion principle, ${}_t p_{\overline{xy}} = {}_t p_x + {}_t p_y - {}_t p_{xy}$.

The corresponding "failure" probabilities follow immediately. The joint-life status fails on the first death: ${}_t q_{xy} = 1 - {}_t p_{xy}$. The last-survivor status fails on the second death: ${}_t q_{\overline{xy}} = 1 - {}_t p_{\overline{xy}} = {}_t q_x \cdot {}_t q_y$ under independence.

Forces of mortality on the two statuses

The joint-life status has the force-of-mortality identity

$$ \mu_{x+t:y+t} \;=\; \mu_{x+t} \;+\; \mu_{y+t}, $$

which falls out of the constant-force decomposition: leaving state 00 happens at the sum of the two single-life rates. The last-survivor status does not have such a clean expression because the rate of "second death" depends on which life is still alive at time $t$.

Assurances on joint and last-survivor statuses

Define the EPV of a unit assurance paying on failure of each status. Continuous version (paying immediately on first death for joint, immediately on second death for last-survivor):

$$ \bar{A}_{xy} \;=\; \int_0^\infty v^t\, {}_t p_{xy}\,\mu_{x+t:y+t}\,dt, \qquad \bar{A}_{\overline{xy}} \;=\; \int_0^\infty v^t\,{}_t q_x \cdot {}_t p_y \cdot \mu_{y+t}\,dt \;+\; \int_0^\infty v^t\,{}_t q_y \cdot {}_t p_x \cdot \mu_{x+t}\,dt. $$

The last-survivor expression is the sum of two pieces: (i) $x$ has already died, $y$ is alive and about to die; (ii) $y$ has already died, $x$ is alive and about to die. The discrete-time annual versions $A_{xy}$ and $A_{\overline{xy}}$ are the same expression replaced with sums over integer $k$.

The headline identity follows by inspection: on any single trajectory, one death is the first and the other is the second. So

$$ A_{xy} + A_{\overline{xy}} \;=\; A_x + A_y. $$

This identity is the most common time-saver on a joint-life exam question: if you have computed two of $\{A_x, A_y, A_{xy}\}$ you can read off the third without doing another integral.

Annuities on joint and last-survivor statuses

Same logic, with the random variable being time-of-payment rather than time-of-death:

$$ \bar{a}_{xy} \;=\; \int_0^\infty v^t\, {}_t p_{xy}\, dt, \qquad \bar{a}_{\overline{xy}} \;=\; \int_0^\infty v^t\, {}_t p_{\overline{xy}}\, dt. $$

The same headline identity holds: $\ddot{a}_{xy} + \ddot{a}_{\overline{xy}} = \ddot{a}_x + \ddot{a}_y$. Reason: a payment at time $k$ is made (i) under $(xy)$ if both alive, (ii) under $(\overline{xy})$ if at least one alive. The sum is a payment of one when both alive plus a payment of one when at least one alive, which equals one payment for each of $x$ alive and $y$ alive (each life contributes a payment regardless of the other's status).

Contingent assurances

$A_{xy}^1$
Contingent assurance paying on the death of $x$ provided $x$ dies first. Position of the "1" indicates which life is the contingent one. EPV: $\bar{A}_{xy}^1 = \int_0^\infty v^t\, {}_t p_y \cdot {}_t p_x \cdot \mu_{x+t}\,dt$ (both alive at time $t$, $x$ dies at the instant).
$A_{xy}^{\;\;1}$
Contingent assurance paying on the death of $y$ provided $y$ dies first (the "1" sits on the $y$ position). EPV: $\bar{A}_{xy}^{\;\;1} = \int_0^\infty v^t\, {}_t p_x \cdot {}_t p_y \cdot \mu_{y+t}\,dt$.

Summing the two contingent assurances gives back the joint-life assurance: $\bar{A}_{xy}^1 + \bar{A}_{xy}^{\;\;1} = \bar{A}_{xy}$. Reason: paying on whichever first death occurs is the joint-life status itself. The decomposition matters whenever a contract conditions on the order of deaths (a typical example: a husband's life-insurance policy that pays only if he predeceases his wife).

Watch out The position of the "1" superscript in $A_{xy}^1$ versus $A_{xy}^{\;\;1}$ is doing real work. Above $x$ it means "pay on the death of $x$ if $x$ dies first". Above $y$ it means "pay on the death of $y$ if $y$ dies first". Misreading the position is a one-mark error that propagates through every subsequent line. Some textbooks use $A_{xy}^{[1]}$ or other notation; whichever convention your source uses, write out the contract in English before plugging numbers.

Reversionary annuities

$a_{y|x}$
Reversionary annuity paying $x$ continuously while $x$ is alive after $y$ has died. EPV from inception: $\bar{a}_{y|x} = \bar{a}_x - \bar{a}_{xy}$. Reason: "$x$ alive after $y$ dead" equals "$x$ alive" minus "$x$ and $y$ both alive".

The decomposition $\bar{a}_{y|x} = \bar{a}_x - \bar{a}_{xy}$ is the cleanest way to compute reversionary annuities. It also generalises: a reversionary annuity payable at rate $b_1$ during the joint life and $b_2$ during the survivorship period can be written as $b_1 \bar{a}_{xy} + b_2 (\bar{a}_{\overline{xy}} - \bar{a}_{xy}) = (b_1 - b_2) \bar{a}_{xy} + b_2 \bar{a}_{\overline{xy}}$. This is precisely the structure of "full pension while both alive, half-pension to the survivor".

A worked example: pension with reversionary feature

A pension scheme pays an annuity of 12,000 per year continuously while both husband (age $x = 65$) and wife (age $y = 62$) are alive, then 8,000 per year continuously to the survivor until the second death. Compute the EPV at retirement using the standard PMA92 table with discount rate 4%. Suppose ${}_t p_{65} \cdot {}_t p_{62}$ leads to $\bar{a}_{xy} = 12.32$ and $\bar{a}_{\overline{xy}} = 16.78$.

Second, the same EPV computed via $4{,}000 \bar{a}_{xy} + 8{,}000\bar{a}_{\overline{xy}}$ gives the same answer because the two decompositions are algebraically identical.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The independence assumption is convenient and wrong

The assumption that $T_x$ and $T_y$ are independent is the engineering simplification that makes every formula above tractable. It is empirically false. The death of a spouse is associated with elevated mortality for the survivor over the following 12 months (the "widowhood effect"), so the conditional force $\mu_{y+t \mid x \text{ died}}$ exceeds $\mu_{y+t}$ for small $t$. Pension valuations that use independent two-life tables therefore under-reserve, by an amount that varies with the mortality basis but is typically 1-3% of the joint-life liability. Modern joint-life valuations sometimes apply an explicit widowhood mortality uplift to the survivor's post-bereavement force; the price for this is one additional parameter and a slightly less clean closed form.

Common-shock and copula extensions

Two more elaborate ways to model joint-life dependence. A common-shock model adds a third intensity $\lambda$ that triggers simultaneous death of both lives (a car accident, a household fire). The two single-life lifetimes are then a deterministic function of three independent exponential random variables, and the joint survival function decomposes cleanly. A copula model couples the marginals of $T_x$ and $T_y$ through a Gaussian or Clayton copula, allowing flexible dependence structures. Both are research-grade extensions; both are overkill for vanilla pension valuations but standard in catastrophe and reinsurance modelling.

Equality of statuses under specific parametric mortality

Under Gompertz mortality the joint-life status $(xy)$ has the same survival function as a single-life status at some "equivalent age" $z$ such that $B c^z = B c^x + B c^y$. Equivalent ages let practitioners reduce a two-life problem to a one-life problem against a standard table. Under Makeham mortality the same reduction works with a small adjustment. For non-parametric tables this trick is unavailable, but it is a useful intuition check: if both lives are healthy and roughly equal in age, the joint-life status fails roughly twice as fast as either single-life status.

Order of deaths is the harder problem

Contingent assurances are conceptually simple but computationally awkward. The integrals for $\bar{A}_{xy}^1$ and $\bar{A}_{xy}^{\;\;1}$ do not factorise under independence the way the joint-life integrals do; both require the joint density of $(T_x, T_y)$. The standard trick is to write $\bar{A}_{xy}^1$ as half of the joint-life assurance plus a correction term proportional to the difference of single-life forces; the correction is zero when the two lives are stochastically identical and grows as the forces diverge.

Watch out A frequent exam trap: questions that quote the "annuity to the surviving spouse" but lead candidates to set up a single-life annuity on the survivor. The survivor is not known at outset, so this is wrong: the correct expression is a reversionary annuity, $b\,\bar{a}_{y|x} + b\,\bar{a}_{x|y}$, summed over the two cases of who is survivor. Catch this by asking: at policy inception, do you know which life is the survivor? If not, you have a reversionary structure.
Step 6

Test yourself

For each prompt, draw the two-life state diagram first and identify which states the contract pays in. Only then write down the EPV expression.

  1. State the definitions of $(xy)$ and $(\overline{xy})$ in English. For each, identify which of the four states 00, 10, 01, 11 the status occupies.
  2. Under independence, show ${}_t p_{xy} = {}_t p_x \cdot {}_t p_y$ and ${}_t p_{\overline{xy}} = {}_t p_x + {}_t p_y - {}_t p_x \cdot {}_t p_y$. Where does independence enter the second?
  3. Derive $\mu_{x+t:y+t} = \mu_{x+t} + \mu_{y+t}$ from the definition of the force of mortality on the joint-life status.
  4. Prove $A_{xy} + A_{\overline{xy}} = A_x + A_y$ from the trajectories interpretation (one death is "first", one is "second"). Generalise to $\ddot{a}_{xy} + \ddot{a}_{\overline{xy}} = \ddot{a}_x + \ddot{a}_y$.
  5. Distinguish in words between $A_{xy}^1$ and $A_{xy}^{\;\;1}$. Confirm $A_{xy}^1 + A_{xy}^{\;\;1} = A_{xy}$.
  6. Write the EPV of a reversionary annuity that pays $b$ per year continuously to $x$ for as long as $x$ is alive after $y$ has died. Show this equals $b(\bar{a}_x - \bar{a}_{xy})$.
  7. For the pension worked example, suppose the survivor pension is two-thirds rather than half. Recompute the total EPV with the same annuity factors.
  8. The widowhood effect adds a 50% uplift to the survivor's force of mortality for the first year after bereavement. Estimate the proportional impact on the joint-life last-survivor annuity factor $\bar{a}_{\overline{xy}}$.
  9. Under Gompertz mortality, find the equivalent age $z$ such that the joint-life status $(xy)$ has the same survival as a single life $(z)$, with $x = 65$, $y = 62$, $c = 1.1$, $B$ arbitrary.
