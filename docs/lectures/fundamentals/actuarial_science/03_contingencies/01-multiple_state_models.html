Multiple-state models

When a life can occupy more than two states, the survival model breaks; a Markov chain on states is the fix, and it unifies everything else in this course.

Lecture 1 of 8 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: UP / Contingencies (standard CM1 syllabus)
Step 1

The 30-second version

A multiple-state model is a finite set of states (healthy, disabled, dead; or active, withdrawn, retired, dead) together with intensities $\mu_x^{ij}$ that say how fast a life flows from state $i$ to state $j$. From the intensities you build transition probabilities ${}_t p_x^{ij}$ by solving the Kolmogorov forward equations. From those you build the expected present value of any contract whose payments depend on which state the life is in. The reserve, treated as a function of time and state, satisfies Thiele's differential equation. That single piece of machinery prices and reserves every contract in the rest of this course.

The single-life survival model is the two-state special case (alive = 0, dead = 1, one transition). The next two lectures specialise the multi-state framework in two different ways: lecture 2 takes one starting state and several absorbing states (multiple decrements), lecture 3 takes a two-life four-state model (joint life). Everything else inherits.

Feynman test A permanent disability income contract pays a benefit while you are disabled, stops paying when you recover or die, and may pay a lump sum on death. To price it you need the probability that the policyholder is in each of three states (healthy, disabled, dead) at every future date. The two-state survival model cannot answer that. The multi-state model is the natural answer: draw three states, write down four intensities (healthy to disabled, disabled to healthy, healthy to dead, disabled to dead), and the price of the contract is one integral against the transition probabilities those four numbers generate.
Step 2

Why this matters

Every modern insurance product is a contract that distinguishes between more than two ways of being or leaving. Income protection pays while sick and stops on recovery. Critical illness pays a lump sum on diagnosis with a survival period. Long-term care pays while in care. Pension valuations distinguish active, deferred, retired, and dependant states. Each of these has separate cashflows for separate states, so each needs more than alive-or-dead bookkeeping. The two-state model can price none of them.

The same machinery is used outside actuarial work. Credit rating transition matrices (the AAA-to-Default chain published by Moody's and S&P) are the discrete-time version, with rating grades as states and default as the absorbing state. Reduced-form credit models (Jarrow-Lando-Turnbull) are the continuous-time version, with a generator matrix in place of a transition matrix. Healthcare cost modelling uses multi-state Markov chains with states like outpatient, hospitalised, in-rehab, dead. Reliability engineering models machines through degradation states. The actuarial framing here is the cleanest available, and once it is fluent the others read easily.

Inside actuarial work the framework also unifies bookkeeping. Multi-decrement is multi-state with one starting state and $k$ absorbing states. Joint-life is multi-state on the product of two single-life chains. Pension funds, profit testing, and unit-linked reserving are all applications of one diagram plus one differential equation. The payoff for absorbing the abstract framework now is that the remaining seven lectures stop introducing new machinery and become exercises in this one.

Step 3

The mental model

Hold this picture in your head whenever a multi-state question hits. A finite set of nodes, each a state the life can occupy. A set of directed arrows, each labelled with an instantaneous rate $\mu_x^{ij}$. The intensities are the only inputs; the probabilities, the EPVs, the premiums and the reserves all flow from them.

Two structural pieces of the model are visible in the diagram. The state space is the set of nodes, $\{0, 1, 2\}$ here. The intensity matrix $\boldsymbol{\mu}_x$ has the off-diagonal entries $\mu_x^{ij}$ and diagonal entries equal to minus the row sums, so every row sums to zero. The diagonal entries are the total rate of leaving each state. The bottom row is all zeros because state 2 is absorbing: nothing leaves it. The Kolmogorov forward equations derived in the next section say that the time-derivative of the transition-probability matrix is ${}_t \mathbf{P}_x \cdot \boldsymbol{\mu}_{x+t}$. Once you can write that equation down, the whole subject reduces to solving it.

Step 4

The detail

State space, intensities, and the Markov assumption

Fix a finite set of states $S = \{0, 1, \dots, n\}$. A life follows a stochastic process $X_t$ taking values in $S$, where $t$ is the age. The process is Markov if

$$ P(X_{s+t} = j \mid X_s = i,\, X_u: u < s) \;=\; P(X_{s+t} = j \mid X_s = i) $$

for every $i, j \in S$ and every $s, t \ge 0$. In plain English: the future depends on the current state only, not on the path that got you there. This assumption is the load-bearing column of the whole framework, and the grandmaster's section returns to when it fails.

$\mu_x^{ij}$
The transition intensity from state $i$ to state $j$ at age $x$, $\mu_x^{ij} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} h^{-1}\, P(X_{x+h} = j \mid X_x = i)$ for $i \neq j$. Units per year. The multi-state analogue of the force of mortality.
${}_t p_x^{ij}$
The transition probability from $i$ to $j$ over $t$ years starting at age $x$, $P(X_{x+t} = j \mid X_x = i)$. The two-state special case ${}_t p_x^{00}$ recovers the single-life survival probability ${}_t p_x$.
${}_t p_x^{\overline{ii}}$
The occupation probability: probability that the life stays in state $i$ continuously from age $x$ to age $x+t$. Distinct from ${}_t p_x^{ii}$, which permits excursions out of $i$ and back. When the model has no return transitions to $i$, the two coincide.
Watch out The distinction between ${}_t p_x^{ii}$ and ${}_t p_x^{\overline{ii}}$ is the most common trap in multi-state pricing. In the income-protection three-state model where recovery is allowed, a life can be healthy at time 0, fall sick at time 1, recover at time 1.5, be healthy again at time 2. That trajectory contributes to ${}_t p_x^{00}$ but not to ${}_t p_x^{\overline{00}}$. A waiver-of-premium contract that pays nothing once the life has ever been sick depends on the occupation probability; an annuity that pays as long as the life is currently healthy depends on the transition probability. Confuse them and the premium is wrong.

The Kolmogorov forward equations

The fundamental ODE governing the transition probabilities. Start from the Chapman-Kolmogorov identity, which decomposes the probability of being in state $j$ at time $t + h$ by conditioning on the state at time $t$:

$$ {}_{t+h} p_x^{ij} \;=\; \sum_{k \in S} {}_t p_x^{ik}\,{}_h p_{x+t}^{kj}. $$

For small $h$, the one-step probabilities are

$$ {}_h p_{x+t}^{kj} \;=\; \begin{cases} h\,\mu_{x+t}^{kj} + o(h) & k \neq j, \\ 1 - h\sum_{l \neq k} \mu_{x+t}^{kl} + o(h) & k = j. \end{cases} $$

Substitute, subtract ${}_t p_x^{ij}$ from both sides, divide by $h$, and let $h \to 0$:

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\, {}_t p_x^{ij} \;=\; \sum_{k \neq j} {}_t p_x^{ik}\, \mu_{x+t}^{kj} \;-\; {}_t p_x^{ij} \sum_{l \neq j} \mu_{x+t}^{jl}. $$

The first sum collects probability flowing into $j$ from every other state; the second subtracts probability flowing out of $j$ to anywhere else. In matrix form,

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\, {}_t \mathbf{P}_x \;=\; {}_t \mathbf{P}_x \cdot \boldsymbol{\mu}_{x+t}, \qquad {}_0 \mathbf{P}_x = \mathbf{I}, $$

a first-order linear ODE system in $n^2$ unknowns. With age-independent intensities the solution is the matrix exponential ${}_t \mathbf{P}_x = \exp(t \boldsymbol{\mu})$. With age-dependent intensities the system is integrated numerically (Euler or Runge-Kutta on a discrete age grid).

The occupation probability has a simpler ODE

For staying continuously in $i$, only the total rate of leaving matters:

$$ {}_t p_x^{\overline{ii}} \;=\; \exp\!\left(-\int_0^t \sum_{j \neq i} \mu_{x+s}^{ij}\, ds\right). $$

Derivation: there is one event of interest, leaving $i$ for anywhere. The total rate of leaving is $\sum_{j \neq i} \mu_{x+s}^{ij}$. The probability of staying in $i$ from age $x$ to age $x+t$ is the exponential of the negative integral of that rate, exactly as in the single-life model where the only "leaving" event was death. Setting $S = 2$ recovers ${}_t p_x = \exp(-\int_0^t \mu_{x+s}\,ds)$.

Expected present values of state-contingent and transition-contingent cashflows

Two kinds of cashflow appear in every multi-state contract.

State-contingent annuity
A continuous payment at rate $b(t)$ while the life is in state $j$. EPV from age $x$, starting in state $i$: $\displaystyle \int_0^\infty v^t \cdot {}_t p_x^{ij}\, b(t)\,dt$ where $v = (1+r)^{-1}$. The factor ${}_t p_x^{ij}$ is the probability the life is in the paying state at time $t$.
Transition-contingent lump sum
A lump sum $S$ on transition from $j$ to $k$. EPV from age $x$, starting in state $i$: $\displaystyle \int_0^\infty v^t \cdot {}_t p_x^{ij} \mu_{x+t}^{jk}\, S\,dt$. The factor ${}_t p_x^{ij}\mu_{x+t}^{jk}$ is the density of transitioning out of $j$ into $k$ at the instant $t$.

Every product reduces to a sum of these two pieces. An income-protection contract sold to a healthy life pays a continuous benefit in state $j=1$ (state-contingent annuity) and a lump sum on transition $0 \to 2$ and on transition $1 \to 2$ (two transition-contingent pieces). The total EPV is the sum of three integrals.

Thiele's differential equation for reserves

Let $V^{(i)}(t)$ be the policy value at time $t$ given the life is in state $i$. By the recursive identity that holding the contract for a short interval $h$ leaves the future EPV unchanged,

$$ V^{(i)}(t) \;=\; b^{(i)}_t h \,-\, P^{(i)}_t h \,+\, e^{-rh}\sum_{j \in S} {}_h p_{x+t}^{ij} V^{(j)}(t+h) \;+\; o(h). $$

Here $b^{(i)}$ is the benefit rate while in state $i$ and $P^{(i)}$ is the premium rate while in state $i$. Expand to first order, subtract $V^{(i)}(t)$, divide by $h$, and let $h \to 0$:

$$ \frac{d}{dt} V^{(i)}(t) \;=\; r\,V^{(i)}(t) \,+\, P^{(i)}_t \,-\, b^{(i)}_t \,-\, \sum_{j \neq i} \mu_{x+t}^{ij}\!\left[S^{(ij)}_t + V^{(j)}(t) - V^{(i)}(t)\right]. $$

Each term has a clean reading. $r V^{(i)}$ is interest accrual on the reserve. $P^{(i)} - b^{(i)}$ is the net cashflow while in state $i$. The last sum costs every possible transition out of $i$: each transition costs the lump sum on that transition plus the change of reserve into the new state. Integrate backwards from the terminal condition $V^{(i)}(n) = 0$. The single-life special case $S = \{0, 1\}$, $\mu^{01} = \mu_{x+t}$, $V^{(1)} = 0$ collapses to the familiar $V'(t) = r V(t) + P - \mu_{x+t}(1 - V(t))$ derived in actuarial mathematics lecture 6.

A worked example: pricing a permanent disability income contract

Consider a 30-year contract sold to a life aged 40, currently healthy. The contract pays 10,000 per year continuously while disabled (state 1), nothing on death, and zero terminal benefit. Premium $P$ is paid continuously while healthy (state 0). Recovery is ruled out, so $\mu^{10} = 0$. Constant intensities: $\mu^{01} = 0.005$, $\mu^{02} = 0.012$, $\mu^{12} = 0.045$. Discount rate $r = 0.04$.

With constant intensities the transition probabilities are closed-form. For the occupation probabilities,

$$ {}_t p_x^{\overline{00}} \;=\; e^{-(\mu^{01} + \mu^{02})\,t} \;=\; e^{-0.017 t}, \qquad {}_t p_x^{\overline{11}} \;=\; e^{-\mu^{12}\,t} \;=\; e^{-0.045 t}. $$

For ${}_t p_x^{01}$, condition on the time $s$ at which the life leaves state 0 to enter state 1:

$$ {}_t p_x^{01} \;=\; \int_0^t {}_s p_x^{\overline{00}}\,\mu^{01}\,{}_{t-s} p_{x+s}^{\overline{11}}\,ds \;=\; \frac{\mu^{01}}{\beta - \alpha}\left(e^{-\alpha t} - e^{-\beta t}\right), $$

with $\alpha = \mu^{01} + \mu^{02} = 0.017$ and $\beta = \mu^{12} = 0.045$. Plug numbers: $\mu^{01}/(\beta - \alpha) = 0.005/0.028 = 0.1786$, so ${}_t p_x^{01} = 0.1786(e^{-0.017 t} - e^{-0.045 t})$.

The three transition probabilities over the policy term tell the story of the contract:

Compute the EPVs. For the disability benefit, the integrand is $v^t \cdot {}_t p^{01}$ which after expanding becomes $e^{-rt}\cdot 0.1786(e^{-\alpha t} - e^{-\beta t}) = 0.1786(e^{-(r+\alpha)t} - e^{-(r+\beta)t})$. Each piece is an annuity-certain in disguise. The premium calculation uses ${}_t p_x^{\overline{00}}$ because premiums stop at the first time the life leaves the healthy state.

ComponentIntegrandClosed formNumerical value
EPV of disability benefit $10{,}000 \cdot e^{-rt}\,{}_t p_{40}^{01}$ $1786\left[\dfrac{1 - e^{-0.057 \cdot 30}}{0.057} - \dfrac{1 - e^{-0.085 \cdot 30}}{0.085}\right]$ $1786 \cdot (14.371 - 10.844) = 6{,}299$
EPV of unit premium $e^{-rt}\,{}_t p_{40}^{\overline{00}}$ $\dfrac{1 - e^{-0.057 \cdot 30}}{0.057}$ $14.371$

The premium of 438 per year is the equivalence-principle answer: it sets the EPV of premiums equal to the EPV of benefits at policy inception. A real-world premium would load this for expenses, profit, and supervisory reserves, all of which become the subject of lectures 7 and 8.

Practitioner insight Notice which lever matters. Halving the discount rate from 4% to 2% would push EPV of both sides up by roughly 30%, leaving the premium roughly unchanged. Doubling $\mu^{01}$ from 0.005 to 0.010 would push the benefit EPV up by roughly 90% while leaving the premium-side annuity factor unchanged, so the premium would roughly double. Pricing sensitivity in income protection lives in the sickness-inception assumption, not the discount rate.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The Markov assumption is the single point of failure

Everything above assumes the future depends only on the current state, not on the path. For mortality this is roughly defensible. For disability and lapse it often is not. The rate of recovery from disability typically falls with duration in the disabled state (longer durations mean less likely to recover). The lapse rate on a unit-linked policy typically jumps in the year after a surrender penalty expires. Semi-Markov extensions add duration in the current state as a second argument to the intensity; Cox models add observed covariates; frailty models add unobserved heterogeneity. All three are research-grade extensions the framework supports if you are willing to inflate the state space.

Time-inhomogeneous, not time-homogeneous

The intensities depend on age $x+t$, not just on time in state. This makes the chain time-inhomogeneous and means the transition matrix is not a matrix exponential $\exp(t \boldsymbol{\mu})$ except in the special case of constant intensities (used in the worked example for tractability). Generic intensities are integrated numerically: discretise time, evaluate $\boldsymbol{\mu}_{x+t}$ on the grid, step the Kolmogorov ODE.

The intensity matrix is the right object to fit

A common newcomer mistake is to fit transition probabilities directly from observed one-year transitions. Two problems: probabilities depend on the window length, and they require independence between observations the multi-state framework does not give you. Maximum-likelihood estimation works on the intensities themselves, using the likelihood of a continuous-time Markov chain (each observed sojourn time in $i$ contributes the corresponding exponential survival, each observed transition $i \to j$ contributes $\mu^{ij}$). This is the same machinery that estimates rating-transition generators in credit risk.

Thiele's equation generalises retrospective and prospective reserves

The single-life equivalence between retrospective and prospective reserves was a single statement: value of future cashflows equals accumulated value of past cashflows under the same basis. Thiele generalises it. Solving the ODE forward from time zero gives the retrospective reserve; solving backward from the terminal time gives the prospective reserve. They agree under the equivalence-principle premium. The single-life agreement was a corollary; the multi-state framework reveals it as a general property of any first-order linear ODE with the equivalence-principle boundary condition.

Watch out Common exam trap: examiners write a question that uses ${}_t p_x^{ii}$ and you assume it means "stays in state $i$ throughout". It usually does not, unless the model has no return transitions to $i$. Whenever the model allows recovery, get into the habit of writing $\overline{ii}$ explicitly when you mean continuous occupation. The notation $\overline{ii}$ is overloaded in some textbooks, so check what your source means before plugging in.

Test yourself

Try to answer each prompt out loud, in plain English, as if to a curious classmate who has just done the actuarial mathematics course but not this one. Do not look back at the lecture until you have tried.

  1. State the Markov assumption for a multi-state model. Give one real product where it clearly fails, and name the standard extension used to handle it.
  2. Define $\mu_x^{ij}$, ${}_t p_x^{ij}$, and ${}_t p_x^{\overline{ii}}$. Explain in one sentence the difference between ${}_t p_x^{00}$ and ${}_t p_x^{\overline{00}}$ in the income-protection three-state model.
  3. Derive the Kolmogorov forward equation starting from the Chapman-Kolmogorov identity.
  4. For the alive-disabled-dead model with constant intensities $\mu^{01} = \alpha$, $\mu^{02} = \beta$, $\mu^{12} = \gamma$, $\mu^{10} = 0$, show that ${}_t p_x^{01} = \frac{\alpha}{\gamma - \alpha - \beta}\left(e^{-(\alpha + \beta)t} - e^{-\gamma t}\right)$.
  5. Write down the EPV from age $x$, state $0$, of (a) a continuous annuity at rate $b$ while in state $1$ until age $x + n$, (b) a lump sum $S$ on transition $0 \to 2$ within $n$ years. Give the explicit integrals.
  6. Derive Thiele's equation from the recursive identity. Specialise it to the two-state alive-dead case and confirm it reduces to $V'(t) = r V(t) + P - \mu_{x+t}(1 - V(t))$.
  7. For the worked example, recompute the net premium if (a) the discount rate falls to 2% and (b) $\mu^{01}$ doubles. Comment on which lever has the bigger effect.
  8. Explain in two sentences why the multi-state framework is sometimes called "the only model you need" for individual life and disability contracts. What does it gain over the survival-model framework, and what does it cost?
