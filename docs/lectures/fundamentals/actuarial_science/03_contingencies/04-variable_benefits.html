Variable benefits and with-profits

When the benefit grows with policy duration the EPV stops being a single annuity factor; the trick is to decompose into pieces that look like ordinary annuities at a modified discount rate.

Lecture 4 of 8 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: UP / Contingencies (standard CM1 syllabus), Chapter 19 increasing endowment
Step 1

The 30-second version

A variable benefit contract pays an amount that grows (or shrinks) with policy duration: increasing endowments, indexed annuities, with-profits bonuses. The EPV is computed by writing the benefit as a sum of pieces, valuing each piece as a separate annuity or assurance, and summing. The key trick is the modified discount rate: when the benefit grows geometrically at rate $g$, the EPV equals the standard EPV at the modified rate $i' = (i - g)/(1 + g)$. Compound increases reduce to level benefits at a lower effective interest rate; arithmetic increases require the "increasing annuity" identities $(I\bar{a})_x$ and $(I\bar{A})_x$.

With-profits contracts overlay this machinery with three classes of bonus: reversionary bonuses (declared each year, added to the sum assured, vest immediately), simple reversionary bonuses (each year's bonus is calculated on the original sum assured), compound reversionary bonuses (each year's bonus is calculated on the cumulative sum assured plus prior bonuses), and terminal bonuses (paid only at maturity or death, not vested in advance). Each class has its own EPV recipe; the rest of this lecture is the menu.

Feynman test Imagine a 20-year endowment that pays 10,000 on death or maturity. Now imagine it instead pays an amount that grows by 5% per year: the year-1 benefit is 10,000, year-2 is 10,500, year-20 is 25,270. How much more does that cost? Not 2.5x, even though the benefit at maturity is 2.5x the original. The compound discount partially offsets the compound growth, and at a 4% discount rate the EPV ratio comes out closer to 1.4x. The intuition is that the growth and the discount partly cancel; with $g = i$ they cancel exactly and the EPV reduces to a single sum assured times the mortality probability.
Step 2

Why this matters

Three big classes of product depend on variable-benefit machinery. Indexed annuities and indexed pensions increase the benefit each year by a stated factor (CPI, RPI, or a fixed escalation like 3%). Without inflation-linking, a typical retirement annuity loses around half its real value over 25 years; this is unacceptable for pensions, so almost every modern retirement product has some form of escalation. Increasing-benefit life insurance grows the sum assured each year (typically by RPI or 5% compound), so the cover keeps pace with the policyholder's likely income trajectory. With-profits endowments, the historic mainstay of UK and Commonwealth long-term savings, smooth the underlying asset returns into reversionary bonuses that are vested as they are declared. The Equitable Life crisis of 2000 was at root a with-profits valuation failure.

The underlying mathematics is universal: any benefit that can be written as a sum of time-dated cashflows can be priced. The two question categories that the framework distinguishes are arithmetic increases (the benefit grows by a fixed monetary amount each year) and geometric increases (the benefit grows by a fixed percentage each year). The two require different tools: increasing-annuity identities for arithmetic, modified discount rates for geometric.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture a timeline of benefit cashflows. For a level benefit the bars are all the same height; for an arithmetically increasing benefit they climb in a staircase; for a geometrically increasing benefit they climb in a compound curve. The EPV is the area under the bars weighted by survival probability and discount factor.

The two questions to ask of any variable-benefit contract are: (i) what is the growth rule, and (ii) when does growth happen — annually, monthly, continuously? The answer determines which annuity or assurance identity to reach for.

Step 4

The detail

Arithmetically increasing annuities and assurances

Define the standard "increasing" annuity, paying $t$ at the end of year $t$ for as long as the life survives:

$$ (I\ddot{a})_x \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^\infty (k+1)\, v^k\, {}_k p_x. $$

The benefit grows by 1 per year, starting from 1 in year zero. The continuous version pays at rate $t$ at age $x+t$:

$$ (I\bar{a})_x \;=\; \int_0^\infty t\, v^t\, {}_t p_x\, dt. $$

The increasing assurance is the same idea applied to the death benefit: the sum assured payable on death in year $t$ is $t$ rather than 1:

$$ (IA)_x \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^\infty (k+1)\, v^{k+1}\, {}_k p_x\, q_{x+k}. $$

A general arithmetically increasing benefit with starting amount $b_0$ and step $\Delta$ has EPV $b_0 \ddot{a}_x + \Delta \cdot (I\ddot{a})_x$ for an annuity, or $b_0 A_x + \Delta \cdot (IA)_x$ for an assurance. The decomposition into a level piece plus an "increasing-by-1" piece is the canonical move.

Geometrically increasing benefits and the modified discount rate

Suppose the benefit grows geometrically at rate $g$ per year, so $b_t = b_0 (1+g)^t$. The EPV of the annuity is

$$ b_0 \sum_{k=0}^\infty (1+g)^k v^k\, {}_k p_x \;=\; b_0 \sum_{k=0}^\infty \left(\frac{1+g}{1+i}\right)^k {}_k p_x \;=\; b_0 \sum_{k=0}^\infty (v')^k\, {}_k p_x \;=\; b_0\,\ddot{a}_x^{i'}, $$

where $v' = (1+g)/(1+i)$ and the modified discount rate is

$$ i' \;=\; \frac{1+i}{1+g} - 1 \;=\; \frac{i - g}{1 + g}. $$

Compound increases reduce to a level benefit at a lower interest rate. For $g = i$ the modified rate is zero and the EPV is $b_0$ times life expectancy. For $g > i$ the EPV is technically infinite (the modified rate is negative), but in practice survival mortality bounds it.

Watch out The modified rate $i' = (i - g)/(1 + g)$ is sometimes mis-quoted as $i' = i - g$. The latter is correct only in continuous compounding when $i$ and $g$ are forces (continuous rates); under discrete compounding the multiplicative form is right. The discrepancy is small when $g$ is small but becomes material at high inflation. The South African experience of using approximate forms during 1980s hyperinflation cost some life offices several percent of their valuation.

With-profits: simple, compound, and terminal bonuses

A with-profits policy declares a series of reversionary bonuses $b_1, b_2, \dots$ during the policy term, plus a possible terminal bonus $T$ at the end. The EPV at outset depends on the bonus structure.

Simple reversionary bonus
Each year's bonus is a percentage $b$ of the original sum assured $S$. After $t$ years, the cumulative bonus is $tbS$, so the sum assured is $S(1 + tb)$. The EPV of the death benefit is $S(A_x + b\,(IA)_x)$, because the death-year amount is $S(1 + (t-1)b)$ if death is in year $t$.
Compound reversionary bonus
Each year's bonus is a percentage $b$ of the cumulative sum assured (original plus prior bonuses). After $t$ years, the sum assured is $S(1 + b)^t$. The EPV of the death benefit is $S \cdot A_x^{i'}$ at the modified rate $i' = (i - b)/(1 + b)$.
Terminal bonus
A bonus paid only on maturity or death, not vested as it is earned. Pricing depends on the assumed terminal-bonus rate; once that is given, the bonus is just an additional sum assured payable on the same triggers.

The choice of bonus structure has a large effect on the policyholder's exposure to investment returns. Simple bonuses are linear in time so they pay out slowly. Compound bonuses are exponential in time so they pay out faster as the policy ages. Terminal bonuses are back-loaded, giving the office the most discretion to smooth investment returns. Most UK with-profits offices use a hybrid: small annual reversionary bonus, large terminal bonus.

A worked example: increasing endowment (Chapter 19)

A 20-year endowment is sold to a life aged 40. The sum assured on death or survival is $10{,}000$ in year 1 and increases by $500$ each year. Premium $P$ is paid annually in advance, level. Discount rate $i = 4\%$. Compute the net premium using AM92 ultimate mortality.

The benefit is $b_t = 10{,}000 + 500(t-1)$ for $t = 1, \dots, 20$, so $b_t = 9{,}500 + 500\,t$. Decompose into a level piece of 9,500 and an arithmetically increasing piece with step 500.

The EPV of the death-and-survival benefit is

$$ EPV(\text{benefit}) \;=\; 9{,}500\, A_{40:\overline{20|}} \;+\; 500\,(IA)_{40:\overline{20|}}, $$

where $A_{40:\overline{20|}}$ is the 20-year endowment assurance at age 40, and $(IA)_{40:\overline{20|}}$ is the 20-year increasing endowment assurance (year-of-death benefit equal to the year number, plus a benefit of 20 on survival). For illustration take $A_{40:\overline{20|}} = 0.464$ and $(IA)_{40:\overline{20|}} = 4.92$. The EPV of unit premium is $\ddot{a}_{40:\overline{20|}} = 13.94$.

The increasing piece contributes 36% of the total benefit cost — a substantial weight for a benefit that only adds 500 per year. The reason: $(IA)$ weights later-year cashflows more heavily than $A$ does, so an additional 500 in year 20 contributes much more to $(IA)$ than 500 in year 1.

Cost of compound bonus relative to simple

Practitioner insight With-profits valuation is sensitive to the assumed terminal-bonus rate, which is the actuary's discretion to set. UK practice (PRA SS3/24) requires terminal-bonus assumptions to be consistent with the underlying asset returns, with explicit smoothing rules and a "policyholders' reasonable expectations" test. Equitable Life's collapse was triggered by promises of guaranteed annuity options that interacted badly with falling interest rates, but the underlying valuation problem was that with-profits asset shares had been allocated to bonuses on bases that left no margin for the guarantee.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Compound increases reduce to level benefits at a modified rate, period

If you remember nothing else, remember the trick: a compound-increasing benefit at rate $g$ valued at rate $i$ is the same as a level benefit valued at rate $i' = (i-g)/(1+g)$. This is the single most useful identity in variable-benefit pricing. It works for annuities, assurances, endowments, joint-life products, anything. The only place it fails is when the increase is arithmetic rather than geometric; for that you need the increasing-annuity identities.

Why simple bonuses are now rare

Simple reversionary bonuses, the historic UK standard, were displaced by compound bonuses in the 1980s and 1990s for two reasons. First, they front-loaded the office's bonus expenditure (early bonuses are a larger fraction of the cumulative bonus declared) which made smoothing the office's surplus harder. Second, they did not keep pace with inflation, which was the policyholder's main concern in that era. Compound bonuses on the cumulative sum assured become much more powerful in later years and align better with the typical accumulation of asset shares.

Asset share is the right valuation primitive

Modern with-profits practice values policies on an asset share basis: the office tracks the accumulated premiums and investment returns attributable to each policy class, then declares bonuses that bring the contractual benefit close to the asset share, with terminal bonus as the closing adjustment. The bonus rates declared each year are then a backwards-looking translation of the asset-share evolution, not a forward-looking commitment. This is the framework the PRA expects UK offices to use for both Solvency II and statutory disclosures.

Indexed annuities and the inflation cap

South African RA contracts and many UK pensions cap the inflation escalation at, say, 5% per year. The valuation cannot use the modified-rate trick directly because the cap introduces a non-linear payoff; the standard approach is to value the uncapped escalation and subtract the EPV of the embedded inflation cap (an inflation derivative). When inflation is well below the cap the correction is small; when realised inflation exceeds the cap (as in 2022-2023 in many developed economies) the correction becomes the dominant item in valuation.

Watch out Exam questions on variable benefits often mix arithmetic and geometric increases (for example, an indexed annuity with a fixed monthly admin charge subtracted, where the gross benefit increases compound but the net benefit also has an arithmetic offset). Decompose into two pieces and value each piece with the right formula. Mixing the two in one factor produces nonsensical answers.

Test yourself

For each prompt, state the growth rule and the timing convention before reaching for a formula.

  1. Define $(I\ddot{a})_x$ and $(IA)_x$ in words and as sums. State the relationship between them and the level $\ddot{a}_x$ and $A_x$.
  2. An annuity pays 1,000 in year 1 and increases by 100 each year. Express its EPV in terms of $\ddot{a}_x$ and $(I\ddot{a})_x$ with the right coefficients.
  3. Derive the modified discount rate $i' = (i - g)/(1 + g)$ for a benefit that grows geometrically at $g$ when the original discount rate is $i$. Where does the assumption of geometric growth enter?
  4. An indexed annuity escalates at 3% per year. The valuation discount rate is 5%. Compute the modified rate and explain why the EPV of the indexed annuity is less than the EPV of a level annuity of the same starting amount.
  5. Distinguish simple, compound, and terminal bonus structures in words. For each, write down the year-$t$ sum assured if the original is $S$ and the bonus rate is $b$.
  6. For the worked example, recompute the net premium with the same factors but assume the benefit grows geometrically at 3% per year instead of arithmetically by 500. State whether the new premium is higher or lower than the original.
  7. An indexed annuity has a 5% inflation cap. Realised inflation is 8% per year. Explain the structural valuation problem this creates and outline the standard approach to handling it.
  8. A 20-year endowment with $S = 100{,}000$ has compound reversionary bonus at 2% per year and a terminal bonus of 30% of the sum assured (including reversionary bonuses) at maturity. Compute the maturity benefit and compare to the original $S$.
