Reserves under profit testing

Early-year negative cashflows cannot be recognised as future profit on a deterministic basis; sterling reserves smooth the profit signature so that no year is loss-making and statutory profit emerges only as cashflows become positive.

Lecture 8 of 8 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: UP / Contingencies (standard CM1 syllabus), Chapter 28 reserves
Step 1

The 30-second version

The profit signature derived in lecture 7 typically has negative early years (new business strain) and positive later years (charges accumulate). Statutory accounting cannot recognise that as a profitable contract until something is done about the timing: by reserving enough sterling each year, the insurer holds back recognition of the positive cashflows until the early-year strain is paid for. Sterling reserves (or non-unit reserves) are the balance carried on the insurer's balance sheet for each in-force policy that, when accumulated forward, exactly meets the future net negative cashflows. Zeroisation is the practice of setting reserves so that no projected year has negative profit, ensuring the contract is statutorily acceptable from inception.

The reserve recursion is the multi-state Thiele equation specialised to the non-unit fund. Setting it backwards from the policy end ensures the policy has been fully reserved at maturity. The reserves are released as cashflows turn positive, freeing capital for new business and producing the smoothed profit emergence the supervisor expects.

Feynman test Recall lecture 7's profit signature: year 1 = -69, years 2-10 positive. If the supervisor required strict zeroisation, the insurer would hold a reserve at the end of year 1 large enough to cover the projected year-2 cashflow of 13 plus everything thereafter, plus a buffer for the projected year-3 cashflow of 25, and so on. In practice the reserve is set so that the discounted future cashflows on a prudent basis cover everything beyond the current year. The result is that statutory profit emerges only as the cashflows are realised, smoothing the new-business strain over the policy term.
Step 2

Why this matters

Reserves are the central object of life-insurance balance-sheet management. They quantify how much capital each policy ties up, when that capital is freed for reuse, and how the regulator views the solvency of the insurer. A unit-linked policy with positive economic NPV but heavy upfront strain can still bankrupt an insurer that has not reserved properly, because statutory profit (and the dividends and capital it supports) emerges only as reserves are released, not as cashflows are received.

From a regulatory perspective, reserves are the difference between solvency and insolvency. Solvency II under the European regime, the UK PRA's supervisory ladder, the South African Insurance Act 2017, and the global IAIS Insurance Capital Standard all centre on the calculation of reserves on a prudent basis. The technical reserves of the European insurance industry total roughly €11 trillion; differences in valuation method between regimes have historically driven multi-billion-pound profit transfers between insurers and supervisors.

For an actuary preparing for F107, the reserve framework also explains why bank and insurance balance sheets work differently. A bank's loan asset is recognised at amortised cost; its loss provision (under IFRS 9 ECL) is the analogue of an insurance reserve. The mechanics are similar — both are prudent estimates of future cashflows discounted at a chosen rate — but the regulatory standards differ in detail. Understanding insurance reserves is the cleanest way into bank credit-loss accounting.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture two profit signatures side by side. On the left, the unreserved signature: large negative year 1, growing positive thereafter. On the right, the reserved signature: a flat zero in years 1-3 (because reserves are being set up), then positive emergence in years 4-10. The reserves themselves are a balance-sheet item: they grow in early years, peak somewhere in the middle, and decline to zero by policy end.

The reserve at any point in time is the value of the future net cashflows on the reserving basis. It is held on the insurer's balance sheet and earns interest at the assumed reserving rate. As cashflows roll in, the reserve is released and contributes to profit; the released reserve plus the year's actual cashflows equal the period's statutory profit.

Step 4

The detail

The reserve recursion

Let $V_t$ be the sterling reserve at the end of year $t$. Let $\text{CF}_t$ be the year-$t$ cashflow on the reserving basis (premium received, charges, investment return, less expenses and benefits). The reserve evolves so that the year's profit, $\pi_t$, plus the reserve carried forward equals the existing reserve grown at interest plus the cashflow received:

$$ V_{t-1}(1 + r_v) + \text{CF}_t \;=\; V_t + \pi_t, $$

where $r_v$ is the assumed reserving interest rate. Rearranging,

$$ \pi_t \;=\; V_{t-1}(1 + r_v) + \text{CF}_t - V_t. $$

The profit in year $t$ is the interest on the prior reserve plus the cashflow received less the reserve carried forward. The reserve, by construction, ensures the policy is solvent: at maturity $V_n = 0$ and the policy has paid all its benefits.

The retrospective and prospective reserve

The reserve has two equivalent constructions, as in single-life reserving (lecture 6 of actuarial mathematics).

Retrospective reserve
The accumulated value of past net cashflows on the reserving basis: $V_t^{\text{retro}} = \sum_{s=1}^t \text{CF}_s (1 + r_v)^{t-s}$. The interpretation is "what the policy is worth based on its history".
Prospective reserve
The discounted value of future net cashflows on the reserving basis: $V_t^{\text{pros}} = \sum_{s=t+1}^n \text{CF}_s (1 + r_v)^{t-s}$ (with appropriate sign flip — the prospective reserve covers future negative cashflows). The interpretation is "what the policy will need based on its future".

Under the equivalence principle (premium set so policy makes zero economic profit on the reserving basis), $V_t^{\text{retro}} = V_t^{\text{pros}}$. The two reserves agree exactly. In practice they differ slightly because the reserving basis is prudent rather than best-estimate, so the policy makes positive profit on the actual experience; this difference is the "valuation surplus" emerging year by year.

Zeroisation

In the worked example from lecture 7, the year-1 cashflow was strongly negative because of the upfront expense. Zeroisation is the practice of setting reserves so that no projected year has negative emerging profit. Concretely, choose $V_1, V_2, \dots$ such that $\pi_t \ge 0$ for every $t$ on the projection.

The simplest zeroisation rule: set $V_t$ to the absolute value of the future expected negative cashflow, discounted back to time $t$. Iteratively, starting from the policy end and working backward:

$$ V_n = 0, \qquad V_{t-1} = \max\!\left(\frac{V_t + \pi_t^{\text{required}} - \text{CF}_t}{1 + r_v},\, 0\right), $$

where $\pi_t^{\text{required}}$ is the target profit in year $t$ (often zero in early years and positive later). The maximum operator ensures the reserve never goes negative (an insurer cannot hold a negative reserve to fund a future deficit; it would be insolvent now).

A worked example: zeroising the lecture 7 profit signature

Take the profit vector from lecture 7: $\pi = (-68.93, 12.69, 24.84, 37.55, 50.89, 64.85, 79.48, 94.81, 110.87, 127.69)$ at $r_v = 4\%$. Apply zeroisation so that years 1-3 show zero profit and years 4-10 emerge as before. The reserve at the end of year 3 must absorb the cumulative profit of years 1-3:

$$ V_3 \;=\; \underbrace{-68.93}_{\text{year 1}} \cdot (1.04)^2 + \underbrace{12.69}_{\text{year 2}} \cdot 1.04 + \underbrace{24.84}_{\text{year 3}} \;\approx\; -36.97. $$

The reserve at the end of year 3 is approximately -37 (the cumulative loss to be carried forward, sign-flipped because it is held as an asset). In practice the reserve is held as a positive balance and the profit released year by year as cashflows turn positive. The full reserve trajectory:

Under zeroisation, the insurer takes no profit until year 4 and the new business strain is recovered from future cashflows. The economic NPV at any given discount rate is similar (small difference due to the time value of reserves) but the profile of statutory profit is much flatter.

How reserves change the profit signature

Watch out Zeroisation defers but does not change total profit. An insurer that has zeroised its statutory profit must still raise enough capital to fund the year-1 strain — the reserve does not magic up cash, it merely shifts the recognition timing. Solvency II addresses this by requiring a "risk margin" on top of the best-estimate reserve, plus a separate Solvency Capital Requirement (SCR) that covers a 1-in-200 year stress. The total capital tied up is the sum of reserves plus risk margin plus SCR, and the insurer must raise this capital from somewhere (retained earnings, new equity, or reinsurance).

Supervisory vs best-estimate reserves

Modern regulatory frameworks (Solvency II in Europe, SAM in South Africa, UK Insurance Authority) require reserves to be calculated on a best-estimate basis, with a separate risk margin for non-hedgeable risks. The best-estimate reserve is the probability-weighted average of future cashflows; the risk margin is the cost of capital required to absorb the remaining variability. The combination is the "technical provisions".

Historic regimes (UK pre-2016, much of the developing world today) use a net premium method or a gross premium method with prudent assumptions throughout. The two approaches produce different reserves on the same policy: best-estimate plus risk margin is usually lower than the equivalent net-premium reserve because the latter typically uses a single conservative discount rate for everything. The transition between regimes is one of the main practical challenges of insurance regulation.

Practitioner insight The single largest sensitivity in a Solvency II reserve is the choice of discount curve. Solvency II uses a risk-free curve based on swap rates, adjusted via the "ultimate forward rate" extrapolation beyond market liquidity. A 50bps change in this curve can move a reserve by 5-10%, depending on duration. Industry has fought hard for the "matching adjustment" (a credit spread loaded onto the discount curve for matched assets) precisely because the headline change in reserves is so material. Future regulatory iterations are likely to revisit these choices as market conditions change.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Reserves are an accounting choice, not an economic one

The economic NPV of a contract is set by its cashflows and the chosen discount rate; reserves do not change the economic NPV. What reserves change is the distribution of reported profit over time and the capital tied up at each point. Higher reserves shift profit recognition later and tie up more capital; lower reserves do the reverse. The choice between regimes (Solvency II, GAAP, statutory) is a policy decision about how the costs of running a long-term contract should be distributed across the contract's lifetime.

Reserves and tax interact

In most jurisdictions, the tax base for life-insurance profit is broadly the statutory profit. Higher reserves mean lower statutory profit, which means lower tax in early years and higher tax in late years. The total tax over the policy lifetime is the same, but the timing matters because of the time value of money. This creates a perverse incentive for insurers to over-reserve: more upfront, less tax now. Regulators are aware of this and enforce specific tests on reserve adequacy. UK tax law has a "minimum required reserve" concept distinct from solvency reserves precisely to limit this.

Run-off and the close-block problem

When an insurance company closes to new business, the existing portfolio enters "run-off": premiums continue but no new business adds to the pool. The reserve trajectory at the company level becomes monotonic (it declines as the portfolio matures and pays out), and the insurer's challenge is to manage capital efficiently as the portfolio shrinks. The run-off insurer (Phoenix in the UK, Resolution Life in the US) is a recurring business model precisely because the reserve mechanics make purchased back books predictable to manage.

The future of reserves: IFRS 17 and the contractual service margin

IFRS 17, effective January 2023, replaces IFRS 4 with a unified approach to insurance contract accounting. The central concept is the contractual service margin (CSM): a balance-sheet liability that defers the contract's expected future profit and releases it over the coverage period. The CSM is essentially a deferred-profit reserve under another name; the mechanics are similar to zeroisation but on a prescribed basis. Under IFRS 17, profit emergence is more uniform across insurers and more comparable across jurisdictions than under historic regimes.

Watch out Common exam trap: "what reserve does the insurer hold under zeroisation?" expects an answer that explicitly walks the reserve back from year $n$ to year 0. Skipping the recursion or assuming the reserve is the cumulative profit so far is wrong: the reserve must reflect future expected cashflows, not historic ones. Sketch the recursion, identify the years where reserves are non-zero, and step through them.
