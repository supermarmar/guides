Profit testing

Profit emerges over time as the contract's actual cashflows roll forward; profit testing chains them deterministically and prices a contract on its discounted profit signature.

Lecture 7 of 8 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: UP / Contingencies (standard CM1 syllabus), Chapter 27 profit testing
Step 1

The 30-second version

Profit testing projects the year-by-year cashflows of a policy on a chosen valuation basis, applies the contract's mechanics (premium, allocation, AMC, expenses, benefit payments, surrenders, reserves), and computes the resulting profit vector: the profit in each year, per policy in force at the start of that year. The profit signature is the profit vector multiplied by the probability of being in force at the start of each year. Discounting the signature back to inception at the risk discount rate gives the net present value (NPV) of the contract; the rate that makes NPV zero is the internal rate of return (IRR).

Profit testing is the modern actuarial pricing and product-design tool. Premium rates are usually set so that the NPV reaches a target margin; product features are added or stripped based on their marginal impact on the profit signature. Reserves (lecture 8) are set to ensure that early-year negative profits do not interact badly with statutory recognition rules.

Feynman test Imagine a 10-year unit-linked savings policy. The insurer's profit in year 1 might be -100 (after the heavy initial expense), year 2 might be +60, year 3 might be +85, growing to +300 by year 10 as the AMC base grows. That sequence is the profit vector. The probability that the policy is still in force at year $t$ is the survival probability times the persistence probability; multiplying gives the profit signature. The NPV is the discounted sum at, say, 8% per year (the insurer's hurdle rate). If the NPV is positive the product makes money; if negative it loses money. The IRR is the discount rate at which NPV is exactly zero, which tells the underwriter the maximum hurdle rate the contract can support.
Step 2

Why this matters

Profit testing is the practical heart of life-insurance pricing. Every major product launch, repricing exercise, or with-profits bonus declaration runs through a profit-testing model. The model takes the chosen valuation basis as input (mortality, expenses, investment returns, surrenders, tax) and computes the expected profit emergence. The output drives pricing, product design, capital allocation, and management's evaluation of new business strain.

The framework also clarifies an important conceptual distinction: value of new business versus statutory profit. The NPV computed by profit testing is the insurer's economic value of writing the contract. Statutory profit, recognised under FRS 102 / IFRS 17 / Solvency II, is a different quantity governed by reserving rules. The difference is the "new business strain": negative statutory profit in year 1 reflecting upfront expenses and reserve setup, even when the economic NPV is positive. Lecture 8 covers how reserves are set to manage this strain.

For the F107 reader: the profit signature is also the building block of the insurance balance sheet. Discounted at the risk discount rate it gives the value of in-force business (VIF). Reported VIF is the disclosure metric for embedded-value reporting (the predecessor of IFRS 17 and still used by some insurers).

Step 3

The mental model

Picture a time grid. In each year, money flows in (premium received, charge income, investment return on reserves) and out (expenses paid, benefits paid, reserve increase). The net is that year's profit. Probability-weighted and discounted, those annual nets sum to the contract's NPV.

The two assumptions that drive every step of the projection are the valuation basis (the parameters used to project cashflows: best-estimate mortality, expenses, returns) and the risk discount rate (the rate at which the resulting profit signature is discounted). The valuation basis is technical; the risk discount rate is a business decision that reflects the insurer's required return on capital.

Step 4

The detail

The cashflow projection

For each policy year $t$ in the projection, build up the in-force cashflow per policy:

$$ \text{CF}_t \;=\; \underbrace{P_t}_{\text{premium}} \;+\; \underbrace{I_t}_{\text{investment return on reserves}} \;-\; \underbrace{E_t}_{\text{expenses}} \;-\; \underbrace{B_t}_{\text{benefits paid}} \;-\; \underbrace{(V_t - V_{t-1})}_{\text{reserve increase}}. $$

For unit-linked policies the components specialise. Premium income is $(1-\alpha) P_t$ (the non-allocated portion). Investment return $I_t$ is the return on the non-unit reserves. Expenses are initial in year 1 and renewal in subsequent years. Benefits paid are the death-benefit top-up $q_{x+t-1}(S - F_t)$ plus, if relevant, the maturity payment from the non-unit fund.

The profit vector and the profit signature

Profit vector $\boldsymbol{\pi} = (\pi_1, \pi_2, \dots, \pi_n)$
Per-policy profit in each year, given the policy is in force at the start of that year. Each $\pi_t = \text{CF}_t$ from the cashflow projection above.
Profit signature $\boldsymbol{\sigma} = (\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \dots, \sigma_n)$
Per-original-policy profit in each year, weighted by the probability of being in force at the start of that year: $\sigma_t = {}_{t-1} p_x \cdot \pi_t$. (When there are multiple decrements, this is the persistence probability accounting for death, surrender, lapse, etc.)

The profit signature differs from the profit vector by the persistency adjustment: an early-year profit accrues to the full original cohort; a late-year profit accrues only to those still in force. For policies with material lapse, the persistency adjustment is substantial.

NPV and IRR

Discount the profit signature back to inception at the chosen risk discount rate $r_{rd}$:

$$ \text{NPV} \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^n \frac{\sigma_t}{(1 + r_{rd})^t}. $$

The NPV is the value to the insurer of writing one policy. Positive NPV means the contract is profitable at the assumed discount rate; negative NPV means it loses money. The internal rate of return (IRR) is the discount rate that makes NPV zero, indicating the maximum return the contract supports:

$$ \text{IRR} \;=\; r^* \text{ such that } \sum_{t=1}^n \frac{\sigma_t}{(1 + r^*)^t} = 0. $$

The profit margin is NPV expressed as a fraction of the EPV of premiums:

$$ \text{Profit margin} \;=\; \frac{\text{NPV}}{\sum_{t=1}^n \frac{{}_{t-1} p_x \cdot P_t}{(1 + r_{rd})^{t-1}}}. $$

UK industry profit margins on retail savings products typically run in the 2-6% range; protection products 5-15%; annuity products 1-4%. Wide variation reflects the different competitive dynamics and the different sensitivity of each product class to underlying assumptions.

The valuation basis vs the experience basis

Profit testing projects cashflows on a chosen basis. The most common conventions:

Best-estimate basis
Each assumption is the actuary's central estimate of the future experience. Mortality from a recent insured-lives experience study, expenses from the company's expense investigation, investment returns from corporate planning, surrenders from a historical lapse study. The profit signature on this basis is the "best estimate" of future profits.
Pricing basis
Best estimate plus margins for adverse deviation. Mortality with a 10% prudence loading, expenses with a 10% buffer, conservative investment returns. The profit signature on this basis is what the insurer would book if experience came in exactly at the prudent assumptions; the difference between best-estimate and pricing profit is the prudence margin.
Statutory basis
The basis required by the supervisor for solvency reporting. Often the most prudent of the three (matching adjustment, risk margin, ultimate forward rate under Solvency II; valuation interest rate under traditional regimes). Used for reserve calculation in lecture 8.
Watch out Profit testing is a deterministic exercise. The basis is a set of point estimates; the signature is the consequence on the basis. A common error is to call the basis "the central estimate" when the basis is actually a set of prudent assumptions producing a margin. The basis matters as much as the model — change the basis and the same product looks very different.

A worked example: profit testing a unit-linked policy with death benefit

Practitioner insight The single largest lever in unit-linked profit testing is the assumed investment return on the unit fund, because it compounds the AMC base. A 1pp uplift in fund return (from 6% to 7%) typically raises the NPV by 15-25% for a typical unit-linked policy. The single largest sensitivity for protection products is mortality, where a 10% mortality stress can flip the NPV from positive to negative. Knowing which lever to push hardest for product-design negotiation is the value the actuary adds beyond the mechanical calculation.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The choice of risk discount rate is a business decision, not a market one

The risk discount rate is not the market discount rate; it is the insurer's required return on capital for the line of business. UK practice typically sets it at the 10-year gilt yield plus a margin of 4-6% for protection products and 3-5% for savings products. The Solvency II framework instead values insurance liabilities using a risk-free rate adjusted for matching or volatility adjustments, and the embedded value framework uses a separate "value of in-force" discount rate. The three are different objects and a profit testing model has to be clear which is being computed.

Sensitivity analysis is mandatory

A profit signature on a single basis is not a complete answer. Standard practice runs the same projection on stressed bases — mortality plus or minus 10%, expenses plus 20%, surrenders plus 50%, investment returns minus 1% — and reports the NPV under each. The pattern of sensitivities is the diagnostic: a stable product has a narrow range of NPVs; an unstable one swings widely. The 2008-2010 collapse of variable-annuity profits in the US was driven by sensitivity to equity returns that had not been correctly tested before launch.

Profit signature versus profit declaration

The signature is the economic profit by year. Statutory profit recognition is different: under most reserving regimes, profit is deferred to recognise the new business strain over the policy term rather than upfront. The "deferred acquisition cost" (DAC) under FRS 102 / IFRS 17 is exactly this: a balance-sheet asset that smooths the upfront expense over the policy's life. The DAC asset increases reported profit in early years and reduces it in late years. The economic NPV is unaffected by this accounting choice; the timing of reported profits is.

The IRR is not the right hurdle in isolation

The IRR is a popular summary but it has known pathologies. Two products with the same NPV can have very different IRRs depending on cashflow timing. The IRR also assumes reinvestment of intermediate profits at the IRR, which is rarely correct. Modern practice prefers NPV-based metrics (return on capital, value of new business / EPV of premium) over IRR-only criteria. Use IRR as a sanity check, not a decision rule.

Watch out Profit testing assumes a single deterministic basis. For products with embedded options (minimum-return guarantees, GMWBs, with-profits bonus options) the deterministic answer is wrong: option values come from variance, which deterministic projection ignores. Such products require stochastic profit testing (Monte Carlo or analytical valuation of the embedded options), which is a separate exam topic (CM2 / SP6) and out of scope here.

Test yourself

For each prompt, state which assumption set the question is asking about (valuation basis, pricing basis, statutory basis) before computing.

  3. For the worked example, recompute the NPV at a risk discount rate of (a) 6%, (b) 10%. Comment on the sensitivity.
  4. Define the IRR of a profit signature. State why two products with the same NPV can have different IRRs.
  5. The profit margin is defined as NPV divided by EPV of premium. Explain why this normalisation is useful and what it would mean if the EPV of premium were small.
  6. For the worked example, compute the profit margin at the 8% risk discount rate.
  7. The deferred acquisition cost (DAC) asset is added to the balance sheet to smooth reported profit. Does it change the economic NPV computed by profit testing? Explain.
