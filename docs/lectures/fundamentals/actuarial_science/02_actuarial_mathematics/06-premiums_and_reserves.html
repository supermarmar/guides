Premiums and reserves

The premium is set so expected income equals expected outgo at policy inception. The reserve, computed every reporting date afterwards, is what the insurer owes on the obligation it has taken on.

Lecture 6 of 6 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: UP / Actuarial Mathematics Summary.pdf §8, §9
Step 1

The 30-second version

A gross premium $G$ is the regular amount the policyholder pays so that, in expectation under the pricing basis, the present value of all future receipts (premiums) equals the present value of all future obligations (benefits, expenses, commission). One equation in one unknown ($G$). This is the equation of value.

A reserve is what the insurer must hold on the balance sheet at any later time $t$ against the future obligations of the policy. Two equivalent definitions. The prospective reserve looks forward: future outgo minus future income, in EPV. The retrospective reserve looks back: past income minus past outgo, accumulated to today on a survivorship basis. Under the pricing basis, the two agree (there is a proof to show why).

The pricing basis is set conservatively at issue to embed a profit margin. The valuation basis is updated over time as experience emerges. When the bases differ, the prospective and retrospective reserves diverge, and that divergence is the actuarial gain or loss on the policy to date.

Feynman test A 40-year-old buys a whole-life policy with sum assured £200,000 and pays £2,000 per year for life. At issue the insurer says: the present value of all the £2,000-per-year premiums I will collect, in expectation, equals the present value of the £200,000 I will pay on death, plus my costs of running this policy. Five years later, the policyholder is 45 and the question is: how much money should I hold today against the future £200,000 payment, less the present value of the £2,000-per-year premiums I will still collect? That number is the reserve. Looking forward (future out minus future in) and looking backward (past in minus past out, rolled up) give the same answer when the basis has not moved.
What is beyond the source The source PDF derives the equality of prospective and retrospective reserves in compact algebra. This lecture adds the verbal interpretation at each step, draws out the pricing-versus-valuation basis distinction (which the source mentions but does not motivate), and traces reserves over the first 30 years of a whole-life policy with a chart.
Step 2

Why this matters

Pricing is a one-shot calculation. Reserving is a perpetual exercise. Every reporting date, every policy on the books must be valued with mortality, expense, and interest-rate assumptions that may have moved since pricing. Reserves are by far the largest line item on the balance sheet of any life insurance company. Aviva's UK life book carries around £200 billion of reserves; PIC's bulk-annuity book is around £45 billion; M&G's is a similar size. The number that gets reported each quarter is a sum of millions of policy-level prospective reserves.

Equitable Life is the classic cautionary tale. Through the 1990s the company sold pension policies with guaranteed annuity rates ("GAR" policies) and reserved for them on the assumption that those guarantees were worthless. When interest rates fell, the guarantees became valuable, the reserve was suddenly inadequate, and the company became insolvent. The actuarial mathematics was correct; the basis was wrong. The lesson sits at the heart of this lecture: the formulae are robust, the assumptions are not.

The phrase "do not include initial expenses in the prospective reserve" is the single most common trap in exam questions. The initial expense $I$ has already been paid; it cannot be a future cash flow. The retrospective reserve does include $I$, because it was a past expense. Carry that distinction with you.

Step 3

The mental model

Three concepts to keep separate:

Equation of value
At policy inception, PV of all expected receipts equals PV of all expected obligations. One equation in one unknown ($G$). Defines the premium.
Prospective reserve
${}_t V^{\text{pro}} = \text{EPV(future benefits)} + \text{EPV(future expenses)} - \text{EPV(future premiums)}$. Initial expenses excluded (in the past). Looks forward from time $t$.
Retrospective reserve
${}_t V^{\text{retro}} = (D_x / D_{x+t})\bigl[\text{EPV(past premiums)} - \text{EPV(past benefits + expenses)}\bigr]$. Initial expenses included. Accumulates past cash flows from time 0 to time $t$, weighted by survival.

The accumulation factor $D_x / D_{x+t}$ in the retrospective formula is the reciprocal of the pure endowment $D_{x+t}/D_x = v^t {}_t p_x$. Multiplying by it converts a present value at time 0 to a fund value at time $t$ on a per-survivor basis.

The pricing basis versus valuation basis distinction: the pricing basis is fixed at issue and never changes. The valuation basis is reset at every reporting date to reflect updated mortality and interest assumptions. When the two diverge, the prospective reserve under the valuation basis differs from the retrospective reserve under the pricing basis. The difference is the gain (or loss) embedded in the policy to date.

Step 4

The detail

The equation of value

For a whole-life policy with sum assured $S$, paid at end of year of death, premiums $G$ per year in advance for life, initial expense $I$, renewal expense $e$ per year (paid in arrears while in force), claim expense $f$ paid at end of year of death, and commission $\pi$ as a fraction of each premium:

$$ G\,\ddot{a}_x \;=\; S\,A_x + I + e\,a_x + f\,A_x + \pi\,G\,\ddot{a}_x. $$

Read left to right: PV of premium income equals PV of the death benefit plus the initial expense plus the renewal expenses plus the claim expenses plus the commission. Collecting the $G$ terms:

$$ G \;=\; \frac{S\,A_x + I + e\,a_x + f\,A_x}{(1 - \pi)\,\ddot{a}_x}. $$

For a term or temporary assurance:

$$ G\,\ddot{a}_{x:\overline{n|}} \;=\; S\,A^1_{x:\overline{n|}} + I + e\,a_{x:\overline{n-1|}} + f\,A^1_{x:\overline{n|}} + \pi\,G\,\ddot{a}_{x:\overline{n|}}. $$

The $a_{x:\overline{n-1|}}$ in the renewal-expense term reflects a convention: the policy is in force for $n$ premium payments (times $0, 1, \dots, n-1$) but only $n-1$ year-end renewal events.

For an endowment:

$$ G\,\ddot{a}_{x:\overline{n|}} \;=\; S\,A_{x:\overline{n|}} + I + e\,a_{x:\overline{n-1|}} + f\,A_{x:\overline{n|}} + \pi\,G\,\ddot{a}_{x:\overline{n|}}. $$

The prospective reserve

Looking forward from time $t$ on the valuation basis:

$$ {}_t V^{\text{pro}} \;=\; \underbrace{S\,A_{x+t} + e\,\ddot{a}_{x+t} + f\,A_{x+t}}_{\text{future outflows}} \;-\; \underbrace{G\,\ddot{a}_{x+t}}_{\text{future inflows}}. $$

Initial expenses are not included. They are in the past at every $t > 0$. Including them is the most common reserving error.

For an endowment policy:

$$ {}_t V^{\text{pro}} \;=\; S\,A_{x+t:\overline{n-t|}} + e\,\ddot{a}_{x+t:\overline{n-t|}} + f\,A_{x+t:\overline{n-t|}} - G\,\ddot{a}_{x+t:\overline{n-t|}}. $$

Every annuity and assurance symbol has its term shortened to the remaining $n - t$.

The retrospective reserve

Looking backward from time $t$, accumulate past premiums minus past benefits minus past expenses on a survivorship basis:

$$ {}_t V^{\text{retro}} \;=\; \frac{D_x}{D_{x+t}}\!\left[ G\,\ddot{a}_{x:\overline{t|}} - \Bigl(S\,A^1_{x:\overline{t|}} + I + e\,\ddot{a}_{x:\overline{t|}} + f\,A^1_{x:\overline{t|}}\Bigr)\right]. $$

The minuend is the EPV at time 0 of premiums collected in years 0 to $t - 1$. The subtrahend collects benefits paid out on deaths in $[0, t]$ (using term-assurance $A^1$), the initial expense $I$, the renewal expenses, and the claim expenses. The factor $D_x / D_{x+t}$ accumulates the net to time $t$ on a survivorship basis.

The initial expense $I$ is included. It was paid in the past, so it is a past expense.

Equality of prospective and retrospective

The theorem: if (a) the same basis is used for both reserves, and (b) that basis is the pricing basis, then ${}_t V^{\text{pro}} = {}_t V^{\text{retro}}$.

Proof (expense terms omitted for clarity; the same logic carries them through):

$$ {}_t V^{\text{retro}} \;=\; \frac{D_x}{D_{x+t}}\!\left[ G\,\ddot{a}_{x:\overline{t|}} - A^1_{x:\overline{t|}} \right]. $$

Use the decomposition identities:

$$ \ddot{a}_{x:\overline{t|}} \;=\; \ddot{a}_x - \frac{D_{x+t}}{D_x}\ddot{a}_{x+t}, \qquad A^1_{x:\overline{t|}} \;=\; A_x - \frac{D_{x+t}}{D_x}A_{x+t}. $$

Substitute and expand:

$$ \begin{aligned} {}_t V^{\text{retro}} &= \frac{D_x}{D_{x+t}}\!\left[ G(\ddot{a}_x - \tfrac{D_{x+t}}{D_x}\ddot{a}_{x+t}) - (A_x - \tfrac{D_{x+t}}{D_x}A_{x+t})\right] \\[2pt] &= \frac{D_x}{D_{x+t}}(G\,\ddot{a}_x - A_x) - G\,\ddot{a}_{x+t} + A_{x+t} \\[2pt] &= 0 - G\,\ddot{a}_{x+t} + A_{x+t} \qquad \text{(pricing equation: } G\ddot{a}_x = A_x \text{)} \\[2pt] &= A_{x+t} - G\,\ddot{a}_{x+t} \\[2pt] &= {}_t V^{\text{pro}}. \end{aligned} $$

The single step that fixes the equality is the pricing equation at $t = 0$. If the basis is not the pricing basis, the term $\frac{D_x}{D_{x+t}}(G\,\ddot{a}_x - A_x)$ does not vanish, and the two reserves differ by exactly this amount.

The reserve recursion

From $A_x = v(q_x + p_x A_{x+1})$ and the prospective definition, you can derive a one-period reserve recursion. For a whole-life policy with level premium $G$ and unit sum assured:

$$ {}_{t+1}V \;=\; \frac{({}_t V + G)(1 + i) - q_{x+t}}{p_{x+t}}. $$

Verbal: take this year's reserve plus this year's premium, accumulate one year of interest, subtract the expected death benefit paid this year, divide by the survival probability (to convert to per-survivor for next year). This is the recursion every actuarial system runs to compute reserves period by period.

A worked example

A 40-year-old buys a whole-life policy with sum assured £100,000, no expenses for simplicity. Pricing basis $i = 4\%$. Given $\ddot{a}_{40} = 17.50$ and $A_{40} = 0.3269$, the level premium is

$$ G \;=\; \frac{100{,}000 \cdot 0.3269}{17.50} \;=\; \pounds 1{,}868\text{ per year}. $$

Five years later, at age 45, given $\ddot{a}_{45} = 16.20$ and $A_{45} = 0.3769$ on the same basis:

5 V pro = 100,000 * A_45 - G * ä_45 = 100,000 * 0.3769 - 1,868 * 16.20 = 37,690 - 30,262 = £7,428 Interpretation: Future obligations (PV at age 45 of £100,000 paid on death): £37,690 Future premium income (PV of £1,868 per year for life): £30,262 Reserve the insurer must hold today: £7,428 The reserve is positive because mortality cost rises with age while the premium stays level. Early on, premiums exceed the EPV of that year's expected benefits and the reserve grows. Later, premiums fall short of expected benefits, and the reserve is drawn down to fund the gap.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The basis is the whole game

Premiums and reserves are deterministic functions of the basis. Change the mortality table by a few percentage points and the reserve moves by tens of per cent. Change the interest rate by a quarter point and the reserve moves by similar amounts. The actuarial calculation is robust; the controversial parts are the assumptions feeding it. A reserve is a number whose stability depends entirely on the stability of the basis behind it.

Prospective is the legal definition; retrospective is the bookkeeper's view

Accounting standards (IFRS 17, US GAAP, statutory reporting) define the reserve prospectively. The retrospective version is conceptually equivalent but operationally rarely needed. The equality theorem matters because it justifies updating the basis without rebuilding the entire history of the policy: the prospective reserve on the new basis is the right answer regardless of what basis was used at issue.

The pricing basis is conservative; the valuation basis is realistic

Pricing typically uses pessimistic mortality (slightly higher than expected) and conservative interest (slightly lower than expected) to embed a profit margin. Valuation uses best-estimate assumptions. The reserve on the valuation basis is therefore lower than on the pricing basis, and the difference is the deferred profit (the "value of in-force"). Accounting standards differ on how much of this gap can be released to current profit.

The classic exam trap

Watch out Every exam question on prospective reserves includes the phrase "do not include initial expenses". This is because the prospective reserve looks forward, and initial expenses are by definition in the past. The retrospective reserve does include them. If you find yourself including $I$ in a prospective formula, stop and rewrite from first principles.

Equitable Life in one paragraph

Practitioner insight Equitable Life sold pension policies with guaranteed annuity rates (GARs) through the 1990s and reserved on the assumption that the guarantees would never bind. When interest rates fell, the guarantees became deeply in the money and the reserve was suddenly inadequate. The arithmetic in this lecture was correct; the basis was wrong. The fund went into run-off in 2000 and was eventually transferred to Utmost in 2020. The lesson: every reserve is a guess about the future. Get the guess wrong and the firm fails, even if every formula in the textbook was applied perfectly.
Step 6

Test yourself

Try to answer each prompt out loud, in plain English, as if to a curious finance graduate. Do not look back at the lecture until you have tried.

  1. State the equation of value for a whole-life policy with sum assured $S$, level premium $G$, initial expense $I$, renewal expense $e$, claim expense $f$, commission rate $\pi$. Solve for $G$.
  2. State the prospective reserve ${}_t V^{\text{pro}}$ for a whole-life policy and explain in one sentence why initial expenses are not included.
  3. State the retrospective reserve ${}_t V^{\text{retro}}$ and explain the role of the accumulation factor $D_x / D_{x+t}$.
  4. Prove ${}_t V^{\text{retro}} = {}_t V^{\text{pro}}$ under the pricing basis. Identify the precise step where the equation of value enters.
  5. Under what circumstances do the two reserves differ? What does the difference represent?
  6. Derive the reserve recursion ${}_{t+1}V = (({}_t V + G)(1 + i) - q_{x+t}) / p_{x+t}$ from the prospective definition.
  7. If the valuation basis assumes 10 per cent lower mortality than the pricing basis, would you expect the prospective reserve to be higher or lower? Justify in two lines.
  8. In two sentences, explain why Equitable Life's reserve was inadequate even though the actuarial mathematics was applied correctly.

  • IFRS 17 valuation of insurance contracts uses prospective reserving with explicit decomposition into best-estimate liability, risk adjustment, and contractual service margin.
  • The reserve recursion is the actuarial analogue of the bank's loan-amortisation schedule: each period the running balance is rolled forward at interest, debited for cash outflows, credited for cash inflows.
  • Pension scheme liabilities measured on a buy-out, technical-provisions, or low-dependency basis are each a different valuation basis applied to the same prospective formula.
