Life tables

A life table is the survival model put into a spreadsheet. The select variant says: a 40-year-old who has just passed a medical is not the same as a 40-year-old who has not.

Lecture 2 of 6 Reading time, 20 minutes Source: UP / Actuarial Mathematics Summary.pdf §2, §4
Step 1

The 30-second version

A life table tabulates $l_x$, the number of people alive at age $x$ starting from a notional cohort of $l_0$. Every probability from the previous lecture is a ratio of two cells in this table. The select variant adds a second axis: the duration $s$ since the policy was underwritten. A life recently underwritten is healthier than a "general" life their age because they have just passed a medical. After a select period of $s$ years the effect wears off and the select life merges into the ordinary (or ultimate) part of the table.

Life expectancy is the mean of the future lifetime. The curtate version $e_x$ counts whole years; the complete version $\mathring{e}_x$ counts the fractional year of death too. Under uniform deaths within each year of age, $\mathring{e}_x \approx e_x + 1/2$.

Feynman test Two 40-year-olds walk into an insurance broker. The first has just passed a medical and applied for cover today. The second was offered cover at age 35, passed the medical then, and is still on the books five years later. Both are 40 today. But the first one's medical contains live information; the second one's medical is stale. The life table says these two have different mortality probabilities for the next year, even though they are the same age. The first is a select life of duration 0; the second is a 40-year-old in the ultimate table.
What is beyond the source The source PDF gives the select-table formulas without explaining the underwriting story behind them. This lecture adds that story, draws the two-dimensional structure of the table, and derives $\mathring{e}_x \approx e_x + 1/2$ from UDD rather than asserting it.
Step 2

Why this matters

UK annuity providers rebooked their mortality assumptions in the late 2010s when life expectancy at age 65 stopped improving. The actual change in $l_x$ at older ages was small, single-digit percent, but it propagated through the annuity-pricing formula into reserve releases worth billions. Aviva, Legal & General, and Just Group all booked material gains. The asset book had not changed; only the table had.

Going the other way: in 2020 to 2022, COVID-related deaths surged at the older end of the table. Annuity reserves were released again, although the question of whether the surge is a permanent shift or a temporary anomaly remains open. Either way, the line item that swung was a function of $l_x$ at ages 75 to 95.

The select adjustment is not cosmetic either. For a healthy 40-year-old who has just passed an insurer's medical, the probability of dying in the next year is materially lower than for a random 40-year-old in the population. Pricing a 5-year term assurance on a freshly underwritten life using ultimate mortality would overstate the expected claim cost by a non-trivial fraction. The select discount is the price of admission for going through the underwriting test.

Step 3

The mental model

A select table is a two-dimensional array. Rows are entry ages (the age at which underwriting happened, written $[x]$ in square brackets). Columns are durations since entry. After the select period $s$ the table collapses into a single ultimate column shared across rows.

The chart below shows what the select effect looks like in practice. The select-duration-0 curve sits noticeably below the ultimate curve at every age. As duration grows, the select curve drifts upward until it merges into the ultimate.

Step 4

The detail

The ultimate life table

The simplest table is one-dimensional. An array of $l_x$ values from $x = 0$ to $x = \omega$, plus the derived columns $d_x = l_x - l_{x+1}$, $q_x = d_x / l_x$, $p_x = 1 - q_x$. Every probability from lecture 1 is a ratio of two $l$-values. This is the ultimate table.

The select life table

The select table is two-dimensional. The entry age, written $[x]$, marks the age at which underwriting happened. The duration $t$ since entry is the second axis. The cell is $l_{[x]+t}$. The select period $s$ is the number of years over which select mortality differs from ultimate mortality.

The key empirical fact: for the first $s$ years after underwriting, $l_{[x]+t} > l_{x+t}$ for the same attained age $x + t$. The select life is healthier. After $s$ years the two have merged: $l_{[x]+s} = l_{x+s}$, and from then on we just write $l_{x+t}$ for $t \ge s$.

Probabilities on a select table

For a select life $[x]$ the probability of dying within $t$ years splits into two cases:

If $t \le s$ (still inside the select period):

$$ {}_t q_{[x]} \;=\; \frac{l_{[x]} - l_{[x]+t}}{l_{[x]}}. $$

If $t > s$ (the life has aged out of selection):

$$ {}_t q_{[x]} \;=\; \frac{l_{[x]} - l_{x+t}}{l_{[x]}}. $$

The numerator switches from select form $l_{[x]+t}$ to ultimate form $l_{x+t}$ at the boundary $t = s$. The denominator is always $l_{[x]}$. The boundary case $t = s$ is the consistency test: $l_{[x]+s} = l_{x+s}$ by construction, so both formulas agree there.

Multiplicativity in the select setting

The same rule from lecture 1, with care:

$$ {}_{m+n} p_{[x]} \;=\; {}_n p_{[x]} \cdot {}_m p_{[x]+n}. $$

If $[x] + n$ is still within the select period ($n < s$), the second factor uses the select column. If $[x] + n$ has aged out ($n \ge s$), the second factor is an ultimate probability at attained age $x + n$:

$$ {}_m p_{[x]+n} \;=\; {}_m p_{x+n} \quad \text{when } n \ge s. $$

Curtate life expectancy

The expected number of whole future years lived by $(x)$:

$$ e_x \;=\; E[K_x] \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{\omega - x - 1} k \cdot {}_k p_x \cdot q_{x+k}. $$

Rearrange by noting that a life surviving $K_x = k$ years contributes $k = 1 + 1 + \dots + 1$ to the expectation, which is the same as contributing 1 for each of years $1, 2, \dots, k$. Equivalently, ${}_k p_x = P(K_x \ge k)$ and $E[K_x] = \sum_{k \ge 1} P(K_x \ge k)$. The much simpler form:

$$ e_x \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{\omega - x - 1} {}_k p_x. $$

Complete life expectancy

The expected total future lifetime, including the fractional year of death:

$$ \mathring{e}_x \;=\; E[T_x] \;=\; \int_0^{\omega - x} {}_t p_x\, dt. $$

This is the continuous analogue: integrate the survival function rather than sum it.

The half-year relationship

Under UDD within each year of age,

$$ \mathring{e}_x \;\approx\; e_x + \tfrac{1}{2}. $$

Derivation. Split $T_x = K_x + S$ where $K_x$ is the curtate part and $S \in [0, 1)$ is the fractional year of death. So $E[T_x] = E[K_x] + E[S]$. Under UDD, deaths are uniform across the year of death, so conditional on $K_x = k$, $S$ is uniform on $[0, 1)$ with $E[S \mid K_x = k] = 1/2$. Unconditionally $E[S] = 1/2$.

The approximation is exact under UDD, near-exact otherwise. The half-year matters for monthly-paid annuities, where it propagates into the $(m - 1)/(2m)$ correction in lecture 3.

The flick from select to ultimate at $t > s$ is the only delicate step. In a printed table the ultimate column is usually broken out separately, indexed by attained age, exactly because $l_{43}$ does not appear in the [40] row (column $t = 3$ is past the select period) but does appear in the [41] row at column $t = 2$. Both refer to the same number.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Select tables exist because underwriting carries information

If insurance were sold without medical screening, every life of attained age $x$ would have the same mortality and there would be no select effect. The select adjustment is the price of admission: the moment you underwrite, you stratify your portfolio into healthier-than-average lives. The size of the select discount measures the value of the underwriting test.

The select period is empirical, not theoretical

Two years for life assurance, five years for income protection, longer for some impaired-life annuities. The right select period is whatever length of time it takes for the post-underwriting cohort's mortality to drift back to ultimate. Published tables fix a convention (often 2 or 5 years) but the convergence is gradual rather than sharp.

Mortality improvement adds a third axis

A modern projection table extends the life table with a calendar-year axis: $q_{x,t}$ depends on the year $t$ as well as the age $x$. A 60-year-old in 2050 will be subject to lower mortality than a 60-year-old in 2020. Annuity reserves for in-force business are routinely revalued as new projections are published. The conceptual machinery is unchanged; the table just has another argument.

The standard tables in practice

Practitioner insight UK practice uses CMI (Continuous Mortality Investigation) tables for life assurance (AM92, AMC00, and successors) and CMI annuitant tables for annuities. SA practice uses ASSA tables, typically calibrated to the SA85-90 family with periodic updates. US practice uses SOA tables, most recently 2017 CSO and PRI. The structure across tables is identical; what differs is the level and shape of mortality at each age, which is what gets calibrated to each market's experience.

A common F107 trap

Watch out Candidates often forget that the denominator in select probabilities is always $l_{[x]}$, the original cohort size, not the cell at the current duration. The numerator switches between select and ultimate at the boundary $t = s$, but the denominator does not move. If you find your probability summing to more than 1 across $k$, you are probably dividing by the wrong cell.

Test yourself

Try to answer each prompt out loud, in plain English, as if to a curious junior actuary. Do not look back at the lecture until you have tried.

  1. In one paragraph, explain why a select life of attained age 40 has different mortality from an ultimate life of age 40.
  2. State both formulas for ${}_t q_{[x]}$ (one for $t \le s$, one for $t > s$). Show they agree at the boundary $t = s$.
  3. Why does the multiplicativity identity ${}_{m+n} p_{[x]} = {}_n p_{[x]} \cdot {}_m p_{[x]+n}$ have a "boundary case" where the second factor switches to an ultimate probability?
  4. State the two life expectancy formulas $e_x$ and $\mathring{e}_x$, and explain in one sentence why $\mathring{e}_x \approx e_x + 1/2$ under UDD.
  5. Given $l_{[60]} = 9{,}680$, $l_{[60]+1} = 9{,}620$, $l_{62} = 9{,}540$, $l_{63} = 9{,}450$ and select period 2, compute ${}_1 q_{[60]}$, ${}_2 q_{[60]}$, ${}_3 q_{[60]}$.
  6. Why is a select adjustment unnecessary in a population-wide life table such as the one ONS publishes for England and Wales?
  7. Explain in two sentences why a 2018 downward revision of UK annuitant mortality improvement projections released billions in reserves.
