Life annuities

An annuity is a stream of payments that lasts as long as you do. Its price is a sum of discount factors weighted by your chance of being alive to collect each one.

Lecture 3 of 6 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: UP / Actuarial Mathematics Summary.pdf §5
Step 1

The 30-second version

A life annuity pays a stream of payments while the annuitant is alive. The expected present value (EPV) at age $x$ is built from one ingredient: the probability that the $k$-th payment is actually made, ${}_k p_x$, multiplied by the discount factor $v^k = (1+i)^{-k}$. Sum across all payment times. That single recipe gives you every annuity in the syllabus.

The variants differ on three axes: when payments start and stop (whole, temporary, deferred, guaranteed), when within the year (annuity-due paying at the start, annuity-immediate paying at the end, or continuous), and how often (annual, monthly, $m$-thly, or continuous). Pick one option from each axis and you have specified the contract.

Feynman test A retired schoolteacher buys a pension. The insurer hands them £1 every January, for as long as they are still alive on that date. The pound paid at the start of year $k$ has present value $v^k$ today, but the insurer only pays it if the retiree is alive then, which has probability ${}_k p_x$. So the expected cost of year $k$'s payment is $v^k \cdot {}_k p_x$. Sum over $k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ and you have the price of the pension. That sum is $\ddot{a}_x$.
What is beyond the source The source PDF lists the six annuity formulas one after another. This lecture organises them along two axes (timing of payment, structure of the term) and derives the monthly correction $(m - 1)/(2m)$ from a UDD argument rather than asserting it.
Step 2

Why this matters

Life annuities are one of the two basic building blocks of insurance pricing (the other is life assurance, in the next lecture). Pensions, lifetime income guarantees, defined-benefit pension scheme liabilities, structured personal-injury settlements, all reduce to the EPV of an annuity. The UK individual annuity market sells around £6 billion a year; the bulk-purchase annuity (BPA) market, where pension schemes offload their liabilities to insurers, transacts £40 to £60 billion annually. Every one of those transactions prices off $\ddot{a}_x$.

The annuity-due versus annuity-immediate distinction is not notational fussiness either. Premiums are paid in advance (annuity-due) and benefits in arrears (annuity-immediate). The identity $\ddot{a}_x = 1 + a_x$ is the bridge between the two sides of an insurance contract. When you write the equation of value in lecture 6, premium income on the left uses $\ddot{a}$ and benefit outflow on the right uses $a$.

The monthly correction $(m - 1)/(2m)$ looks small until you remember that real pensions pay monthly, not annually. A 60-year-old male buying a level annuity might see $\ddot{a}_{60} \approx 14$ and the monthly correction $(12 - 1)/24 = 0.458$, which is 3 per cent of the annuity factor and therefore 3 per cent on the price.

Step 3

The mental model

Three axes organise the entire chapter. First, the term structure:

Whole
Payments for the rest of the annuitant's life. Symbol $\ddot{a}_x$ or $a_x$ or $\bar{a}_x$.
Temporary
Payments for at most $n$ years, or until earlier death. Symbol $\ddot{a}_{x:\overline{n|}}$.
Deferred
Nothing for $n$ years, then a whole annuity from age $x + n$. Symbol ${}_{n|}\ddot{a}_x$.
Guaranteed
At least $n$ years of payment (guaranteed period), then for life. Symbol $\ddot{a}_{\overline{x:n|}}$.

Second, the timing within the year:

$\ddot{a}$ annuity-due
Payment at the start of each year. First payment at time 0, paid with certainty.
$a$ annuity-immediate
Payment at the end of each year. First payment at time 1, contingent on survival.
$\bar{a}$ continuous
A continuous stream of payment, $dt$ per instant $dt$. Integral version of the annuity.

Third, how often. Annually (the default), $m$-thly (with the $(m - 1)/(2m)$ correction), or continuously in the limit. The chart below shows how the annuity-due factor $\ddot{a}_x$ falls with age (you get fewer expected payments) and with interest rate (later payments discount harder).

Step 4

The detail

Whole-life annuities

The annuity-due at age $x$, paying 1 at the start of each year for life:

$$ \ddot{a}_x \;=\; 1 + v \cdot p_x + v^2 \cdot {}_2 p_x + \dots \;=\; \sum_{j=0}^{\omega - x - 1} v^j \cdot {}_j p_x. $$

The first term is the certain payment at time 0; the rest are survival-weighted discount factors.

The annuity-immediate pays 1 at the end of each year:

$$ a_x \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{\omega - x} v^j \cdot {}_j p_x. $$

The structural identity:

$$ \ddot{a}_x \;=\; 1 + a_x. $$

The due is one pound dearer than the immediate, that pound being the time-0 payment which the immediate does not make.

The continuous version, a steady stream of payment:

$$ \bar{a}_x \;=\; \int_0^{\omega - x} v^t \cdot {}_t p_x\, dt. $$

Temporary annuities

Truncate the series at $n$ years:

$$ \ddot{a}_{x:\overline{n|}} \;=\; \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} v^j \cdot {}_j p_x, \qquad a_{x:\overline{n|}} \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{n} v^j \cdot {}_j p_x, \qquad \bar{a}_{x:\overline{n|}} \;=\; \int_0^{n} v^t \cdot {}_t p_x\, dt. $$

The bridge identity:

$$ \ddot{a}_{x:\overline{n|}} + v^n \cdot {}_n p_x \;=\; 1 + a_{x:\overline{n|}}. $$

The due has a $+1$ at $j = 0$ and the immediate has a $+v^n \cdot {}_n p_x$ at $j = n$; all other terms match.

Commutation functions

A notational convenience. Define $D_x = v^x l_x$. Then

$$ \frac{D_{x+n}}{D_x} \;=\; v^n \cdot {}_n p_x, $$

which is the standard "discount and survive" factor that appears all over the syllabus. Modern actuarial software does not need commutation functions, but they remain the cleanest way to write deferred and temporary formulas on paper.

Deferred annuities

Discount and survive across the deferral period, then take the whole-life from there:

$$ {}_{n|}\ddot{a}_x \;=\; v^n \cdot {}_n p_x \cdot \ddot{a}_{x+n} \;=\; \frac{D_{x+n}}{D_x}\, \ddot{a}_{x+n}, \qquad {}_{n|}a_x \;=\; v^n {}_n p_x \cdot a_{x+n}. $$

The decomposition:

$$ \ddot{a}_x \;=\; \ddot{a}_{x:\overline{n|}} + {}_{n|}\ddot{a}_x. $$

A whole-life annuity is a temporary annuity for the first $n$ years plus a deferred whole-life from $n$ onwards.

Guaranteed annuities

Payments for at least $n$ years (guaranteed, paid to estate if annuitant dies), then continuing on a life-contingent basis. The EPV is the certain annuity of length $n$ plus a deferred whole-life:

$$ \ddot{a}_{\overline{x:n|}} \;=\; \ddot{a}_{\overline{n|}} + {}_{n|}\ddot{a}_x, \qquad a_{\overline{x:n|}} \;=\; a_{\overline{n|}} + {}_{n|}a_x. $$

Here $\ddot{a}_{\overline{n|}} = 1 + v + \dots + v^{n-1}$ is the certain annuity-due, with no mortality involved.

Annuities payable $m$-thly

When the annual payment of 1 is split into $m$ payments of $1/m$ across the year, the EPV changes slightly. Under UDD,

$$ \ddot{a}_x^{(m)} \;\approx\; \ddot{a}_x - \frac{m - 1}{2m}, \qquad a_x^{(m)} \;\approx\; a_x + \frac{m - 1}{2m}. $$

Derivation. An annual annuity-due pays 1 at the start of each year (times $0, 1, 2, \dots$). An $m$-thly annuity-due pays $1/m$ at times $0, 1/m, 2/m, \dots$. Compare the year-$k$ payments. The annual version pays 1 at time $k$. The $m$-thly version pays $1/m$ at each of times $k, k + 1/m, \dots, k + (m-1)/m$. The average payment time within year $k$ is

$$ \frac{1}{m}\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}\left(k + \frac{r}{m}\right) \;=\; k + \frac{m-1}{2m}. $$

So the $m$-thly version pays its year-$k$ total of 1 on average $(m-1)/(2m)$ years later than the annual version. Discounted back, it costs slightly less. Hence $\ddot{a}_x^{(m)} = \ddot{a}_x - (m-1)/(2m)$ to first order.

For the immediate, the year-$k$ sub-payments are at times $k + 1/m, \dots, k + 1$, averaging $k + (m+1)/(2m) = k + 1 - (m-1)/(2m)$, which is $(m-1)/(2m)$ earlier than the annual immediate's year-$k$ payment at time $k+1$. Hence $a_x^{(m)} = a_x + (m-1)/(2m)$.

Temporary and deferred variants pick up the same correction with a boundary adjustment for the survival factor at $n$:

$$ \ddot{a}_{x:\overline{n|}}^{(m)} \;\approx\; \ddot{a}_{x:\overline{n|}} - \frac{m - 1}{2m}\!\left(1 - \frac{D_{x+n}}{D_x}\right), \qquad {}_{n|}\ddot{a}_x^{(m)} \;\approx\; {}_{n|}\ddot{a}_x - \frac{m - 1}{2m}\cdot \frac{D_{x+n}}{D_x}. $$

Continuous annuities as the limit

Take $m \to \infty$: $(m - 1)/(2m) \to 1/2$. So

$$ \bar{a}_x \;=\; a_x + \tfrac{1}{2} \;=\; \ddot{a}_x - \tfrac{1}{2}, \qquad \bar{a}_{x:\overline{n|}} \;\approx\; \ddot{a}_{x:\overline{n|}} - \tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(1 - \frac{D_{x+n}}{D_x}\right). $$

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The annuity factor is everything

Once you have $\ddot{a}_x$ on the right basis (mortality table, interest rate, payment frequency), the price of a level pension is a one-line multiplication: premium $=$ income $\times \ddot{a}_x$. Almost every annuity calculation in practice comes down to computing this number accurately and arguing about which basis to use.

Interest-rate risk dominates mortality risk for annuities

A one percentage point move in the interest rate changes $\ddot{a}_{65}$ by 5 to 10 per cent. A one per cent uniform reduction in $q_x$ changes it by under 1 per cent. Annuity providers therefore hedge interest-rate risk with bonds and swaps before they think about longevity hedging. The longevity book is the slow-moving risk; the interest-rate book is the fast-moving one.

Inflation-linked annuities use the same framework

If the annuity escalates at $c$ per cent per year, the formulas in lecture 5 replace $v = 1/(1+i)$ with $v / (1+c)$, which is a discount factor at the modified rate $j = (1+i)/(1+c) - 1$. The cash-flow structure is unchanged.

A common F107 trap

Watch out The $(m - 1)/(2m)$ correction is exact under UDD and a level interest rate. If mortality is markedly non-UDD or the interest rate is stochastic, the correction is approximate and the question may explicitly say so. The default in exam questions is UDD, so unless told otherwise apply the correction as given.

The bulk-purchase annuity market

Practitioner insight The fastest-growing piece of UK life insurance is bulk-purchase annuities: defined-benefit pension schemes paying an insurer to take over their pensioner liabilities. The pricing is the same $\ddot{a}_x$ framework, with mortality calibrated to the specific scheme's lives and an explicit margin for the insurer's capital. PIC, L&G, Aviva, and Just Group have built businesses worth tens of billions on this trade. The actuarial mathematics is exactly what is in this lecture.

Test yourself

Try to answer each prompt out loud, in plain English, as if to a curious finance graduate. Do not look back at the lecture until you have tried.

  1. In one paragraph, explain what $\ddot{a}_x$ measures and why every level-pension price is a single multiplication against it.
  2. Why is the identity $\ddot{a}_x = 1 + a_x$ "obvious" once you accept that the only difference between due and immediate is one extra time-0 payment?
  3. Derive the monthly correction $\ddot{a}_x^{(m)} \approx \ddot{a}_x - (m-1)/(2m)$ from the UDD argument. What would change if mortality were markedly non-UDD at the relevant ages?
  4. State the deferred annuity ${}_{n|}\ddot{a}_x$ in terms of $D_{x+n}/D_x$ and $\ddot{a}_{x+n}$. Explain in words what each factor represents.
  5. Why does the guaranteed annuity have a certain annuity component $\ddot{a}_{\overline{n|}}$ that has no mortality factor in it?
  6. For a level whole-life annuity to a 65-year-old, which is the dominant pricing risk: a 0.5 percentage point move in interest rates, or a 5 per cent uniform reduction in $q_x$? Why?
  7. Given $\ddot{a}_{60:\overline{5|}} = 4.521$, $D_{65}/D_{60} = 0.768$, and $i = 4\%$, compute $\ddot{a}_{60:\overline{5|}}^{(12)}$.
