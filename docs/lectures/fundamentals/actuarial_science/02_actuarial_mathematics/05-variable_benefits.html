Variable benefits

Real pensions escalate with inflation; real sums assured grow over time. Two patterns of growth, and both reduce to the level case with a single trick.

Lecture 5 of 6 Reading time, 20 minutes Source: UP / Actuarial Mathematics Summary.pdf §7
Step 1

The 30-second version

Two patterns of benefit growth dominate practice. Compounding increases multiply each year's payment by $1 + c$; this is the standard inflation linkage. Simple increases add $b$ per cent of the original payment each year; this is the older escalation clause on legacy annuities. Both patterns turn into level-benefit formulas after a single move.

For compounding, define $j = (1+i)/(1+c) - 1$. The level-benefit EPV at the modified rate $j$ is the EPV of the compounding-benefit contract at the original rate $i$. The proof is one line: $(1+c)^k \cdot v^k = (1+c)^k / (1+i)^k = (1+j)^{-k} = v_j^k$.

For simple increases, introduce the increasing annuity $(I\ddot{a})_x = \sum (1+k) v^k {}_k p_x$ and its sibling $(IA)_x$. A benefit paying $P$ at time 0 and rising by $Q$ each year has EPV $(P - Q)\ddot{a}_x + Q (I\ddot{a})_x$. Same trick on the assurance side with $(IA)_x$.

Feynman test A 65-year-old retires with a pension of £10,000 in the first year, escalating at 3 per cent each year. The payment in year $k$ is $10{,}000 \cdot 1.03^k$. The present value of that payment, if it is actually made, is $10{,}000 \cdot 1.03^k / 1.05^k = 10{,}000 \cdot (1.03/1.05)^k$ assuming $i = 5\%$. But $1.03/1.05 = 1/1.0194$, so $j = 1.94\%$. The price of the escalating pension is exactly the price of a level pension at $j = 1.94\%$ instead of $i = 5\%$. The cash-flow growth and the discount rate have collapsed into a single modified rate.
What is beyond the source The source PDF lists the compounding and simple formulas without the structural reason. This lecture derives both from first principles (the $j$-rate trick for compounding, the $(I\ddot{a})$ identity for simple) and explains the half-year correction for continuous on-death benefits.
Step 2

Why this matters

Almost no real contract pays a level benefit. UK statutory pensions escalate at "the lower of CPI and 5 per cent" (the LPI cap, "Limited Price Indexation"). UK defined-benefit pensions historically escalated at "the lower of RPI and 5 per cent". Long-dated sum-assured escalation typically runs at 3 per cent compound. All of these are compounding patterns that reduce to a modified-rate level annuity.

The LPI cap is more than a pricing footnote. It is an option: an embedded inflation cap inside every UK pension scheme. The Bank of England's response to the 2022 inflation spike pushed UK CPI past 11 per cent, well above the LPI cap of 5 per cent. Pension schemes whose liabilities had been valued ignoring the option suddenly saw a windfall release (because the cap kicked in and the realised inflation increase was lower than uncapped CPI would have been). The same option-aware schemes had to revalue carefully through the rate environment that followed.

For an actuary, the $j$-rate trick is the single most reused identity in pension valuations and inflation-linked bond pricing. Dividend discount valuations, perpetuity pricing, all of long-dated cash flow valuation, hinge on the same algebra: growth at $c$ inside the cash flow is the same as discount at $j = (i - c)/(1 + c)$ in the discount factor.

Step 3

The mental model

Three observations to hold in mind.

First, an increasing benefit at the original rate and a level benefit at a modified rate are the same EPV. Multiplying the cash flow by $(1+c)^k$ and discounting at $(1+i)^k$ is identical to keeping the cash flow level and discounting at $(1+i)^k / (1+c)^k$. The compounding case is just a discount-rate substitution.

Second, a benefit with simple increases is a level benefit plus a sloped one. A series paying $1, 2, 3, \dots$ is the sum of a level-1 stream plus a stream paying $0, 1, 2, \dots$, which is itself the level stream from time 1 onwards plus a stream paying $0, 0, 1, 1, \dots$. Inductively, the increasing annuity is a triangle of level annuities, and the identity $(I\ddot{a})_x = (Ia)_x + \ddot{a}_x$ is the algebraic statement.

Third, the half-year correction from lecture 4 extends here at the modified rate. A continuous on-death increasing benefit is paid on average half a year earlier than its end-of-year counterpart, so the discrete-to-continuous conversion picks up $(1+j)^{1/2}$.

Step 4

The detail

The compounding trick

For a benefit growing at rate $c$ per year, define the modified discount rate

$$ j \;=\; \frac{1+i}{1+c} - 1, \qquad v_j \;=\; \frac{1}{1+j} \;=\; \frac{1+c}{1+i}. $$

Then a benefit of $(1+c)^k$ at time $k$, discounted at $i$, has present value $v^k (1+c)^k = v_j^k$. Replace $v$ with $v_j$ everywhere a level benefit would have appeared, and you have priced the compounding contract.

Compounding annuities

Annuity-due paying $1$ at time 0 and $(1+c)^k$ at time $k$:

$$ \mathrm{EPV} \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^\infty (1+c)^k \cdot v^k \cdot {}_k p_x \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^\infty v_j^k \cdot {}_k p_x \;=\; \ddot{a}_x^{@j\%}. $$

Annuity-immediate paying $1$ at time 1, $1+c$ at time 2, and so on:

$$ \mathrm{EPV} \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^\infty (1+c)^{k-1} \cdot v^k \cdot {}_k p_x \;=\; \frac{1}{1+c}\, a_x^{@j\%}. $$

The $1/(1+c)$ factor compensates for the fact that the first payment is just $1$, not $1+c$.

Temporary versions are identical with the upper limit capped at $n - 1$ (due) or $n$ (immediate).

Compounding assurances

End-of-year-of-death benefit of $(1+c)^k$ paid at time $k+1$:

$$ \mathrm{EPV} \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^\infty (1+c)^k \cdot v^{k+1} \cdot {}_{k|}q_x \;=\; \frac{1}{1+c}\, A_x^{@j\%}. $$

For continuous on-death benefit, combine the level continuous-to-discrete relationship with the $j$-rate trick:

$$ \mathrm{EPV} \;=\; \left(\frac{1+i}{1+c}\right)^{1/2} \cdot A_x^{@j\%} \;=\; (1+j)^{1/2} \cdot A_x^{@j\%}. $$

Pure endowment with compounding survival benefit:

$$ (1+c)^n \cdot A_{x:\overline{n|}}^{\,1} \;=\; A^{@j\%,\,1}_{x:\overline{n|}}. $$

Both sides equal $v^n {}_n p_x \cdot (1+c)^n = v_j^n {}_n p_x$.

Simple-increase annuities

A benefit of $1$ at time 0, growing to $1 + k$ at time $k$ (simple, by an amount equal to the original each year):

$$ (I\ddot{a})_x \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{\omega - x - 1} (1 + k) \cdot v^k \cdot {}_k p_x, \qquad (Ia)_x \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{\omega - x} k \cdot v^k \cdot {}_k p_x. $$

Algebraic identity:

$$ (I\ddot{a})_x \;=\; (Ia)_x + \ddot{a}_x. $$

Derivation: $(1 + k) = 1 + k$ inside the sum gives a level $\ddot{a}_x$ plus an immediate $(Ia)_x$.

The two parameterisations

For a benefit starting at $P$ and rising by $Q$ each year, two equivalent forms:

$$ \mathrm{EPV} \;=\; (P - Q)\,\ddot{a}_x + Q\,(I\ddot{a})_x. $$

And in ratio form with $b = Q/P$:

$$ \mathrm{EPV} \;=\; (1 - b)\,\ddot{a}_x + b\,(I\ddot{a})_x. $$

Sanity check: at $k = 0$ the benefit is $P + 0 \cdot Q = P - Q + Q = P$; at $k = 1$ the benefit is $P + Q$. The decomposition is just "level $(P - Q)$ plus increasing $(1+k)\, Q$".

Term-truncation identity

The standard way to write a temporary increasing annuity in commutation form:

$$ (I\ddot{a})_{x:\overline{n|}} \;=\; (I\ddot{a})_x - \frac{D_{x+n}}{D_x}\!\left[n \cdot \ddot{a}_{x+n} + (I\ddot{a})_{x+n}\right]. $$

Derivation. The tail of $(I\ddot{a})_x$ from $k \ge n$ contributes payments $(1+n), (2+n), \dots$ at times $n, n+1, \dots$. Split each into a level $n$ piece (deferred to age $x+n$, contributing $n \cdot D_{x+n}/D_x \cdot \ddot{a}_{x+n}$) plus an increasing $(1+r)$ piece starting at $x+n$ (contributing $D_{x+n}/D_x \cdot (I\ddot{a})_{x+n}$). Subtract to get the temporary version.

Simple-increase assurances

End-of-year-of-death increasing whole-life:

$$ (IA)_x \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{\omega - x - 1} (1 + k) \cdot v^{k+1} \cdot {}_{k|}q_x. $$

The two-parameter form for a death benefit starting at $P$ rising by $Q$:

$$ \mathrm{EPV} \;=\; (P - Q)\, A_x + Q\,(IA)_x, $$

or $(1 - b) A_x + b (IA)_x$ in ratio form. Term truncation by the same argument:

$$ (IA)^1_{x:\overline{n|}} \;=\; (IA)_x - \frac{D_{x+n}}{D_x}\!\left[n \cdot A_{x+n} + (IA)_{x+n}\right]. $$

For the pure endowment with simple increase, the benefit at survival to time $n$ is $1 + nb$:

$$ (1 + nb)\, A_{x:\overline{n|}}^{\,1}. $$

A worked example

A 65-year-old buys a whole-life annuity-due paying £10,000 in the first year and escalating at 3 per cent compound. Basis $i = 5\%$ and $\ddot{a}_{65}^{@1.94\%} = 13.8$.

j = (1 + 0.05) / (1 + 0.03) - 1 = 1.05 / 1.03 - 1 = 0.019417 ≈ 1.94% Escalating EPV = 10,000 * ä_65^@1.94% = 10,000 * 13.8 = £138,000 For comparison, the level £10,000 pension at i = 5%: If ä_65^@5% = 12.1, Level EPV = 10,000 * 12.1 = £121,000. The escalation costs £17,000 more (14%), which is the price of inflation cover. The relative premium scales with the gap (i - c) and with the duration of the annuity.

Now consider simple increases. The same 65-year-old buys a 10-year temporary annuity-due of £8,000 in year 1, increasing by £400 each year. Given $\ddot{a}_{65:\overline{10|}} = 7.5$ and $(I\ddot{a})_{65:\overline{10|}} = 38.0$:

P = £8,000, Q = £400, n = 10 EPV = (P - Q) * ä_{65:10} + Q * (Iä)_{65:10} = 7,600 * 7.5 + 400 * 38.0 = 57,000 + 15,200 = £72,200 Sanity check: total undiscounted payments over 10 years are Sum_{k=0..9} (8,000 + 400*k) = 80,000 + 18,000 = £98,000 (avg £9,800/year). A level £9,800 pension at the same annuity factor would cost £73,500. The escalating version costs slightly less because the increases come later (more discounted), confirming the £72,200 is plausible.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The $j$-rate trick is the most useful identity in long-dated pricing

Compounding growth and discount-rate reduction are mathematically the same operation. Once internalised, every escalation problem collapses to a level cash flow at a modified rate. The same trick is the Gordon dividend discount model in equity valuation, the real-yield-equals-nominal-minus-inflation identity in inflation-linked bond pricing, and the basis of every long-dated cash flow valuation in finance.

Simple and compounding diverge at long durations

A 5 per cent simple increase over 30 years compounds to $1 + 30 \cdot 0.05 = 2.50$ times the original. A 5 per cent compound increase over 30 years grows to $1.05^{30} = 4.32$ times. The two are nearly identical for short terms but diverge sharply on lifetime annuities to younger lives.

LPI and the embedded option

Practitioner insight UK statutory pension escalation is "lower of CPI and 5 per cent" (LPI cap). This is a compound increase with an embedded short option on inflation. Valuing it properly requires either a real-yield curve plus an inflation-cap valuation, or a stochastic inflation model with Monte Carlo. The formulas in this lecture are the level approximation that ignores the option. They are right "on average" but miss the option's convexity, which becomes material in high-inflation regimes.

Increasing assurances are rare; increasing annuities are universal

Most life-assurance products have level or slowly-escalating sums assured. Most annuities and most defined-benefit pensions escalate, often heavily. The $(IA)_x$ identity is therefore mainly a theoretical completeness item; the $(I\ddot{a})_x$ identity and the $j$-rate trick are working tools every annuity actuary uses.

A common F107 trap

Watch out For the immediate annuity with compounding increases at rate $c$ where the first payment is $1$ (not $1+c$), there is a leading factor of $1/(1+c)$ in front of $a_x^{@j\%}$. Skipping this factor is a common error. The simple way to remember: the immediate's series starts one step "early" relative to the standard $(1+c)^k$ pattern at $k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$, so you compensate by dividing by one factor of $(1+c)$.
Step 6

Test yourself

Try to answer each prompt out loud, in plain English, as if to a curious final-year economics student. Do not look back at the lecture until you have tried.

  1. State the modified rate $j$ for compounding increases at $c$. Show that an EPV at rate $i$ with payments $(1+c)^k$ equals an EPV at rate $j$ with level payments.
  2. Derive the EPV of a $c$-escalating whole-life annuity-due from first principles. Where does the $j$-rate trick reduce it to a level formula?
  3. For the immediate version, explain in one sentence where the $1/(1+c)$ factor in $\frac{1}{1+c} a_x^{@j\%}$ comes from.
  4. State the identity $(I\ddot{a})_x = (Ia)_x + \ddot{a}_x$ and prove it by splitting $(1+k) = 1 + k$ inside the sum.
  5. Why does $(I\ddot{a})_{x:\overline{n|}}$ have a subtraction of two terms (a level $n \ddot{a}_{x+n}$ and an increasing $(I\ddot{a})_{x+n}$) when you truncate at $n$? Explain what each represents.
  6. An endowment assurance pays $1 + bk$ at end of year of death in year $k+1$, and $1 + bn$ on survival to $n$. Write the EPV in terms of $A^1_{x:\overline{n|}}$, $(IA)^1_{x:\overline{n|}}$, and $A_{x:\overline{n|}}^{\,1}$.
  7. A 60-year-old buys a £20,000 first-year pension escalating at 4 per cent compound. With $i = 6\%$ and $\ddot{a}_{60}^{@1.923\%} = 17.2$, what is the EPV?
