Survival models

How long does this person have left? Every other lecture in this course is an integral against the answer.

Lecture 1 of 6 Reading time, 25 minutes Source: UP / Actuarial Mathematics Summary.pdf §1, §3
Step 1

The 30-second version

A survival model answers a single question: for a person currently aged $x$, what is the probability they are still alive at age $x + t$? Call that probability ${}_t p_x$. Its complement, ${}_t q_x = 1 - {}_t p_x$, is the probability they have died by then. Every annuity, every assurance, every premium and every reserve in this course is a discounted sum or integral against these two numbers.

The single most useful object in the chapter is the force of mortality $\mu_x$, the instantaneous death rate at age $x$. It is to actuarial science what the hazard rate is to survival analysis and the failure rate is to reliability engineering: the rate at which probability of being alive is shrinking, per unit of probability still in hand. Once you know $\mu_x$ as a function of age, everything else follows by integration.

Feynman test Take 100,000 babies born today. Over time some die, and the number alive at age $x$ is a function called $l_x$. The fraction still alive at age $x + t$, starting from age $x$, is just $l_{x+t} / l_x$. That ratio is ${}_t p_x$. The rate at which the curve is dropping (steeper drop means more dying) is the force of mortality $\mu_x$. Pricing a 30-year mortgage life policy on a 40-year-old is, at heart, just looking at the slope of this curve between 40 and 70.
What is beyond the source The source PDF lists the identities without derivation. This lecture derives each one once, links the force of mortality to the hazard rate from survival analysis (so you can recognise it in adjacent disciplines), and works one concrete numerical example using a life-table fragment so the formulas are not abstract.
Step 2

Why this matters

Life insurance is the only industry whose product is a contract paying out at a random time that the customer cannot control and the seller cannot predict. The whole price of the contract depends on a single distribution: when will this person die? Get that distribution materially wrong and the company prices the policy materially wrong. Underprice by 10 per cent and your shareholders fund the difference. Overprice by 10 per cent and your competitors take the business.

The 2018 mortality slowdown caught most UK life insurers off guard. Life expectancy at age 65, which had risen by about three months per year for decades, suddenly stopped rising. Annuity providers had been pricing on the assumption that mortality would keep improving, and their reserves swelled by billions when projections were revised down. The 2020 COVID surge then went the other way for older-age mortality, briefly releasing reserves before the question of which mortality trend is the "real" one re-opened. Both episodes are conversations about ${}_t p_x$.

For an actuary, the survival model is also the moment the bridge to adjacent disciplines becomes visible. Survival analysis in biostatistics, reduced-form credit models in finance, and reliability engineering all reduce to the same machinery dressed in different notation. Once you can write ${}_t p_x = \exp(-\int_0^t \mu_{x+s} ds)$ you can read any of those literatures without translation.

Step 3

The mental model

Hold a single picture in mind: a curve $l_x$ that starts at $l_0$ (a notional 100,000, say) and falls to zero at some ultimate age $\omega$ (typically 120). Every probability in the chapter is a ratio of two ordinates of this curve.

The four basic identities all come from this picture. Slice it one way and you get ${}_t p_x$, the fraction surviving from age $x$ to age $x + t$. Slice it another and you get ${}_t q_x$, the fraction dying within that interval. Slice a third way and you get ${}_{n|m}q_x$, the fraction surviving $n$ years and then dying in the next $m$. Slice a fourth way and you get $\mu_x$, the local steepness of the curve.

A useful intuition The actuarial discipline is essentially "the integral calculus of the force of mortality". Almost every formula in this course can be written as $\int (\text{benefit at time } t) \cdot v^t \cdot {}_t p_x \cdot \mu_{x+t}\, dt$ for some choice of benefit pattern. The variation across lectures is in what the benefit is and when it is paid, not in the underlying structure.
Step 4

The detail

The future lifetime random variable

For a life currently aged $x$:

$T_x$
The continuous future lifetime, on $[0, \omega - x]$. The CDF is $F_{T_x}(t) = P(T_x \le t) = {}_t q_x$. The survival function is $S_{T_x}(t) = P(T_x > t) = {}_t p_x$.
$K_x$
The curtate future lifetime, $K_x = \lfloor T_x \rfloor$. A non-negative integer. $P(K_x = k) = {}_k p_x \cdot q_{x+k}$: survive $k$ whole years, then die in the next.

The basic probabilities

Two definitions and one identity. The probability of dying within $t$ years from age $x$:

$$ {}_t q_x \;=\; \frac{l_x - l_{x+t}}{l_x} \;=\; \frac{d_x + d_{x+1} + \dots + d_{x+t-1}}{l_x}, $$

where $d_x = l_x - l_{x+1}$ is the number of deaths between ages $x$ and $x + 1$. The probability of surviving for $t$ years:

$$ {}_t p_x \;=\; \frac{l_{x+t}}{l_x}, $$

and the immediate consequence ${}_t q_x + {}_t p_x = 1$. When $t = 1$ the prefix is dropped: $q_x$, $p_x$.

Multiplicativity of survival

To survive $m + n$ years from age $x$ you must first survive $n$ years (probability ${}_n p_x$, ending up aged $x + n$) and then a further $m$ years from there (probability ${}_m p_{x+n}$):

$$ {}_{m+n} p_x \;=\; {}_n p_x \cdot {}_m p_{x+n} \;=\; \frac{l_{x+m+n}}{l_x}. $$

This is the single most useful identity in the subject. It says: survival probabilities chain by multiplication, just like independent coin flips, except the "coin" gets biased toward death as the life ages.

Deferred mortality

The probability that $(x)$ survives $n$ years and then dies in the next $m$ years:

$$ {}_{n|m} q_x \;=\; {}_n p_x \cdot {}_m q_{x+n} \;=\; {}_n p_x - {}_{n+m} p_x \;=\; \frac{l_{x+n} - l_{x+n+m}}{l_x}. $$

Three equivalent forms. The first follows the literal reading ("survive $n$, then die in $m$"). The second uses ${}_m q_{x+n} = 1 - {}_m p_{x+n}$. The third unfolds to the life-table version. When $m = 1$ the second subscript is dropped: ${}_{n|} q_x$ is the probability of dying in the year after age $x + n$.

The force of mortality

The instantaneous death rate at age $x$:

$$ \mu_x \;=\; \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{1}{h}\, {}_h q_x \;=\; -\frac{d}{dx}\ln l_x \;=\; -\frac{1}{l_x}\cdot \frac{d l_x}{dx}. $$

Two equivalent forms after the limit. The logarithmic-derivative form is cleanest theoretically (integration is trivial). The reciprocal form is cleanest numerically (when you have $l_x$ tabulated, you can estimate the derivative by finite differences). Either way the units are per year: $\mu_x$ is a rate, not a probability, and it can exceed 1.

For a life aged $x$ looking $t$ years out:

$$ \mu_{x+t} \;=\; -\frac{d}{dt}\ln l_{x+t} \;=\; -\frac{1}{l_{x+t}}\cdot \frac{d l_{x+t}}{dt}, $$

where the differentiation is now in $t$ at fixed $x$. The source PDF tabulates a parametric form $\mu_{x+t} = m(x+t) + c$ as a worked example, which corresponds to a Gompertz-Makeham model.

Survival from the force of mortality

The recovery formula. Integrate the force from $x$ to $x + n$ and exponentiate:

$$ {}_n p_x \;=\; \exp\!\left( -\int_x^{x+n} \mu_t\, dt \right) \;=\; \exp\!\left( -\int_0^n \mu_{x+t}\, dt \right). $$

Derivation. Write $\mu_{x+t} = -d/dt[\ln l_{x+t}]$, integrate from 0 to $n$ to get $\ln l_x - \ln l_{x+n} = \ln(l_x / l_{x+n})$, exponentiate to get $l_x / l_{x+n}$, take reciprocal to get ${}_n p_x$. The companion identity for the $n$-year death probability is

$$ {}_n q_x \;=\; \int_0^n {}_t p_x \cdot \mu_{x+t}\, dt, $$

which says the death probability is the integral of the density $f_{T_x}(t) = {}_t p_x \cdot \mu_{x+t}$ over $[0, n]$.

Four derivative identities

Each falls out of the above and reappears all over the syllabus:

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\, {}_t q_x \;=\; {}_t p_x \cdot \mu_{x+t}, \qquad -\frac{d}{dt}\, {}_t p_x \;=\; {}_t p_x \cdot \mu_{x+t}, \qquad \mu_{x+t} \;=\; -\frac{d}{dt}\ln({}_t p_x). $$

Density functions

Continuous and discrete versions:

$f_{T_x}(t)$
$f_{T_x}(t) = {}_t p_x \cdot \mu_{x+t}$. The density of $T_x$. Survival probability multiplied by instantaneous mortality.
$P(K_x = k)$
$P(K_x = k) = {}_k p_x \cdot q_{x+k}$. The probability of dying in the $(k+1)$-th year. Survive $k$ whole years, then die in the next.

Mortality at fractional ages

Life tables are typically tabulated only at integer ages. To price contracts whose payoff depends on death within a year, the gap between $q_x$ and $q_{x+s}$ for $0 < s < 1$ has to be filled by assumption. Three standard choices:

UDD
Uniform distribution of deaths. Deaths are spread evenly across the year of age. So ${}_s q_x = s \cdot q_x$ for $0 \le s \le 1$, and the density is constant at $q_x$ on $(0, 1)$. Equivalent identity: ${}_s p_x \cdot \mu_{x+s} = q_x$ throughout the year.
CFM
Constant force of mortality. $\mu_{x+s} = \mu_x$ for $0 \le s \le 1$. So ${}_s p_x = (p_x)^s$ and ${}_s q_x = 1 - (p_x)^s$. Deaths are exponentially distributed within the year.
CMR
Constant mortality rate. $q_x = k$ across a range of ages. Used for crude smoothing where the table changes slowly. Implies a stepped survival curve.

UDD and CFM are the practical assumptions. UDD makes ${}_s q_x$ linear in $s$, which lets you split a one-year probability evenly across the year. CFM makes ${}_s p_x = (p_x)^s$, which is convenient for continuous-time integrals because the force is just $\mu = -\ln p_x$. The two assumptions disagree on the death density within the year (uniform versus exponentially declining) but agree on the survival probability at integer ages.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The force of mortality is the only object you need to remember

Everything else can be reconstructed from $\mu_{x+t}$. Given the force for all $t \ge 0$, you have ${}_t p_x$ by integration, ${}_t q_x$ by complementation, $f_{T_x}(t)$ by multiplication, and every annuity and assurance EPV by a single further integral against $v^t$. When you can write $\mu_x$ for a model, you have written the model.

Notation is local; concepts are universal

The same object appears in four disciplines under four names. Actuaries write ${}_t p_x$. Survival analysts write $S(t \mid x)$. Reliability engineers write $R(t)$. Reduced-form credit modellers write the probability of no default by time $t$. The hazard rate, failure rate, and default intensity are all $\mu_x$. Once you learn the actuarial notation deeply, you read the other three literatures for free.

UDD versus CFM at the margin

Watch out For annuity pricing, the choice of fractional-age assumption is usually immaterial: the integrals are dominated by integer-age points. For assurance pricing with continuous or monthly payment, the choice can move the answer by a few basis points. For options on mortality (longevity swaps), the choice can matter more because the value depends on the variance, which is exactly what UDD and CFM differ on. The default in F107 exam questions is UDD unless explicitly told otherwise.

The ultimate age is a modelling choice

Most tables truncate at $\omega = 120$ or so, treating $l_\omega = 0$. This is a finite-support assumption; in reality nothing tells you that nobody lives past 120, only that nobody has by much. For very long-dated annuity business, the tail mortality assumption can move reserves by several per cent. The standard tables published by actuarial bodies (CMI in the UK, ASSA in South Africa, SOA in the US) update these tails as evidence accumulates.

The select effect is hidden in this lecture

Throughout this lecture, all lives aged $x$ have been treated as interchangeable. In practice, newly-underwritten lives are healthier than the average $x$-year-old in the table because they have just passed a medical. Lecture 2 introduces the select table $l_{[x]+s}$ that adjusts for this. The machinery is identical; the table just has two arguments instead of one.
