Lectures / Financial mathematics

Cashflow models

Every financial instrument, every loan, every project reduces to one thing: a sequence of payments on a timeline. Before any pricing or valuation, the actuarial discipline is to draw the cashflow first. This lecture is the discipline.

Lecture 1 of 7 Reading time, 25 minutes Source: UoP / Financial Mathematics / Summary.pdf §1
Step 1

The 30-second version

A cashflow is a payment of money from one party to another at a specific moment in time. A cashflow model is the complete list of such payments associated with an instrument, a loan, or a project, plotted on a single timeline with sign conventions. Pricing, valuation, and risk all begin by reducing the instrument to this list. The five descriptive features (timing, size, duration, sign, pattern) capture everything you need to draw the timeline; the eight canonical instrument types (zero-coupon bond, fixed-interest security, index-linked security, cash on deposit, equity shares, annuity certain, interest-only loan, repayment loan) are the templates you will recognise inside almost every real product.

The work of this lecture is not arithmetic: it is the disciplined translation from a product description in English to a cashflow diagram. The rest of the course operates on the diagram, not on the words. Get the diagram wrong and every subsequent number is wrong.

Feynman test Pretend a friend asks: "I'm thinking of buying a 10-year UK gilt that pays 3% twice a year." Before you tell them whether it's a good deal, you draw a line on the back of a napkin. A single arrow down today (price paid), twenty small arrows up over the next ten years (coupons), and one big arrow up at the end (principal returned). If you can't draw that line, you can't price the gilt. Every instrument in this lecture is a different shape of that line.
What is beyond the source The source enumerates the eight instrument types and the five descriptive features. This lecture adds a sharper distinction between certain and contingent cashflows (the source treats them together), a treatment of why pattern deserves equal billing with the other four features, and a brief tour of how index-linked, equity, and embedded-option cashflows are handled in practice (forecasted, simulated, or replicated). The certain/contingent split is the bridge into later courses on contingencies and risk modelling.
Step 2

Why this matters

Every later technique in this course is a transformation of a cashflow. Present-value pricing is "discount every cashflow back and add". Yield is "find the discount rate that makes the cashflows balance". Reserving is "estimate the cashflows that have not yet happened, discount them back, set aside that much". Solvency is "are the future cashflows from assets enough to cover the future cashflows from liabilities, with margin?". If the cashflow list is wrong, the answer is wrong no matter how sophisticated the discount machinery.

Beyond actuarial work, the same model underpins corporate finance (DCF valuation), banking (loan amortisation and funds transfer pricing), investment management (bond pricing, yield-to-maturity), and project management (capital-budgeting decisions). The vocabulary differs by industry, but the underlying object is the same: a list of dated payments with signs.

A common professional failure is to confuse a contractual cashflow (the bond will pay 3% twice a year unless the issuer defaults) with an expected cashflow (the bond is likely to pay 3% twice a year, but with a small probability of default and a small probability of early call). Pricing and reserving demand the expected, not the contractual; risk capital demands the distribution around the expected. The cashflow diagram you draw at the start should always carry an implicit annotation: "is this certain or is this an expectation?".

Step 3

The mental model

Think of a horizontal line representing time. Each cashflow is an arrow at a point on the line: upward arrows are receipts (positive sign from the holder's perspective), downward arrows are payments. Length encodes size. The instrument is fully specified by the set of arrows and their dates.

From this diagram, every later calculation falls into place. Present value is the sum of arrows discounted to time 0. Yield is the discount rate that makes the up-arrows and down-arrows balance. Duration is the weighted average time of the up-arrows. Reserving is the present value of the up-arrows still to come.

Step 4

The detail

The five descriptive features

Every cashflow can be described by five attributes. The source PDF labels these timing, size, duration, sign, pattern; the order matters because the first four describe each individual payment while the fifth describes the relationship between payments.

Timing
When the cashflow happens. A specific date, or a date relative to a reference (year 5 from now). May be deterministic (a known date) or stochastic (the date of a future event like a death claim). Discrete cashflows have point-in-time timings; continuous cashflows have a payment rate measured per unit of time.
Size
How much. The monetary amount of the cashflow. May be known in advance (a fixed coupon), inflation-linked (an index-linked coupon), market-linked (a dividend on equity), or contingent (a claim payment if a loss occurs).
Duration
How long. The total span of time over which cashflows occur, from the first arrow to the last. For a bullet bond this is just the term; for an annuity it is the term over which payments are made; for a perpetuity it is infinite.
Sign
Receipt (+) or payment (-) from a specified perspective. The same cashflow has opposite signs for the two counterparties: a coupon is + to the bondholder and - to the issuer. Picking a perspective and sticking to it is the most common modelling discipline learned in week one and forgotten in week three.
Pattern
The shape of the sequence over time: level, arithmetically increasing, geometrically increasing, irregular, deferred, or a combination. The pattern often dictates which annuity formula collapses the cashflow into a single expression in later lectures.

Certain vs contingent cashflows

The source treats all cashflows on equal footing. Practically, a sharper distinction matters:

Certain cashflow
The size and timing are known with effective certainty given the contract. The principal repayment on a default-free government bond is the canonical example. Pricing uses straight discounting at a risk-free rate; reserving is mechanical.
Contingent cashflow
The size, timing, or existence of the cashflow depends on a future event. Insurance claim payments (depend on a loss), dividends (depend on profitability), survival annuities (depend on the annuitant being alive). Pricing uses expected discounted cashflow; reserving requires a probability model.

The bridge to later courses sits here. Financial mathematics, this course, prices certain cashflows. Contingencies (CT5 / CB1) prices cashflows contingent on survival or death. Risk modelling (CT6 / CB1) prices cashflows contingent on loss events. The arithmetic is the same; the probability layer is the difference.

The eight canonical instrument types

The source enumerates eight instrument templates. Recognising them inside a real product is the first move when pricing anything.

Zero-coupon bond (ZCB)
A single up-front payment from the investor, a single redemption payment $K$ at maturity. No coupons. Two arrows on the timeline. Examples: discount T-bills, stripped gilt principal, zero-coupon corporate bonds. The simplest instrument; the building block of yield-curve construction.
Fixed-interest security (FIS)
A coupon-paying bond. Up-front payment, periodic coupons of fixed size $K$, redemption payment at maturity. Examples: gilts, US Treasuries, most corporate bonds. The "vanilla" bond shape; everything else in fixed income is a perturbation of this.
Index-linked security (ILS)
A bond where the coupons and the redemption are scaled by a published index, typically inflation. Same shape as the FIS but with cashflow sizes that grow with the index. Pricing requires a model for the index path (real-yield methods, lecture 4). Examples: UK index-linked gilts, US TIPS.
Cash on deposit
Money placed with a bank earning interest. Two variants: call deposits (withdrawable on demand), term deposits (locked for a fixed term, sometimes with a penalty for early withdrawal). The actuarial model is a continuous accumulation of interest from $t = 0$ to $t = n$, with the principal returned at $n$.
Equity shares
Ownership in a company entitling the holder to dividends (size and timing uncertain) plus a residual claim on the company at exit (sale or liquidation). Strictly a contingent cashflow stream. Pricing uses dividend discount or earnings-based models with explicit forecasts of the cashflow distribution.
Annuity certain
An up-front premium paid in exchange for a stream of fixed payments over a fixed term. The reverse of a repayment loan from the buyer's perspective. The level annuity is the workhorse of lectures 5 and 6.
Interest-only loan
From the borrower's perspective: receive $A$ at $t = 0$, pay interest $I$ each period, pay back $A$ in full at $t = n$. From the lender's perspective: pay $A$ at $t = 0$, receive interest $I$ each period, receive $A$ back at $n$. Identical shape to the fixed-interest security, with roles flipped.
Repayment loan (mortgage)
From the borrower's perspective: receive $A$ at $t = 0$, pay a level repayment $K$ each period (containing a mix of interest and principal) until $t = n$. The principal is repaid gradually; the interest content of each payment falls over time as the outstanding balance shrinks. From the lender's perspective: identical to buying an annuity-certain.

Why pattern deserves equal billing

Of the five descriptive features, pattern is the one a new modeller most often underrates. The reason is that pattern is the property that lets you collapse a long list of cashflows into a single closed-form expression. A 30-year mortgage has 360 monthly cashflows; if you list them out separately every reserving calculation involves 360 discount factors. If you recognise the level pattern, one annuity formula does the same work in one step. Lectures 5 and 6 are entirely about exploiting pattern.

Three pattern classes will dominate the rest of the course:

Level
All payments equal: $K, K, K, \dots, K$. Collapses into the standard annuity $a_{\overline{n}|}$ or $s_{\overline{n}|}$ formulae.
Arithmetic
Each payment differs from the next by a fixed amount: $P, P + Q, P + 2Q, \dots, P + (n-1)Q$. Collapses using $(Ia)_{\overline{n}|}$ and level-plus-rising decompositions.
Geometric
Each payment scales by a fixed factor: $X, X(1+k), X(1+k)^2, \dots$. Collapses by substituting an equivalent rate $j$ defined by $1 + j = (1 + i) / (1 + k)$ and reusing the level formula.

Sign convention: a one-line rule

Pick a perspective at the start of the problem and stick to it. The standard convention in this course is the investor's perspective: outflows (money paid by the investor) are negative, inflows (money received by the investor) are positive. When working with a loan, the borrower's perspective inverts every sign. Mixing the two halfway through is the single most common source of arithmetical errors in this subject; a single misplaced minus sign turns a positive yield into a meaningless one.

A worked classification example

A 5-year UK index-linked gilt has a face value of £100, an indexation lag of three months, a real coupon rate of 0.125% per year paid semi-annually, and an issue price of £104. Classify and draw.

Instrument type: Index-linked security (ILS) Perspective: Investor Cashflows: t = 0 yrs -104 price paid by investor t = 0.5 yrs +0.0625 x I(0.25)/I(-2.75) indexed coupon t = 1.0 yrs +0.0625 x I(0.75)/I(-2.75) indexed coupon ... ... t = 5.0 yrs +100 x I(4.75)/I(-2.75) + 0.0625 x I(4.75)/I(-2.75) redemption + final coupon (both indexed) Pattern: Geometric in expectation (each cashflow grows with inflation) Certain vs cont.: Coupons and redemption are contractually defined but sizes depend on a future index path; treated as forecast/simulated cashflows rather than certain ones Duration: 5 years Number of arrows: 11 (one out, ten in) but the last two coincide at t = 5

The diagram is the same shape as a fixed-interest security. The difference is that each up-arrow's size is a random variable known only in expectation (the actuary forecasts the inflation path). Pricing reduces to the present value of these forecast cashflows at the real yield, treated in lecture 4.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Draw the timeline before reaching for a formula

Every common error in financial mathematics traces back to a misread cashflow: a coupon assumed annual when it was semi-annual, a redemption assumed at par when it was at premium, a payment assumed in arrears when it was in advance. The fastest professional habit is to draw the cashflow diagram first and read every formula off the diagram, not off the problem statement. The diagram is a unit test for your understanding.

Identify the pattern, then pick the formula

Once the diagram is drawn, the question "which formula?" usually answers itself. Level annual cashflows over $n$ years from time 1: that is $a_{\overline{n}|}$. Cashflows that increase by a constant each year: $(Ia)_{\overline{n}|}$. Cashflows that scale by a fixed percentage: substitute $j$ for $i$. The hierarchy is recognise the shape, then reach for the matching closed form. Avoid going formula-first; you will pick the wrong one half the time.

Contingent cashflows are the bridge to later courses

The instruments in this lecture are predominantly certain or near-certain. Real actuarial work is dominated by contingent cashflows: claim payments depend on a loss occurring, annuity payments depend on the annuitant being alive, mortgage repayments depend on the borrower not defaulting. The probability layer comes later (CT5 contingencies, CT6 risk modelling) but the cashflow representation does not change. Build the habit of drawing the cashflow first; the probability machinery slots in on top.

Sign convention is a discipline, not a preference

A surprising fraction of exam-script errors in this subject come from sign convention slips. The professional habit is to write the perspective at the top of every working ("from the investor's perspective: ..."), and then to mentally check every cashflow against that label. The convention does not matter; the consistency does.

The pattern catalogue is short

There are surprisingly few cashflow patterns in finance: level, arithmetic, geometric, exponentially decaying, irregular. Almost every real product reduces to one of these or a sum of two. The skill is the recognition: "this 30-year mortgage with annually increasing payments is a level annuity plus an arithmetic-increasing annuity, both with the same term", which is a sum of two formulas you already know.

Test yourself

  2. Draw the cashflow diagram for a 5-year zero-coupon bond purchased for £80 with face value £100, from the investor's perspective.
  3. Draw the cashflow diagram for the same instrument from the issuer's perspective. What stays the same and what flips?
