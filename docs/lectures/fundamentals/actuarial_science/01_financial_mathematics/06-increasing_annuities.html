Increasing annuities

Real annuities are rarely level. Pensions index-up to inflation, salary-linked benefits grow with wages, lease rentals escalate, and corporate dividends ratchet up over time. Two patterns dominate: arithmetic increase (a fixed pound amount per period) and geometric increase (a fixed percentage per period). Each collapses into a closed form built from the level-annuity machinery of lecture 5.

Step 1

The 30-second version

An increasing annuity pays a non-level stream of cashflows that follow a regular pattern. The two canonical patterns are arithmetic (each payment differs from the next by a fixed amount $Q$) and geometric (each payment scales by a fixed factor $1 + k$). The arithmetic case has its own actuarial symbol $(Ia)_{\overline{n}|}$ defined as the PV of payments $1, 2, 3, \dots, n$ at times $1, 2, \dots, n$, satisfying

$$ (Ia)_{\overline{n}|} = \frac{\ddot{a}_{\overline{n}|} - n v^n}{i} $$

An arbitrary arithmetic stream $P, P+Q, P+2Q, \dots, P+(n-1)Q$ is then a linear combination of a level annuity and an increasing annuity. The geometric case is handled by substituting a transformed rate $j = (i - k)/(1 + k)$ into the level-annuity formula. Irregular patterns reduce to linear combinations of these two.

Feynman test A pension scheme pays £20,000 in year 1, £20,500 in year 2, £21,000 in year 3, and so on, rising by £500 each year for 25 years. To value this you do not need to add up 25 separate terms. Recognise that the pattern is $P = 20{,}000$ plus a £500 arithmetic increase. The PV is $20{,}000 \cdot a_{\overline{25}|} + 500 \cdot (Ia)_{\overline{25}| - 1}$ where the index-shift handles the fact that the first £500 increment doesn't kick in until year 2. One line replaces a 25-row spreadsheet.
What is beyond the source The source gives the arithmetic and geometric formulas with the algebraic identities. This lecture derives each, explains the substitution trick for geometric increases (which the source presents as a recipe), treats the four "in advance" variants explicitly, and adds a section on the continuous-increase analogue $(I \bar a)_{\overline{n}|}$ which is needed for inflation-indexed continuous payment streams in pension valuations.
Step 2

Why this matters

Most retirement income is paid as an inflation-linked annuity: payments rise each year with a price index. The PV of such a stream is the canonical geometric-increase calculation. Without the closed form, every actuarial valuation of an indexed pension would be a 30-row spreadsheet; with it, the same valuation is one line. Multiplied across hundreds of thousands of scheme members, the difference is several days of compute time saved per valuation cycle.

Salary scales in pension funding follow the same pattern. A member's final-salary pension at retirement depends on a projected salary path that typically rises with promotional increments (arithmetic) plus general wage inflation (geometric). The actuarial-mathematics machinery for combining the two is precisely the linear-combination machinery of this lecture, generalised to life-contingent versions in CT5 / CB1.

Commercial leases routinely escalate by a fixed percentage each year (a "stepped lease"). IFRS 16 lease accounting recognises a right-of-use asset and lease liability equal to the PV of the rental stream; if the rentals escalate, the PV is computed using the geometric-increase formula. The same logic applies to long-dated power-purchase agreements, telecoms infrastructure leases, and structured-finance amortisation schedules with escalator clauses.

Bond markets meet increasing annuities in two contexts: index-linked gilts (geometric increase tied to inflation, treated in lecture 4) and step-up bonds (arithmetic increases in coupon at predefined dates). Less common than vanilla fixed-coupon bonds, but the pricing toolkit must accommodate them.

Step 3

The mental model

Three patterns side by side. Level, arithmetic increase, geometric increase. Each is a different shape of cashflow over time; each collapses into a different closed form. The decomposition trick that links them is to write any increasing annuity as a linear combination of level and unit-increasing pieces.

Step 4

The detail

The unit increasing annuity $(Ia)_{\overline{n}|}$

The cleanest building block. Define $(Ia)_{\overline{n}|}$ as the PV at time $0$ of payments $1, 2, 3, \dots, n$ at times $1, 2, 3, \dots, n$. So

$$ (Ia)_{\overline{n}|} = v + 2v^2 + 3v^3 + \dots + n v^n = \sum_{k = 1}^{n} k v^k $$

To derive the closed form, write

$$ (Ia)_{\overline{n}|} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} k v^k $$

and use the identity (a standard trick: multiply $a_{\overline{n}|}$ by $1/(1-v)$):

$$ \sum_{k=1}^{n} k v^k = \frac{v - (n+1) v^{n+1} + n v^{n+2}}{(1 - v)^2} $$

Using $1 - v = d$ and simplifying:

$$ (Ia)_{\overline{n}|} = \frac{\ddot{a}_{\overline{n}|} - n v^n}{i} $$

This is the standard form. Equivalent forms using $(\ddot{a}_{\overline{n}|}) = (1+i) a_{\overline{n}|}$:

$$ (Ia)_{\overline{n}|} = \frac{(1+i) a_{\overline{n}|} - n v^n}{i} = \frac{a_{\overline{n}|} - n v^{n+1}}{1 - v} \cdot \frac{1}{1} $$

Among these the first is the cleanest for memorisation and the second is convenient for proofs.

The increasing-due annuity $(I\ddot{a})_{\overline{n}|}$

The same payments $1, 2, \dots, n$ but at times $0, 1, \dots, n-1$ (i.e., one year earlier each). The PV is one year's interest higher:

$$ (I\ddot{a})_{\overline{n}|} = (1+i) (Ia)_{\overline{n}|} = \frac{\ddot{a}_{\overline{n}|} - n v^n}{d} $$

Continuous increasing annuity $(I\bar{a})_{\overline{n}|}$ and $(\bar{I}\bar{a})_{\overline{n}|}$

Two continuous variants. The first pays at rate $t$ at time $t$ (so a piecewise-constant function jumping at integer multiples — increasing by 1 per year discretely, paid continuously):

$$ (I\bar{a})_{\overline{n}|} = \frac{\ddot{a}_{\overline{n}|} - n v^n}{\delta} $$

The second pays at rate $t$ continuously and increases continuously too (so the rate at time $t$ is exactly $t$, not the integer part of $t$):

$$ (\bar{I}\bar{a})_{\overline{n}|} = \int_0^n t \cdot v^t \, dt = \frac{\bar{a}_{\overline{n}|} - n v^n}{\delta} $$

The pattern $\ddot{a} - n v^n$ (discrete numerator) versus $\bar{a} - n v^n$ (continuous numerator) is the only difference between the two. Both denominators are $\delta$.

Arithmetic payments in arrears: payment $P + (k-1)Q$ at time $k$

The general arithmetic stream. Payments $P, P+Q, P+2Q, \dots, P+(n-1)Q$ at times $1, 2, \dots, n$. Decompose into a level annuity plus an increasing annuity:

$$ P + (k-1) Q = (P - Q) + k Q $$

so

$$ PV = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \left[(P - Q) + k Q\right] v^k = (P - Q) \, a_{\overline{n}|} + Q \cdot (Ia)_{\overline{n}|} $$

This is the standard decomposition. Equivalently:

$$ PV = P \, a_{\overline{n}|} + Q \cdot v \cdot (Ia)_{\overline{n-1}|} $$

which reads "the level part is $P$, the increasing part starts one year later and lasts $n-1$ years". Both forms give the same number.

Arithmetic payments in advance: payment $P + (k-1)Q$ at time $k-1$

Payments now at times $0, 1, \dots, n-1$. The PV is one year's interest higher than the in-arrears version:

$$ PV = (P - Q) \, \ddot{a}_{\overline{n}|} + Q \cdot (I\ddot{a})_{\overline{n}|} $$

or equivalently

$$ PV = P \, \ddot{a}_{\overline{n}|} + Q \cdot (I\ddot{a})_{\overline{n-1}|} $$

Geometric payments: $X(1+k)^{j-1}$ at time $j$

A geometric stream pays $X, X(1+k), X(1+k)^2, \dots, X(1+k)^{n-1}$ at times $1, 2, \dots, n$. The PV is

$$ PV = \sum_{j=1}^{n} X (1+k)^{j-1} v^j = X v \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \left[(1+k) v\right]^j $$

Geometric series with first term $1$ and common ratio $r = (1+k) v = (1+k)/(1+i)$:

$$ PV = X v \cdot \frac{1 - r^n}{1 - r} $$

The substitution: define a transformed rate $j$ by $(1+j) = (1+i)/(1+k)$. Then $v_j = 1/(1+j) = (1+k)/(1+i) = r$, so

$$ PV = X v \cdot \frac{1 - v_j^n}{1 - v_j} $$

and pulling $X v$ out and writing in terms of $j$:

$$ PV = \frac{X}{1 + k} \cdot \frac{1 - v_j^n}{j / (1+j)} = \frac{X}{1 + k} \cdot \ddot{a}_{\overline{n}|j} = X \cdot a_{\overline{n}|j} \cdot \frac{1+j}{1+k} $$

Simpler form most commonly used:

$$ PV = X \cdot \ddot{a}_{\overline{n}|j} \cdot v \quad \text{or equivalently} \quad PV = X \cdot a_{\overline{n}|j} \quad \text{when } X \text{ scales appropriately} $$

The cleanest statement is: a geometric-growth stream at rate $k$ discounted at $i$ equals a level stream of the same first payment discounted at $j = (i - k)/(1 + k)$. The substitution effectively cancels the growth $k$ against part of the discount $i$, leaving a residual rate $j$ to discount with.

Geometric payments when $k = i$

An edge case worth noting. If the growth rate exactly equals the discount rate, then $j = 0$. The level-annuity formula at $j = 0$ is $a_{\overline{n}|0} = n$ (because each cashflow is undiscounted in the transformed frame). So a geometrically growing stream with $k = i$ has $PV = X v \cdot n = n X / (1+i)$. The first payment, divided by $1 + i$, times the number of payments.

This is also the case where the linear approximation breaks: the standard substitution $j = (i - k) / (1 + k)$ correctly handles it, but the limit form $j = 0$ is the cleanest expression.

Irregular payments

Patterns that don't fit neatly as arithmetic or geometric reduce to linear combinations of the building blocks above. Two principles:

Decomposition
Any cashflow stream can be written as a sum of building-block streams (level, increasing, deferred). Value each piece with its closed form; sum the values.
Substitution
If a stream grows by a constant percentage each period, substitute a transformed rate $j$ and reuse the level formula. This trick generalises to any geometric component within an otherwise irregular stream.

Worked example: a pension with inflation indexation

A scheme member retires at age 65 with a pension of £30,000 a year in arrears, increasing by 3% a year on each anniversary. The pension is payable for the rest of life; for this exercise treat it as certain for 25 years. The valuation rate is $i = 6\%$ per annum.

Stream: t = 1 30,000 t = 2 30,000 × 1.03 = 30,900 t = 3 30,000 × 1.03² = 31,827 ... t = 25 30,000 × 1.03^24 = 61,030 Total payments over 25 years = sum = approximately 1,090,000. Geometric: X = 30,000, k = 3%, n = 25. Transformed rate: 1 + j = (1 + i) / (1 + k) = 1.06 / 1.03 = 1.02913 j = 2.913% per annum PV = X × a_n|j = 30,000 × a_25|0.02913 = 30,000 × (1 - 1.02913^(-25)) / 0.02913 = 30,000 × (1 - 0.4891) / 0.02913 = 30,000 × 17.539 = £526,170 Compare to a level pension of £30,000 for 25 years at i = 6%: PV = 30,000 × a_25|0.06 = 30,000 × 12.7834 = £383,500 The indexation premium (the extra PV from rising payments) is 526,170 - 383,500 = £142,670, about 37% of the level PV. This is the scale of the inflation-protection benefit at 3% indexation and 6% valuation rate.

The same calculation under lower inflation indexation (say 1%) gives a smaller premium (about 12%); under higher indexation (5%) the premium balloons (about 90% of the level PV). The PV of indexation is one of the most important sensitivities in pension valuation.

Worked example: an arithmetic-increasing salary scale

An employee's salary in year 1 is £40,000 and rises by £2,000 each year for 30 years. The actuary needs the PV of total salary at $i = 5\%$.

Stream: 40,000, 42,000, 44,000, ..., 40,000 + 29 × 2,000 = 98,000 P = 40,000, Q = 2,000, n = 30, i = 5%. a_30|0.05 = (1 - 1.05^(-30)) / 0.05 = 15.3725 ä_30|0.05 = 1.05 × 15.3725 = 16.1411 v^30 = 1.05^(-30) = 0.2314 (Ia)_30|0.05 = (ä_30 - 30 v^30) / i = (16.1411 - 30 × 0.2314) / 0.05 = (16.1411 - 6.9420) / 0.05 = 183.98 PV = (P - Q) × a_30 + Q × (Ia)_30 = (40,000 - 2,000) × 15.3725 + 2,000 × 183.98 = 38,000 × 15.3725 + 2,000 × 183.98 = 584,155 + 367,960 = £952,115 Verify alternative decomposition: PV = P × a_30 + Q × v × (Ia)_29 (left as exercise; same number).

The total salary in money terms over 30 years is $\sum (40 + 2(k-1)) \cdot 1000 = 40 \cdot 30 + 2 \cdot 29 \cdot 30 / 2 = 1{,}200{,}000 + 870{,}000 = \pounds 2{,}070{,}000$, so the PV (£952k) is about 46% of the nominal total — the discount has roughly halved the value over the 30-year horizon.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Decompose first, value second

Any complicated cashflow pattern, however irregular, reduces to a sum of level annuities, increasing annuities, and deferred versions thereof. The skill is recognising the decomposition before reaching for a formula. A practising actuary draws the cashflow diagram, identifies the building-block patterns visually, and assembles the PV as a sum of closed-form pieces. Never sum row by row in a spreadsheet what one formula can do in a line.

The substitution trick is more general than it looks

The $j = (i - k)/(1 + k)$ substitution is presented as a special-purpose tool for geometric streams. It is more accurately the general "discounted growth" trick: any cashflow that grows at a constant rate within a discounted framework can be re-expressed with the growth and part of the discount cancelled. The same idea appears in stochastic-finance derivations (the "change of numeraire" technique) and in real-options analysis. Recognising it lets you transfer intuition between fields.

Sensitivity to the indexation rate

Pension liabilities are unusually sensitive to assumed indexation: a 1% change in assumed inflation can shift the PV by 15-20% on a typical 30-year scheme. Project appraisal of long-dated infrastructure with inflation-linked cashflows shows the same sensitivity. The professional habit is to present the PV at three or four assumed indexation rates rather than a single point estimate, because the spread itself communicates the inflation exposure.

Arithmetic versus geometric: pick deliberately

Stepped leases and step-up bonds use arithmetic increases by contract. Inflation-linked pensions and salary scales use geometric. Confusing the two is a common error: a £500-a-year increase off a £20,000 base is 2.5% growth in year 1 but only 1.7% by year 20, whereas a 2.5% geometric growth maintains its proportional bite. The shapes diverge over long horizons; choose deliberately.

Continuous variants have a niche role

Outside academic finance and certain insurance reserve calculations, fully continuous increasing annuities are rare in practice. They are valuable as mathematical limits (cleaner derivatives, integration tricks) and as approximations when the payment frequency is very high. For most real work, the discrete $p$-thly form with $p = 12$ is the operational choice.
