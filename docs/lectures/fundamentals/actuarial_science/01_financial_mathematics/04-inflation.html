Inflation: real versus money rates

A 5% return on a deposit means very different things in a 1% inflation environment and a 10% inflation environment. The money rate measures the growth of nominal pounds; the real rate measures the growth of purchasing power. The conversion between them is the Fisher relation, and it underpins everything from index-linked gilts to pension valuation.

Lecture 4 of 7 Reading time, 20 minutes Source: UoP / Financial Mathematics / Summary.pdf §4
Step 1

The 30-second version

A money rate $i_M$ measures the growth of pounds. A real rate $i_R$ measures the growth of purchasing power, which is pounds adjusted for inflation. If inflation runs at rate $f$ per year, the two are linked by the Fisher relation:

$$ 1 + i_M = (1 + i_R)(1 + f) $$

so $i_R = (1 + i_M)/(1 + f) - 1$ exactly, and $i_R \approx i_M - f$ when both rates are small. The real rate is the rate at which goods you can buy grow, regardless of the price level.

Two settings demand the real rate. Pricing index-linked securities, where coupons grow with inflation. Long-horizon valuation (pensions, life annuities, infrastructure), where ignoring inflation produces nonsense at the long end. For most short-horizon arithmetic the money rate is what is observed; the real rate is recovered by deflating.

Feynman test A grandparent puts £1,000 into a deposit account in 1995, earning 6% a year. By 2025 the deposit is worth £5,743 (the money value). But a basket of groceries that cost £30 in 1995 costs £75 in 2025 (inflation around 3.1% a year). So the grandparent's deposit has grown from "33 baskets" to "76 baskets". The real return is the growth in baskets, not in pounds: $76 / 33 - 1 = 130\%$ over 30 years, which is $2.81\%$ a year. The Fisher relation says exactly that: $(1.06)/(1.031) - 1 = 2.81\%$. The money return was 6% and the real return was 2.81%; the difference is inflation.
What is beyond the source The source records the two-line Fisher identity. This lecture motivates it from scratch (it is not an approximation: it is exact when rates compound geometrically), derives the approximation $i_R \approx i_M - f$ and shows when it breaks (high $i_M$ or high $f$), and treats two practical extensions: the real force of interest and the tax-adjusted real rate. It also explains why ILS yields are quoted in real terms.
Step 2

Why this matters

Pensions are the headline example. A pension promise made in 1995 that paid £10,000 a year for life looked generous at the time. Thirty years later, £10,000 buys roughly half as many groceries as it did in 1995. The promise was made in money terms but its value to the recipient is in real terms. Schemes that index pensions to inflation transfer the inflation risk from the recipient to the sponsor; schemes that do not, transfer it the other way. Either way, the actuarial valuation must respect the real-versus-money distinction or the liability will be mismeasured.

Index-linked gilts are the second example. UK ILGs (index-linked gilts) and US TIPS (Treasury inflation-protected securities) pay coupons whose nominal size grows with a published inflation index. The traded yield on these instruments is the real yield: the rate of return after inflation. Real yields are usually substantially lower than nominal yields (because part of the nominal yield compensates the holder for expected inflation). The gap between nominal and real yields gives a market-implied measure of expected inflation, the so-called breakeven inflation rate.

Long-horizon project appraisal is the third. A 30-year infrastructure project with cashflows quoted in today's pounds is a real-cashflow stream; its NPV should be computed at a real discount rate. Same project with cashflows quoted in nominal pounds (the actual amounts in the year they occur) is a money stream; its NPV should be computed at a money rate. Mixing the two halves of the calculation produces an NPV biased by tens of percent. Lecture 7 returns to this.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture two parallel timelines. The top timeline measures pounds; the bottom timeline measures baskets of groceries. The price level $P(t)$ converts between them: 1 pound at time $t$ buys $1 / P(t)$ baskets. The money rate is the growth rate of pounds; the real rate is the growth rate of baskets.

The picture also shows why nominal returns can be misleading. A "6% return" tells a recipient how the pound balance grows but does not say how much more bread they can buy. A 6% return with 3% inflation feels different from a 6% return with 7% inflation, even though the pound balance is identical.

Step 4

The detail

The Fisher relation

Define:

  • $i_M$ = the money rate of interest: the rate at which a nominal pound balance grows over one year.
  • $i_R$ = the real rate of interest: the rate at which the purchasing power of that balance grows over one year.
  • $f$ = the rate of inflation: the rate at which the price level rises over one year.

A pound today buys one basket today (by definition). A pound in a year's time buys $1 / (1 + f)$ baskets (because each basket now costs $1 + f$ pounds). So a pound deposit growing to $1 + i_M$ pounds in a year buys

$$ \frac{1 + i_M}{1 + f} \text{ baskets at } t = 1 $$

and the proportional growth in baskets is

$$ 1 + i_R = \frac{1 + i_M}{1 + f} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad 1 + i_M = (1 + i_R)(1 + f) $$

This is the Fisher equation. It is exact when all three rates are expressed as effective annual rates over the same one-year period.

Solving for $i_R$:

$$ i_R = \frac{1 + i_M}{1 + f} - 1 = \frac{i_M - f}{1 + f} $$

The approximation $i_R \approx i_M - f$

Expand the exact expression:

$$ i_R = (1 + i_M)(1 + f)^{-1} - 1 = (1 + i_M)(1 - f + f^2 - \dots) - 1 = i_M - f - i_M f + O(f^2) $$

For small $i_M$ and $f$, the cross-term $i_M f$ is second-order and the approximation is

$$ i_R \approx i_M - f $$

The approximation works well when both rates are small (a few percent each). It breaks down at moderate-to-high inflation. Example: $i_M = 12\%$, $f = 10\%$ gives exact $i_R = 1.82\%$ but approximation $2\%$, a 10% error in the real-rate estimate. At higher rates the error grows. Always use the exact formula for any rate above about 5%.

Real discount factor and real present value

The discount factor in real terms is $v_R(t) = (1 + i_R)^{-t}$. To compute the present value of a real cashflow stream, discount at $i_R$. To compute the present value of a money cashflow stream, discount at $i_M$. The two routes give the same answer if the cashflows and the discount rate are consistent:

Consistency rule:

$$ PV_{\text{today}} = \sum_t \frac{C_t^{\text{money}}}{(1 + i_M)^t} = \sum_t \frac{C_t^{\text{real}}}{(1 + i_R)^t} $$

where $C_t^{\text{money}} = C_t^{\text{real}} \cdot (1 + f)^t$ converts a real cashflow at time $t$ to its money equivalent.

This is the single most useful identity for project appraisal: real cashflows discounted at real rates equal money cashflows discounted at money rates. Mixing them (real cashflows at money rates) inflates the PV; the opposite mix deflates it.

The real force of interest

If $\delta_M$ is the constant money force and $\delta_f$ is the constant force of inflation, then the constant real force is

$$ \delta_R = \delta_M - \delta_f $$

which is exact (no approximation), because the Fisher equation in force form is just

$$ e^{\delta_R} = \frac{e^{\delta_M}}{e^{\delta_f}} = e^{\delta_M - \delta_f} $$

This is the cleanest argument for working in force terms in inflation problems: the conversion is subtraction rather than the ratio in the effective-rate form. Continuous-time finance papers and central-bank yield-curve work nearly always use forces for this reason.

Tax-adjusted real rate

An investor paying income tax at rate $\tau$ on nominal interest receives a post-tax money rate of

$$ i_M^{\text{after tax}} = i_M (1 - \tau) $$

and the corresponding post-tax real rate is

$$ i_R^{\text{after tax}} = \frac{1 + i_M(1 - \tau)}{1 + f} - 1 $$

The combination of tax and inflation can turn a positive nominal return into a negative real one. Example: $i_M = 4\%$, $f = 3\%$, $\tau = 40\%$. Post-tax money rate $i_M(1-\tau) = 2.4\%$. Post-tax real rate $i_R = (1.024/1.03) - 1 = -0.58\%$. A 4% deposit feels comfortable in pre-tax pre-inflation terms; in post-tax post-inflation terms the saver is losing 58 basis points per year.

This dynamic, persistent during the 1970s, is sometimes called the "inflation tax": even without any explicit tax, inflation that exceeds the rate of return on safe assets erodes wealth. With tax on top of inflation, the erosion accelerates. The professional habit is always to report real, post-tax returns alongside nominal pre-tax ones.

Worked example: index-linked gilt

A 5-year UK index-linked gilt has face value £100 and a real coupon of 0.5% per year paid annually. The investor expects RPI inflation to average 2.5% per year over the next 5 years. What is the expected nominal yield from holding the gilt to maturity?

Cashflows in real (today's purchasing power) terms: t = 1, 2, 3, 4 coupon = 0.50 t = 5 coupon + redemption = 100.50 PV at the gilt's quoted real yield is by definition the gilt's price. Suppose price is 102 (real terms). Real yield i_R: solve 102 = sum 0.5 * v_R^t (t=1..4) + 100.5 * v_R^5 v_R = 1 / (1 + i_R) Numerically (omitting the iteration): i_R = 0.094% per year. Money yield i_M, using Fisher with expected f = 2.5%: 1 + i_M = (1 + i_R)(1 + f) = 1.00094 * 1.025 = 1.02597 i_M = 2.597% per year Compare to a 5-year conventional gilt yielding, say, 3.2% nominal. Implied breakeven inflation = 3.2% - 0.094% (real ILG yield) = 3.106% (approx) or exactly: 1.032 / 1.00094 = 1.03103, so breakeven f = 3.103% If the investor's inflation expectation (2.5%) is below the breakeven (3.10%), the conventional gilt is "expected" to outperform the ILG; if inflation surprises above 3.10%, the ILG outperforms.

This is the standard framework for trading ILGs against conventional gilts: the spread is a market-implied inflation rate, and an investor who has a private view on inflation different from the market view takes a position in the spread.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The exact Fisher equation, never the approximation

In any pricing or reserving context, use $1 + i_M = (1 + i_R)(1 + f)$. The approximation $i_R \approx i_M - f$ is acceptable for mental arithmetic and for low-rate environments only. As inflation has fluctuated between zero and double digits in the past 30 years across major economies, the safer professional habit is to use the exact form by default.

Match the rate to the cashflow

Real cashflows go with real rates; money cashflows go with money rates. The rule sounds trivial but is the source of the largest single class of errors in long-horizon valuation. The fastest sanity check on a pension or infrastructure valuation: ask whether the cashflows and the discount rate are consistently labelled. If not, the answer is wrong.

Breakeven inflation is information, but noisy

The market-implied breakeven inflation rate is the difference (or, more precisely, the Fisher-implied gap) between conventional and index-linked yields at the same maturity. It is widely cited as a real-time measure of inflation expectations. But it also reflects an inflation risk premium (compensation for inflation uncertainty), a liquidity premium (ILGs trade thinner than conventional gilts), and segmentation effects (different buyer bases). Take it as a noisy signal, not a clean expectation.

Indexation lag is a hidden complication

Real-world ILSs have an indexation lag: the inflation index used to scale the coupon at time $t$ is the index at $t - L$ for some lag $L$ (typically 3 to 8 months). The lag matters for valuation: between the cut-off date and payment date, the cashflow is effectively nominal, not real. The bigger $L$, the more nominal exposure remains. UK ILGs moved from an 8-month lag to a 3-month lag in 2005 precisely to reduce this nominal exposure.

The Fisher hypothesis

Beyond the actuarial identity, the macroeconomic "Fisher hypothesis" claims that in the long run, $i_M$ moves one-for-one with $f$, so $i_R$ is constant. Empirically this is approximately but not exactly true; real rates fluctuate with productivity growth, central-bank policy, and risk premia. The financial-mathematics identity is exact; the macro hypothesis is a different claim about how rates and inflation co-vary in time.
