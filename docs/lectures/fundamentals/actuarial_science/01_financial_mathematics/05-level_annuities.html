Level annuities

An annuity is a sum of identical cashflows on a regular schedule. The closed-form formulas $a_{\overline{n}|}, \ddot{a}_{\overline{n}|}, s_{\overline{n}|}, \ddot{s}_{\overline{n}|}$ and their $p$-thly and continuous cousins collapse those sums into one expression each. Recognising which formula applies is the second-most-tested skill in this course after rate conversion.

Lecture 5 of 7 Reading time, 40 minutes Source: UoP / Financial Mathematics / Summary.pdf §5, §6
Step 1

The 30-second version

A level annuity is a stream of equal payments at regular intervals. The two basic versions are annuity in arrears (payments at the end of each period: $a_{\overline{n}|}$ for the present value, $s_{\overline{n}|}$ for the accumulated value) and annuity due (payments at the start: $\ddot{a}_{\overline{n}|}$, $\ddot{s}_{\overline{n}|}$). Each has a $p$-thly variant for payments more frequent than annual ($a^{(p)}_{\overline{n}|}$, $\ddot{a}^{(p)}_{\overline{n}|}$) and a continuous variant ($\bar{a}_{\overline{n}|}$). The perpetuity is the limit $n \to \infty$, and the deferred annuity is the standard form pushed forward by $m$ periods.

All twelve formulas reduce to one rule: discount each payment to time zero (or accumulate to time $n$) using the geometric-series identity. The arrears form $a_{\overline{n}|} = (1 - v^n)/i$ is the parent; every other formula is a manipulation. Memorise the parent and the four shift-of-frame conversions ($\ddot{a} = (1+i) a$; $a^{(p)} = a \cdot i / i^{(p)}$; $\bar{a} = a \cdot i / \delta$; $m | a = v^m a$) and the whole table regenerates.

Feynman test Imagine winning a lottery that pays £10,000 a year for 20 years, with the first payment in a year's time. How much is that worth today, if interest rates are 5%? The answer is $10{,}000 \times a_{\overline{20}|0.05} = 10{,}000 \times 12.4622 = \pounds124{,}622$. The annuity factor 12.4622 is the present value of 20 future payments of £1 each — exactly the number of "today-pounds" each future pound is worth, totalled. If the lottery offered to pay £125,000 today instead, the two are economically equivalent at the assumed rate.
What is beyond the source The source gives all twelve formulas in a single table. This lecture derives each from the geometric-series identity, lays out the four "shift-of-frame" conversions that link them, and adds two pieces of practical machinery the source assumes: a worked mortgage amortisation schedule (the most common real-world use of $a_{\overline{n}|}$) and a treatment of the timing of an annuity's first payment that exam scripts routinely misread.
Step 2

Why this matters

Level annuities sit under almost every retail and wholesale financial product. Mortgages are level annuities from the lender's perspective. Pensions in payment are level annuities from the annuitant's perspective. Bond coupon strips, structured savings products, operating-lease cashflows, fixed-term deposits are all annuities of one form or another. The closed forms in this lecture turn a 30-line discounted-cashflow calculation into one multiplication.

The arithmetic also underpins regulatory and accounting work. IFRS 16 lease accounting computes the right-of-use asset and lease liability as the present value of remaining rentals, which is a level annuity if rentals are level. IFRS 9 effective-interest-rate calculation on a loan starts from the level-annuity payment schedule. Capital requirements on traded annuities use the same machinery.

Beyond the formulas, this lecture also fixes a vocabulary point that often trips up newcomers: "annuity" in the actuarial sense is just a level cashflow stream, with no implication that it is a life-contingent product. Life annuities, which depend on survival, are treated in CT5 / CB1. The annuities in this lecture are annuities-certain: their cashflows are guaranteed.

Step 3

The mental model

Four cashflow shapes. Arrears, due, $p$-thly, continuous. All four describe the same total ($n$ years of payment at rate £1 per year), but with different distributions of when within the year the cash arrives.

The four shapes give four PVs and four accumulated values, all the same up to a constant. Once you have $a_{\overline{n}|}$, the others fall out by simple multiplication. That is the structural insight: a single integral, four conventions.

Step 4

The detail

The two algebraic identities

An arithmetic series of $n$ terms with first term $a$ and common difference $d$ has sum

$$ S_n = \frac{n}{2}\big[2a + (n - 1)d\big] $$

A geometric series of $n$ terms with first term $a$ and common ratio $r \ne 1$ has sum

$$ S_n = a \cdot \frac{1 - r^n}{1 - r} $$

For $|r| < 1$, the infinite geometric series converges:

$$ S_\infty = \frac{a}{1 - r} $$

Every annuity formula in this lecture is the geometric-series sum applied to a sequence of $v^k$ values. The arithmetic series will reappear in lecture 6 for increasing annuities.

Annuity in arrears, annual: $a_{\overline{n}|}$

Definition: the present value at time $0$ of $n$ payments of 1, each at the end of years $1, 2, \dots, n$.

$$ a_{\overline{n}|} = v + v^2 + v^3 + \dots + v^n = v \cdot \frac{1 - v^n}{1 - v} $$

Using $1 - v = d$ and $v = 1 / (1+i)$:

$$ a_{\overline{n}|} = \frac{v(1 - v^n)}{1 - v} = \frac{v - v^{n+1}}{1 - v} = \frac{1 - v^n}{i} $$

The last form is the most common version on exam scripts. Note that $i \cdot a_{\overline{n}|} = 1 - v^n$, a nice mnemonic.

Accumulated value in arrears: $s_{\overline{n}|}$

Definition: the accumulated value at time $n$ of $n$ payments of 1, each at the end of years $1, 2, \dots, n$.

$$ s_{\overline{n}|} = 1 + (1+i) + (1+i)^2 + \dots + (1+i)^{n-1} = \frac{(1+i)^n - 1}{i} $$

The relationship $s_{\overline{n}|} = (1+i)^n a_{\overline{n}|}$ is immediate: accumulating $a_{\overline{n}|}$ from time $0$ to time $n$ multiplies by $(1+i)^n$.

Annuity due, annual: $\ddot{a}_{\overline{n}|}$

Definition: the present value at time $0$ of $n$ payments of 1, each at the start of years $1, 2, \dots, n$, i.e., at times $0, 1, 2, \dots, n-1$.

$$ \ddot{a}_{\overline{n}|} = 1 + v + v^2 + \dots + v^{n-1} = \frac{1 - v^n}{1 - v} = \frac{1 - v^n}{d} $$

The shift-of-frame identity:

$$ \ddot{a}_{\overline{n}|} = (1+i) \, a_{\overline{n}|} $$

which says "moving an annuity's payments one year earlier increases its PV by one year's interest". The corresponding accumulated value:

$$ \ddot{s}_{\overline{n}|} = (1+i) s_{\overline{n}|} = \frac{(1+i)^n - 1}{d} $$

Annuity payable $p$-thly, in arrears: $a^{(p)}_{\overline{n}|}$

Definition: the present value at time $0$ of $np$ payments of $1/p$ each, at the end of every $1/p$-year period.

$$ a^{(p)}_{\overline{n}|} = \frac{1}{p}\left[v^{1/p} + v^{2/p} + \dots + v^n\right] = \frac{1}{p} \cdot v^{1/p} \cdot \frac{1 - v^n}{1 - v^{1/p}} $$

Using $1 - v^{1/p} = d^{(p)}/p$ and $v^{1/p} = 1 - d^{(p)}/p$:

$$ a^{(p)}_{\overline{n}|} = \frac{1 - v^n}{i^{(p)}} $$

This is the cleanest form. By analogy:

$$ \ddot{a}^{(p)}_{\overline{n}|} = \frac{1 - v^n}{d^{(p)}}, \qquad s^{(p)}_{\overline{n}|} = \frac{(1+i)^n - 1}{i^{(p)}}, \qquad \ddot{s}^{(p)}_{\overline{n}|} = \frac{(1+i)^n - 1}{d^{(p)}} $$

Continuous annuity: $\bar{a}_{\overline{n}|}$

Definition: a payment stream at the constant rate of 1 per year over $[0, n]$, paid continuously.

$$ \bar{a}_{\overline{n}|} = \int_0^n v^t \, dt = \int_0^n e^{-\delta t} \, dt = \frac{1 - v^n}{\delta} $$

And the accumulated form:

$$ \bar{s}_{\overline{n}|} = \int_0^n (1+i)^{n-t}\,dt = \frac{(1+i)^n - 1}{\delta} $$

The continuous form is the $p \to \infty$ limit of $a^{(p)}_{\overline{n}|}$ (or $\ddot{a}^{(p)}_{\overline{n}|}$ — the distinction vanishes in the limit because the period becomes infinitesimal).

The unified table

All eight formulas have the same numerator $(1 - v^n)$ for PVs and $((1+i)^n - 1)$ for accumulated values, with a denominator that depends on the convention:

ConventionPVAccumulated value
Annual in arrears$a_{\overline{n}|} = (1 - v^n) / i$$s_{\overline{n}|} = ((1+i)^n - 1) / i$
Annual in advance (due)$\ddot{a}_{\overline{n}|} = (1 - v^n) / d$$\ddot{s}_{\overline{n}|} = ((1+i)^n - 1) / d$
$p$-thly in arrears$a^{(p)}_{\overline{n}|} = (1 - v^n) / i^{(p)}$$s^{(p)}_{\overline{n}|} = ((1+i)^n - 1) / i^{(p)}$
$p$-thly in advance$\ddot{a}^{(p)}_{\overline{n}|} = (1 - v^n) / d^{(p)}$$\ddot{s}^{(p)}_{\overline{n}|} = ((1+i)^n - 1) / d^{(p)}$
Continuous$\bar{a}_{\overline{n}|} = (1 - v^n) / \delta$$\bar{s}_{\overline{n}|} = ((1+i)^n - 1) / \delta$

Five PVs, five accumulated values, one numerator each. The only thing that changes is the rate convention in the denominator. The ordering follows the rate-grid ordering ($d < d^{(p)} < \delta < i^{(p)} < i$):

$$ a_{\overline{n}|} < a^{(p)}_{\overline{n}|} < \bar{a}_{\overline{n}|} < \ddot{a}^{(p)}_{\overline{n}|} < \ddot{a}_{\overline{n}|} $$

which reflects exactly the principle that paying earlier (or more frequently) increases the PV.

Perpetuities: $n \to \infty$

When the annuity goes on forever, $v^n \to 0$ (assuming $i > 0$), so

$$ a_{\overline{\infty}|} = \frac{1}{i}, \qquad \ddot{a}_{\overline{\infty}|} = \frac{1}{d}, \qquad a^{(p)}_{\overline{\infty}|} = \frac{1}{i^{(p)}}, \qquad \bar{a}_{\overline{\infty}|} = \frac{1}{\delta} $$

The classic example is an irredeemable bond ("perpetual" or "consol") paying a fixed coupon forever; its price at yield $i$ is the coupon divided by $i$. Historically the UK consols (issued from 1751, redeemed in full only in 2015) were the textbook example. A 2.5% consol with face value £100 was worth £100 only when the prevailing yield was 2.5%; with yields at 5%, it was worth £50.

Deferred annuities

An annuity that starts $m$ years from now and runs for $n$ years has PV

$$ {}_{m|}a_{\overline{n}|} = v^m \cdot a_{\overline{n}|} = \frac{v^m - v^{m+n}}{i} = a_{\overline{m+n}|} - a_{\overline{m}|} $$

The two equivalent forms reflect two ways to think about deferment: as an ordinary annuity discounted back $m$ years, or as the difference between two annuities (one running $m+n$ years from time $0$, the other running $m$ years from time $0$).

Deferment is the standard form for pension valuations: a 40-year-old's pension starts at age 65, so the relevant annuity is deferred by 25 years.

Relationship table

The four shift-of-frame conversions to keep in mind:

Arrears to due
$\ddot{a}_{\overline{n}|} = (1+i) \, a_{\overline{n}|}$, $\ddot{s}_{\overline{n}|} = (1+i) \, s_{\overline{n}|}$. Multiply by $(1+i)$ to shift payments forward.
Annual to $p$-thly
$a^{(p)}_{\overline{n}|} = a_{\overline{n}|} \cdot i / i^{(p)}$, $\ddot{a}^{(p)}_{\overline{n}|} = \ddot{a}_{\overline{n}|} \cdot d / d^{(p)}$. Convert via the rate ratio.
Annual to continuous
$\bar{a}_{\overline{n}|} = a_{\overline{n}|} \cdot i / \delta$. The continuous version compounds at $\delta$ rather than $i$.
Ordinary to deferred
${}_{m|}a_{\overline{n}|} = v^m \cdot a_{\overline{n}|}$. Multiply by the $m$-year discount factor.

Worked example: a 25-year mortgage

A borrower takes out a £200,000 mortgage at 4.8% nominal annual rate convertible monthly, repaid in 300 monthly instalments in arrears. Compute the monthly payment and the interest paid in year 1.

Convert to effective: i^(12) = 4.8%, so j = 0.4% effective monthly rate. The loan is a 300-month annuity-in-arrears at j = 0.4% per month. Annuity factor: a_300|j = (1 - 1.004^(-300)) / 0.004 = (1 - 0.30197) / 0.004 = 174.508 Monthly payment K satisfies: K * a_300|j = 200,000 K = 200,000 / 174.508 = £1,146.07 Interest paid in year 1 (12 payments): Sum of interest portions over months 1..12 Easier: interest in year 1 = total payments - principal reduction in year 1 Outstanding at start of year 1 = 200,000.00 Outstanding at end of year 1 = K * a_288|j (288 months remaining) = 1,146.07 * 173.110 = £198,402.00 Principal repaid in year 1 = 200,000 - 198,402 = £1,598.00 Total payments in year 1 = 12 * 1,146.07 = £13,752.84 Interest paid in year 1 = 13,752.84 - 1,598 = £12,154.84 Sanity check: interest in year 1 should be roughly i^(12) * 200,000 = 0.048 * 200,000 = £9,600, but that ignores the falling balance. Actual £12,155 reflects monthly compounding over the year and the fact that the balance only barely falls (most of year-1 payments are interest, not principal).

Two things to note. (1) The interest portion of year-1 payments is huge: 88% of the year's payments are interest, only 12% principal. (2) The principal-repayment portion of each payment grows geometrically as the balance falls; by year 20, the ratio is reversed (almost all principal, very little interest). This is the standard mortgage amortisation shape, treated explicitly in lecture 7's project-appraisal context.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The numerator is timing, the denominator is the rate convention

Every annuity formula factors into "the numerator depends on $n$ and $v$ and tells you how many payments there are net of discounting" and "the denominator depends on the rate convention and tells you how to weight each payment". Internalising this factorisation lets you regenerate any formula from $a_{\overline{n}|} = (1 - v^n)/i$ and the rate-conversion grid. Memorising the eight formulas separately is more brittle.

$p$-thly is the practical default

Pure annual annuities are rare in practice. Mortgages are monthly, bonds are semi-annual, pensions are monthly. The $p$-thly forms in this lecture are the formulas that real-world calculations actually use. The annual forms are useful pedagogically and as starting points, but the practical denominator is $i^{(p)}$ or $d^{(p)}$, not $i$ or $d$.

Continuous annuities are mostly pedagogical, but with two exceptions

Most real cashflows are discrete. The continuous annuity formula is useful in two contexts. First, as the analytic limit that smooths out the $p$-thly form: in some problems, $\bar{a}_{\overline{n}|}$ is easier to integrate than $a^{(p)}_{\overline{n}|}$ is to sum. Second, in models where the payment stream is genuinely continuous: continuous compounding in academic finance, certain insurance reserve calculations on benefits that accrue continuously.

The mortgage amortisation shape is general

The pattern of "mostly interest at the start, mostly principal at the end" applies to every annuity-style loan, not just mortgages. Personal loans, car finance, fixed-rate corporate borrowing, all amortise this way. The ratio of interest to principal in each payment depends on the loan term and the rate, but the shape is universal. The skill is reading the shape from the formula rather than tabulating it row by row.

Annuities are linear in the payment amount

If $K$ pounds per period gives PV $K \cdot a_{\overline{n}|}$, then $2K$ per period gives $2K \cdot a_{\overline{n}|}$. This linearity lets you decompose complex schedules: a payment stream that pays £100 for 5 years then £150 for 10 years is the sum of a £100 annuity over 15 years and a £50 annuity over 10 years deferred by 5 years. Linearity is the workhorse of lecture 6's irregular-payments treatment.

Step 6

Test yourself

  1. State the geometric-series identity and use it to derive $a_{\overline{n}|} = (1 - v^n)/i$.
  2. State the relationships among $a_{\overline{n}|}, \ddot{a}_{\overline{n}|}, a^{(p)}_{\overline{n}|}, \ddot{a}^{(p)}_{\overline{n}|}, \bar{a}_{\overline{n}|}$. Which is largest and which is smallest at $i > 0$?
  3. Compute $a_{\overline{10}|0.06}$, $\ddot{a}_{\overline{10}|0.06}$, $a^{(12)}_{\overline{10}|0.06}$, $\bar{a}_{\overline{10}|0.06}$. Verify the ordering.
  4. State the formulas for $s_{\overline{n}|}, \ddot{s}_{\overline{n}|}, s^{(p)}_{\overline{n}|}$ and explain when each is the right choice.
  5. State and derive the formula for a perpetuity in arrears and in advance.
  6. A pension scheme owes 30 years of payments of £20,000 a year in arrears starting from a member's age 65. The member is currently 40. At $i = 4\%$ effective annual, what is the present value of the liability?
  7. A 10-year, semi-annually paying corporate bond has face £100 and a coupon rate of 5% per annum. Express the bond's price as a sum of annuity and ZCB components.
  8. Derive $\bar{a}_{\overline{n}|}$ from first principles by integrating $v^t$ from $0$ to $n$.
  9. The PV of a 20-year level annuity at $i = 5\%$ is £125,000. What is the annual payment?
  10. A 30-year mortgage of £300,000 at $i^{(12)} = 5.5\%$. Compute the monthly payment and the outstanding balance after 10 years.
