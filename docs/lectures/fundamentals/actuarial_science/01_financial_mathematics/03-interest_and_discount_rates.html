Lectures / Financial mathematics

Interest and discount rates

The same accumulation factor can be written six different ways. Effective annual, nominal $p$-thly, effective $p$-thly, the discount versions of each, and the force of interest. The conversion grid linking them is the most-tested piece of CT1; it is also the most error-prone, because every entry is a special case of every other. This lecture builds the grid from first principles.

Lecture 3 of 7 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: UoP / Financial Mathematics / Summary.pdf §3
Step 1

The 30-second version

An interest rate is a number that, with a convention, specifies an accumulation factor. The convention has three axes: interest versus discount (is the rate quoted on the start balance or the end balance?), effective versus nominal (is the quoted figure the rate per period or the annualised figure?), and compounding frequency ($p = 1$ annual, $p = 12$ monthly, $p = \infty$ continuous). Pick a point on each axis and you have one of six standard rate definitions: $i$, $i^{(p)}$, $d$, $d^{(p)}$, $\delta$, plus the simple rate $s$ that sits outside this grid. All six are different names for the same one-year accumulation factor.

The two operating identities to memorise:

$$ 1 + i = \left(1 + \frac{i^{(p)}}{p}\right)^p = \left(1 - \frac{d^{(p)}}{p}\right)^{-p} = e^{\delta} $$

and the relation between interest and discount at the same frequency:

$$ d = \frac{i}{1+i}, \qquad d^{(p)} = \frac{i^{(p)}}{1 + i^{(p)} / p} $$

Feynman test A bank advertises a savings rate of "4.8% APR compounded monthly". A second bank advertises "4.91% effective annual rate, no compounding catch". Which is better? The first quotes a nominal monthly rate $i^{(12)} = 4.8\%$, meaning the monthly rate is $0.4\%$ and the effective annual is $(1.004)^{12} - 1 = 4.91\%$. They are identical. The conversion grid is the rule that lets you spot this in 10 seconds. The exam question is the same question wearing a different costume.
What is beyond the source The source gives the grid as a table with formulas. This lecture derives each entry, explains the intuition behind why "nominal" and "effective" $p$-thly rates differ (the nominal divides by $p$ before compounding; the effective compounds first), and explains the role of the discount-rate quote convention historically (it predates compound interest and survives in the money market). It also derives the $\lim_{p \to \infty}$ relationships that bring you to the force of interest, completing the bridge from lecture 2.

Step 2

Why this matters

Rates are the universal currency of finance, and finance has many quote conventions. Money-market instruments quote a discount rate; bond markets quote a yield to maturity (an effective rate convertible at the coupon frequency); credit cards quote an APR; mortgages quote either nominal monthly or effective annual depending on the regulator. Every actuarial calculation needs to convert between conventions before the arithmetic can proceed. Forgetting to convert is the single most frequent cause of wrong answers in CT1 / CB1.

Beyond exams: in practice, the conversion grid is what every yield-curve construction tool does under the hood. Bloomberg's "Generic Government" yields are quoted as effective annual; SOFR is quoted as an in-arrears compounded rate; LIBOR (historically) was a simple rate with act/360 daycount; gilt yields are semi-annually compounded. A risk model that ingests rates from multiple sources without normalising will produce nonsense. The conversion grid is the normaliser.

In banking: the regulatory definition of "APR" (annual percentage rate) for retail credit is a nominal $p$-thly rate with $p$ matching the payment frequency. The displayed customer rate is therefore systematically lower than the effective annual rate, and the spread between them grows with the rate. Consumer-protection regulators have argued for displaying both; in practice the nominal still dominates marketing, with the effective hidden in the documentation. Reading any rate disclosure requires the grid.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture an effective annual rate of 6%. Now compound it monthly. The "monthly rate" can be defined two ways: either as the rate $j$ such that twelve months of $j$ compound to 6% ($j = 1.06^{1/12} - 1 = 0.4868\%$, the effective monthly rate), or as the rate $i^{(12)}$ such that $i^{(12)} / 12$ is the per-month figure ($i^{(12)} = 5.84\%$, the nominal annual rate convertible monthly). Both describe the same growth, but the first is "per month" and the second is "per year, divided by 12". Get the units wrong and your present values are off by hundreds of basis points.

The picture explains the role of each convention. $i$ is the cleanest "per year" measure but does not respect intra-year compounding. $i^{(p)}$ is the standard market quote when payments happen $p$-thly. $\delta$ is the continuous limit, mathematically the cleanest but rarely a market quote. $d$ and $d^{(p)}$ are the same ideas from the discount side, surviving in money-market quoting conventions. The grid in section 4 collapses all six into one table.

Step 4

The detail

Effective annual interest rate $i$

The benchmark. Defined as the proportional growth of £1 over one year:

$$ A(1) = 1 + i \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad i = A(1) - 1 $$

Equivalently, $i$ is the answer to "how much does £1 grow in one year?". Under constant compounding, $A(t) = (1+i)^t$ and $v(t) = (1+i)^{-t}$.

Effective annual discount rate $d$

The same growth, quoted on the end balance. If £1 today grows to $1 + i$ in a year, equivalently £$(1 - d)$ today grows to £1 in a year, so

$$ 1 - d = \frac{1}{1 + i} = v \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad d = 1 - v = \frac{i}{1+i} $$

The standard relations:

$$ d = i v, \qquad i - d = i d, \qquad d < i $$

Historically, $d$ predates compound interest as a quote convention. Money-market instruments (treasury bills, banker's acceptances) are still quoted with a discount yield in many markets. A T-bill with face value 100 sold for 98.5 in 90 days quotes a "discount yield" of $1.5\% \times (360/90) = 6\%$ in the US convention, which is a simple-interest annualisation of the discount rate, not an effective annual return.

Nominal interest rate convertible $p$-thly, $i^{(p)}$

An annualised figure such that interest of $i^{(p)} / p$ accrues each $1/p$ of a year, with compounding within the year. The effective rate per $1/p$-year is $i^{(p)} / p$, so the one-year accumulation is

$$ A(1) = \left(1 + \frac{i^{(p)}}{p}\right)^p $$

and setting this equal to $1 + i$ gives the conversion

$$ 1 + i = \left(1 + \frac{i^{(p)}}{p}\right)^p \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad i^{(p)} = p \left[(1+i)^{1/p} - 1\right] $$

Practically: "nominal monthly $i^{(12)} = 6\%$" means $0.5\%$ per month, compounding monthly, giving an effective annual rate of $(1.005)^{12} - 1 = 6.17\%$. The nominal rate is what banks display ("6% APR"); the effective rate is what the customer actually pays.

Nominal discount rate convertible $p$-thly, $d^{(p)}$

The symmetric concept on the discount side. £$(1 - d^{(p)}/p)$ today grows to £1 in $1/p$ of a year, so

$$ v^{1/p} = 1 - \frac{d^{(p)}}{p} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad d^{(p)} = p \left[1 - v^{1/p}\right] = p \left[1 - (1+i)^{-1/p}\right] $$

and equivalently

$$ 1 - d = \left(1 - \frac{d^{(p)}}{p}\right)^p $$

Treasury bill discount yields in the US are quoted with $p$ implicit in the daycount: a 90-day T-bill at a 5% discount yield means $d^{(4)} = 5\%$ (treating the year as having four 90-day pieces), which corresponds to a different effective annual rate from "5% nominal annual compounded quarterly".

The force of interest $\delta$ as a limit

Take the nominal interest rate $i^{(p)}$ and send $p \to \infty$:

$$ \lim_{p \to \infty} i^{(p)} = \lim_{p \to \infty} p \left[(1+i)^{1/p} - 1\right] $$

Write $h = 1/p \to 0^+$ and use the expansion $a^h - 1 = h \ln a + O(h^2)$:

$$ \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{(1+i)^h - 1}{h} = \ln(1+i) = \delta $$

The same limit applied to $d^{(p)}$ also produces $\delta$, approaching from below. Hence the inequality chain $d < d^{(p)} < \delta < i^{(p)} < i$ at any finite $p > 1$, collapsing to a single point $\delta$ in the continuous limit.

Simple interest stands outside the grid

Simple interest rate $s$ accumulates $1 + s t$ over $t$ years, with no compounding. This is not consistent with any constant effective rate, so there is no clean grid entry. Simple interest is widely used in money markets (short-term deposits, T-bills, repo) for two reasons: it is easy to compute by hand, and the difference from compound interest is small over short horizons. The conversion from simple to effective annual is

$$ 1 + i = 1 + s \quad \text{at } t = 1 $$

so at $t = 1$, $s$ and $i$ coincide. They diverge for $t \ne 1$.

For periods other than one year, $s$ and $i$ disagree:

$$ A_s(t) = 1 + s t, \qquad A_i(t) = (1+i)^t $$

At $t = 0.25$ year and $s = i = 8\%$: simple gives $1.02$, compound gives $(1.08)^{0.25} = 1.01943$. Very close, but a real spread for large nominal amounts.

Worked example: a full grid round-trip

Given an effective annual rate of 8%, compute $i^{(2)}, i^{(12)}, d, d^{(12)}, \delta$.

i = 8%, so v = 1 / 1.08 = 0.92593 i^(2) = 2 * [(1.08)^(1/2) - 1] = 2 * 0.03923 = 7.846% (nominal half-yearly) i^(12) = 12 * [(1.08)^(1/12) - 1] = 12 * 0.006434 = 7.721% (nominal monthly) d = i / (1 + i) = 0.08 / 1.08 = 7.407% (effective annual discount) d^(12) = 12 * [1 - (1.08)^(-1/12)] = 12 * 0.006392 = 7.671% (nominal monthly discount) delta = ln(1.08) = 7.696% (force of interest) Sanity check ordering at p = 12: d = 7.407% < d^(12) = 7.671% < delta = 7.696% < i^(12) = 7.721% < i = 8% ✓

Note how tightly clustered the rates are at $p = 12$: the spread from $d^{(12)}$ to $i^{(12)}$ is only 5 basis points. As $p$ rises further the four central numbers all converge on $\delta = 7.696\%$. The "annualised" rate the customer sees depends entirely on which corner of the grid the bank's marketing department picks.

Pitfall: the unit-of-time confusion

The two most common rate-conversion errors:

Confusing effective and nominal $p$-thly
$i^{(p)} / p$ is the effective rate per $1/p$ year. The effective $p$-thly rate is $j = i^{(p)} / p$. A statement like "monthly rate 0.5%" most often means $j = 0.5\%$, hence $i^{(12)} = 6\%$ and effective annual $1.005^{12} - 1 = 6.17\%$. A statement like "6% annual rate, monthly compounding" also means $i^{(12)} = 6\%$. They are the same. A statement like "6% effective annual, monthly compounding" is a contradiction unless the bank is sloppy.
Confusing discount and interest at the same frequency
$d$ and $i$ both describe the one-year accumulation $1 + i$. A 5% T-bill discount yield does not mean a 5% effective annual return; it means $d \approx 5\%$ (or more precisely $d^{(p)}$ for some $p$ implied by the daycount), hence $i = d / (1 - d) \approx 5.26\%$. The difference looks small but is systematic, and bond traders historically arbitraged it.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The grid is just one identity

Every entry in the conversion grid follows from $1 + i = e^{\delta}$ combined with the definition of each frequency-$p$ rate as either an interest annualisation or a discount annualisation. The grid looks like 25 separate formulas but is, conceptually, one identity solved five different ways. Internalise the identity and you can regenerate the grid in real time; memorise the grid in isolation and you will forget half of it under exam pressure.

Quote convention is information, not noise

When a bank quotes APR on a credit card or a mortgage, the convention is deliberately chosen to be either the most favourable or the most legally required. The professional habit is to ignore the quoted rate's marketing label and ask: "what is the effective annual rate?". That is the only number you can compare across products.

Continuously compounded is the trading-floor default

Outside of retail markets, traders and quants almost always work with $\delta$ rather than $i$ or $i^{(p)}$. The reasons: $\delta$ is additive over time, integrates cleanly over yield curves, and matches the short rate in continuous-time stochastic models. Bond yields are usually quoted as $i^{(2)}$ in markets with semi-annual coupons, but for cash-flow modelling traders convert to $\delta$ as the first step.

The grid breaks down at extreme rates

All the conversions assume positive rates. With negative interest rates (as in EUR markets since 2014), the conversions still work formally but several quantities lose their interpretive validity: $d$ becomes negative ("I have to pay you to take my money today"), $d^{(p)} > \delta > i^{(p)}$, and the ordering inverts. The arithmetic is robust; the intuitions need recalibrating.

The relationship to bond yields

A bond yield to maturity is the rate that makes the bond's discounted cashflows equal its price. By market convention this rate is $i^{(2)}$ for US Treasuries and gilts, $i^{(1)}$ for many continental European government bonds, and $\delta$ on the trading floor. When you read "10-year yield 4.2%", check whether that is gilt-style $i^{(2)}$ or trading-floor $\delta$ before doing anything with it.

Test yourself

  1. State the four-way identity that unifies $i, i^{(p)}, d^{(p)}, \delta$. Why is $1 + i = e^{\delta}$ the cleanest member?
  2. Derive $d = i / (1 + i)$ from the definition of effective annual discount.
  3. Convert an effective annual rate of 5% to: $i^{(2)}, i^{(4)}, i^{(12)}, d, d^{(12)}, \delta$.
  4. A bank quotes 12% nominal annual compounded monthly. What is the effective annual rate? What is the force of interest?
  5. A T-bill is sold for 98.5 in 90 days and redeems at 100. State the effective 90-day discount rate, the annualised simple discount rate, and the effective annual rate of return.
  6. Derive $\delta = \ln(1+i)$ as the limit of $i^{(p)}$ as $p \to \infty$.
  7. Show that at $p = 12$ and $i = 6\%$, the ordering $d < d^{(12)} < \delta < i^{(12)} < i$ holds.
  8. Distinguish "simple interest at 6%" from "compound interest at 6%" and "force of interest 6%". After 5 years, what is the accumulation under each?
  9. Two banks quote: bank A "5% APR compounded monthly", bank B "5.12% effective annual". Which has the higher return for the customer?
  10. Under negative effective annual rates ($i = -1\%$), what happens to $d$? Is the ordering $d < d^{(p)} < \delta < i^{(p)} < i$ still valid?
