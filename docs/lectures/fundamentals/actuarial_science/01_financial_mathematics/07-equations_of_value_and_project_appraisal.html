Equations of value and project appraisal

An equation of value sets the present value of receipts equal to the present value of payments. Solving it for the rate gives a yield; solving it for the time gives a payback period; solving it with cashflows fixed and the rate given gives an NPV. The three together are the workhorses of capital budgeting and the bridge between this course and corporate-finance decision-making.

Step 1

The 30-second version

An equation of value for a cashflow stream $\{C_k\}$ at times $\{t_k\}$ is the algebraic statement

$$ \sum_k C_k \cdot v(t_k; i) = 0 $$

where $v(t; i)$ is the discount factor at rate $i$ over $t$ years, and sign conventions make outflows negative and inflows positive. Three special cases give three decision rules. Net Present Value (NPV) fixes $i$ at the cost of capital and computes the equation's value. Internal Rate of Return (IRR) fixes the equation at zero and solves for $i$. Payback period ignores discounting (or applies it) and finds the time at which cumulative cashflows turn positive.

Decision rule: accept a project if NPV is positive at the cost of capital, reject if negative. Equivalently, accept if IRR exceeds the cost of capital. Equivalent in single-project cases; can disagree on ranking when projects are mutually exclusive or have non-conventional cashflows (multiple IRRs, capital rationing). NPV is the safer decision tool because it directly measures wealth created; IRR is more intuitive but has edge cases.

Feynman test Imagine a friend wants to invest £100,000 in a side business that promises £30,000 a year for five years. Is it a good deal? Two checks. NPV: discount the five £30,000s at your cost of capital (say 6%) and subtract £100,000. If the answer is positive, you create wealth; if negative, you destroy it. IRR: ask "what rate makes the £30,000s discount back to exactly £100,000?". That rate is 15.24%. If your cost of capital is below 15.24%, the project is profitable. The two answers agree.

Step 2

Why this matters

Every capital allocation decision in a business reduces to a project appraisal. Should we build the new factory? Buy the smaller competitor? Replace the IT system? Roll out the mobile app to a second market? Each of these is a sequence of cashflows; each is evaluated by comparing its NPV to alternatives or to zero. The same arithmetic decides whether a bank should originate a loan (Yes, if NPV of the loan at the bank's cost of capital is positive) and whether a pension fund should buy a particular asset (Yes, if NPV at the fund's discount rate is positive).

Actuarial work specifically uses this machinery in three places. Insurance product pricing: an insurer prices a new product by computing the NPV of expected premiums minus expected claims and expenses, then setting the price to give a target NPV. Reserve valuation: the technical provisions on the balance sheet are the discounted expected cashflows of in-force business; reserve adequacy is an equation-of-value statement. Embedded value calculations: the present value of in-force business and the present value of future new business written today, both discounted at a risk-adjusted rate.

The banking application is equally direct: a bank loan generates a stream of interest and principal cashflows, the bank funds it at its cost of funds, and the loan's NPV at the cost of funds is the profit the bank books (in IFRS 9 effective-interest-rate form, this is the "Day 1 P&L"). Loan pricing, FTP, and origination decisions all reduce to NPV comparisons.

For ASSA F107 specifically: equations of value underpin the bank-pricing material covered in section 4 of the F107 syllabus (loan pricing, FTP, derivative pricing). Project appraisal proper sits at the intersection of financial mathematics and corporate finance.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture an NPV-versus-rate curve. The y-axis is the project's NPV; the x-axis is the discount rate. At rate zero, NPV equals the simple sum of cashflows (no discounting). As the rate rises, future inflows shrink and the NPV falls. The rate at which the NPV crosses zero is the IRR. The vertical distance from zero to the NPV at the firm's cost of capital is what the project contributes to shareholder wealth.

The NPV curve is a simple summary of a project's economics. Where it sits at $i = 0$ tells you the project's nominal return; where it crosses zero tells you the breakeven rate; the slope at any point tells you the duration. Real-world appraisal is mostly the discipline of reading this curve correctly.

Step 4

The detail

The equation of value

For a cashflow stream $\{C_k\}$ at times $\{t_k\}$ and an interest rate $i$:

$$ EOV(i) = \sum_k C_k \cdot v(t_k; i) = \sum_k C_k \cdot (1+i)^{-t_k} $$

$EOV(i)$ is a function of $i$ once the cashflows are fixed. Three things to do with it:

  • Evaluate at a specified $i$ (the cost of capital) to compute NPV.
  • Solve $EOV(i) = 0$ to compute the IRR.
  • Hold $i$ fixed and vary $n$ to compute sensitivities or breakeven times.

Sign convention: outflows are negative, inflows are positive, all from the same perspective (typically the project sponsor's). The equation is asking "do the present-valued inflows balance the present-valued outflows at this rate?".

Net Present Value (NPV)

Definition:

$$ NPV(i^*) = \sum_k C_k \cdot (1 + i^*)^{-t_k} $$

where $i^*$ is the firm's cost of capital (the rate that reflects the riskiness of the project and the opportunity cost of capital tied up).

Decision rule: accept the project if $NPV(i^*) > 0$; reject if negative.

Interpretation: the NPV is the wealth created (in today's pounds) by accepting the project. A positive NPV means the project's discounted inflows exceed the discounted outflows by that amount, so doing the project makes the sponsor strictly better off than the next-best alternative use of capital at $i^*$.

For mutually exclusive projects, pick the one with the highest NPV at $i^*$. This rule is robust: it gives the right answer regardless of the cashflow shape, the scale, or the timing.

Internal Rate of Return (IRR)

Definition: the rate(s) $i$ that solve

$$ EOV(i) = \sum_k C_k \cdot (1+i)^{-t_k} = 0 $$

For conventional cashflows (one initial outflow followed by all-positive inflows), the IRR is unique and the equation has exactly one positive real root. Use Newton's method, bisection, or any numerical solver to find it.

Decision rule: accept the project if $IRR > i^*$; reject if below.

For conventional cashflows, this rule agrees with the NPV rule for accept/reject decisions on single projects. They can disagree on ranking among mutually exclusive projects, especially when projects differ in scale or timing of cashflows. Two specific failure modes:

Multiple IRRs
When cashflows change sign more than once (e.g., a project with an upfront outflow, inflows in the middle, and a remediation outflow at the end), the equation can have multiple positive real roots. Standard example: a mine with closure costs. NPV is well-defined; IRR is ambiguous. Use NPV.
Mutually exclusive scale mismatch
A small project might have a higher IRR than a large project but a lower NPV. If both are feasible, pick by NPV: it measures total wealth created, not rate of return. IRR favours scale-free productivity; NPV favours absolute return.

Modified IRR (MIRR)

A patch for the multiple-IRR problem: reinvest intermediate inflows at the cost of capital rather than at the IRR itself. The MIRR is the rate that equates the final value of reinvested inflows to the initial outflow's accumulated value. Formally:

$$ MIRR = \left(\frac{FV(\text{inflows at } i^*)}{PV(\text{outflows at } i^*)}\right)^{1/n} - 1 $$

MIRR is unique and avoids the multiple-IRR pathology, but it has its own interpretive issues: the cost-of-capital reinvestment assumption is sometimes wrong, and the formula obscures the source of the project's profitability. Few practitioners use it; most just use NPV.

Payback period

The simplest, oldest decision rule. Undiscounted payback: the time at which the cumulative undiscounted cashflows first turn positive. Discounted payback: the time at which the cumulative discounted cashflows first turn positive.

Decision rule: accept if payback is less than a target horizon (e.g., "we want money back within 5 years"). Reject otherwise.

Payback ignores cashflows beyond the cut-off and ignores the time value of money in its undiscounted form. It is widely used despite these flaws because (a) it is easy to compute and explain, and (b) for short-term liquidity-constrained firms it captures something the NPV rule misses: how long capital is tied up. It is best treated as a supplementary check rather than a primary criterion.

The NPV-versus-IRR debate

A perennial in corporate finance. NPV directly measures wealth created in pounds. IRR measures the rate of return without scaling. For an individual investor with unlimited capital, NPV is the right rule: take all positive-NPV projects. For a firm with limited capital and many candidate projects, the picture is more nuanced: a high-IRR project that is small in scale is sometimes preferable when capital is rationed.

Under capital rationing, the formal correct answer is the profitability index: $PI = PV(\text{inflows}) / |PV(\text{outflows})|$. Rank projects by PI and accept the top ones until the budget is exhausted. This gives the optimal NPV per pound of capital.

Profitability index (PI)
The ratio of PV of inflows to absolute PV of outflows. PI > 1 is equivalent to NPV > 0; PI is most useful for ranking projects under a capital constraint. PI = 1 + (NPV / |PV outflows|).

Sign conventions and the equation-of-value notation

Two equivalent forms of the same equation. Single-sided: outflows are positive, inflows are positive, set up as $PV(\text{inflows}) = PV(\text{outflows})$. Double-sided: outflows are negative, inflows are positive, sum to zero. The double-sided form is cleaner algebraically and is what NPV uses.

The two forms produce identical IRRs but reading them is different. The actuarial habit is to use the single-sided form ("PV of receipts equals PV of payments") in derivations and the double-sided form ("set the algebraic sum to zero") in implementations. Either is fine; mixing them in the same equation is the source of about half of all sign-related errors.

Worked example: comparing two projects

A firm has two mutually exclusive projects available, both lasting 5 years. The firm's cost of capital is 10%.

Project A: -£100k now, +£40k a year for 5 years Project B: -£200k now, +£70k a year for 5 years A's cashflows: -100, +40, +40, +40, +40, +40 NPV_A(10%) = -100 + 40 * a_5|0.10 = -100 + 40 * 3.7908 = -100 + 151.63 = £51.63k (positive: profitable) IRR_A: solve -100 + 40 * a_5|j = 0 → a_5|j = 2.500 From tables / Newton: j ≈ 28.65% B's cashflows: -200, +70, +70, +70, +70, +70 NPV_B(10%) = -200 + 70 * a_5|0.10 = -200 + 70 * 3.7908 = -200 + 265.36 = £65.36k (positive: profitable) IRR_B: solve -200 + 70 * a_5|j = 0 → a_5|j = 2.857 From tables / Newton: j ≈ 22.11% Decision: A has the higher IRR (28.65% vs 22.11%), but B has the higher NPV (£65.36k vs £51.63k). Pick B if capital is not constrained (creates more wealth). Pick A if capital is constrained and B's incremental £100k cannot otherwise earn 10%. The incremental analysis: the extra £100k spent on B vs A returns +£70 - £40 = £30k extra a year. NPV of "B minus A" = -100 + 30 * 3.7908 = +£13.72k > 0, so B is preferred when capital is not constrained.

The incremental analysis is the cleanest way to resolve disagreements between NPV and IRR for mutually exclusive projects. Compute the NPV of the difference ("B minus A"); accept B over A if and only if that incremental NPV is positive at the cost of capital.

Worked example: a project with multiple IRRs

A mining project: spend £100k now (development), receive £230k a year later (production), spend £132k two years from now (closure and remediation).

Cashflows: -100, +230, -132 Equation of value: -100 + 230 v - 132 v² = 0 132 v² - 230 v + 100 = 0 v = [230 ± sqrt(230² - 4·132·100)] / (2·132) v = [230 ± sqrt(52,900 - 52,800)] / 264 v = [230 ± 10] / 264 v₁ = 240/264 = 0.9091 → i₁ = 10.0% v₂ = 220/264 = 0.8333 → i₂ = 20.0% Two IRRs: 10% and 20%. Both valid roots. Neither alone tells you whether the project is profitable. NPV at i = 5%: -100 + 230/1.05 - 132/1.05² = -100 + 219.05 - 119.73 = -0.68 → reject NPV at i = 15%: -100 + 230/1.15 - 132/1.15² = -100 + 200.00 - 99.81 = +0.19 → accept (just) NPV at i = 25%: -100 + 230/1.25 - 132/1.25² = -100 + 184.00 - 84.48 = -0.48 → reject So NPV is positive between IRR₁ and IRR₂, negative outside. Unusual: most projects are profitable at low rates and unprofitable at high rates. Here the profile is inverted because of the late outflow. Use NPV at the firm's cost of capital; ignore the IRRs entirely.

The multiple-IRR phenomenon is what makes IRR untrustworthy as a stand-alone decision rule. NPV at the relevant rate is unambiguous.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

NPV is the canonical decision rule

Brealey, Myers and Allen open their treatment of project appraisal with this point and never let it go. NPV directly measures wealth created in pounds; it works for any cashflow shape; it adds across projects. IRR is intuitive and survives in practice because rates of return are easier to discuss than absolute amounts, but it breaks in three places (multiple IRRs, mutually exclusive ranking, scale mismatches). For any non-trivial appraisal, NPV is the safer tool.

The cost of capital is the hardest input

Picking the right discount rate is harder than the arithmetic. Too low and you accept too many marginal projects; too high and you reject good ones. Standard practice: use the firm's weighted-average cost of capital (WACC) as a baseline, adjust for project-specific risk if the project's risk differs materially from the firm's average risk. CAPM-based estimates are standard for equity capital cost; debt cost is the marginal borrowing rate. The 100-basis-point error in the cost-of-capital estimate is bigger than the 100-basis-point error in cashflow forecasts for most projects.

Cashflow forecasts dominate the answer

Far more important than the choice between NPV and IRR is the quality of the cashflow forecasts. A model that gets the discount-rate method right but uses optimistic revenue forecasts produces the wrong answer. The professional discipline is to do scenario analysis (best, base, worst) on the cashflows and report the NPV range, not a point estimate.

The CAPM-WACC framework is a tool, not a ritual

Corporate-finance textbooks compute WACC mechanically: weighted average of after-tax debt cost and equity cost. The mechanical answer is wrong if the project's capital structure, risk, or tax position differs from the firm's average. The harder professional habit is to ask: what is the marginal investor's expected return for the marginal project? Then back out the discount rate from that.

Payback persists despite its flaws

Payback ignores cashflows beyond the cut-off and (in its undiscounted form) ignores the time value of money. It is methodologically weak. It survives because in practice it captures a real concern: capital tied up for too long is itself risky, and short payback proxies for liquidity safety. The professional habit is to report payback alongside NPV, treating it as a complementary signal rather than a primary criterion.

Real options change the calculation

The classical NPV framework assumes a fixed cashflow plan. Many real projects embed managerial flexibility: the option to expand, contract, defer, or abandon based on future information. The value of these options is real but doesn't show up in a static DCF. Real-options analysis extends the NPV framework using option-pricing methods; for ASSA F107 it is beyond scope, but it is the most important practical extension to know about.
