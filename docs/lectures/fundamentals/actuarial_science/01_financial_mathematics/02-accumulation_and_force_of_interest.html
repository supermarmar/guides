Accumulation, discounting, and the force of interest

A single cashflow at one date is worth a different amount at another date. The accumulation function $A(t)$ moves it forwards, the discount function $v(t)$ moves it backwards, and the force of interest $\delta$ glues both to a continuous-time growth rate. Everything else in the course is built on this triangle.

Lecture 2 of 7 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: UoP / Financial Mathematics / Summary.pdf §2
Step 1

The 30-second version

If you place £1 into an account at time $0$ and it grows to $A(t)$ pounds by time $t$, then $A(t)$ is the accumulation function. Its reciprocal, $v(t) = 1 / A(t)$, is the discount function: the present value today of £1 due at time $t$. The accumulation between two arbitrary dates $t_1$ and $t_2$ is the ratio $A(t_2) / A(t_1)$. The continuously-compounded growth rate at any instant is the force of interest $\delta(t)$, defined as the logarithmic derivative of $A(t)$. These three objects, and the integral identity that links them, are the entirety of single-cashflow time-value-of-money.

The reason the force of interest matters more than any specific interest rate is that it is additive in time. Discrete rates compound multiplicatively, which makes algebra messy; the force, being a rate of continuous growth, integrates over time additively, which makes the algebra of variable-rate environments tractable.

Feynman test Imagine a savings account where the bank tells you, hour by hour, the instantaneous growth rate of your balance. At 9 am it's growing at the equivalent of 5% per year; by noon it's grown to the equivalent of 5.2%; by 3 pm back to 4.9%. The hour-by-hour reading is the force of interest. The balance at the end of the day is the original balance multiplied by $\exp$ of the area under that hour-by-hour curve. That picture, with $\delta(t)$ as the curve and $\int \delta(s)\,ds$ as the area, is what this lecture builds.
What is beyond the source The source tabulates the formulas for $A(t)$, $v(t)$ and the integral form of $A(t_1, t_2)$. This lecture derives each from first principles, including the limit argument that takes you from compound interest at frequency $p$ to a constant force, and the time-substitution argument that handles a variable force. It also makes explicit the geometric and algebraic interpretations of the force as a logarithmic derivative, which is the conceptual key to every later result.
Step 2

Why this matters

Every present-value, every reserve, every bond price reduces to a sum or integral of cashflows multiplied by a discount factor. The discount factor is $v(t)$. If you cannot compute $v(t)$ for a given environment, you cannot price anything. The constant-force regime gives $v(t) = e^{-\delta t}$ and the constant-effective-rate regime gives $v(t) = (1 + i)^{-t}$; both are the same idea, written with different rate-units.

The variable-force regime is essential because real interest rates change over time. A yield curve is, mathematically, a specification of $\delta(t)$ (or equivalently of $v(t)$) for every future date. Lectures 3 and 7 treat the constant-rate case in detail because it produces clean closed forms; this lecture's most powerful machinery is the variable-rate case, which is what real-world pricing actually requires.

Beyond actuarial work: the force of interest is what continuous-time finance calls the short rate $r(t)$. Stochastic interest-rate models (Vasicek, Cox-Ingersoll-Ross, Hull-White) all start by writing $\delta(t)$ as a stochastic process. The actuarial notation $\delta$ and the financial notation $r$ refer to the same object.

Step 3

The mental model

Three pictures, all of the same idea. Picture one: a balance growing through time. Picture two: an instantaneous growth rate that varies through time. Picture three: an area under that growth-rate curve. The accumulation function is picture one; the force of interest is picture two; their relationship is picture three.

The key geometric idea: the force of interest is the slope of $\ln A(t)$. So integrating the force from $0$ to $t$ gives you $\ln A(t)$, and exponentiating gets you back to $A(t)$. Under a constant force, the slope is constant, $\ln A(t)$ is linear in $t$, and $A(t) = e^{\delta t}$. Under a variable force, the slope wobbles and $A(t) = \exp(\int_0^t \delta(s)\,ds)$.

Step 4

The detail

The accumulation function

Define $A(t)$ as the value at time $t$ of £1 placed in an investment at time $0$. Then $A(0) = 1$ by definition. Under constant effective annual interest $i$:

$$ A(t) = (1 + i)^t $$

This is the compound-interest formula. Note that $t$ need not be an integer: if you place £1 today and withdraw it after $t = 2.5$ years, you receive $(1+i)^{2.5}$ regardless of compounding frequency, because the formula assumes continuous reinvestment of accrued interest at the same rate.

More generally, the accumulation from $t_1$ to $t_2$ is the factor by which a balance grows over that period. Write $A(t_1, t_2)$ for this factor. Then:

$$ A(t_1, t_2) = \frac{A(t_2)}{A(t_1)} $$

and accumulation is multiplicative across disjoint periods:

$$ A(t_0, t_n) = A(t_0, t_1) \cdot A(t_1, t_2) \cdots A(t_{n-1}, t_n) $$

This consistency property is the formal name for the requirement that the value of an investment over a long period must equal the compound value over any partition of that period. Any sensible interest-rate environment must satisfy it.

The discount function

The reciprocal:

$$ v(t) = \frac{1}{A(t)} = \frac{\text{PV today}}{\text{FV at } t} $$

so under constant $i$:

$$ v(t) = (1 + i)^{-t} = v^t \quad \text{where } v = \frac{1}{1+i} $$

The single-symbol $v$ (with no argument) is the standard actuarial shorthand for the one-year discount factor, with $v(t) = v^t$ for general $t$. The discount function inherits multiplicativity from accumulation:

$$ v(t_1, t_2) = \frac{v(t_2)}{v(t_1)}, \qquad v(t_0, t_n) = v(t_0, t_1) \cdot v(t_1, t_2) \cdots v(t_{n-1}, t_n) $$

An immediate identity that the source records:

$$ A(t_1, t_2) \cdot v(t_1, t_2) = 1 $$

which simply says that accumulating then discounting (or discounting then accumulating) leaves the value unchanged.

The force of interest, constant case

Define the force of interest $\delta$ as the continuously-compounded rate equivalent to the effective rate $i$. Equivalently, $\delta$ is the limit of compounding $i^{(p)} / p$ as $p \to \infty$, where $i^{(p)}$ is the nominal rate convertible $p$-thly (treated fully in lecture 3). The relationship is

$$ 1 + i = e^{\delta} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \delta = \ln(1 + i) $$

so the accumulation function in force-of-interest form is

$$ A(t) = e^{\delta t} \qquad v(t) = e^{-\delta t} $$

The advantage of working with $\delta$ rather than $i$ is that $\delta$ is the natural rate for continuous processes: a savings rate of $\delta$ per unit time means the balance solves the ODE $A'(t) = \delta A(t)$ with $A(0) = 1$, whose solution is exactly $A(t) = e^{\delta t}$.

The force of interest, variable case

Suppose the instantaneous rate varies with time: at time $t$, the balance grows at the rate $\delta(t)$. The defining differential equation is

$$ \frac{dA(t)}{dt} = \delta(t) \cdot A(t), \quad A(0) = 1 $$

which separates and integrates to

$$ \ln A(t) = \int_0^t \delta(s) \, ds \quad \Longrightarrow \quad A(t) = \exp\!\left(\int_0^t \delta(s)\,ds\right) $$

For an arbitrary interval from $t_1$ to $t_2$:

$$ A(t_1, t_2) = \exp\!\left(\int_{t_1}^{t_2} \delta(s)\,ds\right), \qquad v(t_1, t_2) = \exp\!\left(-\int_{t_1}^{t_2} \delta(s)\,ds\right) $$

This is the most general single-cashflow time-value formula in the course. Every other formula in lectures 3 to 7 is a special case obtained by choosing a particular form for $\delta(s)$ (constant, piecewise constant, linear, exponential).

The logarithmic derivative

Going the other direction, given $A(t)$ how do you recover $\delta(t)$? Differentiate $\ln A(t)$:

$$ \delta(t) = \frac{d}{dt} \ln A(t) = \frac{A'(t)}{A(t)} = -\frac{v'(t)}{v(t)} $$

The force of interest is the logarithmic derivative of the accumulation function. This identity is the cleanest definition: $\delta(t)$ is whatever instantaneous proportional growth rate $A(t)$ happens to have at time $t$.

Note the asymmetry of simple interest: although it is the simplest to compute by hand, its associated force of interest is not constant, it decreases with $t$. This is the precise sense in which simple interest is "less natural" than compound interest in continuous time: a constant force is what you get from compound interest, not from simple interest.

Multi-period composition

When $\delta(s)$ is piecewise constant (a frequent practical case), the integral simplifies into a sum:

$$ \delta(s) = \delta_k \quad \text{for } s \in [t_{k-1}, t_k), \quad k = 1, 2, \dots, n $$

$$ A(0, t_n) = \exp\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n} \delta_k \cdot (t_k - t_{k-1})\right) = \prod_{k=1}^{n} e^{\delta_k (t_k - t_{k-1})} $$

This is the standard form for stepped yield curves where the short rate is constant within each maturity bucket. It is also the discrete analogue of the multi-period accumulation result: each piece contributes its own factor and the factors multiply.

Worked example: variable force of interest

Suppose the force of interest at time $s$ (measured in years from now) is

$$ \delta(s) = \begin{cases} 0.04 & 0 \le s < 3 \\ 0.05 + 0.001(s - 3) & 3 \le s < 8 \\ 0.06 & s \ge 8 \end{cases} $$

Find the present value at $t = 0$ of £10,000 payable at $t = 10$.

Total integral I = integral_0^10 delta(s) ds. Piece 1, 0 to 3: integral = 0.04 * 3 = 0.12 Piece 2, 3 to 8: integral_3^8 (0.05 + 0.001(s - 3)) ds = 0.05 * 5 + 0.001 * [(s-3)^2 / 2]_3^8 = 0.25 + 0.001 * 12.5 = 0.2625 Piece 3, 8 to 10: integral = 0.06 * 2 = 0.12 I = 0.12 + 0.2625 + 0.12 = 0.5025 Discount factor v(0,10) = exp(-0.5025) = 0.6050 PV today = 10,000 x 0.6050 = £6,050.30

The three pieces correspond to a flat short-end of the yield curve, an upward-sloping mid-section, and a flat long-end, in exactly the kind of stepped pattern you might fit to observed yield-curve data. The total integral, 0.5025, is the "log of the discount factor with a minus": with $\delta \approx 5\%$ on average for 10 years, the discount is about $e^{-0.5}$, very close to the more precise $e^{-0.5025}$.

Reading the integral as an average rate

A natural summary of a variable rate environment is the average force over a period:

$$ \bar{\delta}(0, t) = \frac{1}{t} \int_0^t \delta(s) \, ds $$

which gives $A(0, t) = \exp(\bar{\delta}(0, t) \cdot t)$. In the worked example, $\bar{\delta}(0, 10) = 0.5025 / 10 = 0.05025$, so the equivalent constant force is 5.025%. The equivalent effective annual rate is $i = e^{0.05025} - 1 = 5.15\%$. This is the rate a bank would quote to a customer who wanted a single "average effective rate over 10 years" rather than the curve.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The force of interest is the bridge to continuous-time finance

Every continuous-time finance model (Black-Scholes, Vasicek, Heath-Jarrow-Morton) writes the riskless growth rate as $r(t)$ or $\delta(t)$ and the bond price as the conditional expectation of $\exp(-\int r(s)\,ds)$. The actuarial $\delta(t)$ is identical to the finance $r(t)$; the notation is the only difference. If you can compute $A(t)$ given $\delta$ you can compute a zero-coupon bond price given the short rate.

Consistency is not optional

An interest-rate environment that fails $A(0, t_1) \cdot A(t_1, t_2) = A(0, t_2)$ admits arbitrage. The reason: an investor could place £1 at $t = 0$ until $t_2$ (one route), or place it until $t_1$ and reinvest until $t_2$ (another), and the two routes would produce different ending balances. The difference is a free lunch. Real markets enforce consistency; quoted rates that appear to violate it usually do so because the rates are defined over different conventions (act/365 vs 30/360, ex-dividend timing, etc).

Simple interest is a model, not an approximation

Simple interest is sometimes treated as a rough approximation to compound interest for short periods. It is more correctly treated as a different model with its own force-of-interest profile ($\delta(t) = s / (1 + s t)$, which falls over time). The reason banks use simple interest for very short money-market instruments (T-bills, certificates of deposit) is partly convention, partly that the difference from compound interest is negligible over short horizons. Do not confuse the conventional choice with mathematical superiority of either form.

The logarithmic derivative is the conceptual key

$\delta(t) = A'(t) / A(t)$ is the cleanest definition because it makes the force a property of the growth process, not a parameter the modeller picks. Whatever $A(t)$ looks like, the force is whatever the logarithmic derivative happens to be. This perspective is especially useful in dealing with stochastic interest rates: $\delta(t)$ becomes a stochastic process, but the relationship $A(t) = \exp(\int \delta\,ds)$ continues to hold path by path.

Pricing is integration over the discount function

Every present value in finance is a sum of cashflows times $v(t)$. Lecture 5 will collapse the sum into a closed form when cashflows are level and $\delta$ is constant. Lecture 7 will revisit it for project-appraisal purposes. But the underlying object is always $\sum c_k v(t_k)$, or for continuous payment streams, $\int c(t) v(t)\,dt$. If you internalise this, every later formula in the course is a special case.
