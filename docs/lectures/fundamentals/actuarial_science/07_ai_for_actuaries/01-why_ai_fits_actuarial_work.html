Lectures / AI for actuaries

Why AI fits actuarial work

An actuary building a GLM and a data scientist building a gradient-boosted tree are, underneath the different vocabulary, doing the same thing: fitting an empirical model to predict an uncertain future outcome from observed covariates. This lecture argues that claim directly, then sets out the two respects in which actuarial data genuinely differs from the mainstream machine-learning setting, a difference that runs through every remaining lecture in this course.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Every model in this repository's machine-learning course, from a logistic GLM to the deepest transformer, and every actuarial model in the earlier courses of this track, from a life table to a Bühlmann credibility premium, answers the identical underlying question: given observed covariates, what is the best empirical estimate of an uncertain future outcome. The two disciplines have historically emphasised different halves of that shared question. Statistics and actuarial science have traditionally emphasised explain, a model whose parameters have a direct, interpretable meaning a regulator or a pricing committee can scrutinise; machine learning has traditionally emphasised predict, whatever architecture achieves the lowest genuine out-of-sample error, interpretability be damned. A useful predictive model in either tradition shares eight desirable properties: accuracy, interpretability, robustness (to small input perturbations), fairness, computational efficiency, calibration, the ability to quantify its own uncertainty, and reproducibility. No single architecture dominates on all eight simultaneously, and the actuarial literature's own contribution, developed across the rest of this course, is a family of architectures deliberately engineered to trade the eight properties against each other more favourably than either a pure GLM or a pure black-box network can.

Feynman test An actuary fitting a Bühlmann credibility premium and a data scientist fitting a gradient-boosted tree are both, underneath their different jargon, doing the identical thing: taking whatever data is available, however imperfect or scarce, and producing the best possible estimate of something not yet observed, a claim not yet made, a death not yet occurred, a default not yet triggered. The actuary calls the process credibility weighting; the data scientist calls it regularisation; both are answers to the same question, how much should a sparse, noisy observation be trusted relative to a broader, more stable prior.
What is beyond the source The source's introduction sets out the explain-versus-predict framing and the eight desirable properties list. This lecture adds the direct, explicit mapping from each of the eight properties back onto a named actuarial concept already covered earlier in this track (credibility weighting as regularisation, the life table's structural interpretability as a direct analogue of a GLM's, IFRS 9's staging discipline as an early form of calibration monitoring), a synthesis the source leaves implicit.
Step 2

Why this matters

Three reasons this framing is worth establishing explicitly before a single new technique is introduced in this course.

First, an actuary who sees machine learning as an alien discipline rather than a continuation of their own has already made the hardest part of adopting these tools unnecessarily difficult: recognising that credibility theory, GLM regularisation, and IFRS 9 staging are already, in substance, doing the work modern machine learning names differently (shrinkage, penalisation, calibration monitoring) removes an entirely unnecessary conceptual barrier before the genuinely new material in the rest of this course is introduced.

Second, the eight desirable properties give a concrete, checkable vocabulary for a genuine, recurring trade-off decision: whenever a model-development team debates a black-box network against an interpretable GLM, the debate is usually really a debate about which subset of the eight properties matters most for the specific decision at hand, and naming the properties explicitly turns an unproductive "which is better" argument into a productive "which properties does this decision actually need" one.

Third, the two respects in which actuarial data differs from mainstream machine learning, scarce data and heavy tails, are not minor caveats, they are the reason this entire course exists rather than simply pointing an actuary at the generic machine-learning literature: an architecture or technique that performs well on a million-row, light-tailed image dataset can fail outright on a thousand-row, heavy-tailed claims dataset, and every architecture developed in lectures 3 and 4 of this course is a direct, deliberate engineering response to these two specific differences.

A worked comparison Consider mortality graduation, a genuinely old actuarial technique, against a modern smoothing spline fitted by a data scientist to the identical raw mortality-rate-by-age data. Both take a noisy, small-sample empirical curve and produce a smoother, more stable estimate by explicitly trading a small amount of in-sample fit for a large reduction in out-of-sample variance; a graduation's smoothness penalty and a spline's roughness penalty are, mathematically, close cousins. An actuary encountering fused-LASSO penalties for the first time in lecture 2 of this course is not meeting an alien technique: they are meeting a modern, more flexible version of a technique their own discipline has used for over a century.
Step 3

The mental model

Picture a single spectrum running from a fully interpretable, low-flexibility model (a one-way mortality table) at one end to a fully flexible, low-interpretability model (an unconstrained deep network) at the other, with every actuarial and machine-learning technique this course covers occupying some point on that spectrum, and the eight desirable properties as the axes any given point trades off against each other.

Two slogans. Explain and predict are two ends of one spectrum, not two different disciplines. Every technique this course covers is a specific, deliberate position on that spectrum, not an unrelated alternative to it. No architecture wins on all eight properties at once. Every architectural choice in this course is a genuine trade-off among accuracy, interpretability, robustness, fairness, efficiency, calibration, uncertainty quantification, and reproducibility, not a free upgrade.

Step 4

The detail

Explain versus predict

The explain tradition, dominant historically in statistics and actuarial science, prioritises a model whose parameters can be directly interpreted and justified: a GLM's coefficients each have a clear marginal effect, a life table's mortality rates are directly inspectable by age, and both can be explained to a validator or regulator in plain, causal-sounding language even though the underlying model is, strictly, only ever a correlational fit. The predict tradition, dominant historically in machine learning, instead prioritises whatever architecture achieves the best genuine out-of-sample accuracy, with interpretability treated as a secondary, sometimes entirely absent, concern. Neither tradition is simply correct; they are two different weightings of the eight properties developed next, and the specific weighting appropriate for a given problem depends directly on the regulatory, commercial, and ethical stakes attached to the decision the model supports.

The eight desirable properties

A genuinely useful predictive model, in either tradition, is judged against eight properties. Accuracy is genuine, honestly measured out-of-sample predictive performance, not in-sample fit. Interpretability is the degree to which a model's internal workings, not just its final prediction, can be explained in terms a domain expert can scrutinise. Robustness is stability of the prediction under small, plausible perturbations to the input. Fairness is the absence of systematic, unjustified bias against protected or proxy subgroups. Computational efficiency is the cost of training and, separately, of generating a prediction at deployment time. Calibration, developed in full in this repository's own calibration sub-module, is whether the model's predicted probabilities or expected values match observed outcomes on average, at every predicted level, not merely overall. Uncertainty quantification is whether the model supplies a genuine estimate of its own confidence, not merely a point prediction. Reproducibility is whether an identical model, refitted on identical data with an identical procedure, produces the identical result, a property that seeded random initialisation and stochastic optimisation can silently undermine unless deliberately controlled for.

Scarce data

Actuarial data is, relative to the mainstream machine-learning setting (internet-scale text, millions of labelled images), routinely scarce: a newly launched line of business, a rare but consequential event (a catastrophic claim, a policy lapse following a specific regulatory change), or a niche product line can supply orders of magnitude fewer observations than a typical deep-learning benchmark assumes as a baseline. This is precisely why credibility theory, entity embedding with exposure-scaled regularisation, and the deliberately constrained architectures of lectures 3 and 4 all exist: each is an engineering response to needing a stable, reliable estimate from far less data than an unconstrained, purely flexible model would need to fit safely.

Heavy tails

Actuarial outcomes, claim sizes, aggregate losses, mortality shocks, are routinely heavy-tailed, with a small number of extreme observations dominating the quantity actuaries actually need to price and reserve against, developed extensively in this track's risk-modelling course. Many mainstream machine-learning benchmarks and default loss functions are implicitly tuned for lighter-tailed, more homogeneous outcomes, and applying them naively to actuarial data without adjustment (the choice of deviance developed in the statistics course's exponential dispersion family lecture, for instance) can silently underweight exactly the tail behaviour that matters most for solvency and pricing.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The "explain versus predict" framing is a trade-off, not a war

Junior practitioners on both sides of the actuarial-machine-learning divide sometimes treat the explain-versus-predict distinction as an ideological choice, GLMs are principled and networks are reckless, or GLMs are outdated and networks are simply better. The grandmaster view is that this is never the right frame: the correct question for a specific decision is which of the eight properties actually matter most for that decision's regulatory and commercial stakes, and the correct architecture is whichever point on the interpretability-flexibility spectrum best serves that specific weighting, not a fixed ideological preference applied identically to every problem.

Scarce data is a permanent feature of actuarial work, not a temporary inconvenience

A common junior mistake is treating data scarcity as a problem that will simply be solved once "more data" becomes available. For many genuinely important actuarial problems, catastrophic risk, newly regulated products, emerging perils, more data will never become available at anything like mainstream machine-learning scale, because the underlying events are inherently rare or the product is inherently new. The architectures in lectures 3 and 4 of this course are not stopgaps awaiting a data-rich future; they are the permanent, correct engineering response to a permanent feature of the problem.

Reproducibility is the property most often quietly sacrificed

Of the eight properties, reproducibility is the one most often overlooked in practice, because a model that is merely "close enough" on refitting often looks fine in a demonstration. In a regulated setting, an examiner asking "can you reproduce this exact model output" and receiving a materially different answer on a second run, because a random seed was never fixed or a training procedure was never fully specified, is a genuine, avoidable governance failure, developed in full in lecture 7's closing treatment of validation discipline.

Test yourself

  1. State the explain-versus-predict distinction, and give one actuarial technique and one machine-learning technique that each sit closer to one end.
  2. List the eight desirable properties of a predictive model, and give a one-sentence definition of interpretability and of calibration.
  3. Name the two respects in which actuarial data differs from the mainstream machine-learning setting, and explain why each matters for architecture choice.
  4. Explain the parallel drawn between Bühlmann credibility weighting and regularisation in a machine-learning model.
  5. Explain why reproducibility is described as the property most often quietly sacrificed, and name one specific cause.
