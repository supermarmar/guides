AI for actuaries

A seven-lecture synthesis course sitting above the machine-learning and data-analysis tracks, drawing together the deep-learning, unsupervised-learning, generative-modelling, large-language-model, and reinforcement-learning material built across this repository and asking, lecture by lecture, what any of it is actually for in an actuary's own regulated, small-sample, heavy-tailed world. The course opens by arguing that actuarial modelling and machine learning share a single empirical-prediction method, develops the specific covariate-engineering and network architectures the actuarial literature has built on top of the generic machine-learning toolkit (CANN, LocalGLMnet, ICEnet, the credibility transformer), surveys where generative models and large language models genuinely help an actuary and where they are dangerous, develops the premium-control framing of reinforcement learning properly, and closes with the governance and validation discipline a regulated user must apply to every technique in the course.

What this course is

This is not a repeat of the machine-learning course's technical content. It assumes the reader has already met feed-forward networks, entity embedding, autoencoders, transformers, VAEs and GANs, large language models, and reinforcement learning in the fundamentals machine-learning track, and it assumes the reader already has the actuarial grounding this track's earlier courses supply. What this course adds is the specifically actuarial layer on top: which generic machine-learning idea maps onto which specific actuarial architecture, why actuarial data (scarce, heavy-tailed, regulated) breaks some of machine learning's usual assumptions, and the governance discipline that turns a promising technique into a defensible, deployable model.

The material is not banking risk management either (for the regulated credit-risk application of these ideas, see the credit risk modelling track); this course is scoped to general and life insurance actuarial practice, the audience the source material itself is written for.

Prerequisites and companions

This course assumes, in full, the machine learning course's twelve sub-modules, in particular deep learning (entity embedding, CNNs, RNNs, transformers), unsupervised learning (autoencoders), deep generative modelling (VAEs, GANs, diffusion, decoder transformers), large language models, and reinforcement learning. It also assumes the statistics course's exponential dispersion family and strictly consistent loss functions, and the risk modelling course's credibility theory, referenced directly throughout. Read those first if any of the vocabulary below is unfamiliar.

How to read this

Read in order; the course builds cumulatively towards governance. Lecture 1 sets the frame (why actuarial work and machine learning share a method). Lecture 2 develops the covariate-engineering layer every architecture in lectures 3 and 4 depends on. Lecture 3 and lecture 4 are the actuarial-specific network architectures, in increasing order of sophistication. Lecture 5 surveys generative and language-model applications; lecture 6 develops the reinforcement-learning premium-control framing properly, closing the loop opened in the machine-learning course's own reinforcement-learning sub-module. Lecture 7 closes the course with the governance discipline every earlier lecture's techniques are subject to. Status badges show what is built (built) or planned.

Course sequence

Part 1 · Frame and foundations (lectures 1 to 2)

Why actuarial modelling and machine learning share a method, and the covariate-engineering layer every later architecture depends on.

  • builtLecture 1: why AI fits actuarial work. Explain versus predict. The eight desirable properties of a predictive model. The two ways actuarial problems differ from mainstream machine learning: scarce data, heavy tails.
  • builtLecture 2: covariate engineering for actuaries. Ordinal, one-hot, and dummy coding. Entity embedding. Target encoding with Bühlmann credibility shrinkage and its leakage risk. Hierarchical categorical structures. Fused and group penalties as mortality graduation.

Part 2 · Actuarial network architectures (lectures 3 to 4)

The specific architectures the actuarial literature has built by combining a GLM's structure with a network's flexibility, from the shallowest to the deepest.

  • builtLecture 3: actuarial network architectures. CANN (a GLM boosted by network features, the ResNet reading). LocalGLMnet (varying coefficients, attention weights, the sparsity test). ICEnet for smoothness and monotonicity. Nagging in a pricing context. Balance correction.
  • builtLecture 4: the credibility transformer. Feature tokenizer and CLS token recap. Prior versus transformer token. The Bernoulli credibility mechanism during SGD. The hidden Bühlmann credibility formula inside the attention row. Integrated variable importance.

Part 3 · Generative AI, decision-making, and governance (lectures 5 to 7)

Where the newest tools in the machine-learning course genuinely help, where they are dangerous, and the discipline that separates the two.

  • builtLecture 5: generative and LLM applications. Synthetic portfolios and telematics anonymisation. GAN discriminators as unfair-discrimination detectors. LLM extraction from claims texts. TabPFN on reserving triangles. In-context learning as a credibility update. RLHF as an analogue of rate shape constraints.
  • builtLecture 6: reinforcement learning for pricing. The premium control problem properly. A multi-objective reward under solvency constraints and price elasticity. Why an action that changes the portfolio breaks the forecasting paradigm.
  • builtLecture 7: governance and validation. Validating AI models in a regulated setting. Use tests. Reproducibility and seeds. Numeric-score discipline for LLM outputs. Monitoring and drift. Cross-linked to the banking track's model-risk and A-IRB governance material.

Where this maths reappears

The course is taught in general and life insurance framing, but the same architectures and disciplines reappear directly in credit risk.

CANN and LocalGLMnet in credit scoring
A-IRB PD models increasingly combine a logistic GLM baseline with a network-learned residual, exactly the CANN structure of lecture 3, and challenger-model validation increasingly uses LocalGLMnet-style varying-coefficient diagnostics to test whether a black-box model's local behaviour is stable and monotone.
Entity embedding and target encoding in banking feature engineering
The feature engineering sub-module's target-encoding-with-credibility-shrinkage material, developed for banking covariates, is the identical technique lecture 2 develops for actuarial ones.
Governance and use tests
Lecture 7's governance framework is drawn directly from, and cross-references, the banking track's model-risk management and A-IRB governance material; the underlying discipline (accountable ownership, use tests, monitoring) is identical across both regulated contexts.
