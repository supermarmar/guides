Bayesian estimation

The Bayesian apparatus the credibility lectures need: prior, posterior, conjugate updates for the gamma-Poisson and normal-normal pairs, and the three loss functions that pick out the three point estimators. Companion to the broader Bayesian statistics lecture in the statistics track, which covers MCMC, hierarchical models, and Bayes factors; this lecture is the slice of that material the actuarial credibility chapters lean on.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A Bayesian treats an unknown parameter $\theta$ as a random variable. The prior $f(\theta)$ encodes belief about $\theta$ before seeing the data. Data $X = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ enters through the likelihood $L(\theta) = \prod f(x_i \mid \theta)$. Bayes's theorem combines them into the posterior:

$$ f(\theta \mid X) \propto L(\theta) \cdot f(\theta) $$

The proportionality constant is the marginal $P(X) = \int L(\theta) f(\theta) \, d\theta$, which normalises the posterior to integrate to one. Given the posterior, the optimal point estimate $\hat{\theta}$ depends on the loss function: squared error gives the posterior mean, absolute error gives the posterior median, all-or-nothing gives the posterior mode.

The lecture has three jobs. State Bayes's theorem properly. Work two conjugate examples (gamma-Poisson and normal-normal) because they will reappear in the credibility lectures. Connect the three loss functions to the three point estimators, because every actuarial textbook glosses this connection and it is worth pinning down.

Feynman test Imagine you are estimating the average claim frequency for a new line of business. Before seeing data, you have a belief based on similar lines (the prior). You then see one year of data (the likelihood). The Bayesian answer is not "throw out the prior and use the data" or "stick with the prior and ignore the data". It is a weighted average of the two, with the weights determined by how confident you were in the prior and how much data you have. More data shifts the weight toward the likelihood; a stronger prior keeps the weight there. The maths formalises that weighting.

Step 2

Why this matters

Three reasons. First, the credibility formula in the next lecture is a Bayesian posterior estimate in disguise. The credibility weight $Z$ is determined by the relative weight of likelihood-information to prior-information. Without the Bayesian framing, the credibility formula looks like a piece of actuarial folklore; with it, the formula is a one-line consequence of squared-error-loss Bayesian estimation.

Second, parameter uncertainty in risk models (lecture 3) is naturally Bayesian. The "heterogeneous portfolio" model with $\theta_i \sim G(\theta)$ is a hierarchical Bayesian model with $G$ as the population prior. Empirical Bayes (lecture 8) is the technique for estimating $G$ from data.

Third, modern risk modelling beyond classical actuarial work is overwhelmingly Bayesian. Stochastic reserving, IBNR estimation, parameter uncertainty in stress tests, model risk in pricing: all rely on posterior distributions over model parameters, computed by either conjugate updates (when available) or Markov chain Monte Carlo (when not).

Step 3

The mental model

A simple picture. The prior is your belief before you see data; the posterior is your belief after. Bayes's theorem is the update rule. The posterior is a compromise between prior and likelihood, with the compromise weighted by sample size.

The compromise weight is the credibility factor $Z$ of the next lecture. In this gamma-Poisson example with prior $\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$ and $n$ years of data,

$$ Z = \frac{n}{\beta + n} $$

For $\alpha = 3$, $\beta = 5$, $n = 20$: $Z = 20/25 = 0.8$. The data gets 80% weight, the prior 20%. As $n \to \infty$, $Z \to 1$ and the posterior collapses on the MLE. For small $n$, the prior carries the day.

Step 4

The detail

Bayes's theorem

For events $A$ and $B_1, B_2, \dots, B_k$ partitioning the sample space, with $P(A) > 0$:

$$ P(B_r \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B_r) P(B_r)}{P(A)} = \frac{P(A \mid B_r) P(B_r)}{\sum_{i=1}^k P(A \mid B_i) P(B_i)} $$

For a continuous parameter $\theta$ and observed data $X$:

$$ f(\theta \mid X) = \frac{f(X \mid \theta) f(\theta)}{\int f(X \mid \theta) f(\theta) \, d\theta} $$

The denominator is a constant in $\theta$ (it depends only on $X$), which is why the posterior is most often written as a proportionality:

$$ f(\theta \mid X) \propto L(\theta) \cdot f(\theta) $$

where $L(\theta) = f(X \mid \theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i \mid \theta)$ is the likelihood.

The prior

Choice of prior is the philosophically loaded part of Bayesian work. Three common options:

Informative
Encodes substantive belief from past data, expert judgement, or related lines. Example: a $\operatorname{Gamma}(3, 5)$ prior on a claim rate reflects "I think the rate is around 0.6 with some uncertainty".
Conjugate
A prior chosen from a family that produces a posterior in the same family. Allows closed-form updates. The gamma is conjugate to the Poisson likelihood, the beta to the binomial, the normal to the normal-with-known-variance, the inverse-gamma to the normal-with-known-mean. Conjugate priors are mathematically convenient even when expert judgement is the substantive basis.
Uninformative
A prior that contributes minimally to the posterior, intended for cases without prior belief. Examples: uniform on a bounded parameter; Jeffreys's prior $f(\theta) \propto \sqrt{I(\theta)}$ where $I$ is Fisher information; reference priors. Uninformative priors generally cannot be both invariant under reparametrisation and proper (integrate to one), so they are accepted with caveats.

The posterior

The posterior $f(\theta \mid X)$ is a probability distribution over $\theta$, capturing what we know after seeing the data. Three operations on it:

Point estimate. Reduce the distribution to a single number $\hat{\theta}$. The choice of point estimate depends on a loss function, treated below.

Credibility interval. An interval $[a, b]$ such that $P(a \le \theta \le b \mid X) = 1 - \alpha$. Distinct from a frequentist confidence interval: the credibility interval has a probabilistic interpretation directly about $\theta$ given the data.

Predictive distribution. The distribution of a future observation $X_{n+1}$ given the data:

$$ f(x_{n+1} \mid X) = \int f(x_{n+1} \mid \theta) f(\theta \mid X) \, d\theta $$

This integrates parameter uncertainty into the prediction, so the predictive variance exceeds the in-sample variance.

Loss functions and the three estimators

A loss function $L(\hat{\theta}, \theta)$ measures the penalty for estimating $\theta$ as $\hat{\theta}$. The Bayes estimator minimises the posterior expected loss:

$$ \hat{\theta} = \arg\min_{a} E\big[L(a, \theta) \mid X\big] $$

Three canonical loss functions, three canonical estimators:

Squared error
$L(\hat{\theta}, \theta) = (\hat{\theta} - \theta)^2$. The Bayes estimator is the posterior mean: $\hat{\theta} = E(\theta \mid X)$. By far the most common choice in actuarial work.
Absolute error
$L(\hat{\theta}, \theta) = |\hat{\theta} - \theta|$. The Bayes estimator is the posterior median. Robust to outliers in the posterior; less commonly used because the closed form is rare.
All-or-nothing
$L(\hat{\theta}, \theta) = 1$ if $\hat{\theta} \ne \theta$, $0$ otherwise. The Bayes estimator is the posterior mode. Common in classification problems and in maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation.

The proofs are standard exercises in calculus. For squared error:

$$ E[L(a, \theta) \mid X] = E[(a - \theta)^2 \mid X] = a^2 - 2a E(\theta \mid X) + E(\theta^2 \mid X) $$

$$ \frac{d}{da} = 2a - 2 E(\theta \mid X) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; a = E(\theta \mid X) $$

For absolute error and all-or-nothing the calculus is similar but yields median and mode respectively.

Conjugate example: Poisson likelihood, gamma prior

The most useful single example in this course because it feeds directly into credibility theory.

Likelihood: $X_i \mid \lambda \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda)$, iid, for $i = 1, \dots, n$.

$$ L(\lambda) = \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^{x_i}}{x_i!} \propto e^{-n \lambda} \lambda^{\sum x_i} $$

Prior: $\lambda \sim \operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$.

$$ f(\lambda) = \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} \lambda^{\alpha - 1} e^{-\beta \lambda} \propto \lambda^{\alpha - 1} e^{-\beta \lambda} $$

Posterior:

$$ f(\lambda \mid X) \propto L(\lambda) f(\lambda) \propto \lambda^{\alpha + \sum x_i - 1} e^{-(\beta + n) \lambda} $$

This is the kernel of a $\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha + \sum x_i, \beta + n)$. So

$$ \lambda \mid X \sim \operatorname{Gamma}\!\left(\alpha + \sum_{i=1}^n x_i, \, \beta + n\right) $$

Bayes estimator under squared-error loss:

$$ \hat{\lambda} = E(\lambda \mid X) = \frac{\alpha + \sum x_i}{\beta + n} $$

Rewrite this in credibility form:

$$ \hat{\lambda} = \frac{\alpha + n \bar{x}}{\beta + n} = \frac{\beta}{\beta + n} \cdot \frac{\alpha}{\beta} + \frac{n}{\beta + n} \cdot \bar{x} = (1 - Z) \mu + Z \bar{x} $$

where $\mu = \alpha / \beta$ is the prior mean and $Z = n / (\beta + n)$. The Bayes estimator is a weighted average of the prior mean and the sample mean, with weight $Z$ on the data. This is the formal underpinning of the credibility formula in lecture 7.

Conjugate example: normal likelihood, normal prior

The standard "normal-normal" example. Likelihood: $X_i \mid \theta \sim N(\theta, \sigma_1^2)$, iid, with $\sigma_1$ known. Prior: $\theta \sim N(\mu, \sigma_2^2)$ with $\mu, \sigma_2$ known.

The posterior is also normal. Working through the algebra:

$$ \theta \mid X \sim N\!\left(\frac{\mu \sigma_1^2 + n \bar{x} \sigma_2^2}{\sigma_1^2 + n \sigma_2^2}, \, \frac{\sigma_1^2 \sigma_2^2}{\sigma_1^2 + n \sigma_2^2}\right) $$

Bayes estimator (under squared-error loss):

$$ \hat{\theta} = \frac{\mu \sigma_1^2 + n \bar{x} \sigma_2^2}{\sigma_1^2 + n \sigma_2^2} = (1 - Z) \mu + Z \bar{x}, \quad Z = \frac{n}{\sigma_1^2 / \sigma_2^2 + n} $$

Same structure: weighted average of prior mean and sample mean, with weight depending on $n$ and the ratio $\sigma_1^2 / \sigma_2^2$ (likelihood variance over prior variance). When prior variance $\sigma_2^2$ is large (vague prior), $Z \to n / n = 1$ and the data dominates. When prior variance is small (strong prior), $Z$ is small and the prior dominates. Sample size $n$ shifts $Z$ toward 1 either way.

The posterior predictive distribution

To predict a future observation $X_{n+1}$, integrate over the posterior of $\theta$:

$$ f(x_{n+1} \mid X) = \int f(x_{n+1} \mid \theta) f(\theta \mid X) \, d\theta $$

For the gamma-Poisson case, $X_{n+1} \mid X$ is a negative binomial: the gamma mixture of Poissons. Specifically

$$ X_{n+1} \mid X \sim \operatorname{NB}\!\left(\alpha + \sum x_i, \, \frac{\beta + n}{\beta + n + 1}\right) $$

This is the formal reason why insurance claim counts, even with a stable rate, exhibit overdispersion: the Bayesian prediction (which integrates rate uncertainty) is wider than the conditional Poisson.

A worked example: Poisson rate with gamma prior

A new line of cyber insurance. Past experience on related lines suggests an annual claim rate of $\lambda = 0.6$ per policy, with some uncertainty captured by a $\operatorname{Gamma}(3, 5)$ prior (mean 0.6, variance 0.12). After 20 years of data on the new line, total observed claims is $\sum x_i = 8$.

Prior: lambda ~ Gamma(3, 5) mean = 3 / 5 = 0.6 variance = 3 / 25 = 0.12 Likelihood data: n = 20 policies, total claims = 8. MLE = 8 / 20 = 0.40 Posterior: alpha' = alpha + sum(x_i) = 3 + 8 = 11 beta' = beta + n = 5 + 20 = 25 lambda | X ~ Gamma(11, 25) posterior mean = 11 / 25 = 0.44 Credibility decomposition: mu = prior mean = 0.6 x_bar = sample mean = 0.4 Z = n / (beta + n) = 20 / 25 = 0.80 hat(lambda) = (1 - Z) mu + Z x_bar = 0.2 * 0.6 + 0.8 * 0.4 = 0.44 95% credibility interval (from Gamma(11, 25)): [qgamma(0.025, 11, 25), qgamma(0.975, 11, 25)] approx [0.22, 0.71] Predictive for a 21st year of one policy: X_21 | X ~ NB(11, 25/26) mean = 11 * (1 - 25/26) / (25/26) = 11 / 25 = 0.44 (matches posterior mean) variance = mean * (1 + 1 / beta') = 0.44 * 1.04 = 0.4576
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The credibility formula is a Bayesian posterior in disguise

The whole of credibility theory is "the Bayesian posterior estimate under squared-error loss is a weighted average of prior mean and sample mean". Bühlmann credibility (lecture 7) generalises this to arbitrary distributions (not just conjugate pairs) by restricting attention to linear estimators and computing the best linear approximation to the Bayes estimator. Empirical Bayes credibility (lecture 8) goes further by estimating the prior from data when one is not assumed. Bayesian statistics is the foundation; credibility is one branch.

The choice of loss function reflects what you do with the estimate

Squared error is the default because it is differentiable and gives closed-form means. Absolute error is appropriate when the cost of being wrong is roughly linear (catastrophe-reserve overage). All-or-nothing is appropriate when only the exact correct decision matters (classification). The decision-theoretic interpretation is: the Bayes estimator is whatever number minimises the posterior expected loss, with loss measuring how much wrong-ness costs you. Choose the loss function to match the problem, not the maths.

Conjugate priors are mathematically convenient, not necessarily right

The gamma prior on a Poisson rate is mathematically tidy and produces closed-form updates. But the right prior is the one that reflects your actual belief. When the convenient prior conflicts with the substantive prior, use the substantive one and compute the posterior numerically (Markov chain Monte Carlo is now standard). Pretending a conjugate prior is real when it isn't, just to get a closed form, is the Bayesian equivalent of fitting an exponential to lognormal data.

Vague priors are not the absence of priors

An "uninformative" prior is still a prior. Different uninformative priors give different posteriors. The posterior depends on the parametrisation, on whether the prior is improper (does not integrate to one), and on subtle choices like flat-on-$\theta$ versus flat-on-$\log\theta$. The defensive position is to do sensitivity analysis: try the analysis with several reasonable priors and report whether the conclusion depends on the choice. If it does not, your conclusion stands. If it does, you have an honest source of uncertainty to communicate.

The posterior predictive is the operationally relevant distribution

The point estimate $\hat{\theta}$ summarises the posterior. But to make a forecast or set a reserve, you usually want the predictive distribution of $X_{n+1}$, not of $\theta$. The predictive integrates parameter uncertainty into the prediction. For a Poisson rate with gamma prior, the predictive is negative binomial, with variance larger than the mean: parameter uncertainty creates overdispersion. Quoting the predictive variance, not the conditional Poisson variance, is the Bayesian's way of acknowledging "we don't actually know $\lambda$".

MCMC has changed the practical scope

Until 1990, Bayesian analysis was limited to conjugate models because the posterior had to be tractable. Markov chain Monte Carlo (Metropolis-Hastings, Gibbs, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo) removed that restriction: now any posterior can be sampled from. This has made Bayesian methods the dominant approach in stochastic reserving, IBNR, model uncertainty, and most modern actuarial research. The closed-form conjugate examples in this lecture remain pedagogically central and reappear in credibility theory, but professional practice is now overwhelmingly MCMC-based.
