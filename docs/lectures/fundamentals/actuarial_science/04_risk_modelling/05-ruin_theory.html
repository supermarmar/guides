Ruin theory

An insurer's surplus is a stochastic process. It starts at $U$, grows linearly with premium income, and drops at each claim. Ruin is the event of surplus going negative. The probability of ruin and the adjustment coefficient that bounds it are the technical content of the entire actuarial solvency framework. Lundberg's inequality is the central result.

Step 1

The 30-second version

An insurer's surplus process $U(t) = U + c t - S(t)$ is initial surplus plus premium accrued, minus aggregate claims. Ruin is the event $U(t) < 0$ for some $t$. The classical setup assumes $N(t)$ is a Poisson process with rate $\lambda$ and the $X_i$ are iid; then $S(t)$ is a compound Poisson process. The premium loading $\theta > 0$ is defined by $c = (1 + \theta) \lambda m_1$, ensuring the process drifts upward on average.

The headline result, Lundberg's inequality:

$$ \psi(U) = P[U(t) < 0 \text{ for some } t > 0] \le e^{-R U} $$

where $R > 0$ is the adjustment coefficient, defined as the unique positive root of

$$ M_X(R) = 1 + (1 + \theta) m_1 R $$

The adjustment coefficient is an inverse measure of risk: large $R$ means tight upper bound, small $R$ means loose bound. Reinsurance, by reducing the variance of the loss process, increases $R$ and reduces $\psi(U)$. Almost every result in this lecture is a manipulation of these two equations.

Feynman test Imagine the insurer's bank balance as a sawtooth line. It starts at the initial surplus $U$, slopes steadily upward as premiums come in, and drops vertically each time a claim is paid. If the upward slope is steeper than the average rate of claim-payouts, the line drifts upward in the long run. Ruin is the event of the line crossing zero at any moment. The bigger your starting balance, the less likely you ever cross. Lundberg's inequality says the probability of ever crossing decays exponentially in $U$.
What is beyond the source The source covers continuous and discrete ruin probabilities, the adjustment coefficient, Lundberg's inequality, upper and lower bounds on $R$, and the effect of reinsurance on $R$. This lecture adds the Cramer-Lundberg approximation for the exponential-severity case (where the inequality becomes an equality up to a constant factor) and a brief preview of finite-time ruin via numerical methods.
Step 2

Why this matters

Solvency is the question every regulator and every actuary cares about most. The classical actuarial answer is to compute, or bound, the probability that surplus ever goes negative, given starting surplus, premium income, and the loss distribution. The two extreme answers (full simulation; exact closed form) are usually unavailable; Lundberg's inequality occupies the middle ground: it gives a usable upper bound for the infinite-time ruin probability in terms of a single parameter $R$ that summarises the trade-off between premium income and loss process severity.

Beyond classical insurance: the surplus process language carries over to any cash-flow process with stochastic outflows and a deterministic inflow. Bank capital ratios in stress tests, pension-fund funding levels under stochastic liabilities, and reservation-of-capital decisions for any risk-bearing entity all admit the same setup. Lundberg's framework is the prototype quantitative-finance probability bound.

Step 3

The mental model

The surplus process is a sawtooth: continuous linear rise from premium, vertical drops at each claim. Picture three sample paths starting from the same $U$:

The exponential decay in $U$ is the key intuition. Doubling the initial surplus does not double the safety: it squares the upper bound on ruin probability (because $e^{-2RU} = (e^{-RU})^2$). Capital is therefore exponentially powerful at reducing solvency risk, which is the structural reason regulators set capital requirements at levels well above expected loss.

The adjustment coefficient $R$ depends on two inputs: the premium loading $\theta$ and the loss MGF $M_X$. Higher $\theta$ (the insurer charges more) increases $R$. Heavier tails (slower-decaying MGF) decrease $R$. Reinsurance, by carving off the tail, increases the cedant's $R$.

Step 4

The detail

The surplus process

Let $N(t)$ be a Poisson process with rate $\lambda$: number of claims in $[0, t]$. Let $X_i$ be iid claim sizes with finite mean $m_1$ and MGF $M_X(r)$ on some interval $r \in [0, r_\infty)$. The aggregate claims process is

$$ S(t) = X_1 + X_2 + \dots + X_{N(t)} $$

a compound Poisson process. Let $c$ be the premium income per unit time. The surplus is

$$ U(t) = U + c t - S(t) $$

with initial surplus $U \ge 0$. The premium loading factor $\theta > 0$ is defined by

$$ c = (1 + \theta) \lambda m_1 $$

and ensures expected premium income exceeds expected claims per unit time: $E[c t - S(t)] = \theta \lambda m_1 t > 0$.

Probability of ruin

Ultimate ruin
$\psi(U) = P[U(t) < 0 \text{ for some } t \in (0, \infty)]$. The probability of ever going negative.
Finite-time ruin
$\psi(U, t) = P[U(\tau) < 0 \text{ for some } \tau \in (0, t)]$. The probability of going negative within a finite horizon. $\psi(U, t) \uparrow \psi(U)$ as $t \to \infty$.

Five immediate properties:

  1. $\psi(U)$ is decreasing in $U$: more starting capital, less risk.
  2. $\psi(U, t)$ is decreasing in $U$, increasing in $t$, and bounded above by $\psi(U)$.
  3. $\lim_{t \to \infty} \psi(U, t) = \psi(U)$.
  4. $\lim_{U \to \infty} \psi(U) = 0$: with infinite capital, ruin is impossible.
  5. $\psi(0) < 1$ provided $\theta > 0$: even starting at zero, the positive drift means ruin is not certain.

The adjustment coefficient

The adjustment coefficient $R$ is the unique positive solution of

$$ \lambda + c R = \lambda M_X(R) $$

or, equivalently using $c = (1 + \theta) \lambda m_1$,

$$ M_X(R) = 1 + (1 + \theta) m_1 R $$

Geometrically: the curve $M_X(r)$ is convex, starts at $M_X(0) = 1$ with slope $m_1$, and rises faster than the straight line $1 + (1 + \theta) m_1 r$. The two cross at $r = 0$ trivially; the next positive intersection is $R$. Existence requires that $M_X$ is finite for some positive $r$, which fails for heavy-tailed distributions like the lognormal and Pareto (with $\alpha < \infty$ but no finite MGF). For heavy-tailed severity, the adjustment coefficient does not exist and Lundberg's inequality does not apply.

For exponential severity $X \sim \operatorname{Exp}(1/\mu)$:

$$ M_X(r) = \frac{1}{1 - \mu r}, \quad r < 1/\mu $$

The equation $M_X(R) = 1 + (1 + \theta) \mu R$ gives

$$ R = \frac{\theta}{\mu (1 + \theta)} $$

For gamma severity with parameters $(\alpha, \beta)$, the equation

$$ \left(1 - \frac{R}{\beta}\right)^{-\alpha} = 1 + (1 + \theta) \frac{\alpha}{\beta} R $$

has no closed form; solve numerically (Newton-Raphson is fine because the function is convex).

Lundberg's inequality

The theorem. For all $U \ge 0$,

$$ \psi(U) \le e^{-R U} $$

The bound is tight for exponential severity (the inequality becomes an equality up to a multiplicative constant, the Cramer-Lundberg approximation below). For other severities the bound is conservative but informative.

Two interpretive points. First, $R$ is an inverse measure of risk: larger $R$ means tighter bound on $\psi(U)$. Second, the bound depends only on $R$, which collapses the entire claim-process information ($\lambda$, severity distribution, premium loading) into a single number. Two portfolios with the same $R$ have the same upper bound on ruin probability, regardless of how that $R$ was achieved.

Upper bound on R

From the source. Use the Taylor expansion of $M_X(R) = 1 + R m_1 + \frac{1}{2} R^2 m_2 + O(R^3)$ and substitute into the equation $M_X(R) = 1 + (1 + \theta) m_1 R$:

$$ 1 + R m_1 + \tfrac{1}{2} R^2 m_2 + \dots = 1 + (1 + \theta) m_1 R $$

$$ \tfrac{1}{2} R^2 m_2 < (1 + \theta) m_1 R - m_1 R = \theta m_1 R $$

$$ R < \frac{2 \theta m_1}{m_2} $$

So the heavier the second moment of $X$, the smaller $R$, the looser the ruin bound. This is the formal version of "heavy tails are dangerous". Note the inequality uses only the first two moments, which makes it broadly applicable but loose.

Lower bound on R when claims are bounded

If $X \le M$ almost surely (claims are capped at $M$, which is the situation under XOL reinsurance from the cedant's perspective), then

$$ R > \frac{1}{M} \ln\!\left(\frac{c}{\lambda m_1}\right) = \frac{1}{M} \ln(1 + \theta) $$

The proof exploits the bound $e^{Rx} \le \tfrac{x}{M} e^{RM} + (1 - \tfrac{x}{M})$ for $x \in [0, M]$, then integrates against the severity. The result is operationally useful: an XOL retention $M$ gives an explicit lower bound on the cedant's $R$ and thus an explicit upper bound on the cedant's ruin probability.

The Cramer-Lundberg approximation

For exponential severity $X \sim \operatorname{Exp}(1/\mu)$, the ruin probability has an exact form:

$$ \psi(U) = \frac{1}{1 + \theta} \exp\!\left(-\frac{\theta U}{\mu(1 + \theta)}\right) = \frac{1}{1 + \theta} e^{-R U} $$

where $R = \theta / [\mu(1 + \theta)]$ as above. The constant in front is $1 / (1 + \theta) < 1$, so the bound from Lundberg is tight (it has the right exponential decay rate) but loose by a factor of $1/(1 + \theta)$. For typical loadings $\theta = 0.1$ to $0.3$, the bound overstates the ruin probability by 10% to 30%.

For non-exponential light-tailed severities, the Cramer-Lundberg approximation generalises:

$$ \psi(U) \sim C \cdot e^{-R U} \quad \text{as } U \to \infty $$

where $C$ depends on the severity distribution. The exponential decay rate $R$ is universal across light-tailed cases; the constant $C$ is family-specific.

Reinsurance and ruin

Reinsurance reduces the variance of the loss process and therefore increases $R$ and reduces $\psi(U)$. The setup. Let $\theta$ be the cedant's premium loading, $\xi$ be the reinsurer's loading on its ceded portion, and consider proportional or XOL reinsurance.

Net premium income to the cedant:

$$ c_{\text{net}} = (1 + \theta) \lambda E(X) - (1 + \xi) \lambda E(Z) $$

For this to be positive (so the cedant still drifts upward after paying for the reinsurance):

$$ (1 + \theta) E(X) > (1 + \xi) E(Z) $$

For proportional with retention $\alpha$ (so $Y = \alpha X$, $Z = (1 - \alpha) X$, $E(Z) = (1 - \alpha) E(X)$):

$$ c_{\text{net}} = [(1 + \theta) - (1 + \xi)(1 - \alpha)] \lambda m_1 $$

Positivity requires

$$ \alpha > \frac{\xi - \theta}{1 + \xi} $$

If $\xi > \theta$ (reinsurer charges more than the cedant), the cedant must retain at least a minimum fraction; ceding 100% would leave a net loss for the cedant.

The cedant's adjustment coefficient under reinsurance is the root of

$$ \lambda M_Y(R) = \lambda + c_{\text{net}} R $$

where $M_Y$ is the MGF of the cedant's per-claim retained amount $Y$. For proportional reinsurance, $M_Y(r) = M_X(\alpha r)$. For XOL, $M_Y$ is the MGF of $\min(X, M)$, computed by integration. In both cases the cedant's $R$ is larger than the gross $R$ (because $Y \le X$ pointwise, so $M_Y \le M_X$ for $r > 0$, and the intersection happens further out).

Worked example: ruin under proportional reinsurance

From the source, simplified. $N(t) \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(30 t)$ (30 claims per year on average). $X \sim \operatorname{Lognormal}(\mu = 3, \sigma^2 = 1.1)$. Premium $c t = 1200 t$ per year. Initial surplus $U = 1000$.

Gross moments: E(X) = exp(3 + 0.55) = exp(3.55) = 34.81 E(X^2) = exp(2*3 + 2*1.1) = exp(8.2) = 3,640.95 Gross premium loading: Expected annual claims = 30 * 34.81 = 1,044.39 Premium = 1,200, so theta = 1,200/1,044.39 - 1 = 0.149 The insurer considers proportional reinsurance: Retention alpha = 0.8 (cede 20% of each claim) Reinsurer's loading xi = 0.5 Net premium income: c_net = (1 + theta) * lambda * m_1 - (1 + xi) * lambda * (1 - alpha) * m_1 = (1 + 0.149) * 1,044.39 - 1.5 * 30 * 0.2 * 34.81 = 1,200 - 313.30 = 886.70 Net positive: yes (cedant still profits on average). Probability of ruin at t = 2: Using normal approximation for S_I(2): E(S_I(2)) = 2 * alpha * lambda * m_1 = 2 * 0.8 * 30 * 34.81 = 1,671.1 Var(S_I(2)) = 2 * alpha^2 * lambda * m_2 = 2 * 0.64 * 30 * 3,640.95 = 139,812.5 SD(S_I(2)) = 374.0 Reinsurance premium for 2 years = (1+xi) * 2 * lambda * (1-alpha) * m_1 = 1.5 * 2 * 30 * 0.2 * 34.81 = 626.6 U(2) < 0 iff S_I(2) > U + 2c - RP = 1,000 + 2,400 - 626.6 = 2,773.4 P[S_I(2) > 2,773.4] = P[Z > (2,773.4 - 1,671.1) / 374.0] = P[Z > 2.948] ~= 0.0016 So under this reinsurance arrangement the probability of finite-time ruin at t=2 is about 0.16%, computed via a normal approximation to S_I(2). Without reinsurance the cedant would face higher net volatility and a higher probability of ruin at the same horizon.

Optimal reinsurance retention to minimise ruin

The optimisation problem. Choose $\alpha$ (or $M$ for XOL) to maximise $R$, which equivalently minimises Lundberg's bound on $\psi(U)$. For proportional reinsurance, the cedant's $R$ as a function of $\alpha$ is differentiable; the first-order condition

$$ \frac{dR}{d\alpha} = 0 $$

locates the optimum. The optimum is typically interior (not 0 or 1): some cession reduces variance enough to outweigh the reinsurance cost, but too much cession leaves too little net premium.

For exponential severity, the optimisation has a closed form. For other severities, numerical optimisation. The qualitative result: heavier tails on $X$ push the optimal $\alpha$ down (cede more); higher reinsurer loadings $\xi$ push $\alpha$ up (cede less).

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

R does not exist for heavy-tailed severity

Lundberg's inequality requires $M_X(R) < \infty$ for some $R > 0$. The lognormal, Pareto, Burr, and any other "heavy-tailed" loss distribution has no finite MGF: $E[e^{tX}]$ diverges for every $t > 0$. The adjustment coefficient does not exist, and the exponential bound does not apply. In its place, ruin probabilities for heavy-tailed severities decay polynomially in $U$, not exponentially. This is the technical reason that catastrophe-prone lines need much more capital per pound of premium than light-tailed lines: doubling capital gains you a much smaller marginal reduction in ruin probability.

The premium loading $\theta$ is the survival lever

$R$ scales with $\theta$ for moderate loadings (this is what the upper bound $R < 2\theta m_1 / m_2$ encodes). Doubling the premium loading roughly doubles $R$ and squares the Lundberg bound. This is the formal version of "higher premiums = more solvent insurer". In a soft market where loadings compress, insurers' $R$ compresses with them, and ruin probabilities rise faster than the premium reduction would suggest.

Capital is exponentially powerful

$\psi(U) \le e^{-RU}$. Doubling $U$ squares the bound. This is the structural reason that capital requirements are set at high multiples of expected loss: marginal capital buys exponentially more safety. Solvency II's 99.5% one-year value-at-risk is the modern analogue, but the underlying mechanism (capital provides exponentially decaying tail protection) is the Lundberg machinery from a hundred years ago.

Reinsurance buys $R$, not $\psi$

The grandmaster's habit is to evaluate reinsurance not by "how much premium did we cede" but by "how much did $R$ increase". The increase in $R$ is the marginal information for the ruin bound. A cession that costs 20% of gross premium but increases $R$ by 50% is a great trade. A cession that costs 20% of gross premium and increases $R$ by 10% is mediocre. Without the $R$ calculation, the trade is invisible.

Cramer-Lundberg is tight; the bound is informative

For exponential severity, the Cramer-Lundberg formula $\psi(U) = (1 + \theta)^{-1} e^{-RU}$ shows the Lundberg inequality is exact in shape and loose by a factor of $(1 + \theta)^{-1}$ in the constant. So for typical loadings, the Lundberg upper bound overstates ruin probability by 10-30%. This is "informative", not "tight": reserve setting based on Lundberg gives a conservative answer, which is generally what regulators want.

Finite-time ruin matters for short-horizon solvency

Infinite-time ruin is mathematically clean but operationally less relevant than finite-time ruin over a regulatory horizon (one year for Solvency II, ten years for ICS). Finite-time ruin has no Lundberg analogue with closed-form bound; the standard tool is numerical computation via integral equations or simulation. CS2's coverage is mostly infinite-time; practitioners almost always need finite-time.
