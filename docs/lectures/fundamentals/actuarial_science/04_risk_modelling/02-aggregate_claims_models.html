Aggregate claims models: individual and collective

An insurer's aggregate claims are a sum of random claim amounts over a random number of claims. The whole of risk modelling is the question of how to handle this sum: which decomposition, which moments, which MGF, which approximations. This lecture builds the two canonical models and the universal formulae that fall out.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Aggregate claims $S$ are a sum of individual claims. Two ways to set this up. The collective risk model writes $S = X_1 + \dots + X_N$ where $N$ is the random number of claims (from the portfolio as a whole) and the $X_i$ are independent identically distributed claim amounts independent of $N$. The individual risk model writes $S = Y_1 + \dots + Y_n$ where $n$ is fixed (one $Y_j$ per policy), each $Y_j$ has at most one claim, and the claim amounts can vary by policy. The two models look different but, with the right parametrisation, are connected by the same conditional expectation machinery.

The three formulae you must internalise (collective model):

$$ E(S) = E(N) \, E(X) \qquad \operatorname{Var}(S) = E(N) \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(N) \, [E(X)]^2 \qquad M_S(t) = M_N\big[\ln M_X(t)\big] $$

Everything in this lecture and the next is a special case or a worked example of these three.

Feynman test Imagine the insurer running a year. Every now and then a claim arrives, and when it does it has a size. Two things are random: how many arrive (the "claim count") and how big each one is (the "claim severity"). The total payout is the count times the average size, in expectation, but the variance of the total has to add the variance from a random count to the variance from random sizes. That two-source variance is the whole game.
What is beyond the source The source covers compound Poisson, compound binomial, compound negative binomial, and the additivity result for independent compound Poissons. This lecture adds the intuition for why the MGF identity $M_S(t) = M_N[\ln M_X(t)]$ holds, why the compound Poisson is closed under addition while the compound binomial is not, and a brief preview of Panjer's recursion (which is the natural algorithmic complement, treated in full in CS2-style references).
Step 2

Why this matters

The collective risk model is the standard pricing engine for short-term insurance. A pure premium for a portfolio is $E(S)$. A solvency capital requirement is a high quantile of $S$. A reinsurance attachment is a level $d$, and the ceded expected loss is $E[(S - d)_+]$. Every one of these is a functional of the distribution of $S$. The three formulae above are how you get from a frequency model (Poisson, binomial, negative binomial) and a severity model (lecture 1's loss distributions) to a usable distribution of $S$.

The individual risk model is the right framing when policies are heterogeneous and the natural unit is the policy, not the claim. Life-insurance term assurance is the canonical example: each of $n$ policies either has a claim (death during the year) with probability $q_j$ or it does not, and the claim amount is the sum assured for that policy. Setting that up as a collective model would lose the per-policy information.

Beyond actuarial work, the same machinery is everywhere. Bank operational risk uses a frequency-severity decomposition for the loss-distribution approach. Credit-portfolio losses are a sum of default-indicator-times-LGD-times-EAD, an individual risk model in everything but name. The compound Poisson additivity result is the lemma behind reinsurance pool aggregation and the central limit theorem for compound Poisson processes.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture a time line. Claims arrive at random points. Each arrival has a size drawn from the loss distribution. The aggregate $S$ is the height of the cumulative payout at year-end. Two layers of randomness: how many claims, and how big each one.

The conditional-expectation route to the moments of $S$:

  1. Condition on $N = n$. Then $S \mid N = n$ is a sum of $n$ iid claim amounts. Its mean is $n m_1$, its variance is $n (m_2 - m_1^2)$, where $m_k = E(X^k)$.
  2. Take expectation over $N$. Use $E(S) = E[E(S \mid N)]$ to get $E(S) = E(N) m_1$.
  3. Use the law of total variance $\operatorname{Var}(S) = E[\operatorname{Var}(S \mid N)] + \operatorname{Var}[E(S \mid N)]$ to get $\operatorname{Var}(S) = E(N) \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(N) [E(X)]^2$.

Notice that there are two sources of variance in $S$: variability in how many claims arrive (the second term) and variability in how big each one is (the first term). For a Poisson claim count, $E(N) = \operatorname{Var}(N) = \lambda$, so both terms scale with $\lambda$ and the relative contribution of each depends on whether $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ or $[E(X)]^2$ is larger. For thin claim distributions the count dominates; for heavy-tailed claims the size dominates.

Step 4

The detail

The collective risk model

Set up. Let $N$ be a non-negative integer-valued random variable representing the number of claims from a portfolio over a fixed period. Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be iid claim amounts, independent of $N$. Define

$$ S = \begin{cases} X_1 + X_2 + \dots + X_N & \text{if } N \ge 1 \\ 0 & \text{if } N = 0 \end{cases} $$

Let $m_k = E(X^k)$ denote the moments of a single claim, and let $M_X(t) = E[e^{tX}]$ be the MGF.

Mean, variance, and MGF of S

The headline results. Each is a one-line consequence of conditional expectation, but they govern everything in this course.

Mean:

$$ E(S) = E\!\left[E\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^N X_i \,\Bigm|\, N\right)\right] = E\big[N \, m_1\big] = E(N) \, m_1 $$

Variance, via the law of total variance:

$$ \operatorname{Var}(S) = E\big[\operatorname{Var}(S \mid N)\big] + \operatorname{Var}\big[E(S \mid N)\big] $$

$$ \operatorname{Var}(S \mid N = n) = n \operatorname{Var}(X) = n (m_2 - m_1^2), \quad E(S \mid N) = N m_1 $$

$$ \operatorname{Var}(S) = E(N)(m_2 - m_1^2) + m_1^2 \operatorname{Var}(N) = E(N) \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(N) [E(X)]^2 $$

MGF, by conditioning and using the iid property of the $X_i$:

$$ M_S(t) = E[e^{tS}] = E\!\left[E\!\left(e^{t \sum X_i} \,\Bigm|\, N\right)\right] = E\!\left[\prod_{i=1}^N M_X(t)\right] = E\!\big[(M_X(t))^N\big] = M_N(\ln M_X(t)) $$

The last step uses $a^N = e^{N \ln a}$, then recognises $E[e^{N \ln a}] = M_N(\ln a)$.

Specific compound distributions

Three pairs of (frequency, severity) account for the bulk of practical work.

Compound Poisson
$N \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda)$. Then $E(N) = \operatorname{Var}(N) = \lambda$, $M_N(t) = e^{\lambda(e^t - 1)}$. So $E(S) = \lambda m_1$, $\operatorname{Var}(S) = \lambda m_2$, $M_S(t) = e^{\lambda(M_X(t) - 1)}$. The compound Poisson is the canonical model and has the powerful additivity property below.
Compound binomial
$N \sim \operatorname{Bin}(n, p)$. Then $E(N) = np$, $\operatorname{Var}(N) = np(1-p)$, $M_N(t) = (pe^t + 1 - p)^n$. $E(S) = npm_1$, $\operatorname{Var}(S) = np m_2 - np^2 m_1^2$, $M_S(t) = (pM_X(t) + 1 - p)^n$. Useful when there is a hard cap on the number of policies.
Compound negative binomial
$N \sim \operatorname{NB}(k, p)$ in the "number of failures before $k$ successes" parametrisation, so $E(N) = kq/p$ with $q = 1 - p$. The compound NB is the standard fit when the claim count is overdispersed ($\operatorname{Var}(N) > E(N)$), which is empirically common. It is also the predictive distribution under a gamma-Poisson Bayesian model (lecture 6).

For the compound NB, the variance is most simply written as $\operatorname{Var}(S) = E(N) m_2 + \operatorname{Var}(N) m_1^2 - E(N) m_1^2 = E(N) (m_2 - m_1^2) + \operatorname{Var}(N) m_1^2$, i.e. the universal formula.

Additivity of independent compound Poissons

The most important structural result in this lecture. If $S_1, S_2, \dots, S_n$ are independent and each $S_i \sim \operatorname{CompPoi}(\lambda_i, F_{X_i})$, then

$$ A = S_1 + S_2 + \dots + S_n \sim \operatorname{CompPoi}(\lambda, F) $$

where

$$ \lambda = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i \qquad F(x) = \frac{1}{\lambda} \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i F_{X_i}(x) $$

In words: the sum of independent compound Poissons is a compound Poisson, with rate equal to the sum of the rates and severity equal to the rate-weighted mixture of the individual severities.

The proof is the cleanest application of the MGF identity. Each $M_{S_i}(t) = \exp[\lambda_i(M_{X_i}(t) - 1)]$. Then

$$ M_A(t) = \prod_{i=1}^n M_{S_i}(t) = \exp\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i (M_{X_i}(t) - 1)\right] = \exp\!\left[\lambda(M(t) - 1)\right] $$

with $M(t) = \frac{1}{\lambda} \sum \lambda_i M_{X_i}(t)$. The latter is the MGF of the rate-weighted mixture distribution.

This is the lemma behind every reinsurance pool aggregation (sum of cedant compound-Poisson exposures), behind the construction of a portfolio's overall loss process from line-of-business components, and behind the parameter variability arguments in lecture 3. The compound binomial does not have this property: a sum of independent compound binomials is generally not compound binomial.

The individual risk model

Set up. Let $n$ be a fixed (non-random) number of policies. For each policy $j$, let $N_j$ be the indicator of whether a claim occurred (so $N_j \in \{0, 1\}$, $P(N_j = 1) = q_j$), and let $X_j$ be the claim amount given a claim. The claim from policy $j$ is

$$ Y_j = N_j X_j = \begin{cases} X_j & \text{if } N_j = 1 \\ 0 & \text{if } N_j = 0 \end{cases} $$

Aggregate claims are

$$ S = Y_1 + Y_2 + \dots + Y_n = \sum_{j=1}^n Y_j $$

The mean and variance of $Y_j$ are special cases of the collective formulae with $N_j \sim \operatorname{Bin}(1, q_j)$:

$$ E(Y_j) = q_j \mu_j \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y_j) = q_j \sigma_j^2 + q_j(1 - q_j) \mu_j^2 $$

where $\mu_j = E(X_j)$ and $\sigma_j^2 = \operatorname{Var}(X_j)$.

Aggregate moments, assuming policies are independent:

$$ E(S) = \sum_{j=1}^n q_j \mu_j \qquad \operatorname{Var}(S) = \sum_{j=1}^n \big[q_j \sigma_j^2 + q_j(1 - q_j) \mu_j^2\big] $$

The individual model is the natural framing for life insurance term assurance, where each policy has its own sum assured $\mu_j$ and mortality rate $q_j$. It is also the framing of choice when an MLE on parameters per policy is what you want, rather than a portfolio-level frequency-severity split.

A worked example: extra-question from the source

A small group of policies can have at most 2 claims; $P(N = 0) = 0.5$, $P(N = 1) = 0.25$, $P(N = 2) = 0.25$. Each claim is 1 with probability 0.3 or 3 with probability 0.7.

Compute the moments of N and X: E(N) = 0(0.5) + 1(0.25) + 2(0.25) = 0.75 E(N^2) = 0(0.5) + 1(0.25) + 4(0.25) = 1.25 Var(N) = 1.25 - 0.75^2 = 0.6875 E(X) = 1(0.3) + 3(0.7) = 2.4 E(X^2) = 1(0.3) + 9(0.7) = 6.6 Var(X) = 6.6 - 2.4^2 = 0.84 Aggregate moments: E(S) = E(N) E(X) = 0.75 * 2.4 = 1.8 Var(S) = E(N) Var(X) + Var(N) [E(X)]^2 = 0.75*0.84 + 0.6875*5.76 = 4.59 Probability mass function of S (small enumeration): P(S = 0) = P(N = 0) = 0.5 P(S = 1) = P(N = 1, X = 1) = 0.25 * 0.3 = 0.075 P(S = 2) = P(N = 2, X1 = X2 = 1) = 0.25 * 0.3^2 = 0.0225 P(S = 3) = P(N = 1, X = 3) = 0.25 * 0.7 = 0.175 P(S = 4) = P(N = 2, X1=1, X2=3) + perm = 2*0.25*0.3*0.7 = 0.105 P(S = 6) = P(N = 2, X1 = X2 = 3) = 0.25 * 0.7^2 = 0.1225 Check: 0.5 + 0.075 + 0.0225 + 0.175 + 0.105 + 0.1225 = 1.0000 Check: sum s P(S = s) = 1.8 (matches E(S))

A worked example: a small life-insurance portfolio

An individual-model setup. 1,000 one-year term assurance policies. Each policy has a claim probability $q = 0.004$ and claim amount $X \sim \operatorname{Gamma}(5, 0.002)$ if a claim occurs.

E(X) = alpha / lambda = 5 / 0.002 = 2,500 Var(X) = alpha / lambda^2 = 5 / 0.002^2 = 1,250,000 Per policy: E(Y_j) = q E(X) = 0.004 * 2,500 = 10 Var(Y_j) = q Var(X) + q(1-q) E(X)^2 = 0.004*1,250,000 + 0.004*0.996*6,250,000 = 5,000 + 24,900 = 29,900 Aggregate (n = 1,000 independent policies): E(S) = 1,000 * 10 = 10,000 Var(S) = 1,000 * 29,900 = 29,900,000 SD(S) = sqrt(29,900,000) = 5,468 A normal approximation gives the 99.5th percentile of S as: 10,000 + 2.576 * 5,468 = 24,086 The insurer needs about 24,000 in reserves on top of expected claims to maintain 99.5% solvency on this portfolio (assuming the normal approximation holds; in practice for skewed S this should be combined with a translated-gamma approximation, see lecture 3).
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Variance is two-sourced; intuition for which dominates

$\operatorname{Var}(S) = E(N) \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(N) [E(X)]^2$. The first term is "given the count, how much do claim sizes wobble"; the second is "given the average size, how much does the count wobble". For Poisson claim counts with light-tailed severities, the count term tends to dominate when expected claim counts are small and the size term dominates when expected counts are large. For heavy-tailed severities the size term almost always dominates. This is the technical reason why catastrophe lines, with $E(N)$ tiny and $E(X^2)$ huge, are dominated by severity uncertainty, while motor lines are dominated by frequency uncertainty.

Compound Poisson additivity is the workhorse

The result "sum of independent compound Poissons is compound Poisson, with rate-weighted mixture severity" is the reason actuaries default to compound Poisson when they can. Sums aggregate cleanly across lines, branches, calendar periods, and reinsurance layers. Once you cross into compound binomial or compound NB the model loses this closure property, and aggregation requires tracking joint distributions or resorting to simulation.

The negative binomial is the right frequency model when overdispersion is real

Poisson assumes $\operatorname{Var}(N) = E(N)$, which empirically is rarely true. Real claim counts have $\operatorname{Var}(N) / E(N)$ around 1.2 to 1.5 for stable lines, and much higher when there is parameter uncertainty (heterogeneous portfolio with unknown rate parameters). The negative binomial arises naturally as a Poisson mixed over a gamma rate, which is also exactly the predictive distribution under a gamma-Poisson Bayesian model (lecture 6). When in doubt, fit a negative binomial; the Poisson is the special case.

The individual model is more general than it looks

The constraint $N_j \in \{0, 1\}$ feels restrictive but is exact for the policy-year framing of most lines (a policy either has a claim or it does not, in a single year). When a single policy can generate multiple claims (e.g. multi-peril home cover), each peril is its own "policy" inside the individual model, and the same formulae apply. The collective model is then the right framing only when you genuinely want to aggregate the count across policies and lose per-policy structure.

Algorithmic complement: Panjer's recursion

The CS2 and Klugman-Panjer-Willmot tradition develops Panjer's recursion as a way to compute the distribution of $S$ exactly for severity distributions with arithmetic support and frequency distributions in the (a, b, 0) class (Poisson, binomial, NB). The recursion is

$$ g_S(k) = \frac{1}{1 - a f_X(0)} \sum_{j = 1}^k \left(a + \frac{b j}{k}\right) f_X(j) g_S(k - j) $$

where $f_X$ is discretised severity, $g_S$ is the resulting pmf of $S$, and the constants $(a, b)$ depend on the frequency family. This is not in the source PDF but is the standard algorithmic engine in CS2 work; the analytic formulae in this lecture give the moments and the MGF, and Panjer gives the full distribution. Lecture 3 returns to approximations when Panjer is too slow.
