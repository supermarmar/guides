Empirical Bayes credibility

Bayesian credibility (lecture 7) assumed the prior and the conditional likelihood were known. Empirical Bayes Credibility Theory (EBCT) is the technique for when neither is fully known: estimate the structural parameters from a portfolio of risks. The result is a credibility formula whose weights are themselves estimated, the operational form used by most experience-rating systems.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Empirical Bayes credibility theory (EBCT) drops the conjugate-prior assumption of lecture 7. We still assume there is a risk parameter $\theta$ that determines each risk's claim distribution, but we no longer specify $f(\theta)$. Instead, we estimate the structural parameters $E[m(\theta)]$, $E[s^2(\theta)]$, and $\operatorname{Var}[m(\theta)]$ from a portfolio of $N$ risks observed over $n$ years. Two models:

  • EBCT Model 1: equal exposures, each risk has $n$ observations of equal weight. The credibility premium is $Z \bar{X}_i + (1 - Z) \overline{\overline{X}}$ with $Z = n / (n + k)$ and $k$ estimated from the data.
  • EBCT Model 2: varying exposures, each risk has a known volume $P_{ij}$ in year $j$. Credibility premium accounts for the volume weighting.

The mathematics is the Bühlmann linear-credibility from lecture 7. The novelty is the estimation step: how to compute $E[m(\theta)]$, $E[s^2(\theta)]$, and $\operatorname{Var}[m(\theta)]$ from the observed claims data across all risks in the portfolio.

Feynman test Imagine an experience-rating engine for motor insurance. You have data on thousands of drivers over five years. You do not have a prior distribution for "true" claim rate handed to you. But you do have all the data, and you can use it to estimate (1) the average claim rate across the portfolio (the prior mean), (2) how much claim rates vary across drivers (the prior variance), and (3) how much claim numbers wobble within a driver (the within-risk variance). Plug those into the Bühlmann formula and you have an experience-rated premium for each driver, with the credibility weight computed from the same data set.
What is beyond the source The source PDF specifies both EBCT Models 1 and 2, including the credibility formula and the structural-parameter notation, but omits the estimator derivations ("derivation not required"). This lecture fills in the estimators (the moment-based estimators are standard) and adds practical commentary on the bias and stability of EBCT estimates in real portfolios.
Step 2

Why this matters

Bühlmann credibility requires you to know $E[m(\theta)]$, $E[s^2(\theta)]$, $\operatorname{Var}[m(\theta)]$. In practice you almost never do. You only have the portfolio data. EBCT is the technique for fitting the credibility model to the data itself. Without it, the credibility framework is mathematical machinery without an operational pricing engine.

The two models match the two common data structures. EBCT Model 1 is appropriate when each risk produces one observation per year (one driver, one year, one claim count) and exposures are uniform. EBCT Model 2 is appropriate when exposures vary year-to-year (a fleet with growing or shrinking number of vehicles, a commercial book with annual premium changes), so each observation has a known weight $P_{ij}$. Most real experience-rating systems are Model-2-shaped.

Beyond actuarial work, the same machinery is used in any setting where shrinkage estimates are needed and the prior must be estimated from data. James-Stein estimators in statistics, hierarchical Bayesian models in machine learning, multi-level regression in econometrics: all are forms of empirical Bayes.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture a panel of $N$ risks, each observed for $n$ years. The data forms an $N \times n$ table.

The estimation logic. The grand mean $\bar{\bar{X}}$ estimates $E[m(\theta)]$. The average within-risk variance (averaged across rows) estimates $E[s^2(\theta)]$. The between-risk variance (of the row means $\bar{X}_i$) estimates $\operatorname{Var}[m(\theta)] + E[s^2(\theta)]/n$, so subtracting gives $\operatorname{Var}[m(\theta)]$.

Step 4

The detail

EBCT Model 1: equal exposures

Setup. A portfolio of $N$ risks, each observed for $n$ years with equal weight (one policy-year per cell). For risk $i$, the observations are $X_{i1}, X_{i2}, \dots, X_{in}$, conditionally independent given the risk parameter $\theta_i$.

Assumptions:

  • $\{\theta_i\}_{i=1}^N$ are iid random variables (the structure variable).
  • $\{X_{ij}\}_{j=1}^n$ are conditionally iid given $\theta_i$, with $E(X_{ij} \mid \theta_i) = m(\theta_i)$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X_{ij} \mid \theta_i) = s^2(\theta_i)$.
  • $X_{ij}$ and $X_{kl}$ are independent for $i \ne k$ (different risks are independent).

We want to estimate $m(\theta_i)$ for each risk $i$, then use it as the rate-rated premium for risk $i$.

The EBCT Model 1 credibility premium

The Bühlmann formula:

$$ \widehat{m(\theta_i) \mid X_i} = (1 - Z) \, E[m(\theta)] + Z \, \bar{X}_i $$

where

$$ Z = \frac{n}{n + k}, \quad k = \frac{E[s^2(\theta)]}{\operatorname{Var}[m(\theta)]} $$

and $\bar{X}_i = (1/n) \sum_{j=1}^n X_{ij}$ is the risk's own sample mean.

Three structural parameters need estimating: $E[m(\theta)]$, $E[s^2(\theta)]$, and $\operatorname{Var}[m(\theta)]$.

Estimating the structural parameters (Model 1)

Method of moments. Define:

$$ \bar{X}_i = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^n X_{ij}, \qquad \bar{\bar{X}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \bar{X}_i $$

(1) Estimator of $E[m(\theta)]$:

$$ \widehat{E[m(\theta)]} = \bar{\bar{X}} $$

This is an unbiased estimator because $E[\bar{\bar{X}}] = E[\bar{X}_i] = E[E(X_{ij} \mid \theta_i)] = E[m(\theta)]$.

(2) Estimator of $E[s^2(\theta)]$. The within-risk sample variance, averaged across risks:

$$ \widehat{E[s^2(\theta)]} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{n - 1} \sum_{j=1}^n (X_{ij} - \bar{X}_i)^2 $$

The inner sum is the sample variance for risk $i$, with $(n - 1)$ in the denominator for unbiasedness. The outer average across risks pools these estimates.

(3) Estimator of $\operatorname{Var}[m(\theta)]$. The between-risks variance is estimated by

$$ \frac{1}{N - 1} \sum_{i=1}^N (\bar{X}_i - \bar{\bar{X}})^2 $$

but this estimates $\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_i)$, which by the law of total variance is

$$ \operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_i) = \operatorname{Var}[E(\bar{X}_i \mid \theta_i)] + E[\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_i \mid \theta_i)] = \operatorname{Var}[m(\theta)] + \frac{E[s^2(\theta)]}{n} $$

So the estimator for $\operatorname{Var}[m(\theta)]$ is

$$ \widehat{\operatorname{Var}[m(\theta)]} = \frac{1}{N - 1} \sum_{i=1}^N (\bar{X}_i - \bar{\bar{X}})^2 - \frac{1}{n} \widehat{E[s^2(\theta)]} $$

If this comes out negative (which can happen at small $N$ or when within-risk noise is large), it is conventional to set it to zero, which gives $Z = 0$ and full reliance on the collateral.

Plug the three estimators into $Z = n / (n + k)$ where $k = \widehat{E[s^2(\theta)]} / \widehat{\operatorname{Var}[m(\theta)]}$, and into the Bühlmann formula, to get the experience-rated premium for each risk.

EBCT Model 2: varying exposures

Setup. A portfolio of $N$ risks, each observed for $n$ years, but with year-specific exposure $P_{ij}$ (number of vehicle-years, payroll, sums insured) for risk $i$ in year $j$. The data is $Y_{ij}$ = aggregate claims (or number of claims) for risk $i$ in year $j$, with $Y_{ij}$ proportional to exposure on average.

Standardise to the per-exposure rate:

$$ X_{ij} = \frac{Y_{ij}}{P_{ij}} $$

Assumptions:

  • $\{\theta_i\}$ iid as in Model 1.
  • $X_{ij} \mid \theta_i$ conditionally independent (but not identically distributed).
  • $E(X_{ij} \mid \theta_i) = m(\theta_i)$ (same conditional mean) and $\operatorname{Var}(X_{ij} \mid \theta_i) = s^2(\theta_i) / P_{ij}$ (variance inversely proportional to exposure).

The variance assumption captures the natural property of aggregate-claim rates: a larger exposure (more policy-years) averages over more underlying claims, so the rate has smaller sampling variance.

The EBCT Model 2 credibility premium

The credibility premium for risk $i$ is

$$ \widehat{m(\theta_i) \mid X_i} = (1 - Z_i) \, E[m(\theta)] + Z_i \, \bar{X}_i $$

where

$$ Z_i = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^n P_{ij}}{\sum_{j=1}^n P_{ij} + k}, \quad k = \frac{E[s^2(\theta)]}{\operatorname{Var}[m(\theta)]} $$

The risk's own weighted sample mean is

$$ \bar{X}_i = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^n P_{ij} X_{ij}}{\sum_{j=1}^n P_{ij}} = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^n Y_{ij}}{\sum_{j=1}^n P_{ij}} $$

The expected claims per exposure for risk $i$ over the observation window. The credibility factor $Z_i$ now depends on the total exposure for risk $i$: larger total exposure gives more weight to the risk's own experience.

The Bühlmann property "$Z$ tends to 1 as data grows" generalises: $Z_i \to 1$ as $\sum P_{ij} \to \infty$. If exposure $\sum P_{ij}$ is small, $Z_i$ is small, and the premium is mostly the collateral mean.

Estimating the structural parameters (Model 2)

The estimation is more involved than Model 1 because the variance assumption is heteroscedastic. The standard estimators are:

(1) Grand mean (weighted by exposure):

$$ \widehat{E[m(\theta)]} = \frac{\sum_{i,j} P_{ij} X_{ij}}{\sum_{i,j} P_{ij}} = \frac{\sum_{i,j} Y_{ij}}{\sum_{i,j} P_{ij}} $$

(2) Within-risk variance estimator: for each risk $i$, compute the variance of its observations weighted by their exposures, then pool across risks. The standard formula (which the source PDF takes as given) is

$$ \widehat{E[s^2(\theta)]} = \frac{1}{N(n-1)} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{j=1}^n P_{ij} (X_{ij} - \bar{X}_i)^2 $$

where $\bar{X}_i$ is the weighted mean for risk $i$.

(3) Between-risks variance estimator: after subtracting the within-risk noise contribution, the residual cross-risk dispersion estimates $\operatorname{Var}[m(\theta)]$. The closed form involves a fairly tangled algebraic expression that the source PDF leaves implicit and notes as "see tables". In practice the calculation is done with built-in functions in actuarial software or spreadsheets.

A worked example: motor portfolio EBCT Model 1

Three drivers, each observed for four years. Claim counts:

Yr 1 Yr 2 Yr 3 Yr 4 x_bar_i Driver 1 0 1 0 1 0.50 Driver 2 2 1 3 2 2.00 Driver 3 0 0 1 0 0.25 Grand mean: x_bar_bar = (0.50 + 2.00 + 0.25) / 3 = 0.9167 Estimator of E[m(theta)]: 0.9167 Within-risk variances (using divisor n-1 = 3): Driver 1: ((0-0.5)^2 + (1-0.5)^2 + (0-0.5)^2 + (1-0.5)^2) / 3 = 0.3333 Driver 2: ((2-2)^2 + (1-2)^2 + (3-2)^2 + (2-2)^2) / 3 = 0.6667 Driver 3: ((0-0.25)^2 + (0-0.25)^2 + (1-0.25)^2 + (0-0.25)^2) / 3 = 0.2500 Average within-risk variance: (0.3333 + 0.6667 + 0.2500) / 3 = 0.4167 Estimator of E[s^2(theta)]: 0.4167 Between-risks variance (using divisor N-1 = 2): ((0.50 - 0.9167)^2 + (2.00 - 0.9167)^2 + (0.25 - 0.9167)^2) / 2 = 0.7986 Estimator of Var[m(theta)]: 0.7986 - 0.4167 / 4 = 0.6944 Bühlmann constants: k = E[s^2] / Var[m] = 0.4167 / 0.6944 = 0.60 Z = n / (n + k) = 4 / 4.60 = 0.870 Experience-rated premiums: Driver 1: 0.130 * 0.9167 + 0.870 * 0.50 = 0.555 Driver 2: 0.130 * 0.9167 + 0.870 * 2.00 = 1.860 Driver 3: 0.130 * 0.9167 + 0.870 * 0.25 = 0.337 Compare with portfolio mean 0.9167: Driver 1 is rated 0.555, below average (small claim history) Driver 2 is rated 1.860, well above average (consistent claims) Driver 3 is rated 0.337, well below average (only one claim) The high Z = 0.87 reflects four years of data and high between-risks variance (0.69) relative to within-risk variance (0.42), so own data dominates.

EBCT Model 2 example sketch

A commercial property portfolio with varying premium volumes. Risk 1 has $P_{ij} = 100, 110, 120, 130$ over four years and aggregate losses $Y_{ij} = 5, 8, 15, 12$. Risk 2 has $P_{ij} = 50, 50, 50, 50$ and losses $1, 0, 2, 1$. Following Model 2 mechanics:

  • $\bar{X}_1 = 40/460 = 0.0870$ losses per unit premium, with total exposure 460.
  • $\bar{X}_2 = 4/200 = 0.0200$ losses per unit premium, with total exposure 200.
  • $Z_1 = 460 / (460 + k)$, $Z_2 = 200 / (200 + k)$, with $k$ estimated as in Model 2.

Even though both risks have $n = 4$ years, Risk 1 (with higher total exposure) gets a higher credibility weight, exactly because its sampling variance is smaller. This is the operational power of Model 2 over Model 1: it accounts for the heteroscedasticity created by exposure variation.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

EBCT is the operational engine of experience rating

Bayesian credibility tells you the structure of the answer. Bühlmann gives you the best linear form when conjugacy is unavailable. EBCT closes the loop by estimating the structural parameters from data. Every commercial experience-rating system (motor BMS, workers' comp, commercial fleet) is either an EBCT calculation or a regulatory-approved approximation of one. Without EBCT, the credibility framework is mathematics; with it, the framework is the rate book.

Negative variance estimates and the floor at zero

The method-of-moments estimator for $\operatorname{Var}[m(\theta)]$ can be negative when the between-risks variance falls below the within-risk noise floor. Operationally this is "the data is so noisy I cannot tell the risks apart". The convention is to floor at zero, which forces $Z = 0$, which forces every risk to be rated at the portfolio mean. This is the credibility-theoretic statement that "with this data, the experience does not justify rate differentiation".

EBCT estimates are themselves noisy

The estimators above are method-of-moments estimators; they are unbiased but not particularly efficient. The standard errors of the estimated structural parameters can be substantial when $N$ or $n$ is small. The credibility factors $Z$ and the experience-rated premiums then have their own sampling distributions. Best practice is to report not just the rated premium but a confidence interval around it; commercial systems rarely do.

Exposure measurement is the silent driver of Model 2 performance

EBCT Model 2 takes $P_{ij}$ as known. In practice the choice of exposure measure (vehicle-years versus driver-years versus premium-amount versus number of policies) matters enormously and is rarely studied formally. A pricing engine that uses the wrong exposure measure produces credibility weights that are wrong by a factor of the ratio of the misspecified exposures to the true exposures. The model maths assume the exposure is right; the pricing actuary's job is to make sure it is.

EBCT and modern generalised linear models

The classical EBCT machinery has been largely superseded in commercial practice by generalised linear models (GLMs) and generalised linear mixed models (GLMMs) that handle the same problem more flexibly. The GLMM with a normal random effect on the risk is exactly the EBCT Model 1 setup; the GLMM with an offset by exposure is exactly Model 2. The modern advantages are easy inclusion of covariates (driver age, vehicle class, postcode) that the classical credibility framework treated as separate stratifications. But the conceptual content (shrinkage toward a population mean, weighted by data volume) is unchanged.

The framework generalises to multilevel hierarchies

Two-level EBCT (drivers within a country) can be extended to three levels (drivers within agencies within a country) by recursion: each level has its own credibility weight, and the within-level mean shrinks toward the next-level-up mean. This is the multilevel-model framework in statistics. Bayesian hierarchical models with Markov chain Monte Carlo are the modern computational tool. The classical EBCT machinery breaks down at three levels but the multilevel-Bayesian framework continues to work.
