Credibility theory

Pricing a new risk, a small portfolio, or an unusual cover requires combining two sources of information: the risk's own claim history (typically thin) and a collateral data set from related risks (typically broader but less specific). Credibility theory is the formal rule for combining them.

Lecture 7 of 8 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: UP / Ch 14 - Credibility Theory.pdf
Step 1

The 30-second version

The credibility premium is a weighted average of the estimate from the risk's own data and the estimate from collateral (industry, peer, or portfolio-wide) data:

$$ \text{Premium} = Z \bar{X} + (1 - Z) \mu $$

where $\bar{X}$ is the risk-specific sample mean, $\mu$ is the collateral mean, and $Z \in [0, 1]$ is the credibility factor. $Z$ depends on the volume of risk-specific data (more data, higher $Z$) and on the relative variability of the risk parameter across the collateral pool (more variability, higher $Z$). The factor should be independent of $\bar{X}$.

The lecture has two main jobs. First, derive the Bayesian credibility premium for two conjugate models (Poisson-gamma and normal-normal) and identify $Z$ in each. Second, introduce the general Bayesian credibility framework that produces the credibility formula across families.

Feynman test Picture a small insurance underwriter pricing a unique factory. The factory's own claim history is three years of data with two claims. The industry-wide rate is 0.3 claims per factory-year. You should not price the factory at 2/3 (the MLE on its own data, only three observations) and you should not price it at 0.3 (the industry average, ignoring what you actually know about this factory). The credibility premium is a weighted average that gives more weight to the factory's data as it grows.
What is beyond the source The source covers Bayesian credibility with two conjugate examples: the gamma-Poisson model for claim frequencies and the normal-normal model for claim amounts. This lecture frames both as special cases of the Bayes estimator under squared-error loss (lecture 6), and previews the Bühlmann model (which is the linear-credibility generalisation, leading into lecture 8 on empirical Bayes credibility).
Step 2

Why this matters

Three classes of problem make credibility unavoidable. First, new risks with no own data: an underwriter pricing a novel exposure (cyber for a new sector, parametric weather for a region without history) needs to combine sparse own-data with collateral. Second, small risks with thin own data: a single small commercial fleet, a single building. Third, experience-rated portfolios: motor or workers' compensation books where each risk has some history and is priced as a blend of its own experience and the rate-class average.

Without credibility, the alternative is either the MLE on own data (high variance, often nonsense at small sample sizes) or the collateral mean (low variance, but ignores risk-specific information). Credibility is the statistically optimal way (under squared-error loss) to combine them. It is therefore the foundation of every experience-rating system, every prior-loss-loading adjustment, and every blended-rate calculation in commercial insurance.

Beyond actuarial work: bank stress testing uses credibility-style blending of historical losses with peer-bank or supervisor benchmarks. Credit-risk PD models often combine internal bank estimates with external rating agency or sector-wide medians, with weights that look exactly like a credibility $Z$.

Step 3

The mental model

Two pieces of evidence, one optimal blend. The blend depends on how much you trust each piece.

Two facts to internalise. First, $Z$ depends on how much own data you have ($n$) and on how variable the underlying parameter is across the collateral pool. More own data, higher $Z$. More heterogeneity in the pool, higher $Z$ (because the pool mean is a worse proxy for any individual risk). Second, $Z$ should be independent of $\bar{X}$: the weight depends only on data volume and structural parameters, not on the observed values.

Step 4

The detail

Setup and notation

A risk is characterised by an unknown parameter $\theta$. We have $n$ observations $X = (X_1, \dots, X_n)$ from the risk, with each $X_i \mid \theta$ following some distribution (frequency or severity). We also have, implicitly, a collateral data set summarised by a prior $f(\theta)$.

The Bayesian credibility programme:

  1. Specify likelihood $X_i \mid \theta$ and prior $f(\theta)$.
  2. Compute the posterior $f(\theta \mid X)$ via Bayes's theorem.
  3. Bayes estimate under squared-error loss: $\hat{\theta} = E(\theta \mid X)$.
  4. Express $\hat{\theta}$ in the credibility form $(1 - Z) \mu + Z \bar{X}$.

The conditional-expectation identities from lectures 1 to 3 are used throughout:

$$ E(X) = E[E(X \mid Y)], \quad \operatorname{Var}(X) = E[\operatorname{Var}(X \mid Y)] + \operatorname{Var}[E(X \mid Y)] $$

$$ E(X_1 X_2 \mid Y) = E(X_1 \mid Y) E(X_2 \mid Y) \quad \text{when } X_1, X_2 \text{ are conditionally independent given } Y $$

The Poisson-gamma model: credibility for claim frequency

Goal: estimate the expected number of claims $\lambda$ for a risk, given $n$ years of data and a collateral-based prior.

Likelihood: $X_i \mid \lambda \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda)$, iid.

Prior: $\lambda \sim \operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$.

Data: $x_1, \dots, x_n$ with $\sum x_i = $ total observed claims, $\bar{x} = (\sum x_i)/n$ the sample claim rate.

From lecture 6, the posterior is

$$ \lambda \mid X \sim \operatorname{Gamma}\!\left(\alpha + \sum x_i, \, \beta + n\right) $$

Bayes estimator under squared-error loss is the posterior mean:

$$ E(\lambda \mid X) = \frac{\alpha + \sum x_i}{\beta + n} = \frac{\alpha + n \bar{x}}{\beta + n} $$

Rewriting as a credibility formula:

$$ E(\lambda \mid X) = \frac{\beta}{\beta + n} \cdot \frac{\alpha}{\beta} + \frac{n}{\beta + n} \cdot \bar{x} = (1 - Z) \mu + Z \bar{x} $$

where $\mu = \alpha / \beta$ is the prior mean (collateral mean) and

$$ Z = \frac{n}{\beta + n} $$

Three properties of $Z$:

  • $0 < Z < 1$ for any $n > 0$ and $\beta > 0$.
  • As $n \to \infty$, $Z \to 1$: own data dominates.
  • $Z$ is independent of the data (it depends on $\beta$ and $n$ only, not on $\bar{x}$).

The parameter $\beta$ in the prior governs how much weight the collateral retains. Large $\beta$ means strong prior (concentrated around $\mu$); $Z$ is small for small $n$, and the collateral wins. Small $\beta$ means vague prior; $Z$ is close to 1 quickly, and the data wins.

The normal-normal model: credibility for claim severity

Goal: estimate the expected aggregate claims (pure premium) for a risk, modelled as normal with known variance.

Likelihood: $X_i \mid \theta \sim N(\theta, \sigma_1^2)$, iid, with $\sigma_1^2$ known.

Prior: $\theta \sim N(\mu, \sigma_2^2)$, with $\mu$ and $\sigma_2^2$ known.

Data: $x_1, \dots, x_n$ with $\bar{x} = (\sum x_i)/n$.

The posterior is also normal (closure under conjugate update). The posterior mean is

$$ E(\theta \mid X) = \frac{\mu \sigma_1^2 + n \bar{x} \sigma_2^2}{\sigma_1^2 + n \sigma_2^2} $$

which can be written as

$$ E(\theta \mid X) = (1 - Z) \mu + Z \bar{x}, \quad Z = \frac{n}{\sigma_1^2 / \sigma_2^2 + n} $$

The same three properties of $Z$ apply. The key difference from the Poisson-gamma case is what governs $Z$. Here it is the ratio $\sigma_1^2 / \sigma_2^2$: the within-risk variance over the between-risks variance. Large ratio means the likelihood is noisy relative to the prior; $Z$ is small for small $n$ and the collateral mean wins. Small ratio means own data is informative; $Z$ is close to 1 quickly.

Properties of the credibility factor

The general structure that both examples share:

  1. $Z = 0$: pure collateral; ignore own data entirely. Justified when there is no own data ($n = 0$) or when the within-risk variance is infinite relative to the between-risks variance.
  2. $Z = 1$: full credibility; own data alone. Justified when $n$ is large enough that the data dominates the prior, or equivalently when the prior is vague.
  3. $0 < Z < 1$: partial credibility; a blend. The interesting case.

Across both models, $Z$ is increasing in $n$ (more own data), decreasing in the within-risk variability (own data noisier), and increasing in the between-risks variability (collateral less informative for this risk specifically). $Z$ is independent of $\bar{X}$ (the observed value of the data), which is the structural condition called "linearity of the credibility estimator".

From Bayesian credibility to Bühlmann credibility

The two conjugate examples above are special. For non-conjugate likelihood-prior pairs, the posterior may not be in closed form, and the Bayes estimator $E(\theta \mid X)$ may not be linear in $\bar{X}$. The Bühlmann approach restricts attention to linear estimators and finds the best one.

Bühlmann setup. The risk parameter is $\theta$, drawn from some structure distribution. We do not specify the prior; we only need its mean and variance. Define:

$$ m(\theta) = E(X \mid \theta) \qquad s^2(\theta) = \operatorname{Var}(X \mid \theta) $$

$$ E[m(\theta)], \quad E[s^2(\theta)], \quad \operatorname{Var}[m(\theta)] $$

The Bühlmann credibility premium is

$$ \hat{m}(\theta \mid X) = (1 - Z) E[m(\theta)] + Z \bar{X} $$

where

$$ Z = \frac{n}{n + \frac{E[s^2(\theta)]}{\operatorname{Var}[m(\theta)]}} = \frac{n}{n + k}, \quad k = \frac{E[s^2(\theta)]}{\operatorname{Var}[m(\theta)]} $$

This is the best linear estimator (in the squared-error-loss sense) of $m(\theta)$ given the data. The constant $k = E[s^2(\theta)] / \operatorname{Var}[m(\theta)]$ is the ratio of within-risk variance to between-risks variance. Larger $k$ means more within-risk noise relative to between-risks signal, so $Z$ is smaller, more weight to the collateral.

The Bühlmann formula generalises the conjugate examples. Specifically, the Poisson-gamma case has $m(\lambda) = \lambda$, $s^2(\lambda) = \lambda$, $E[m(\lambda)] = \alpha/\beta$, $E[s^2(\lambda)] = \alpha/\beta$, $\operatorname{Var}[m(\lambda)] = \alpha/\beta^2$, so $k = \alpha/\beta \cdot \beta^2/\alpha = \beta$, matching $Z = n/(\beta + n)$ derived directly.

For the normal-normal case, $m(\theta) = \theta$, $s^2(\theta) = \sigma_1^2$, $E[m(\theta)] = \mu$, $E[s^2(\theta)] = \sigma_1^2$, $\operatorname{Var}[m(\theta)] = \sigma_2^2$, so $k = \sigma_1^2 / \sigma_2^2$, matching the Bayesian derivation.

The Bühlmann framework is the operational tool when conjugacy is not available. The next lecture (Empirical Bayes Credibility Theory) goes one step further: it estimates $E[m(\theta)]$, $E[s^2(\theta)]$, and $\operatorname{Var}[m(\theta)]$ from the data when even those are unknown.

A worked example: motor portfolio with experience rating

A motor portfolio is rated using a Poisson-gamma credibility model. The collateral data set is the rest of the portfolio, with a fitted prior of $\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha = 6, \beta = 30)$. So the collateral mean claim rate is $6/30 = 0.20$ per policy-year. A specific risk has $n = 5$ years of data, with $\sum x_i = 2$ claims observed.

Prior (collateral): lambda ~ Gamma(6, 30) mean = 6/30 = 0.20 Own data: n = 5, sum(x_i) = 2, x_bar = 2/5 = 0.40 Posterior: lambda | X ~ Gamma(6 + 2, 30 + 5) = Gamma(8, 35) posterior mean = 8/35 = 0.2286 Credibility decomposition: mu = 0.20 x_bar = 0.40 Z = n / (beta + n) = 5 / 35 = 0.1429 Premium = (1 - Z) * mu + Z * x_bar = 0.857 * 0.20 + 0.143 * 0.40 = 0.2286 (matches posterior mean) Interpretation: The risk's own data points to 0.40 claims per year. Only 14% weight is given to this because n is small. The credibility premium 0.23 is mostly the collateral rate (0.20) with a small upward adjustment from the risk's own experience. If n grows to 25 with the same x_bar = 0.40: Z = 25 / 55 = 0.4545 Premium = 0.5455 * 0.20 + 0.4545 * 0.40 = 0.2909 If n grows to 100 with x_bar = 0.40: Z = 100 / 130 = 0.7692 Premium = 0.2308 * 0.20 + 0.7692 * 0.40 = 0.3538

Convergence is visible: as $n$ grows, the credibility premium moves from the collateral mean ($0.20$) toward the sample mean ($0.40$).

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The credibility premium is the Bayes estimator dressed up

The whole of Bayesian credibility theory in this lecture is: posterior mean under squared-error loss, written as a weighted average of the prior mean and the sample mean. Stripped of actuarial notation, this is the Bayesian update from lecture 6. The credibility weight $Z$ is the relative information content of the likelihood compared to the prior.

Limited-fluctuation credibility was an early approximation

Before Bühlmann, actuaries used limited-fluctuation credibility: assign $Z = 1$ once $n$ exceeds a threshold computed to control the relative error of the MLE within some tolerance, and $Z = \sqrt{n/n_0}$ below. This is heuristic; it is not the Bayes-optimal blend. Bühlmann showed that the optimal linear blend is $Z = n/(n + k)$ for a $k$ derived from the structural variances, and that this dominates limited-fluctuation under squared-error loss. Modern practice uses Bühlmann or its empirical Bayes generalisation.

The collateral mean is rarely just a number

In practice the "collateral" data set is a peer portfolio, an industry table, or a regulatory benchmark. The credibility weight $Z$ assumes the collateral is exchangeable with the risk (same risk class, no systematic bias). If the collateral is from a different risk class or measured under different conditions, the credibility blend is wrong. Practitioners spend more time on collateral construction (segmentation, exposure adjustment, trending) than on the credibility calculation itself.

Independence of $Z$ from $\bar{X}$ is structural

The Bayes estimator under squared-error loss is linear in $\bar{X}$ in conjugate models. Non-conjugate models can give non-linear Bayes estimators, in which case the "credibility weight" depends on $\bar{X}$ itself. The Bühlmann approach restricts to linear estimators, which keeps the credibility factor data-free. This is a useful constraint operationally (the rate book can be computed once and applied to many risks) but it is an approximation, not the truth.

Credibility scales as $n / (n + k)$, not as $\sqrt{n / n_0}$

The classical limited-fluctuation formula $Z = \sqrt{n/n_0}$ implies that doubling the data multiplies the credibility weight by $\sqrt{2}$. The Bühlmann formula implies that doubling the data brings $Z$ closer to 1 in a way that depends on where you started. At $Z = 0.5$ (i.e. $n = k$), doubling $n$ brings $Z$ to $0.67$. At $Z = 0.9$ ($n = 9k$), doubling brings $Z$ to $0.95$. The marginal value of more data is largest at small $Z$ and tapers as $Z$ approaches 1.

Credibility is everywhere a forecast meets a benchmark

The structure "weighted average of own estimate and collateral estimate, with weight scaling in the data" is the most common forecast-combination scheme in applied statistics. Robust statistics calls it shrinkage. Bayesian statistics calls it the posterior mean. Machine learning calls it regularised regression (the Lasso and ridge regression are exactly shrinkage estimators with specific priors). Credibility theory is the actuarial naming convention for this universal idea.
