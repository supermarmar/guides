Reinsurance

An insurer's tool for transferring risk to another insurer. Two families of treaty (proportional and non-proportional), one set of arithmetic for each, plus the practical complications of inflation, policy excess, and the censored data the reinsurer actually receives. This is the operational sister to lecture 3.

Lecture 4 of 8 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: UP / Ch 18 - Reinsurance.pdf
Step 1

The 30-second version

Reinsurance is insurance for insurers. The direct writer pays a premium to a reinsurer in exchange for partial coverage of its claims. Two families. Proportional treaties (quota share, surplus) share each claim pro-rata. Non-proportional treaties (excess of loss, stop loss) pay only when a loss exceeds a defined threshold. The arithmetic of each is one or two integrals over the loss distribution; the practical art is knowing the data problems that arise (censoring of small reinsurer-observed claims, inflation that shifts the gross distribution but leaves retention fixed, conditional distributions for what the reinsurer actually sees).

The notational triple to keep in mind:

  • $X$: gross claim amount.
  • $Y$: amount paid by the insurer (direct writer).
  • $Z$: amount paid by the reinsurer.
  • $W = Z \mid Z > 0$: amount paid by the reinsurer, conditional on the reinsurer actually paying (the reinsurer's data).

$X = Y + Z$ holds for every treaty. The question is how the split is computed.

Feynman test Imagine a small primary insurer and a large reinsurer. The primary writes a policy with sum insured of one million pounds. It cannot bear a million-pound loss on its own balance sheet, but it can bear a hundred thousand. So it transfers everything above a hundred thousand to the reinsurer. The reinsurer collects a small premium for taking the tail risk. Both parties are happier: the primary's variance is reduced; the reinsurer pools many such treaties and diversifies.
What is beyond the source The source PDF covers proportional and XOL splits, inflation effects, policy excess, normal and lognormal truncated moments, and MLE under censored data. This lecture adds two practical extensions: pricing the reinsurance premium under different loading assumptions, and the rate-on-line shorthand reinsurers use to compare layers.
Step 2

Why this matters

No primary insurer of meaningful size operates without reinsurance. The reasons stack up: variance reduction (smooth annual results), large-loss protection (cap exposure to single events), capital relief (regulatory capital scales with net risk, not gross), access to expertise (reinsurers see many lines and price the tail better), and technical-account smoothing (cede high-volatility lines, retain low-volatility ones). The reinsurance decision is therefore a portfolio-level optimisation: what mix of cessions maximises retained return on capital subject to a tolerance for net volatility?

This lecture builds the arithmetic that turns a treaty specification into an answer. Three integrals over the loss distribution and the work is done: $E(Y)$, $E(Z)$, and the conditional distribution of $W$. Everything else (variance, MGF, ruin under reinsurance) follows.

Step 3

The mental model

A claim of size $X$ is a number on a horizontal axis. A treaty is a rule for splitting that number between insurer and reinsurer. Proportional treaties split it by a fixed ratio; non-proportional treaties split it at one or more thresholds.

Step 4

The detail

Quota share (proportional)

Retention $\alpha \in (0, 1)$ is the cedant's share of every claim. Cession $1 - \alpha$ is the reinsurer's.

$$ Y = \alpha X \qquad Z = (1 - \alpha) X $$

$$ E(Y) = \alpha E(X), \quad \operatorname{Var}(Y) = \alpha^2 \operatorname{Var}(X), \quad E(Z) = (1 - \alpha) E(X) $$

Cession applies uniformly across all risks in the treaty. Useful for high-volume, low-variance lines (motor, household) where the cedant wants administrative simplicity. The reinsurer takes the same shape of loss the cedant takes, just smaller.

Surplus (proportional)

Same mechanics as quota share but with $\alpha$ chosen per risk. The cedant fixes a maximum retained sum insured (the "line"), and any risk above the line is ceded in proportions that bring the retained portion down to the line. Useful for portfolios with heterogeneous sums insured (commercial property, marine), where uniform quota share over-cedes small policies and under-cedes large ones.

Individual excess of loss (XOL)

Retention $M$. The cedant pays the first $M$ of each claim; the reinsurer pays the excess.

$$ Y = \min(X, M) = \begin{cases} X & X \le M \\ M & X > M \end{cases} \qquad Z = \max(0, X - M) $$

The integrals:

$$ E(Y) = \int_0^M x f_X(x) \, dx + M \cdot P(X > M) $$

$$ E(Z) = \int_M^\infty (x - M) f_X(x) \, dx = E(X) - E(Y) $$

And the reinsurer's observed claims (only those with $X > M$):

$$ W = Z \mid Z > 0 = X - M \mid X > M $$

$$ g_W(w) = \frac{f_X(w + M)}{1 - F_X(M)}, \quad w > 0 $$

$$ E(W) = \frac{E(Z)}{1 - F_X(M)} $$

The distinction between $Z$ (all gross claims, with zero payouts below $M$) and $W$ (only the claims the reinsurer actually receives) matters operationally. The reinsurer's dataset is $W$-typed; any MLE on the reinsurer's data must treat the data as truncated at $M$.

XOL layer (R in excess of M)

Two thresholds. The reinsurer pays $\min(\max(X - M, 0), R)$.

$$ Y = \begin{cases} X & X \le M \\ M & M < X \le M + R \\ X - R & X > M + R \end{cases} \qquad Z = \begin{cases} 0 & X \le M \\ X - M & M < X \le M + R \\ R & X > M + R \end{cases} $$

The reinsurer's exposure caps at $R$ per claim. Markets quote XOL programmes as a "tower" of layers: 100 xs 100, 300 xs 200, and so on, each layer a separate treaty with its own premium and reinsurer.

Inflation

If claim amounts inflate by a factor $k$ (so $X' = k X$) but the retention $M$ stays nominal, the cedant retains relatively less over time. Formally:

$$ Y' = \min(k X, M), \quad Z' = \max(0, k X - M) $$

$$ E(Y') = \int_0^{M/k} k x f_X(x) \, dx + M \cdot P(X > M/k) $$

$$ E(Z') = \int_{M/k}^\infty (k x - M) f_X(x) \, dx $$

The reinsurer's exposure grows faster than $k$: inflation moves probability mass over a fixed threshold, so $E(Z')$ grows faster than gross losses do. This is why XOL pricing typically includes index clauses that automatically scale $M$ with inflation or sums insured.

Stop loss

Reinsurance triggered when the aggregate annual loss $S$ exceeds a threshold $L$. The reinsurer pays $\max(0, S - L)$. Per-policy or per-claim retention does not feature; only the year-end total. The arithmetic is the same as XOL but applied to the distribution of $S$, not $X$:

$$ E[\max(0, S - L)] = \int_L^\infty (s - L) f_S(s) \, ds = \int_L^\infty (1 - F_S(s)) \, ds $$

The last form (the integral of the survival function) is the standard stop-loss premium formula. It is the cedant's expected ceded loss and the natural basis for the reinsurer's pricing.

Policy excess (insurer-side analogue)

The same arithmetic as XOL, but from the policyholder's side: the policyholder pays the first $L$ of every claim, and the insurer pays the excess.

$$ Y_I = \max(0, X - L) \qquad W_I = Y_I \mid Y_I > 0 = X - L \mid X > L $$

$$ E(W_I) = \frac{E(Y_I)}{1 - F_X(L)} $$

From the insurer's perspective, a policy excess turns its claim distribution into a left-truncated version of the gross. The insurer's MLE on data above the excess must condition on $X > L$.

Truncated moments of the normal and lognormal

Two formulae from the source that come up routinely in XOL pricing.

Normal. If $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, the truncated integral on $[L, U]$ is

$$ \int_L^U x f_X(x) \, dx = \mu \big[\Phi(U^*) - \Phi(L^*)\big] - \sigma \big[\phi(U^*) - \phi(L^*)\big] $$

where $L^* = (L - \mu) / \sigma$, $U^* = (U - \mu) / \sigma$, $\phi$ is the standard normal PDF, and $\Phi$ the CDF.

Lognormal. If $X \sim \operatorname{Lognormal}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, the truncated $k$-th moment on $[L, U]$ is

$$ \int_L^U x^k f_X(x) \, dx = e^{k \mu + k^2 \sigma^2 / 2} \big[\Phi(U_k) - \Phi(L_k)\big] $$

where $L_k = (\ln L - \mu) / \sigma - k \sigma$ and $U_k = (\ln U - \mu) / \sigma - k \sigma$.

These are the workhorse identities for computing $E(Y)$, $E(Z)$, $E(Y^2)$, $E(Z^2)$ when severity is normal or lognormal. Other families (Pareto, Burr) have analogous closed forms; for some heavy-tailed cases numerical integration is the only option.

Estimation from reinsurer's data: the censoring problem

The reinsurer's claim record is a mix of exact observations ($x_i > M$) and right-censored observations (the reinsurer knows a claim was incurred but only that it was at most $M$, recorded as exactly $M$). For an unlimited XOL with retention $M$:

Likelihood split:

$$ L_1(\theta) = \prod_{i: x_i > M} f(x_i; \theta) \qquad L_2(\theta) = \big[P(X \le M; \theta)\big]^m = \big[F(M; \theta)\big]^m $$

$$ L(\theta) = L_1(\theta) \cdot L_2(\theta) $$

where $m$ is the count of censored claims. (For the cedant's-side analogue, the censoring is at the retention's other side; same formula, swap $F$ for $1 - F$.) Take logs, differentiate, set to zero, solve for $\hat{\theta}$. This is the MLE under censored data.

The mistake to avoid: treating censored claims as exact claims of size $M$. That gives a likelihood

$$ L_{\text{wrong}}(\theta) = \prod_{i: x_i > M} f(x_i; \theta) \cdot f(M; \theta)^m $$

which is wrong by exactly the factor $\prod [F(M) / f(M)]^m$. The fitted parameter underestimates the variance of the distribution and overestimates the mass near $M$.

Worked example: pricing an XOL layer

An insurer has a motor portfolio. Gross severity is $\operatorname{Lognormal}(\mu = 8, \sigma^2 = 1.5)$ (so $E(X) = e^{8 + 0.75} \approx 6{,}311$ and $E(X^2) = e^{16 + 3} \approx 178\,M$). Annual claim count is $\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda = 500)$. The insurer buys an XOL layer of 50,000 in excess of 10,000.

Per-claim expected cession (Z): E(Z) = integral_{M=10,000}^{M+R=60,000} (x - 10,000) f_X(x) dx + 50,000 * P(X > 60,000) = [moment integrals using lognormal truncated formula above] Approximately: P(10,000 < X < 60,000) approx 0.12 E(X | 10,000 < X < 60,000) - 10k approx 14,000 contribution from M to M+R approx 0.12 * 14,000 = 1,680 P(X > 60,000) approx 0.012 contribution from cap approx 0.012 * 50,000 = 600 Total E(Z) per claim approx 2,280 Aggregate cession: E(S_R) = lambda * E(Z) = 500 * 2,280 = 1,140,000 Reinsurer premium (assume 30% loading): Premium = 1.3 * E(S_R) = 1,482,000 Rate-on-line (industry shorthand): RoL = premium / layer limit = 1,482,000 / 25,000,000 = 5.9% Interpretation: The reinsurer is charging 5.9% of the layer limit to cover this exposure. RoLs above ~15% are non-standard; below ~2% suggest either remote risk or a soft market.

Premium pricing under different loading assumptions

The reinsurer's premium is some loading of $E(S_R)$. The three standard loading principles:

Expected value
$P = (1 + \theta) E(S_R)$ with $\theta$ a percentage loading. Simple, fair to a risk-neutral insurer, but ignores variance.
Variance principle
$P = E(S_R) + \beta \operatorname{Var}(S_R)$ with $\beta$ a variance loading. Charges more for tail-heavy treaties. Standard for catastrophe XOL where variance is high.
Standard-deviation
$P = E(S_R) + c \, \operatorname{SD}(S_R)$. Compromise between the two: not scale-invariant (which matters under measurement-unit changes), but tractable.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The treaty is the data filter

The reinsurer never sees the gross distribution. It sees a censored sample, biased towards large claims, with a hard threshold at $M$. Any estimation must condition on this filter. The single most common error in reinsurance pricing is fitting an unconditional model to conditional data, which systematically understates the parameter of polynomial decay (Pareto $\alpha$) and overstates the price.

Inflation is the silent treaty drift

A retention $M$ fixed in nominal terms erodes against an inflating loss distribution. Five years of 3% claims inflation reduces the effective retention by roughly 15%. A treaty originally pricing in 5% of gross losses ends up ceding 8 to 10%. Index clauses (mechanical adjustment of $M$ with an external index) are the standard fix; without them the reinsurer captures more risk than was bargained for, and the cedant pays the same premium for less protection.

XOL diversifies but does not eliminate

XOL protects against the largest individual claim but not against a year of many medium-sized claims. Stop loss is the corresponding aggregate protection. Mature reinsurance programmes use a combination: XOL on the individual claim severity and stop loss (or aggregate XOL) on the annual total. The interaction of the two is non-trivial; pricing them jointly requires the joint distribution of $(\max_i X_i, \sum_i X_i)$, which is rarely tractable analytically.

Surplus is the right answer for heterogeneous portfolios

Quota share is convenient but inefficient when sums insured vary widely. A 30% quota share on a portfolio with sums insured from 10k to 10m is over-cession on the small risks and under-cession on the large ones. Surplus reinsurance solves this by setting the cession ratio per risk to bring the retained sum insured to a fixed line. The trade-off is administrative cost: surplus requires per-policy data, where quota share works at the aggregate level.

The reinsurance buyer's optimisation

For fixed total premium budget, what cession structure minimises retained variance? In the XOL case, this is a constrained optimisation over $M$: lower $M$ cedes more but costs more (under typical loadings). The first-order condition equates marginal cost (the reinsurer's loading per pound of $E(Z)$) to marginal variance reduction (per pound of $E(Z)$). For light-tailed severities the optimum is high $M$ (retain almost everything); for heavy-tailed severities the optimum is low $M$ (cede aggressively). This is the formal version of "the heavier the tail, the more reinsurance you should buy".
