Risk modelling for non-life insurance

An eight-lecture course in the actuarial machinery of general (non-life) insurance: how to model the size of a single claim, how to aggregate many claims into an annual loss, how reinsurance carves that aggregate into cedant and reinsurer shares, how the surplus process tracks solvency over time, and how the Bayesian update underwrites every credibility premium. Rebuilt from the University of Pretoria Reinsurance and Risk Models course (WST322), aligned to CS2 chapters 13 to 20.

What this course is

Non-life insurance asks four standing questions. How much should we charge for cover? How much capital should we hold against the chance of an unusually bad year? How much of the risk should we cede to a reinsurer, on what treaty, at what price? And when own-data is thin, how should we blend it with collateral data from a wider pool? This course is the actuarial answer to all four. The maths is the maths of frequency-severity models, compound Poisson processes, ruin theory, and Bayesian credibility, and it is exactly the same maths that bank operational-risk capital, credit-portfolio loss modelling, and insurance-style banking pricing models reuse under different vocabulary.

The course is not a generic statistics course (for that, see the statistics track, which covers the broader inferential framework). Nor is it banking risk management (for that, see the banking risk management track). It is the eight-lecture CS2 slice on the actuarial side of non-life insurance pricing and capital, kept tight so the same reader can finish it in a week of evenings.

Prerequisites and companions

Three short prerequisite blocks. If any is unfamiliar, read it first.

  • Statistics 1 to 3: probability distributions, moments, MGFs, maximum-likelihood estimation. Lecture 1 (loss distributions) assumes all of this.
  • Statistics 9: Bayesian statistics. The full Bayesian framework (Bayes' theorem, conjugate pairs, MCMC, hierarchical models). Lecture 6 here is a tighter actuarial-specific slice of the same material, scoped to what credibility theory needs; read this if you want the broader picture.
  • Financial mathematics. The time-value-of-money arithmetic that ruin theory and reinsurance pricing use as background. Most relevant: cashflow models, force of interest, equations of value.

Companion tracks that reuse this material under other vocabularies: operational risk capital (loss-distribution approach, compound Poisson with lognormal or Burr severity), credit risk capital (the Vasicek portfolio loss model is the individual risk model in disguise), and the bank pricing course (loss-cost loading is a direct cousin of pure premium plus security loading).

How to read this

Each lecture rebuilds one source chapter as a teaching session: intuition first, formulae fully derived where the source skipped steps, a worked example at the end, and a Connections panel that flags what the same machinery is called elsewhere. The eight lectures are sequenced so each one depends on the previous; if you skip ahead, expect notation to be unfamiliar. Status badges show what is built (built) or planned.

Course sequence

Part 1 · Foundations of loss modelling (lectures 1 to 3)

The building blocks: a single claim's size, the year's aggregate claims, and what happens when you stress that aggregate with reinsurance or parameter uncertainty.

  • builtLecture 1: loss distributions. Choosing a family for the size of a single claim. Exponential, gamma, lognormal, Pareto, Burr, Weibull. Light-tailed versus heavy-tailed. Fitting and goodness-of-fit. Censoring and truncation in reinsurer data.
  • builtLecture 2: aggregate claims models. The collective risk model ($S = X_1 + \dots + X_N$) and the individual risk model ($S = Y_1 + \dots + Y_n$). Means, variances, MGFs. Compound Poisson, compound binomial, compound negative binomial. The additivity result.
  • builtLecture 3: reinsurance and parameter uncertainty. What proportional and excess-of-loss treaties do to $E(S)$ and $\operatorname{Var}(S)$. The mixed Poisson and the extra variance term when the rate parameter is itself uncertain. Normal and translated-gamma approximations to $S$.

Part 2 · Treaty arithmetic and solvency (lectures 4 to 5)

Reinsurance in operational detail, and the surplus process the cedant runs over time.

  • builtLecture 4: reinsurance. Quota share, surplus share, excess of loss, stop loss. Pricing principles (expected value, variance, standard deviation, exponential utility). Inflation, policy excess, the censored data the reinsurer actually receives. Optimal retention.
  • builtLecture 5: ruin theory. The surplus process $U(t) = u + ct - S(t)$. The adjustment coefficient $R$. Lundberg's inequality on the ruin probability. Reinsurance's effect on $R$ and on the bound. Finite-time ruin and the Solvency II one-year horizon as a modern operational cousin.

Part 3 · Bayesian credibility (lectures 6 to 8)

The formal rule for blending a risk's own claim history with collateral data, derived as a Bayesian posterior estimate.

  • builtLecture 6: Bayesian estimation. Prior, posterior, the gamma-Poisson and normal-normal conjugate updates, and the three loss functions (squared error, absolute error, all-or-nothing) that pick out the three point estimators. The actuarial slice of the broader Bayesian statistics lecture, scoped to what lectures 7 and 8 will use.
  • builtLecture 7: credibility theory. The credibility premium as a weighted average of own-data and collateral. Bayesian credibility (gamma-Poisson, normal-normal). Bühlmann linear credibility as the best linear approximation. The credibility factor $Z$ in each case.
  • builtLecture 8: empirical Bayes credibility. Estimating the prior (the "structural parameters") from data when one is not assumed. EBCT models 1 and 2. The bridge from the textbook credibility formula to a model that can be fitted from a real claims triangle.

Where this maths reappears

The course is taught in actuarial framing, but the machinery is general. A short map.

Operational risk capital (Basel)
The loss-distribution approach uses a compound Poisson with lognormal or Burr severity. Lecture 1 fits the severity; lecture 2 builds the aggregate; lecture 3's parameter-uncertainty term motivates the Pillar 2 capital add-on.
Credit-portfolio loss (Basel IRB)
Portfolio loss $L = \sum_i (\text{default}_i) \cdot \text{LGD}_i \cdot \text{EAD}_i$ is the individual risk model with default indicators as $Y_i$. The Vasicek single-factor loss distribution is what you get when you let the rate parameter (the underlying systemic factor) be uncertain. Lecture 2 and lecture 3 are the foundation, in actuarial vocabulary.
Credibility-style blending in banking
Internal estimates blended with sector benchmarks, peer-bank stress losses blended with supervisor scenarios, and IFRS 9 segment-level ECL parameters all use a $Z$-weighted average. Lectures 7 and 8 are the formal derivation.
Solvency II and the one-year VaR
The Solvency II SCR is a one-year 99.5% VaR on the loss distribution of the technical provisions. The surplus process of lecture 5, restricted to a one-year horizon, is the operational model. Lundberg's inequality is the asymptotic cousin.
