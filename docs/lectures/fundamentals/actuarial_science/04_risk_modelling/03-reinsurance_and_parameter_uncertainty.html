Lectures / Risk modelling

Reinsurance and parameter uncertainty

Lecture 2 built the aggregate $S$. This lecture stress-tests it. What happens to $E(S)$ and $\operatorname{Var}(S)$ when reinsurance carves out a layer? What happens when the rate parameter is itself uncertain? The arithmetic is short. The implications run through every later result in this course.

Lecture 3 of 8 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: UP / Ch 20 - Risk Models 2.pdf
Step 1

The 30-second version

Three extensions of the collective risk model. First, what happens to $E(S)$, $\operatorname{Var}(S)$, and the distribution of $S$ when an insurer cedes part of the risk to a reinsurer? Two cases: proportional (cede a fixed fraction of each claim, so $S$ just scales) and excess of loss (cede the part of each claim above a retention $M$, so the severity distribution gets capped and shifted). Second, what if the Poisson rate parameter is itself uncertain (heterogeneity across risks or parameter uncertainty)? Conditional expectation handles it cleanly; the unconditional variance gains an extra term. Third, what if the severity parameter is uncertain? Same machinery, same answer.

The four key results to leave with:

  • Under proportional reinsurance with retention $\alpha$: $S_I = \alpha S$ and $S_R = (1 - \alpha) S$. Means and variances scale by $\alpha$ and $(1 - \alpha)$ accordingly.
  • Under XOL with retention $M$: severity becomes $Y = \min(X, M)$ for the insurer and $Z = \max(0, X - M)$ for the reinsurer. The insurer's aggregate $S_I$ is compound Poisson with the truncated-at-$M$ severity; the reinsurer's, restricted to claims actually received, is compound Poisson with thinned rate $\lambda \pi$ where $\pi = P(X > M)$.
  • Heterogeneous-portfolio variance: $\operatorname{Var}(S) = E[\operatorname{Var}(S \mid \theta)] + \operatorname{Var}[E(S \mid \theta)]$ adds a term that the homogeneous model misses.
  • The mixed Poisson is the canonical model for parameter uncertainty in the claim count.
Feynman test Last lecture you priced the gross aggregate $S$. Now imagine you sell part of it to a reinsurer. If you sell a fixed share (proportional), the maths is unchanged, just scaled. If you sell the part above some level $M$ (excess of loss), the maths changes because every claim now passes through a cap or kink. The cap changes both moments. The number of claims the reinsurer sees is the number of claims above $M$, which is itself random. Working out the new $E(S)$ and $\operatorname{Var}(S)$ is mostly a careful integration over the part of the severity distribution above $M$.
What is beyond the source The source covers proportional and individual XOL effects on the aggregate, plus parameter variability for both rate and severity. This lecture adds the standard aggregate approximations (normal, translated gamma) used to compute quantiles of $S$ when no closed form is available, plus a brief preview of Panjer's recursion for the exact distribution.
Step 2

Why this matters

Almost no insurer carries gross losses on its own balance sheet. Reinsurance is bought to reduce volatility, cap exposures to large losses, and free regulatory capital. Pricing the reinsurance treaty and capitalising the net position both require the distribution of $S$ after reinsurance, not before. The change of measure from gross to net (and from net to ceded) is the operational pricing question that this lecture turns into formulae.

Parameter uncertainty matters because the standard models assume known $\lambda$ and known severity parameters. Real-world parameters are estimated from limited data and are themselves random variables. Ignoring this understates the variance of $S$ and therefore the capital required. The argument generalises beyond insurance: model-risk capital, parameter add-ons under Pillar 2, and prudential margins in IFRS 17 all reflect the same idea.

The mental model

Picture a single claim's path under three treaties:

From this picture two principles fall out. First, proportional reinsurance preserves the shape of every distribution in this course: $S_I = \alpha S$, $S_R = (1 - \alpha) S$, so quantiles, moments, and MGFs all rescale by $\alpha$ or $(1 - \alpha)$. Second, XOL changes the shape: the insurer's severity $Y = \min(X, M)$ has a point mass at $M$, and the reinsurer's severity $Z = X - M$ (given $X > M$) is a truncated distribution. The arithmetic of XOL is genuinely new.

Step 4

The detail

Proportional reinsurance: scale-and-go

Retention $\alpha \in (0, 1)$. The insurer keeps a fraction $\alpha$ of every claim; the reinsurer takes $1 - \alpha$. Per-claim split:

$$ Y_i = \alpha X_i \qquad Z_i = (1 - \alpha) X_i $$

Aggregates:

$$ S_I = \sum_{i=1}^N Y_i = \alpha S \qquad S_{R1} = \sum_{i=1}^N Z_i = (1 - \alpha) S $$

And therefore:

$$ E(S_I) = \alpha E(S), \quad \operatorname{Var}(S_I) = \alpha^2 \operatorname{Var}(S) $$

$$ E(S_R) = (1 - \alpha) E(S), \quad \operatorname{Var}(S_R) = (1 - \alpha)^2 \operatorname{Var}(S) $$

The whole proportional case is "everything scales". The interesting case is XOL.

Individual excess of loss reinsurance

Retention $M$. The split:

$$ Y_i = \min(X_i, M) = \begin{cases} X_i & \text{if } X_i \le M \\ M & \text{if } X_i > M \end{cases} \qquad Z_i = \max(0, X_i - M) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } X_i \le M \\ X_i - M & \text{if } X_i > M \end{cases} $$

The insurer's per-claim moments:

$$ E(Y) = \int_0^M x f_X(x) \, dx + M \, P(X > M) $$

The reinsurer's per-claim moments (over all claims, including those that pay zero):

$$ E(Z) = \int_M^\infty (x - M) f_X(x) \, dx = E(X) - E(Y) $$

Now the aggregate structure. Assume gross $N \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda)$ and gross severity $X$.

Insurer's aggregate:

$$ S_I = \sum_{i=1}^N Y_i \sim \operatorname{CompPoi}(\lambda, F_Y) $$

so $E(S_I) = \lambda E(Y)$, $\operatorname{Var}(S_I) = \lambda E(Y^2)$.

Reinsurer's aggregate (counting every gross claim, with zero payments for claims under $M$):

$$ S_{R1} = \sum_{i=1}^N Z_i \sim \operatorname{CompPoi}(\lambda, F_Z) $$

so $E(S_{R1}) = \lambda E(Z)$.

Reinsurer's aggregate restricted to claims actually received: the reinsurer only sees the claims for which $X_i > M$. Let $N_R$ be the count of such claims. By Poisson thinning,

$$ N_R \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda \pi) \quad \text{where } \pi = P(X > M) $$

and the conditional severity is $W = X - M \mid X > M$, with

$$ g_W(w) = \frac{f_X(w + M)}{P(X > M)} \quad \text{for } w > 0 $$

$$ E(W) = \frac{E(Z)}{\pi} $$

So the reinsurer's restricted aggregate $S_{R2} = \sum_{i=1}^{N_R} W_i$ is also compound Poisson, $S_{R2} \sim \operatorname{CompPoi}(\lambda \pi, F_W)$, and

$$ E(S_{R2}) = (\lambda \pi) E(W) = \lambda E(Z) = E(S_{R1}) $$

This is the cleanest result in the lecture: $S_{R1} = S_{R2}$ in distribution. The two parametrisations (gross claim count with zeros, thinned count with strict positives) are interchangeable.

Worked example: Poisson + uniform under XOL

From the source: $S \sim \operatorname{CompPoi}(\lambda = 10, X \sim \operatorname{Unif}(0, 2000))$, retention $M = 1600$.

Gross: E(X) = 1000 (uniform mid-point) E(S) = lambda * E(X) = 10 * 1000 = 10,000 Insurer's per-claim mean: E(Y) = integral_0^1600 x * (1/2000) dx + 1600 * P(X > 1600) = [x^2/4000]_0^1600 + 1600 * (1 - 1600/2000) = 640 + 320 = 960 Insurer's aggregate mean: E(S_I) = lambda * E(Y) = 10 * 960 = 9,600 Reinsurer's per-claim mean (over all gross claims): E(Z) = E(X) - E(Y) = 1000 - 960 = 40 Reinsurer's aggregate (over all gross claims): E(S_R1) = lambda * E(Z) = 10 * 40 = 400 Reinsurer thinned, only claims actually received: pi = P(X > 1600) = 1 - 1600/2000 = 0.2 N_R ~ Poisson(lambda * pi) = Poisson(2) E(W) = E(Z) / pi = 40 / 0.2 = 200 E(S_R2) = E(N_R) * E(W) = 2 * 200 = 400 (matches S_R1) Conditional severity W = X - 1600 | X > 1600: g_W(w) = f_X(w + 1600) / pi = (1/2000) / 0.2 = 0.0025 on the interval 0 < w < 400. So W ~ Uniform(0, 400). Mean = 200. Confirms E(W) above.

Aggregate distribution approximations

Closed-form distributions for $S$ exist only in special cases (e.g. compound Poisson with exponential severity gives a particular hypergeometric form, and compound geometric with exponential severity gives a special exponential mixture). For the general case, three approximations are routine.

Normal
$S \approx N(E(S), \operatorname{Var}(S))$. Works for large $E(N)$ and light-tailed severity. Fails on the upper tail when skewness is non-trivial. The source's note: "as $\lambda$ increases the skewness of a compound Poisson decreases and the normal becomes more appropriate".
Translated gamma
Match the first three moments of $S$ to a shifted gamma $k_0 + \operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha_0, \beta_0)$. Captures the skewness that the normal misses. Standard tool when CoS$(S) > 0.5$.
Normal-power (NP)
The Edgeworth-style refinement of the normal that adds a skewness correction. Specifically the NP-2 approximation: if $Z$ is standard normal, $S \approx E(S) + \operatorname{SD}(S) \cdot \left[Z + \tfrac{\gamma_1}{6}(Z^2 - 1)\right]$ where $\gamma_1$ is the coefficient of skewness of $S$. Useful for one-shot quantile estimates.

None of these is exact. For exact computation, Panjer's recursion is the standard algorithm for arithmetic-support severities and frequency families in the (a, b, 0) class. The recursion was previewed in lecture 2; in practice the severity is discretised onto a grid (mass at multiples of $h$) and the pmf of $S$ is built up term by term. Run-time is quadratic in the grid size, which makes it tractable up to several hundred grid points.

Parameter variability: heterogeneous portfolio

Suppose the portfolio is not homogeneous: different risks have different Poisson rates. Let $\theta_i$ be the rate parameter for risk $i$, treated as a random draw from some distribution. Then conditional on $\theta_i$,

$$ N_i \mid \theta_i \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\theta_i), \quad S_i \mid \theta_i \sim \operatorname{CompPoi}(\theta_i, F_X) $$

The unconditional moments of $S_i$:

$$ E(S_i) = E[E(S_i \mid \theta_i)] = E[\theta_i m_1] = E(\theta_i) \, m_1 $$

$$ \operatorname{Var}(S_i) = E[\operatorname{Var}(S_i \mid \theta_i)] + \operatorname{Var}[E(S_i \mid \theta_i)] = E[\theta_i] m_2 + \operatorname{Var}(\theta_i) m_1^2 $$

Compare with the homogeneous case (single fixed $\theta$): $\operatorname{Var}(S_i) = \theta m_2$. Heterogeneity adds an extra $\operatorname{Var}(\theta) m_1^2$ to the variance. The portfolio $S = \sum S_i$ over $n$ heterogeneous (but identically distributed across $i$) risks gives

$$ E(S) = n E(\theta) m_1 \qquad \operatorname{Var}(S) = n \big[E(\theta) m_2 + \operatorname{Var}(\theta) m_1^2\big] $$

Parameter variability: homogeneous portfolio with shared parameter

If instead all risks share a common but unknown $\theta$ (parameter uncertainty rather than heterogeneity), the $S_i \mid \theta$ are conditionally iid, and the variance picks up a different term:

$$ \operatorname{Var}(S) = E[n \theta m_2] + \operatorname{Var}[n \theta m_1] = n E(\theta) m_2 + n^2 \operatorname{Var}(\theta) m_1^2 $$

The crucial difference: the parameter-uncertainty variance term scales with $n^2$, not $n$. With many risks, $\operatorname{Var}(S) / n^2 \to \operatorname{Var}(\theta) m_1^2$, a positive constant. The portfolio does not diversify away the parameter uncertainty, only the within-risk noise. This is the formal reason that diversification alone cannot insure away model risk.

Parameter variability: severity

The same machinery applies if the severity parameter is uncertain. Example from the source: a windstorm process where the number of storms is Poisson, the number of claims per storm is Poisson with a random rate, and the claim sizes are lognormal with random mean. The unconditional moments compound: each level of conditioning contributes a term to the variance via the law of total variance. The arithmetic is unpleasant but mechanical; the discipline is to write out the conditioning hierarchy carefully and apply the variance decomposition at each level.

Worked example: windstorm losses

From the source, simplified. $K \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\Lambda)$ storms per year. The $i$-th storm has $N_i \mid \theta_i \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\theta_i)$ claims, with $\theta_i$ iid having mean $n$ and variance $s_1^2$. Each claim has size $X_{ij} \mid \mu_i \sim \operatorname{Lognormal}(\mu_i, \sigma^2)$, with $\nu_i = e^{\mu_i + \sigma^2/2}$ iid having mean $p$ and variance $s_2^2$.

The total annual loss $R = \sum_{i=1}^K S_i$ where $S_i$ is the aggregate loss from storm $i$. Repeated conditioning gives

$$ E(R) = \Lambda \, n \, p $$

$$ \operatorname{Var}(R) = \Lambda \big[(s_2^2 + p^2) e^{\sigma^2} (n + s_1^2 + n^2) - n^2 p^2 + n^2 p^2\big] = \Lambda (s_2^2 + p^2) e^{\sigma^2} (n + s_1^2 + n^2) $$

This is a fully nested compound-compound model. The point of the worked example is not the formula itself but that compound-compound risk models are tractable as long as you apply the law of total variance at each level of nesting.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Why XOL is the dominant non-proportional treaty

Proportional reinsurance shares risk pro-rata. Excess of loss reinsurance carves out specifically the tail. Reinsurers prefer XOL because the tail risk is genuinely their specialism; cedants prefer XOL because it leaves the body of the loss distribution (which they price routinely) with them and removes only the volatility they cannot diversify. The transformation $X \mapsto \min(X, M)$ is, from a capital standpoint, the cleanest variance-reduction operation available to an insurer.

Choosing M is a variance optimisation

For any given premium budget, the cedant's choice of $M$ is the choice between cheap retention with high variance and expensive retention with low variance. The mathematical question is: for fixed reinsurance premium, minimise $\operatorname{Var}(S_I)$. The optimisation is straightforward in principle but depends sharply on the loss distribution and reinsurer's loading. Lecture 4 develops this in full.

Parameter uncertainty is the long-tailed risk no one diversifies

The $n^2$ scaling of the parameter-uncertainty variance term is, structurally, the reason that insurers cannot pool their way out of model error. Diversification deals with idiosyncratic variability in claims; it does not deal with shared mis-specification of the parameter that governs all claims. Solvency II's "operational risk" capital is, in part, a reserve against precisely this term. The grandmaster's habit is to quote variance with the parameter-uncertainty term separated out, so the reader can see what diversification does and does not buy.

Mixed Poisson is the workhorse for overdispersed counts

The heterogeneous-portfolio model with $\theta_i \sim \operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$ gives an unconditional $N_i \sim \operatorname{NB}(\alpha, \beta/(\beta+1))$, which is the canonical overdispersed Poisson. Mixed-Poisson is therefore not an exotic model: it is the negative binomial wearing a hat. Whenever you fit an NB to claim count data, you are implicitly assuming a gamma distribution of rates across risks.

Compound-compound is more common than people think

The windstorm example with two-level nesting (storms; claims per storm; size per claim) is not exotic. It is the standard structure for catastrophe modelling, for IBNR (incurred-but-not-reported) reserving where the calendar year of accident is nested inside development year, and for any "event then claims" process. The arithmetic is hierarchical but mechanical. Practitioners almost always implement this with Monte Carlo simulation rather than closed-form formulae, but the formulae give the moments and act as a sanity check.
