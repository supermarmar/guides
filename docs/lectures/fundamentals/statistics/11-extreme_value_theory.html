Lectures / Statistics

Extreme value theory

The classical statistical apparatus, from descriptive statistics through Bayesian inference, targets the central tendency and the bulk of the distribution. The tail, where rare and consequential events live, is left to extrapolation by parametric assumption (which is suspect) or to non-parametric quantile estimation (which fails past the largest observation). Extreme value theory is the limit-theorem-driven framework that fixes this. Two theorems organise the subject: Fisher-Tippett-Gnedenko (the limit distribution of normalised sample maxima is one of three named families, unified as the generalised extreme value distribution), and Pickands-Balkema-de Haan (the limit distribution of excesses over a high threshold is the generalised Pareto distribution). The framework is the natural foundation for market risk expected shortfall at 99.0 or 97.5 per cent, FRTB tail-risk modelling, operational risk LDA severity tails, climate-stress tail thresholds, and any inferential problem where the analyst needs to extrapolate past the observed sample.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Extreme value theory (EVT) models the tail of a distribution by an asymptotic argument analogous to the central limit theorem. Where the CLT says the average of $n$ IID variables tends to a normal distribution after standardisation, EVT says the maximum of $n$ IID variables tends, after the appropriate normalisation, to one of three named families: the Gumbel (light tail, like the normal or exponential), the Fréchet (heavy tail, like the Pareto or Student t), or the Weibull (bounded tail, like the uniform). The three families are special cases of the generalised extreme value (GEV) distribution with shape parameter $\xi$: $\xi = 0$ gives Gumbel, $\xi > 0$ gives Fréchet, $\xi < 0$ gives Weibull. The shape parameter is the single most important number in the tail: it determines whether tail quantiles grow linearly, polynomially, or hit a finite upper bound as you go further out.

The two practical approaches are block maxima (collect the maximum of successive blocks and fit a GEV) and peaks-over-threshold (POT) (collect every observation above a high threshold and fit a generalised Pareto distribution (GPD)). The POT approach uses the data more efficiently and is the modern default in financial and operational risk applications. The Pickands-Balkema-de Haan theorem (1974-75) gives the theoretical foundation: as the threshold rises, the conditional distribution of excesses converges to a GPD. Threshold selection is the practical art, balancing bias (a too-low threshold draws on body-of-distribution behaviour rather than the asymptotic regime) against variance (a too-high threshold leaves too few exceedances for stable estimation).

Feynman test EVT is the CLT for maxima rather than for means. Where the CLT says many small things added together look normal, EVT says one big thing extracted from many looks like a GEV. The shape parameter $\xi$ is the new ingredient: it has no analogue in CLT. $\xi$ tells you what kind of tail you are in: $\xi = 0$ is exponential decay (your sample max grows like $\log n$), $\xi > 0$ is polynomial decay (your sample max grows like $n^\xi$, much faster), $\xi < 0$ is a bounded support (your sample max approaches a hard limit). Three pictures, three regimes, one parameter. Everything in EVT is the asymptotic algebra of these three behaviours.

Where this sits in the stats world

Extensions, the tail problem This lecture sits in Part V of the stats world map. The relevant pillars are 2 (theoretical) and 3 (inferential): EVT is a limit theorem (pillar 2) applied to inferential questions about quantiles past the data (pillar 3). The classical apparatus assumes the analyst is interested in central tendency and uses the CLT-driven machinery for that purpose. EVT is the parallel apparatus for the opposite question: when the rare event is the inferential target, the EVT limit theorems supply the relevant asymptotic distributions, the IID-quantile estimators are replaced by parametric GEV/GPD estimators, and the inferential focus shifts from "what is $\mu$?" to "what is $F^{-1}(0.999)$?".

Three structural links to earlier lectures. To lecture 02 (probability): the GEV and GPD are specific named distributions (often missing from elementary probability courses but routinely required in risk modelling), and EVT is the limit theorem that supplies the conditions under which they emerge. The three GEV families relate directly to the tail behaviour of the named distributions in lecture 02's distribution zoo: normal and exponential are in the Gumbel domain ($\xi = 0$); Pareto, $t$, and Cauchy are in the Fréchet domain ($\xi > 0$); uniform and beta on a finite interval are in the Weibull domain ($\xi < 0$). To lecture 06 (time series): financial returns are not IID, and EVT for time series requires modifications. The standard fix is to decluster the time series (separate independent extreme events from clustered ones, since GARCH-style volatility clustering causes extreme observations to cluster), or fit the EVT model to GARCH residuals rather than raw returns. To lecture 10 (copulas): EVT supplies the marginal-tail models that copulas leave unspecified. A serious portfolio-loss model uses EVT marginals (GPD tails for each asset return) and a copula (a tail-dependent copula like the $t$ or a vine) for the joint behaviour. This pairing is the modern standard for market-risk VaR and ES in production.

One scoping note. This lecture focuses on univariate EVT and the standard estimation workflows. Multivariate EVT (max-stable processes, point-process representations, extremal dependence) is sketched in section 8 but not developed; full multivariate EVT belongs in a specialist course or in the risk-modelling track. The relationship between EVT, the heavy-tail-distribution literature (Pareto, Zipf), and the regularly varying functions framework is mentioned in passing.

Step 3

Why this matters

Three reasons EVT deserves a lecture. First, the FRTB internal-model approach requires expected shortfall at the 97.5 per cent confidence level over a 10-day holding period, on a graded liquidity-horizon basis. Internal-model VaR at 99 per cent was the Basel 2.5 standard; FRTB replaced it with ES at 97.5 per cent because ES is a coherent risk measure (subadditive) and because the tail of the loss distribution is what matters for capital. Computing ES at 97.5 per cent on a sample of 250 daily P&L observations means relying on the empirical quantile (which uses the average of the 6 worst observations) or on a parametric tail model. The latter is dominant in production for stability; the GPD-tail model is the modern default. The same logic applies to the FRTB stressed-ES requirement, where the model is fitted on a particularly bad historical year.

Second, operational risk loss distribution approach (LDA) modelling models annual operational loss as a compound Poisson sum of severities, with the severity distribution typically split into a body (lognormal, Weibull) and a tail (GPD). The capital requirement under Basel II AMA (advanced measurement approach) and now under the Basel IV SMA is sensitive to the tail behaviour, which the GPD models. Operational risk severity data is sparse, very heavy-tailed, and dominated by a handful of large events; nothing other than EVT supplies a credible framework for the 99.9 per cent annual VaR that AMA targeted.

Third, extrapolation past the observed sample is the inferential question for which EVT is genuinely the only credible answer. Climate-stress modelling requires return-period estimates of 1-in-100-year and 1-in-1000-year events from at most 50 years of data: a non-parametric quantile estimator gives no useful answer past the largest observation; a body-of-distribution parametric fit extrapolates by force of assumption rather than by limit theorem; an EVT-based POT estimator extrapolates using the asymptotic regime that the data supports under the limit theorem's conditions. The same logic underwrites flood-defence design (the original Dutch-engineering application that motivated von Mises and Fisher-Tippett in the 1920s), nuclear safety analysis, insurance reinsurance pricing, and credit-portfolio stress scenarios at extreme percentiles.

The mental model

The picture below shows the three EVT regimes and the two estimation approaches.

Two slogans pin the workflow. The shape parameter is everything. The location and scale parameters $(\mu, \sigma)$ are nuisance parameters; the shape $\xi$ is the inferential target because it determines tail-decay rate. Estimating $\xi$ well is the central challenge of EVT, and the standard error on $\xi$ is what dominates the uncertainty in extrapolated extreme quantiles. Threshold selection is the practical art of POT. The Pickands-Balkema-de Haan theorem says the GPD limit holds as the threshold rises; the data say there is only so much above any chosen threshold. The diagnostic apparatus (mean residual life plot, parameter-stability plots, Hill plot) guides the choice. There is no automatic answer; the analyst must inspect the diagnostics and choose.

Step 5

The detail

5.1 The Fisher-Tippett-Gnedenko theorem

Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ be IID with common distribution $F$, and $M_n = \max(X_1, \ldots, X_n)$. If there exist normalising sequences $a_n > 0$ and $b_n$ such that

$$P\left(\frac{M_n - b_n}{a_n} \leq x\right) \to G(x) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty,$$

where $G$ is a non-degenerate distribution, then $G$ must be one of three named families.

Type I (Gumbel): $G(x) = \exp(-e^{-x})$, $x \in \mathbb{R}$.

Type II (Fréchet): $G(x) = \exp(-x^{-\alpha})$, $x > 0$, $\alpha > 0$.

Type III (Weibull): $G(x) = \exp(-(-x)^\alpha)$, $x < 0$, $\alpha > 0$.

This is the Fisher-Tippett-Gnedenko theorem (Fisher and Tippett 1928, Gnedenko 1943). It is the CLT for maxima, with three (rather than one) limit distributions, parameterised by the tail-decay rate of the underlying $F$. The theorem assumes IID; the generalisation to stationary dependent sequences (under a mixing condition) is the Leadbetter theorem (Leadbetter 1974).

The three families are unified by the generalised extreme value (GEV) distribution:

$$G(x; \mu, \sigma, \xi) = \exp\left(-\left[1 + \xi \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\right]^{-1/\xi}\right),$$

defined on $\{x : 1 + \xi (x - \mu)/\sigma > 0\}$, with $\mu \in \mathbb{R}$, $\sigma > 0$, $\xi \in \mathbb{R}$. The case $\xi = 0$ is the Gumbel limit $G(x; \mu, \sigma, 0) = \exp(-\exp(-(x - \mu)/\sigma))$. The case $\xi > 0$ corresponds to Fréchet with $\alpha = 1/\xi$; the case $\xi < 0$ to Weibull with $\alpha = -1/\xi$ and an upper endpoint at $\mu - \sigma/\xi$.

5.2 Domains of attraction

For a given distribution $F$, which of the three GEV limits does it converge to? The answer is determined by the tail of $F$. A distribution $F$ is in the domain of attraction of the GEV with shape $\xi$ (written $F \in \mathcal{D}(\text{GEV}_\xi)$) according to its tail behaviour:

  • Gumbel domain ($\xi = 0$): light tails with exponential decay. Includes normal, exponential, gamma, lognormal, Weibull, Gumbel itself. The "thin-tail" regime.
  • Fréchet domain ($\xi > 0$): heavy tails with polynomial decay $1 - F(x) \sim c x^{-1/\xi}$. Includes Pareto, Student $t$ with $\nu$ degrees of freedom (which gives $\xi = 1/\nu$), Cauchy ($\xi = 1$), stable distributions with index $\alpha < 2$, the Fréchet itself.
  • Weibull domain ($\xi < 0$): distributions with bounded support. Includes uniform on $[0, 1]$ ($\xi = -1$), beta, any distribution truncated above. The "hard upper bound" regime.

The tail-index $\alpha = 1/\xi$ (Fréchet domain) is the more familiar quantity in much of the heavy-tail literature; finite mean requires $\alpha > 1$ (i.e. $\xi < 1$), finite variance requires $\alpha > 2$ (i.e. $\xi < 0.5$), finite $k$-th moment requires $\alpha > k$ (i.e. $\xi < 1/k$). Financial returns typically estimate $\xi$ in the range 0.2 to 0.4, corresponding to $\alpha \in (2.5, 5)$: finite variance but no finite fourth moment, which has implications for kurtosis-based diagnostics.

5.3 Pickands-Balkema-de Haan: the POT theorem

An alternative limiting framework for tail estimation. Let $X$ be a random variable with distribution $F$, and define the excess distribution over threshold $u$:

$$F_u(y) = P(X - u \leq y \mid X > u) = \frac{F(u + y) - F(u)}{1 - F(u)}, \quad y > 0.$$

Pickands (1975) and Balkema and de Haan (1974) showed that for $F$ in the domain of attraction of $\text{GEV}_\xi$, as $u$ approaches the right endpoint of $F$,

$$F_u(y) \to H_{\xi, \beta(u)}(y) \quad \text{uniformly in } y,$$

where $H_{\xi, \beta}$ is the generalised Pareto distribution (GPD):

$$H(y; \xi, \beta) = 1 - \left(1 + \xi \frac{y}{\beta}\right)^{-1/\xi}, \quad y > 0,$$

defined for $y \in [0, \infty)$ if $\xi \geq 0$ and for $y \in [0, -\beta/\xi]$ if $\xi < 0$, with scale parameter $\beta > 0$.

The theorem is the foundation of the POT approach. As the threshold rises, the excesses above the threshold follow a GPD asymptotically. The scale $\beta$ depends on the threshold $u$ (through a linear relation $\beta(u) = \beta(u_0) + \xi (u - u_0)$), but the shape $\xi$ does not: it is the same $\xi$ as in the GEV limit of the same $F$. Estimating $\xi$ from POT data is therefore a way to estimate the tail behaviour of the parent $F$.

5.4 Block maxima: GEV estimation

Take the data series, divide into $m$ non-overlapping blocks of length $n$, extract $M_j = \max(X_{(j-1)n + 1}, \ldots, X_{jn})$ for $j = 1, \ldots, m$. Each $M_j$ is an approximate draw from the GEV (the approximation improving as $n$ grows). Fit $\text{GEV}(\mu, \sigma, \xi)$ to the $M_j$'s by maximum likelihood:

$$\ell(\mu, \sigma, \xi) = -m \log \sigma - (1 + 1/\xi) \sum_{j=1}^m \log\left(1 + \xi \frac{M_j - \mu}{\sigma}\right) - \sum_{j=1}^m \left(1 + \xi \frac{M_j - \mu}{\sigma}\right)^{-1/\xi}.$$

Maximisation is numerical. The asymptotic variance of $\hat{\xi}$ is approximately $1/m$ in samples where the GEV approximation is good; with monthly maxima from 10 years of data ($m = 120$), the standard error on $\hat{\xi}$ is about 0.09, which is non-trivial.

The classical alternative is probability-weighted moments (PWM) or its close cousin $L$-moments (Hosking 1990): match a small set of order-statistic-based moments of the sample to their GEV-theoretical values. PWM/$L$-moments often performs better than MLE in small samples and avoids the boundary issues of MLE when $\xi$ is close to zero. The Bayesian alternative (with weakly-informative priors on $\xi$) is also standard in modern applied work, especially for very small block-count situations.

The cost of block maxima is data inefficiency: with 250 trading days per year and 20 years, you have $m = 20$ annual maxima while throwing away $250 \cdot 20 - 20 = 4980$ observations. Block maxima also forces the analyst to commit to a block size that may not match the natural data structure.

5.5 POT estimation

The standard modern approach. Choose a threshold $u$; extract the exceedances $\{Y_i = X_i - u : X_i > u\}$; fit a GPD to the $Y_i$'s by MLE. The GPD log-likelihood is

$$\ell(\xi, \beta) = -k \log \beta - (1 + 1/\xi) \sum_{i=1}^k \log\left(1 + \xi \frac{Y_i}{\beta}\right),$$

where $k$ is the number of exceedances. Maximisation is numerical; the asymptotic variance of $\hat{\xi}$ is approximately $(1 + \xi)^2 / k$, so the standard error is $(1 + \hat{\xi}) / \sqrt{k}$ in samples large enough for the asymptotic regime. With $k = 100$ exceedances and $\hat{\xi} = 0.3$, the standard error is about 0.13.

Once $\hat{\xi}, \hat{\beta}$ are estimated, the tail of $F$ above $u$ is approximately

$$\widehat{1 - F}(x) = \frac{k}{n} \left(1 + \hat{\xi} \frac{x - u}{\hat{\beta}}\right)^{-1/\hat{\xi}}, \quad x > u.$$

The factor $k/n$ is the empirical exceedance proportion. Tail quantiles follow by inversion: the $1 - p$ quantile for small $p$ is

$$\hat{q}_p = u + \frac{\hat{\beta}}{\hat{\xi}} \left[\left(\frac{n p}{k}\right)^{-\hat{\xi}} - 1\right].$$

This is the workhorse formula. It extrapolates the empirical tail to quantiles beyond the data using the GPD-fitted asymptotic regime.

5.6 Threshold selection

The single most important practical choice. Too low and the GPD approximation is poor (bias dominates); too high and there are too few exceedances for stable estimation (variance dominates). Three standard diagnostic tools.

Mean residual life plot. Plot $u$ against the empirical mean excess $e(u) = \frac{1}{k_u} \sum_{X_i > u}(X_i - u)$. Under the GPD, $E[Y \mid Y > 0] = \beta/(1 - \xi)$, which is linear in $u$ (substituting $\beta(u) = \beta(u_0) + \xi(u - u_0)$). The lowest threshold above which the plot is approximately linear is a candidate threshold. The plot looks like a continuous function of $u$ with confidence bands; choose the lowest $u$ at which linearity holds within the bands.

Parameter stability plot. Fit the GPD for a sequence of candidate thresholds $u_1 < u_2 < \ldots$ and plot $\hat{\xi}(u)$ and $\hat{\beta}^*(u) = \hat{\beta}(u) - \hat{\xi}(u) u$ (the modified scale, which should be constant in $u$ if the GPD assumption holds). Choose the lowest $u$ above which both plots are stable (flat within confidence bands). This is the most-used diagnostic in practice.

Hill plot. Specific to Fréchet domain. The Hill estimator of $\xi$ uses the top $k$ order statistics: $\hat{\xi}_H = (1/k) \sum_{i=1}^k \log(X_{(n-i+1)}) - \log(X_{(n-k)})$. Plot $\hat{\xi}_H$ against $k$ (or against the threshold). Choose the smallest $k$ above which the plot stabilises into a flat region; this gives the corresponding threshold. The Hill estimator is asymptotically equivalent to GPD MLE in the Fréchet domain and is sometimes preferred for its simplicity.

No automatic method works universally. The standard production workflow is to inspect all three diagnostics, pick a threshold in the consensus region, and conduct sensitivity analysis at $\pm 10$ to $\pm 20$ per cent of the chosen threshold to verify that tail quantiles are robust.

5.7 Return levels and extreme quantiles

The $T$-year return level is the threshold expected to be exceeded once every $T$ years; equivalently, the $(1 - 1/T)$ quantile of the annual maximum distribution. In the GEV framing, with location $\mu$, scale $\sigma$, shape $\xi$:

$$z_T = \mu - \frac{\sigma}{\xi} \left(1 - (-\log(1 - 1/T))^{-\xi}\right), \quad \xi \neq 0.$$

The 100-year return level is $z_{100}$; the 1000-year is $z_{1000}$. Return levels in EVT are how engineers communicate tail risk (flood defences are typically designed to the 1-in-1000-year level; the Thames Barrier is calibrated to the 1-in-2000-year North Sea storm surge). In finance, the analogue is the VaR confidence level: a 1-day 99.9 per cent VaR is the daily-maximum return level for the 1-in-1000-day event.

Confidence intervals on return levels are constructed via the delta method on the GEV likelihood, by profile likelihood (more accurate, especially in the upper tail), or by parametric bootstrap. The intervals widen rapidly with the return period because the extrapolation is asymptotic; the 1000-year return level has a substantially wider CI than the 100-year, and the 10000-year level is usually a placeholder rather than a credible estimate.

5.8 EVT for time series

Financial returns are not IID: GARCH-style volatility clustering means extreme observations cluster in time, in violation of the IID assumption of the basic EVT theorems. Three standard responses.

Declustering. Identify runs of exceedances over the threshold (consecutive or near-consecutive in time) and keep only the maximum per run. This converts a clustered time series into approximately independent extreme events, to which IID EVT applies. The standard declustering scheme (Smith 1989, Ferro and Segers 2003) uses an "extremal index" $\theta \in (0, 1]$ that quantifies the degree of clustering: $\theta = 1$ for IID, smaller $\theta$ for stronger clustering. The asymptotic theory generalises and the practical workflow is: estimate $\theta$, decluster, fit GPD on the declustered exceedances.

EVT on GARCH residuals. Fit a GARCH model to the returns (lecture 06); extract the standardised residuals; apply EVT to the residuals (which are approximately IID under correct GARCH specification). This is the McNeil-Frey (2000) approach and is the standard for market-risk VaR/ES at extreme percentiles. The advantage is that the GARCH model handles the volatility clustering; the EVT layer handles the residual tail heaviness.

Block maxima with longer blocks. Use annual or quarterly blocks, accepting the small sample size, on the rationale that block maxima are approximately independent at long block intervals even when within-block observations are dependent. Less efficient than the above but conceptually simple.

5.9 Multivariate EVT, briefly

The multivariate generalisation is more delicate than the univariate. The key concept is the extremal dependence between two variables: do they cluster together at extreme values, or do extreme values in one occur independently of extreme values in the other? The natural measure is the tail dependence coefficient of lecture 10, which is the upper or lower limit of the conditional exceedance probability as the threshold approaches the boundary. For a Gaussian copula, the tail dependence is zero; for a $t$-copula or a stable copula, it is positive.

The full multivariate framework characterises max-stable distributions via the spectral measure on the simplex of the standard-Fréchet representation; the practical tools include the extremal coefficient, the chi and chi-bar measures (Coles, Heffernan, Tawn 1999), and the conditional-extremes framework (Heffernan and Tawn 2004). Vine copulas with tail-dependent pair-copulas are a flexible alternative to direct multivariate EVT. The R package evd and extRemes, the Python package scipy.stats.genextreme and pyextremes, and the specialised package texmex for multivariate work implement these.

Step 6

Worked example: market-risk ES at 97.5 per cent for an equity index

The standard FRTB-style application. Compute expected shortfall at 97.5 per cent over a 10-day holding period for a long position in a major equity index, using 5 years of daily data ($n = 1250$).

Step 1: data and pre-processing.

Daily log-returns over 5 years; the empirical distribution has mean approximately 0.0003 per day, standard deviation 0.012, skewness $-0.4$, kurtosis 6.8. Heavy left tail by inspection. The largest one-day loss in the sample is approximately $-7$ per cent (occurring during a volatility cluster). The 97.5 per cent empirical quantile (negated) is $-2.4$ per cent.

Step 2: GARCH filter.

Fit a GARCH(1, 1) to the returns: $\hat{\alpha}_0 \approx 10^{-6}$, $\hat{\alpha}_1 \approx 0.08$, $\hat{\beta}_1 \approx 0.91$, with $\hat{\alpha}_1 + \hat{\beta}_1 \approx 0.99$ (high persistence, typical). Extract the standardised residuals $\hat{z}_t = (r_t - \hat{\mu}_t) / \hat{\sigma}_t$. The residuals should be approximately IID; verify with Ljung-Box on $\hat{z}_t^2$ (should fail to reject IID).

Step 3: threshold selection for POT on residuals.

Negate the residuals (we want left-tail losses); inspect the mean residual life plot and the parameter stability plot. Linearity in the MRL plot starts around the 90th percentile ($u \approx 1.3$ standard residuals). The parameter stability plot is flat from $u = 1.3$ to $u = 2.0$, then becomes noisy due to small sample. Choose $u = 1.3$ (the 90th percentile), giving $k \approx 125$ exceedances.

Step 4: GPD fit.

Fit GPD to the 125 exceedances. MLE: $\hat{\xi} \approx 0.18$ (SE $\approx 0.10$), $\hat{\beta} \approx 0.55$ (SE $\approx 0.07$). The positive $\hat{\xi}$ confirms a heavy-tailed (Fréchet domain) residual distribution. The estimated tail index is $\hat{\alpha} = 1/\hat{\xi} \approx 5.5$, consistent with finite second moment, marginally infinite fifth.

Step 5: one-day VaR and ES on residuals.

For 97.5 per cent confidence: $p = 0.025$, $n = 1250$, $k = 125$, $u = 1.3$. Apply the POT quantile formula: $\hat{q}_{0.025} = 1.3 + (0.55 / 0.18)[(1250 \cdot 0.025 / 125)^{-0.18} - 1] \approx 2.1$. So $\widehat{\text{VaR}}_{0.025}$ on residuals is $-2.1$ standard residuals (in the loss direction). The 97.5 per cent ES on residuals is $\widehat{\text{ES}}_{0.025} = (\hat{q}_{0.025} + \hat{\beta} - \hat{\xi} u) / (1 - \hat{\xi}) \approx 2.6$.

Step 6: scale to one-day return space.

The one-day-ahead conditional mean and SD are $\hat{\mu}_{t+1}, \hat{\sigma}_{t+1}$ from the GARCH. One-day-ahead 97.5 per cent VaR is $\hat{\mu}_{t+1} - 2.1 \hat{\sigma}_{t+1}$; one-day ES is $\hat{\mu}_{t+1} - 2.6 \hat{\sigma}_{t+1}$. With $\hat{\sigma}_{t+1} \approx 0.012$ and $\hat{\mu}_{t+1} \approx 0$, the one-day VaR is approximately $-2.5$ per cent and the ES is approximately $-3.1$ per cent.

Step 7: scale to 10-day under FRTB.

FRTB requires the 10-day ES. Two routes. (i) Square-root scaling (assumes IID Gaussian: $\widehat{\text{ES}}_{10} \approx \sqrt{10} \cdot \widehat{\text{ES}}_1$, giving approximately $-9.8$ per cent). The route preferred in practice when capital is conservative. (ii) Re-fit the model on overlapping 10-day returns and re-apply the EVT machinery. The latter is more accurate and is what FRTB internal-model implementations actually do, since the square-root-of-time assumption substantially understates 10-day ES under realistic GARCH dynamics.

Three sanity checks. (1) Compare the EVT-based ES to the historical empirical ES (average of the worst 2.5 per cent of returns): EVT-based is typically 5 to 15 per cent larger because it extrapolates past the largest observation. (2) Sensitivity to threshold: re-run with $u$ at the 85th and 95th percentiles; the ES estimate should be stable within $\pm 5$ per cent. (3) Sensitivity to GARCH model: re-fit with EGARCH or GJR-GARCH instead of GARCH(1, 1); ES should be stable within $\pm 10$ per cent. Reporting the sensitivity envelope is the standard practice.

Step 7

Common pitfalls and cognitive traps

Fitting EVT to the body of the distribution

Easy to forget: EVT is a theory about the tail, not about the body. Fitting a GPD to a sample that uses a threshold near the median, or fitting a GEV to short blocks of daily data, violates the asymptotic regime that the limit theorems assume. The result is parameters that are determined more by the body of the distribution than by the tail, and tail quantiles that are systematically biased. Use high thresholds and long blocks; check threshold diagnostics rigorously.

Reading too much into a small-sample $\hat{\xi}$

$\hat{\xi}$ has standard error around $(1 + \xi) / \sqrt{k}$ in the POT framework. With $k = 50$ exceedances and a true $\xi$ near 0.3, the standard error is about 0.18, which means the 95 per cent CI roughly spans $(0, 0.7)$. The shape is not well-determined in finite samples. Reporting a point estimate of $\hat{\xi}$ to two decimal places without a CI is misleading. Always report the CI; better, use profile-likelihood or bootstrap intervals.

Extrapolating much past the data

EVT extrapolates by force of the asymptotic theorem, but the asymptotic argument has uncertainty. A 99.9 per cent quantile from 5 years of daily data is asking the model to predict an event that the data has barely observed (1.25 events expected, by definition). The 10,000-year return level from 50 years of climate data is largely a function of the model's parametric form. Trust EVT extrapolations one or two decades past the data; treat further extrapolations as scenarios rather than estimates.

Ignoring time-series dependence

Naive EVT applied to clustered financial returns (without GARCH filtering or declustering) understates tail risk by a substantial factor: the IID assumption inflates the effective sample size and produces over-tight CIs. The McNeil-Frey two-stage approach (GARCH + EVT on residuals) is the standard fix; the EVT-only approach is appropriate for genuinely IID data like operational loss severities at the event level (where time-clustering is weak).

Conflating return periods with prediction intervals

A 100-year return level is the level expected to be exceeded once every 100 years on average under the stationary process. It is not "the level that will not be exceeded in any 100-year window with high probability" (which is a different quantity, smaller by a factor approximately related to $1 - e^{-1}$). The distinction matters in flood-defence design and in stress-testing communications; engineers and risk modellers regularly confuse them.

Picking the threshold by goodness-of-fit only

A goodness-of-fit test on the GPD against the exceedance data will accept the GPD at many candidate thresholds, and the "best" by some single statistic may not be the right one. The proper workflow uses the visual diagnostics (MRL plot, parameter stability plot, Hill plot) to choose a threshold in the asymptotic regime, then verifies the GPD fit by goodness-of-fit as a check. Goodness-of-fit alone is necessary but not sufficient.

EVT requires the modeller to make choices the data cannot answer Threshold selection, block size, dependence handling, and the choice between Hill and MLE estimation are all decisions that the asymptotic theory does not pin down. Different reasonable choices can produce different tail quantiles by 20 per cent or more. EVT is genuinely useful but is not an objective procedure; the modeller must document choices, report sensitivities, and treat extreme-quantile estimates as carrying a model-risk band wider than the formal CI. In regulated applications this band is part of the required documentation.
Step 8

Frontiers and what's beyond

Five frontiers.

Multivariate EVT and max-stable processes. The natural generalisation to spatial or multi-asset extremes. A max-stable process is one whose finite-dimensional distributions are max-stable GEV families with a coherent dependence structure. The standard models include the Smith model, the Brown-Resnick process, the Schlather process, and the extremal-$t$ process. Estimation is computationally intensive (composite likelihood is the typical workaround) but has matured into a routine tool for spatial extremes (rainfall, temperature, wave heights) and is reaching financial portfolio applications. The R package SpatialExtremes implements the main models.

Conditional extremes (Heffernan-Tawn). An alternative to multivariate EVT for asymmetric dependence: model the conditional distribution of a random vector given that one component is extreme. Avoids the strong assumption of asymptotic dependence in the standard multivariate framework; allows asymptotic independence (where joint extremes are rarer than joint moderate values). Widely used in environmental and engineering applications; gaining traction in finance.

Bayesian EVT. Priors on $(\mu, \sigma, \xi)$ with MCMC posterior sampling. Useful for small block-counts, for incorporating expert knowledge about the tail-index, and for propagating tail-quantile uncertainty into downstream risk computations. The R package extRemes and the Bayesian variant evdbayes are standard. Modern probabilistic programming (Stan, PyMC) makes flexible Bayesian EVT models straightforward to fit.

Non-stationary EVT and climate applications. Allow $(\mu, \sigma, \xi)$ to depend on covariates: $\mu_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t + \beta_2 X_t$ for a time trend and a covariate. Allows the EVT framework to handle non-stationary extremes (climate change inducing trends in temperature maxima, for instance). The framework is implemented in extRemes and climextRemes; it is the natural tool for climate stress-testing in finance, where the historical tail is no longer informative about the future tail under a different climate state.

Machine learning for extremes. Recent work uses neural networks and tree-based methods to estimate quantile-regression-style extreme conditional quantiles, sometimes combined with EVT asymptotic constraints to ensure tail behaviour. The R package quantregForest and the Python scikit-survival family include some implementations. Performance on high-quantile prediction is competitive with classical EVT but with substantially less interpretability; regulated applications still favour the classical approach.

Step 9

Synthesis and what's next

You now have the EVT toolkit. The Fisher-Tippett-Gnedenko theorem says normalised sample maxima converge to one of three families, unified by the GEV with shape $\xi$. The Pickands-Balkema-de Haan theorem says excesses over a high threshold converge to the GPD with the same $\xi$. Block maxima fits the GEV; peaks-over-threshold fits the GPD and is the modern default for its data efficiency. Threshold selection is the practical art, driven by the mean residual life plot, parameter stability plot, and Hill plot. EVT for time series requires either GARCH filtering (McNeil-Frey) or declustering (Smith, Ferro-Segers); the basic IID theorems do not apply directly to clustered financial data. The credit and market risk applications are direct: FRTB expected shortfall at 97.5 per cent, operational risk LDA severity tails, stress-test return-period thresholds.

The final lecture turns to causal inference, the framework for extending the inferential pillars 3 and 4 to questions of treatment effect. Where the previous eleven lectures have all been about descriptive or predictive relationships, lecture 12 asks "what would have happened under a different policy or treatment?" - a fundamentally different inferential question that requires its own apparatus (potential outcomes, DAGs, instrumental variables, regression discontinuity, difference-in-differences, propensity scores). It closes the extension trilogy and the course.
