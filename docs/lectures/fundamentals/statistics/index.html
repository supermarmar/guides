Course companion

Statistics

A bachelor's-level statistics course written for the working credit risk modeller. Fourteen lectures across six parts: the nine-lecture methodological core (foundations, modelling, stochastic processes, Bayesian), a three-lecture Part V on topics that matter for credit and market risk in their own right (copulas, extreme value theory, causal inference), and a two-lecture Part VI that carries the modelling pillar forward into the machine-learning track (the exponential dispersion family, strictly consistent loss functions). Theory first, with banking and credit-risk applications threaded through. Assumes calculus from the maths track but no prior statistics. Sits beneath the machine-learning and risk-modelling tracks: every model in those courses is a statistical model first.

The stats world map

Statistics is not one subject. It is five overlapping pillars plus a transverse alternative framework. Every lecture in this course can be located on the map below, and every later lecture should be read as "the pillar to the left, with assumption X relaxed". Putting the map up front is the difference between memorising techniques and understanding how they relate.

The information flow is left to right and one-directional. Descriptive statistics produces a number; theoretical statistics gives that number a sampling distribution; inferential statistics uses the sampling distribution to attach a confidence statement to it; modelling generalises confidence to an entire parameter vector; processes generalise everything to dependent data. The Bayesian band underneath provides an alternative route from pillar 1 to all later pillars: instead of asking "what would happen if I repeated this experiment many times" (the frequentist conditional), it asks "what should I believe about $\theta$ given the data I have in hand" (the Bayesian conditional). The two routes give different answers to different questions, and the modern practice (in credit risk and elsewhere) is to know both and pick the one that matches the question.

A small thing the diagram hides: lectures 01-03 are methodological (descriptive, probability, inference are the tools). Lectures 04-09 are applied, each one a working model family. Section by section, every lecture from 03 onwards opens with a "where this sits" callout that names the pillar and the relaxations being applied. Lecture 06 (time series), for instance, is pillar 5 with the dependence indexed by time; lecture 07 (survival) is pillar 5 with the dependence imposed by censoring; lecture 08 (Markov chains) is pillar 5 with a finite discrete state space. The map is the unifying picture; the lectures are the elaborations.

How to use this

Lectures are sequenced bottom up and each one is a prerequisite for the next. Part I builds the descriptive, theoretical, and inferential foundations. Part II walks through statistical modelling, with GLMs as the workhorse. Part III drops the IID assumption and covers the three big stochastic-process families. Part IV introduces the Bayesian alternative. Status badges show what is built (built), planned, or a gap.
