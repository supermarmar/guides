Lectures / Statistics

Probability theory

The theoretical foundation underneath every sample summary. This lecture builds the random-variable model that turns "the mean of these 1,000 observations" into "the mean of these 1,000 observations and its sampling distribution under the data-generating mechanism". Without this layer, descriptive statistics is just bookkeeping and inferential statistics is impossible. With it, every sample statistic becomes a random variable, and every later lecture in the course is built on top.

Lecture 2 of 9 Reading time, 80 minutes Part I: foundations
Step 1

The 30-second version

Probability theory is the mathematics of random experiments. It does three things that the rest of statistics depends on. First, it gives us a precise language for talking about uncertainty, in which a random variable is a function from outcomes to numbers, a distribution is the recipe that says how often each value occurs, and the operations of expectation, variance, and conditioning are well-defined. Second, it produces a small zoo of named distributions, twenty or so, that describe almost every random phenomenon a working analyst will meet. Third, it proves two universal results, the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem, that say what happens to sample averages as the sample grows. Without these two theorems there is no statistics; with them, every sample mean inherits a sampling distribution that we can compute, attach a confidence interval to, and test hypotheses against.

The lecture is long because it has to be. Most courses skip too quickly over the move from "a sample of n numbers" (descriptive) to "a sample of n independent draws from a distribution" (theoretical). That move is the entire trick. Once you have internalised that every sample statistic is itself a random variable, with its own distribution governed by the laws below, the rest of the course is a sequence of footnotes.

Feynman test A random variable is a number you do not yet know. A distribution is the rule that says how that number could turn out. An expectation is a long-run average; a variance is its squared spread. Conditioning means asking the same question on a smaller sample. The law of large numbers says: if you average enough copies of a random variable, the average stops being random. The central limit theorem says: as you do this, the leftover randomness in the average is normally distributed, no matter what the original variable looked like. Almost every confidence interval and hypothesis test in classical statistics rests on those two sentences.
Step 2

Where this sits in the stats world

Pillar 2 This is pillar 2 of the stats world map: theoretical statistics, or probability theory. Where pillar 1 (descriptive) describes the sample in front of you, pillar 2 describes the random machinery underneath: a model in which the sample is one realisation of many that could have been observed. The objects are random variables, distributions, expectations, and limit theorems. Pillar 2 is purely mathematical and does not touch data directly; it instead supplies the sampling distribution for every descriptive statistic, which is what makes the next pillar (inference) possible.

The dependency runs in one direction only. Pillar 1 produces a number (the sample mean, say). Pillar 2 says: if the data were IID draws from a distribution with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, the sample mean is itself a random variable with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2 / n$, and for large $n$ it is approximately normal. Pillar 3 (lecture 03, inferential statistics) takes that fact and turns it into a confidence interval and a hypothesis test. Every model in the course from lecture 05 onwards is also resting on this pillar: maximum-likelihood estimation, the bootstrap, GLM standard errors, time-series unit-root tests, survival-analysis log-rank tests, all of them are pillar 2 results applied to a specific parametric model. If you only learn one lecture in this course deeply, this is the one.

Step 3

Why this matters

Three reasons to invest carefully in this lecture. First, every method later in the course is a probability statement in disguise. A confidence interval is a statement about the sampling distribution of an estimator. A p-value is a statement about the distribution of a test statistic under the null. A logistic-regression standard error is the square root of an entry of the inverse Fisher information. A Kaplan-Meier confidence band is Greenwood's variance, which is itself a delta-method expansion. None of these objects exist outside the probability model. The user who has internalised pillar 2 reads any one of them as a single, coherent statement; the user who hasn't accumulates a library of unrelated recipes.

Second, the choice of distribution determines the model. In credit risk modelling, the choice between Poisson, negative binomial, and Tweedie for a frequency response is the choice of which random-variable model is being assumed; the choice between Weibull and lognormal for a time-to-default is the same; the choice between normal and Student's t for a market-risk return is the same. Each choice carries with it the entire apparatus of the relevant distribution (its parameters, expectation, variance, moment-generating function, the way it behaves in sums). Getting that choice right is a far larger source of model performance than the choice of optimiser or feature set.

Third, the two universal limit theorems are how we know what happens at scale without having to enumerate. The law of large numbers is the reason you can read a Monte Carlo result. The central limit theorem is the reason you can attach a confidence interval to almost any reasonably behaved sample average. Together, they convert finite-sample statistics into statements about underlying populations that are precise to a quantifiable margin. The cost of not understanding them is a career of treating finite samples as if they were infinite, or vice versa, neither of which produces defensible inference.

Step 4

The mental model

Hold the following four-layer picture in your head. It is the entire architecture of classical statistics and every later lecture refers back to it.

Two things deserve emphasis. First, a random variable is not a "variable that is random" in the everyday sense, it is a deterministic function defined on the sample space. The randomness lives in $\Omega$, not in $X$. This sounds like pedantry until you try to make sense of conditional expectation or convergence in distribution, both of which only make sense under this framing.

Second, the distribution is the central object of the lecture, and almost everything that follows is a way of summarising it. The expectation is a one-number summary of location; the variance is a one-number summary of spread; the moments are a sequence of refinements; the moment-generating function is a complete summary (it uniquely determines the distribution where it exists); the characteristic function is the complete summary that always exists. Sampling distributions, addressed in section 5.7, are distributions of summary statistics computed from $n$ IID copies of $X$, and they are what lets us pull inferential statements out of the probability machinery.

Step 5

The detail

5.1 Foundations: outcomes, events, probability axioms

A random experiment is any procedure whose outcome cannot be predicted with certainty. The sample space $\Omega$ is the set of all possible outcomes; an event is a subset of $\Omega$. For a single coin toss $\Omega = \{H, T\}$ and the event "heads" is $\{H\}$. For the time of a default $\Omega = [0, \infty)$ and "default within one year" is $[0, 1]$.

A probability measure $P$ assigns to each event a number in $[0, 1]$ subject to three axioms (Kolmogorov, 1933):

$$\begin{aligned} &\text{(i) } P(A) \ge 0 \text{ for every event } A,\\ &\text{(ii) } P(\Omega) = 1,\\ &\text{(iii) } P(A_1 \cup A_2 \cup \cdots) = \sum_i P(A_i) \text{ for any sequence of disjoint events.} \end{aligned}$$

The third axiom (countable additivity) is the one that lets us pass from finite to infinite sample spaces and is what underwrites limit theorems. The collection of events on which $P$ is defined must form a $\sigma$-algebra: a collection closed under complements and countable unions. For finite $\Omega$ you can take the power set; for $\Omega = \mathbb{R}$ you cannot (there exist non-measurable sets) and the right choice is the Borel $\sigma$-algebra. This is the only fact from measure theory we will need.

Three interpretations of probability coexist. The classical interpretation (Laplace) assigns equal probability to symmetric outcomes: $P(\text{head}) = 1/2$ because the coin is symmetric. The frequentist interpretation defines probability as the long-run relative frequency in repeated trials: $P(\text{default in a year}) = \lim_{n \to \infty} (\text{defaults in } n \text{ years}) / n$. The subjective (Bayesian) interpretation defines probability as a coherent degree of belief, calibrated by betting behaviour. The Kolmogorov axioms are agnostic to the interpretation; they are the rules that any of the three must obey. Lecture 09 (Bayesian statistics) takes the subjective route seriously; lecture 03 (frequentist inference) takes the second.

5.2 Conditional probability and Bayes' theorem

The conditional probability of $A$ given $B$ is

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(B) > 0.$$

It re-scales the universe to the event $B$ and asks how much of the original mass on $A$ survives. Two events are independent if $P(A \cap B) = P(A) P(B)$, equivalently $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$. Independence is a substantive assumption, not a definition; two events are not independent simply because the analyst would prefer them to be.

The law of total probability says that if $\{B_1, \ldots, B_k\}$ is a partition of $\Omega$,

$$P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^k P(A \mid B_i) P(B_i).$$

Bayes' theorem rearranges the definition of conditional probability,

$$P(B_i \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B_i) P(B_i)}{P(A)} = \frac{P(A \mid B_i) P(B_i)}{\sum_j P(A \mid B_j) P(B_j)}.$$

Bayes is a one-line consequence of the definition; it earns its name from the way it is used. The denominator $P(A)$ is computed once and forgotten; the substantive content is that the posterior probability of $B_i$ given $A$ is proportional to the likelihood $P(A \mid B_i)$ times the prior $P(B_i)$. Lecture 09 will return to this as a complete inferential framework. For now it is just an arithmetic identity, but a famously useful one.

Base-rate fallacy The famous medical-screening example. A test for a rare disease has sensitivity 99 per cent (true positive rate) and specificity 99 per cent (true negative rate). The disease has prevalence 0.1 per cent. A patient tests positive. What is the probability they have the disease? Apply Bayes: $P(D \mid +) = (0.99)(0.001) / [(0.99)(0.001) + (0.01)(0.999)] \approx 9$ per cent. Most people, including most physicians, answer 99 per cent. The error is the failure to multiply by the base rate $P(D) = 0.001$. The same error appears in fraud detection, credit-default early warning, and any binary screening system applied to a base rate well below the test's false-positive rate.

5.3 Random variables: PMF, PDF, CDF

A random variable $X$ is a measurable function $X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$. "Measurable" means that for every Borel set $B \subseteq \mathbb{R}$, the preimage $X^{-1}(B) = \{\omega : X(\omega) \in B\}$ is an event. This guarantees that we can ask "what is $P(X \in B)$" for any reasonable $B$.

$X$ is discrete if it takes values in a countable set (the integers, the rationals in $[0, 1]$, the set of bond rating categories coded as 1 through 7). Its probability mass function (PMF) is

$$p_X(x) = P(X = x), \qquad \sum_x p_X(x) = 1.$$

$X$ is continuous if there is a non-negative function $f_X$ such that

$$P(X \in B) = \int_B f_X(x) \, dx \text{ for every Borel set } B,$$

called the probability density function (PDF). Note that $P(X = x) = 0$ for any specific $x$ in the continuous case; the density is not a probability but a probability per unit. A continuous $X$ has $f_X(x) \ge 0$ and $\int f_X = 1$.

The cumulative distribution function (CDF) is defined for both types as

$$F_X(x) = P(X \le x).$$

It is non-decreasing, right-continuous, with $\lim_{x \to -\infty} F = 0$ and $\lim_{x \to +\infty} F = 1$. The CDF is the most universal description of a distribution, because it is well-defined for every random variable (discrete, continuous, or mixed), and it is also the object that drives non-parametric estimation: the empirical CDF, $\hat{F}_n(x) = (1/n) \sum_i \mathbb{1}\{X_i \le x\}$, converges to $F$ uniformly by the Glivenko-Cantelli theorem.

The quantile function is the (generalised) inverse of the CDF, $F^{-1}_X(u) = \inf\{x : F_X(x) \ge u\}$ for $u \in (0, 1)$. The quantile function is the natural object for value-at-risk (VaR is the 99% or 99.5% quantile of the loss distribution), for the median (the 50% quantile), and for the inverse-CDF method of simulation (if $U \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)$ then $F^{-1}(U) \sim F$, a fact that powers most Monte Carlo schemes).

5.4 Expectation and variance

The expectation (mean, expected value) of $X$ is the long-run average, weighted by probability:

$$E[X] = \begin{cases} \sum_x x \, p_X(x) & \text{discrete}\\ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x \, f_X(x) \, dx & \text{continuous} \end{cases}$$

It is a property of the distribution, not of the sample, and exists only when the integral or sum is absolutely convergent (the Cauchy distribution is the textbook example of a distribution with no mean). Expectation is linear: $E[aX + bY] = aE[X] + bE[Y]$ for any random variables $X$, $Y$ and constants $a$, $b$, regardless of independence. This is the single most useful property in the subject.

The variance is the expected squared deviation from the mean:

$$\text{Var}(X) = E[(X - E[X])^2] = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2.$$

Its square root is the standard deviation $\sigma = \sqrt{\text{Var}(X)}$. For independent $X$, $Y$: $\text{Var}(aX + bY) = a^2 \text{Var}(X) + b^2 \text{Var}(Y)$. For general (possibly dependent) $X$, $Y$: $\text{Var}(X + Y) = \text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(Y) + 2 \text{Cov}(X, Y)$ where $\text{Cov}(X, Y) = E[(X - E[X])(Y - E[Y])]$. The covariance is the moment-generalisation of the descriptive correlation introduced in lecture 01.

Two inequalities deserve names. Markov's inequality: for non-negative $X$ and $a > 0$, $P(X \ge a) \le E[X]/a$. Chebyshev's inequality: $P(|X - \mu| \ge k\sigma) \le 1/k^2$. The latter is universal (no distributional assumption) but loose; for $k = 2$ it gives $P(|X - \mu| \ge 2\sigma) \le 0.25$, where the normal gives $\approx 0.046$. Chebyshev is the workhorse of the weak law of large numbers.

5.5 Joint, marginal, conditional distributions

For two random variables $X$, $Y$ defined on the same probability space, the joint distribution is described by the joint PMF $p_{X,Y}(x, y) = P(X = x, Y = y)$ (discrete) or joint PDF $f_{X,Y}(x, y)$ (continuous). The marginal distribution of $X$ is recovered by summing or integrating out $Y$:

$$p_X(x) = \sum_y p_{X,Y}(x, y), \qquad f_X(x) = \int f_{X,Y}(x, y) \, dy.$$

The conditional distribution of $X$ given $Y = y$ is

$$p_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y) = \frac{p_{X,Y}(x, y)}{p_Y(y)}, \qquad f_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y) = \frac{f_{X,Y}(x, y)}{f_Y(y)}.$$

$X$ and $Y$ are independent if and only if $p_{X,Y}(x, y) = p_X(x) p_Y(y)$ for all $(x, y)$. Equivalently, the conditional and marginal distributions coincide.

The conditional expectation $E[X \mid Y]$ is the random variable that equals $\int x \, f_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y) \, dx$ on the event $\{Y = y\}$. It is the best predictor of $X$ in mean-square sense given $Y$: among all functions $g(Y)$, $E[X \mid Y]$ minimises $E[(X - g(Y))^2]$. This single fact is the foundation of regression. The tower property $E[E[X \mid Y]] = E[X]$ and the conditional variance formula $\text{Var}(X) = E[\text{Var}(X \mid Y)] + \text{Var}(E[X \mid Y])$ are the two consequences that come up everywhere downstream (the second is the bias-variance decomposition in disguise).

A subtle distinction: zero correlation does not imply independence (a uniform $X$ on $[-1, 1]$ and $Y = X^2$ have zero covariance but are deterministically dependent). The converse does hold: independent variables have zero covariance. The exception that fixes the implication is the multivariate normal: for jointly normal variables, zero covariance does imply independence.

Three patterns are worth remembering. Sums of independent random variables stay in the same family for several distributions (sums of independent Poissons are Poisson, sums of independent normals are normal, sums of independent gammas with common scale are gamma). This is the stability property, derivable in each case from the moment-generating function. Limits of one family give another: a binomial $(n, p)$ with $np$ fixed and $n \to \infty$ converges to a Poisson($\lambda$); a Poisson($\lambda$) with $\lambda \to \infty$ is approximately normal. Mixtures of distributions are usually not in the same family: a Poisson with random $\lambda \sim \text{Gamma}$ is negative binomial, not Poisson; a normal with random variance $\sigma^2 \sim$ inverse-gamma is Student's t.

5.7 Moments, MGFs, characteristic functions

The $k$th raw moment of $X$ is $\mu_k' = E[X^k]$; the $k$th central moment is $\mu_k = E[(X - E[X])^k]$. The first raw moment is the mean; the second central moment is the variance. The third and fourth standardised central moments are the skewness $g_1 = \mu_3 / \sigma^3$ and the kurtosis $g_2 = \mu_4 / \sigma^4$, encountered in lecture 01.

The moment-generating function (MGF) is

$$M_X(t) = E[e^{tX}],$$

where the expectation is taken over all $t$ in a neighbourhood of zero where the integral converges. It generates moments by repeated differentiation, $\mu_k' = M_X^{(k)}(0)$, and is multiplicative for independent sums, $M_{X+Y}(t) = M_X(t) M_Y(t)$. The MGF, when it exists, uniquely determines the distribution; this is the easy proof of "the sum of independent normals is normal" and a dozen similar facts.

The MGF does not always exist (Cauchy, Pareto with heavy tails). The characteristic function,

$$\varphi_X(t) = E[e^{itX}],$$

where $i = \sqrt{-1}$, exists for every random variable and shares the multiplicative property and the uniqueness theorem. It is the right tool for proving the central limit theorem: the characteristic function of a normalised sum of IID variables converges to $e^{-t^2/2}$, the characteristic function of $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, by a Taylor expansion that is two lines long.

5.8 Limit theorems: LLN and CLT

Four modes of convergence are used in statistics, in order from strongest to weakest:

  1. Almost sure convergence: $X_n \to X$ almost surely if $P(\lim_n X_n = X) = 1$.
  2. Convergence in $L^p$: $X_n \to X$ in $L^p$ if $E[|X_n - X|^p] \to 0$. The $L^2$ case is mean-square convergence.
  3. Convergence in probability: $X_n \to X$ in probability if $P(|X_n - X| > \epsilon) \to 0$ for every $\epsilon > 0$.
  4. Convergence in distribution: $X_n \to X$ in distribution if $F_{X_n}(x) \to F_X(x)$ at every continuity point of $F_X$.

Almost-sure implies in-probability implies in-distribution; in-$L^p$ implies in-probability. The reverse implications generally fail. The terminology matters because the two big theorems use different modes.

Weak law of large numbers (WLLN). Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ be IID with finite mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$. Then the sample mean $\bar{X}_n = (1/n) \sum_{i=1}^n X_i$ converges to $\mu$ in probability:

$$P(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| > \epsilon) \to 0 \text{ as } n \to \infty.$$

The proof, with finite variance, is one line: by Chebyshev, $P(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| > \epsilon) \le \text{Var}(\bar{X}_n)/\epsilon^2 = \sigma^2/(n\epsilon^2) \to 0$.

Strong law of large numbers (SLLN). Same setup; the convergence is almost sure, not just in probability. Proof requires Kolmogorov's three-series theorem and is significantly harder, but the conclusion is stronger: with probability one, the sample mean tracks $\mu$ as $n$ grows; there is no exceptional sequence of $n$ on which it could fail.

Central limit theorem (CLT). Same setup. The standardised sample mean converges in distribution to a standard normal:

$$\sqrt{n} \cdot \frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma} \to \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \text{ in distribution as } n \to \infty.$$

The Lindeberg-Lévy version (IID, finite variance) is the textbook case; the Lindeberg-Feller version drops "identically distributed" and replaces it with a uniform-integrability condition on the variances. The proof via characteristic functions: $\varphi_{\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)/\sigma}(t) = [\varphi_{(X_1 - \mu)/\sigma}(t/\sqrt{n})]^n \to e^{-t^2/2}$ by Taylor expansion. The Berry-Esseen theorem quantifies the rate: $|F_n(x) - \Phi(x)| \le C \rho / (\sigma^3 \sqrt{n})$ where $\rho = E|X_1 - \mu|^3$, so the approximation improves at the parametric rate $1/\sqrt{n}$.

Slogan The LLN says the average becomes deterministic. The CLT says its leftover wobble is normal. The CLT does not say the original distribution becomes normal, it says the wobble of the average around its limit, scaled by $\sqrt{n}$, is normal. The original distribution can be wildly non-normal; the standardised sample mean is still asymptotically normal.

Three companions earn their keep. Slutsky's theorem: if $X_n \to X$ in distribution and $Y_n \to c$ in probability for a constant $c$, then $X_n + Y_n \to X + c$ in distribution, and similar for products and ratios. The continuous mapping theorem: if $X_n \to X$ in distribution and $g$ is continuous, then $g(X_n) \to g(X)$ in distribution. The delta method: if $\sqrt{n}(X_n - \theta) \to \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ in distribution and $g$ is differentiable at $\theta$, then $\sqrt{n}(g(X_n) - g(\theta)) \to \mathcal{N}(0, [g'(\theta)]^2 \sigma^2)$. Every standard-error formula in classical statistics is one of these three results, applied to a specific estimator.

5.9 Sampling distributions: the bridge to inference

The pieces assemble into the punchline of the lecture. Take $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ IID from a distribution with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$. The sample mean $\bar{X} = (1/n) \sum X_i$ is a function of the sample, so it is itself a random variable. By linearity, $E[\bar{X}] = \mu$ (the sample mean is unbiased). By independence and the variance formula, $\text{Var}(\bar{X}) = \sigma^2 / n$. The square root, $\text{SE}(\bar{X}) = \sigma / \sqrt{n}$, is the standard error of the sample mean: how much it would fluctuate from sample to sample.

By the CLT, for large $n$:

$$\bar{X} \approx \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2/n).$$

This is the fact that drives every classical confidence interval and z-test. When $\sigma$ is unknown and replaced by the sample standard deviation $s$, the exact distribution of $(\bar{X} - \mu) / (s / \sqrt{n})$ under normality is Student's t with $n - 1$ degrees of freedom; for large $n$ the t converges to the normal and the two are interchangeable.

The sample variance $s^2 = (n-1)^{-1} \sum (X_i - \bar{X})^2$ is also a random variable. Under normality, $(n-1) s^2 / \sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{n-1}$ exactly. The sample proportion in a Bernoulli sample is binomial / $n$ exactly and approximately normal by the CLT applied to a binomial. These three sampling distributions (normal for the mean, chi-square for the variance, Student's t for the standardised mean) plus the F distribution for the ratio of two chi-squares are the complete toolkit for normal-theory inference in lecture 03.

The deeper claims of pillar 2, treated again in lecture 03, are about how good a sample statistic is as an estimator of a population parameter. Unbiasedness: $E[\hat{\theta}] = \theta$. Consistency: $\hat{\theta} \to \theta$ in probability as $n \to \infty$. Efficiency: the variance of $\hat{\theta}$ attains the Cramér-Rao lower bound, $\text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) \ge 1/I(\theta)$ where $I(\theta) = -E[\partial^2 \log f(X; \theta)/\partial \theta^2]$ is the Fisher information. Maximum-likelihood estimators are asymptotically unbiased, consistent, and efficient under regularity conditions, which is why they dominate practice. The full proofs belong in lecture 03; the existence of the framework belongs here.

Step 6

Worked example: the CLT in action

To see the central limit theorem doing its work, draw samples of increasing size from an unmistakably non-normal distribution and watch the sampling distribution of the mean. The exponential distribution with rate $\lambda = 1$ is a good test case: it has mean 1, variance 1, and is right-skewed with infinite-looking tail. The CLT predicts that for IID exponentials $X_1, \ldots, X_n$, the standardised sample mean $\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - 1)$ becomes approximately standard normal as $n$ grows.

What this means for finance.

A daily return on a credit portfolio is far from normal: skewed, kurtotic, with discrete jumps. A monthly return is the sum of about 21 daily returns, and is therefore much closer to normal than the daily returns themselves, even though no one made it so. An annual portfolio P&L is the sum of 252 daily returns and is, for all practical purposes, normal in its centre (though not in its extreme tails, where the CLT's leftover error matters). This is why almost all classical risk and capital methodology, value-at-risk for normal-tailed exposures, Basel risk-weighted assets under Vasicek, the Merton model for distance to default, leans on normal approximations: the underlying daily phenomena are not normal, but the time-aggregated quantities are.

The caveat is exactly where the CLT fails. The CLT requires finite variance. When daily returns have power-law tails (as equity returns visibly do), the variance is either large or undefined, and the convergence is slow or absent. Levy-stable distributions and Pareto tails are the technical apparatus for that regime; lecture 06 on time series and any market-risk lecture will revisit it.

Step 7

Common pitfalls and cognitive traps

The law of small numbers and the gambler's fallacy

The law of large numbers is asymptotic: it promises the sample mean converges as $n \to \infty$. Most people apply it to $n = 10$. After three reds in a row on a fair roulette wheel, the wheel does not "owe" a black; the next spin is independent and still 50/50. The CLT version of the same error is expecting a sample of $n = 5$ to look normal because of the theorem.

The prosecutor's fallacy

Confusing $P(\text{evidence} \mid \text{innocent})$ with $P(\text{innocent} \mid \text{evidence})$. A DNA match has a probability of $10^{-6}$ under innocence; this is not the probability of innocence given the match. Without a prior on the suspect pool size, the two cannot be related. The base-rate fallacy of section 5.2 is the same error in medical screening. The fix is always to apply Bayes' theorem with the prior made explicit.

Confusing the distribution of the sample with the distribution of a statistic

"The data are not normal, so I cannot use a t-test" is the most common version. The t-test assumes the sampling distribution of the mean is approximately normal, not the data themselves. For $n \ge 30$ and not-too-pathological parent distributions, the CLT handles this automatically. For genuinely tiny samples or heavy-tailed parents, non-parametric or bootstrap inference is the answer, not abandoning the test.

Independence assumed where it is not present

Default events within the same industry are not independent; market returns on consecutive days are not independent (volatility clustering); insurance claims after a hurricane are not independent. Computing standard errors as $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ under dependence understates uncertainty, sometimes by an order of magnitude. The cure is to model the dependence (lectures 06, 08), to use cluster-robust standard errors, or to use a block bootstrap. Treating dependent data as IID is the single most common technical mistake in applied statistics.

Zero correlation interpreted as independence

The classical counterexamples ($X \sim$ Uniform$(-1, 1)$ and $Y = X^2$ have zero covariance but are dependent) appear in finance whenever a non-linear payoff is in play. Two options written on the same underlying can have zero correlation in their returns but are obviously dependent. Linear measures of association are not measures of dependence, except in the multivariate-normal case.

Why pillar 2 matters in regulated work Every standard error and confidence interval in a model development document is a pillar-2 statement. A bank that reports a 95% CI of $[a, b]$ on a PD calibration is implicitly invoking the CLT, the IID assumption, and either a parametric model or a bootstrap. Regulators will probe each assumption. The bank that has internalised the probability theory writes a single coherent paragraph; the bank that hasn't writes a recipe and gets challenged.
Step 8

Frontiers and what's beyond

Three directions sit just past the syllabus, in widening order of remove.

Heavy-tailed limit theorems. When the variance is infinite (Pareto with $\alpha \le 2$), the CLT fails and the right limit is the family of $\alpha$-stable distributions (Levy 1925). The empirical relevance is operational-risk tails, severe insurance claims, and extreme market moves. Extreme value theory (Fisher-Tippett, Gnedenko) gives the analogue of the CLT for sample maxima: under broad conditions, the standardised maximum converges to one of three families (Gumbel, Fréchet, Weibull). The generalised Pareto distribution is the corresponding excess-over-threshold limit and is the standard tool for value-at-risk above the 99% quantile.

Dependence and copulas. The marginals of $(X, Y)$ pin down their univariate behaviour but not their joint behaviour. Copulas (Sklar 1959) are the joint distributions of $(F_X(X), F_Y(Y))$, which are uniform on $[0, 1]^2$, and they decouple marginal from dependence. The Gaussian copula is the basis of the Vasicek single-factor model used in Basel A-IRB; its limitations were on global display during 2008. Modern credit-portfolio modelling uses Student-t, Clayton, and Gumbel copulas to capture tail dependence that the Gaussian misses.

Concentration inequalities. Chebyshev and Markov are the elementary tools. Modern statistics, machine learning theory, and high-dimensional inference rely on Bernstein, Hoeffding, McDiarmid, and Talagrand inequalities, which give exponential rather than polynomial tail bounds for sums and functions of independent variables. Hoeffding's inequality, in particular, is the workhorse of statistical learning theory and PAC-Bayes bounds. These are not on the F107 syllabus but underpin most modern theoretical justifications for ML methods.

Beyond IID: stochastic processes. The IID assumption is the simplest dependence structure (none). Once dropped, the natural objects are martingales, Markov chains (lecture 08), and continuous-time processes (Brownian motion, Lévy processes, jump-diffusions). Each has its own limit theory (the functional CLT / Donsker's theorem; the Doob martingale convergence theorem). Lectures 06, 07, 08 develop pieces of this, but the full theory is its own subject.

Step 9

Synthesis and what's next

You now have the probability machinery. A random experiment has a sample space; a random variable is a function on the sample space; a distribution is the recipe for how that function's values are spread; an expectation is the mean of the recipe; a variance is its squared spread; conditional distributions specialise the recipe to given information; the moment-generating and characteristic functions are complete summaries; and the law of large numbers plus the central limit theorem are the two universal facts about averages of independent copies. Twenty named distributions, with three families of relationships between them (sums-stay-in-family, limits-give-new-family, mixtures-give-new-family), cover almost every modelling problem you will meet.

The bridge to inference is built in section 5.9. Every sample statistic is a random variable. Its sampling distribution is a probability object computed from the parent distribution. The standard error is the square root of the variance of the sampling distribution. The CLT says the sampling distribution of the mean is approximately normal for $n$ not-too-small. The next lecture turns these facts into a confidence interval, a test statistic, and a p-value, and asks: given the sample, what can we conclude about the population?

Recall
  1. A test for a disease has 99 per cent sensitivity and 99 per cent specificity, and the disease has 0.1 per cent prevalence. State Bayes' theorem and compute the posterior probability of disease given a positive test.
  2. State the law of large numbers in one sentence and the central limit theorem in one sentence. What does each one promise about the sample mean, and which mode of convergence does each use?
  3. $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ are IID with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$. Give expressions for $E[\bar{X}]$, $\text{Var}(\bar{X})$, and the standard error of $\bar{X}$. Which one shrinks with $n$, and at what rate?
  4. Why does the moment-generating function uniquely determine a distribution, where it exists? Give an example of a distribution whose MGF does not exist.
  5. True or false: if $\text{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$ then $X$ and $Y$ are independent. Give a counterexample to the false claim.
