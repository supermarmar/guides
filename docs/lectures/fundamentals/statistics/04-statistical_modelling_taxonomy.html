Statistical modelling: a taxonomy

A map of the model zoo before any specific model is fitted. Three orthogonal axes (supervised vs unsupervised, parametric vs non-parametric, generative vs discriminative) plus two unifying frames (the bias-variance trade-off and the flexibility-interpretability frontier) tell you which corner of the zoo to look in for any given problem. The lecture ends with a decision flowchart calibrated to regulated credit-risk practice, which is the domain where this map is hardest to navigate.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Statistical modelling begins where inference ends. Pillar 3 asked "what is the parameter $\theta$ of the population?" Pillar 4 asks "what is the parameter vector $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ of a structural relationship $Y = f(X; \boldsymbol{\theta}) + \epsilon$?" The two questions use the same inferential machinery; the second supplies a richer object (a function from $X$ to $Y$) that lets you do prediction as well as inference.

Models can be classified along three independent axes. The learning paradigm axis splits them into supervised (a target $Y$ is observed and modelled), unsupervised (no target; structure is discovered), and reinforcement (an agent learns from rewards). The parametricity axis splits them into parametric (fixed-dimensional $\boldsymbol{\theta}$), non-parametric (infinite-dimensional $\boldsymbol{\theta}$), and semi-parametric (a mix). The conditional vs joint axis splits them into discriminative (model $p(Y \mid X)$) and generative (model the joint $p(X, Y)$). Almost every modelling decision is a choice among the eighteen cells of this cube. Two transverse principles, the bias-variance trade-off and the flexibility-interpretability frontier, govern the choice within any cell.

Feynman test A model is a rule that predicts $Y$ from $X$. The rule has parameters that you tune to data. Some rules are short and rigid (a line: two parameters), some are long and flexible (a deep neural network: millions). Short rules generalise well from little data, miss complex patterns, and are easy to explain. Long rules capture complex patterns, need a lot of data, and are hard to explain. The art of modelling is choosing the shortest rule that captures enough of the pattern, where "enough" is defined by the use case. Everything else, every algorithm, every tuning trick, every diagnostic, is a refinement of that one choice.
Step 2

Where this sits in the stats world

Pillar 4 (the bridge) This lecture sits at the bridge into pillar 4 of the stats world map. It is the framing chapter, not the working chapter. Pillar 3 (lecture 03) gave us inference about a single scalar parameter under IID sampling. Pillar 4 extends that to inference about a vector $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ inside a structural relationship $Y = f(X; \boldsymbol{\theta}) + \epsilon$. This lecture says nothing new about inference; the MLE, the score, the Wald and likelihood-ratio tests, and the bootstrap from lecture 03 all carry over unchanged. What it does is map out the universe of choices for $f$: which functional forms are reasonable, what the trade-offs are, and how to choose one. Lecture 05 (GLMs) then works through the most important slice of that universe in detail.

Two adjacencies clarify the lecture's role. To the left of pillar 4 sit lectures 1-3 (descriptive, probability, inference). Without them, modelling is alchemy: fitting curves with no idea what the residuals mean. To the right of pillar 4 sit lectures 6-8 (time series, survival, Markov), which are pillar-4 modelling under non-IID data: dependence indexed by time, by censoring, or by a discrete state. To the bottom sits lecture 09 (Bayesian), which re-derives pillar-4 modelling under a different inferential logic. This lecture is the hub all of those connect through.

A scoping note. The boundary between "classical statistical modelling" and "machine learning" is more a matter of culture than of mathematics. A logistic regression is a statistical model; a gradient-boosted tree ensemble is an ML model; both estimate $E[Y \mid X]$. The differences are in the priorities (interpretability, inferential validity, regulator-friendliness vs predictive accuracy), the volume of data assumed, and the conventions for reporting uncertainty. Breiman's "two cultures" essay (section 6) is the canonical articulation of this split, and it matters because regulated finance lives mostly on one side and academic ML lives mostly on the other.

Step 3

Why this matters

Three reasons to invest in a map of the model zoo before reaching for the nearest tool. First, choice of model class is the largest source of model performance, larger than features, larger than hyperparameters, larger than optimiser. A linear model fitted to a fundamentally non-linear relationship will underperform a kernel regression by a wide margin regardless of how carefully you select features. A neural network fitted to 200 observations will overfit no matter how cleverly you regularise. Getting the class right at the outset saves a quarter of all model-development time and a third of all model-validation failures.

Second, regulators care about interpretability and stability, and the model class is the level at which these are negotiated. EBA, PRA, and the BCBS principles for ML in IRB models, and the SR 11-7 framework in the US, all favour parametric, interpretable models for capital-relevant decisions, with non-parametric and ensemble methods restricted to challenger or auxiliary roles. The decision flowchart at the end of this lecture is calibrated to that regulatory reality: pillars 4 and 5 are an over-the-counter zoo; only some animals are kept on regulated premises.

Third, the modern data-science workflow is comparison across model classes, not within. The right answer to "what model should I use?" is rarely "the one I always use" and almost always "let me compare three on a hold-out set and pick the one that does the job at the cost I can afford". The vocabulary in this lecture (parametric vs non-parametric, generative vs discriminative, bias vs variance) is what makes that comparison principled rather than ad hoc.

Step 4

The mental model

Every model in this lecture, and every model in lectures 5 through 9, can be located inside the same three-axis cube. The cube has 18 cells (3 paradigms × 3 parametricities × 2 conditionalities), of which about a dozen are commonly populated.

Two transverse principles cut across all three axes. The bias-variance trade-off (section 5.4) says that total prediction error decomposes into squared bias, variance, and irreducible noise, and that flexibility (axis 2) trades bias for variance. The flexibility-interpretability frontier (section 5.5) says that the most flexible models are the least interpretable, with no escape route. Together these principles dictate that every modelling problem has an optimal flexibility level, set by the data volume and the use case, and that "use the most flexible model possible" is not a defensible default.

Step 5

The detail

5.1 Axis 1: supervised, unsupervised, reinforcement

Supervised learning. A target $Y$ is observed alongside the features $X$. The model is trained on $(X_i, Y_i)_{i=1}^n$ pairs and learns to predict $Y$ given $X$. Within supervised, the type of $Y$ splits the problem in two. Regression models a continuous $Y$ (loan loss given default, claim severity, default rate over a window). Classification models a categorical $Y$ (default vs not, rating bucket, fraud flag). The evaluation metrics differ accordingly (MSE, $R^2$, MAE for regression; accuracy, AUC, log-loss, F1 for classification), but the modelling apparatus is otherwise common.

Unsupervised learning. No target $Y$. The model is trained on $X_i$ alone and learns the structure of the predictor distribution. Three big sub-families. Clustering partitions the observations into groups of similar units (k-means, hierarchical, DBSCAN, Gaussian mixtures); used in credit risk for portfolio segmentation, customer typology, and fraud-ring detection. Dimensionality reduction projects high-dimensional $X$ into a lower-dimensional latent space (PCA, factor analysis, t-SNE, UMAP, autoencoders); used to compress correlated risk factors into a few principal components before downstream modelling. Density estimation and anomaly detection estimate $p(X)$ and flag low-density points as outliers (KDE, isolation forests, one-class SVM); the workhorse of operational-risk and AML monitoring.

Reinforcement learning. An agent interacts with an environment, takes actions, observes rewards, and updates its policy to maximise long-run cumulative reward. The training data are generated by the agent's own behaviour, not handed in advance. Two broad sub-families: value-based methods (Q-learning, deep Q-networks) estimate the value function $V(s)$ or action-value $Q(s, a)$; policy-based methods (REINFORCE, policy gradients, actor-critic) directly parameterise the policy $\pi(a \mid s)$. Reinforcement learning is dominant in robotics, game-playing, and online recommendation; it is creeping into algorithmic trading and credit-line management, where actions taken now affect the distribution of future observations. Out of scope for this course beyond this paragraph; mentioned for completeness.

A fourth paradigm, self-supervised learning, has emerged from the language-model literature. The target $Y$ is constructed from the data itself (predict the next word, predict a masked patch, predict whether two augmentations of the same image are similar). Self-supervision is technically supervised but operationally unsupervised in that it requires no human labels. It powers BERT, GPT, CLIP, and most modern foundation models. Not on the F107 syllabus but relevant to anyone using pre-trained models in credit risk text analysis.

5.2 Axis 2: parametric, semi-parametric, non-parametric

The formal distinction is about the dimension of the parameter set.

A parametric model assumes the data come from a family of distributions indexed by a finite-dimensional parameter $\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^p$, where $p$ is fixed and does not grow with $n$. Linear regression has $p$ slopes plus an intercept plus a noise variance; logistic regression has $p$ slopes plus an intercept; an exponential survival model has one rate parameter. Parametric models make strong assumptions (the functional form, the noise distribution) and reap two rewards: low variance (few parameters to estimate, so each is estimated precisely from any $n \gg p$) and interpretability (the parameters have direct meaning, with standard errors and confidence intervals from pillar 3). Their failure mode is bias: when the true relationship does not match the assumed functional form, no amount of data will fix it.

A non-parametric model leaves the functional form unspecified. The effective parameter space is infinite-dimensional. k-nearest-neighbours, kernel regression, decision trees, random forests, neural networks (when the depth and width grow with $n$), Gaussian processes, and splines without a fixed knot set are non-parametric in this sense. The reward is flexibility: any smooth enough function can be approximated. The cost is variance (many implicit parameters need data to pin down), data appetite (a non-parametric model trained on 100 observations is a memoriser, not a learner), and interpretability (no compact summary). Non-parametric models are the right answer when the functional form is unknown and the data volume is large.

A semi-parametric model is part parametric, part non-parametric. The canonical example is the Cox proportional hazards model from lecture 07: the hazard is $h(t \mid X) = h_0(t) \exp(X^\top \boldsymbol{\beta})$, where $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ is a finite-dimensional parameter of interest and $h_0(t)$ is an infinite-dimensional nuisance baseline hazard. The model estimates $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ efficiently without specifying $h_0$. Generalised additive models (GAMs), partial linear models, and the partially linear regression $Y = X_1^\top \boldsymbol{\beta} + g(X_2) + \epsilon$ are all semi-parametric. The promise is the best of both worlds: parametric interpretability for the components of interest, non-parametric flexibility for the nuisance.

5.3 Axis 3: discriminative vs generative

A discriminative model estimates only the conditional distribution $p(Y \mid X)$, the object directly needed for prediction. Logistic regression, all GLMs, support vector machines, decision trees, random forests, gradient boosting, and most neural networks are discriminative. The advantage is that you do not waste statistical effort modelling the (often complex) distribution of $X$. The cost is that you cannot directly do things that require $p(X)$ or $p(X, Y)$: generate new examples, impute missing predictors, detect distributional shift, or compute $p(X \mid Y)$ for class-conditional reasoning.

A generative model estimates the joint $p(X, Y)$, usually by factoring it as $p(X \mid Y) p(Y)$ (the class-prior $p(Y)$ times a class-conditional distribution of features). Naive Bayes, linear discriminant analysis (LDA), quadratic discriminant analysis (QDA), Gaussian mixture models, hidden Markov models, and the modern generative ML stack (variational autoencoders, normalising flows, diffusion models, large language models) are generative. The advantages are several: you can sample from the model, impute missing data, detect anomalies, and combine information from labelled and unlabelled examples (semi-supervised learning). The cost is statistical: modelling $p(X)$ is harder than modelling $p(Y \mid X)$, and a misspecified generative model produces a worse classifier than a well-specified discriminative one (this is the central argument of Ng and Jordan 2002).

A useful rule of thumb (Vapnik 1998, often paraphrased): "When solving a problem of interest, do not solve a more general problem as an intermediate step." For pure prediction, discriminative dominates; for the auxiliary uses above, generative is necessary.

5.4 The bias-variance decomposition

For a regression problem with target $Y$ and prediction $\hat{f}(X)$ trained on a random sample, the expected mean squared prediction error at a new point $x_0$ decomposes as

$$E[(Y - \hat{f}(x_0))^2] = \underbrace{[\text{Bias}(\hat{f}(x_0))]^2}_{\text{systematic error}} + \underbrace{\text{Var}(\hat{f}(x_0))}_{\text{sample-to-sample wobble}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible noise}}.$$

The expectation is over the random training sample and the noise in the new observation. Bias measures how far the average prediction is from the truth; variance measures how much the prediction fluctuates across training samples; the irreducible noise is the part of $Y$ that no model can predict.

The trade-off arises because model flexibility changes bias and variance in opposite directions. A constant model (predict the sample mean for every $x$) has high bias and zero variance. A nearest-neighbours model with $k = 1$ has near-zero bias (it interpolates the training data) and very high variance (a different training sample would give a wildly different fit). The optimal flexibility minimises the sum.

Several modelling techniques can be understood as moves along the bias-variance axis. Regularisation (ridge, lasso, elastic net) introduces controlled bias to reduce variance. Ensemble methods (bagging, random forests) reduce variance by averaging many high-variance models. Boosting (gradient boosting, XGBoost) sequentially reduces bias by fitting to residuals. Cross-validation is the estimation procedure for the bias-variance trade-off; the flexibility level that minimises CV error is, in expectation, the one that minimises total error.

5.5 The flexibility-interpretability frontier

A second axis runs roughly parallel to flexibility: the harder a model is to summarise in human-readable terms, the more flexible it tends to be. The frontier is not strictly monotone (a deep GAM can be both flexible and interpretable for additive effects) but the rough ordering holds.

The frontier is not a law; it is a default. Two recent developments push back. Post-hoc interpretability tools (SHAP, LIME, integrated gradients, attention maps) supply per-prediction explanations for any black-box model. They do not make the model itself interpretable, but they make individual decisions auditable, which is enough for many regulatory regimes. Inherently interpretable models that compete on accuracy (explainable boosting machines, neural additive models, monotonic gradient boosting) are an active research area; Rudin (2019) "Stop explaining black box models for high-stakes decisions" argues that for regulated settings the right answer is always an inherently interpretable model, not a post-hoc explanation.

5.7 Breiman's two cultures

Leo Breiman's 2001 essay "Statistical Modeling: The Two Cultures" is the canonical articulation of a split that every working analyst feels but rarely names. Two cultures co-exist within data analysis:

The data-modelling culture (about 98 per cent of academic statistics, per Breiman's estimate at the time) starts from a probabilistic model: $Y = f(X; \boldsymbol{\theta}) + \epsilon$ with $\epsilon$ from a known distribution. The model is validated by goodness-of-fit tests, by interpretability of $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, and by the inferential apparatus of pillar 3. The aim is to understand the data-generating process; prediction is a byproduct of understanding.

The algorithmic-modelling culture (the machine-learning tradition) starts from an algorithm that maps $X$ to $\hat{Y}$, treats the relationship as a black box, and validates by hold-out prediction error. The aim is accurate prediction; understanding the data-generating process is unnecessary if the predictions are correct.

Breiman's polemical point was that the algorithmic culture had produced better predictive performance across most applied domains and that statistics was missing the wave by clinging to the data-modelling tradition. Twenty-five years later the picture is more nuanced. Algorithmic methods dominate in domains where prediction accuracy is the sole objective (machine translation, image recognition, recommendation). Data modelling dominates where inferential validity, regulatory acceptance, or scientific understanding matter (medical efficacy trials, econometric policy analysis, regulated credit risk capital). The mature view treats them as complementary tools with different objectives, not rival worldviews.

Modern reconciliation runs along two tracks. Post-hoc interpretability (SHAP, LIME, partial dependence plots) lets algorithmic models be interrogated as if they were parametric. Inherently interpretable algorithmic methods (rule lists, monotonic GBMs, EBMs) try to deliver algorithmic flexibility with parametric clarity. Causal machine learning (double machine learning, causal forests) plugs algorithmic models into a causal-inference framework to recover interpretable treatment effects from black-box predictions. The split that Breiman named in 2001 is still real; the bridges across it are now the active frontier.

Step 7

Common pitfalls and cognitive traps

"Use the most flexible model possible" is not a default

Random forests and gradient boosting are powerful and easy to fit. They are also the wrong choice for small samples ($n \lesssim 1000$), for problems requiring inferential validity (confidence intervals on coefficients, hypothesis tests on treatment effects), for problems where the regulator wants to see the decision rule, and for problems where the data-generating process is approximately parametric. Reaching for them by default produces overfitted, unstable, uninterpretable models. The right default is the simplest model that solves the problem; algorithmic flexibility is the response to evidence that the simple model does not.

Confusing prediction with inference

A model can predict well and still have meaningless coefficients (random forests). A model can have well-identified coefficients and predict badly out of sample (an under-fit linear regression). The two virtues are correlated but not identical, and the model class determines which is the natural output. Always ask "is the deliverable a prediction or a parameter estimate?" before choosing the class.

Mistaking interpretation for explanation

A logistic regression coefficient is interpretable but not causal. The coefficient on age in a default model is a partial correlation, not a causal effect. To make a causal claim about age, you need a causal-inference framework (randomisation, instrumental variables, regression discontinuity), not just a more interpretable model. Conflating the two is the standard error in early-career applied analysis.

Ignoring data volume

The bias-variance optimum moves rightward with $n$: more data tolerates more flexibility. A model class that is the right default at $n = 50{,}000$ is wrong at $n = 500$ (overfits) and wrong at $n = 50{,}000{,}000$ (underfits). The first question when choosing a model class is "how much data do I have?", not "what is the state of the art?".

Treating axis 3 as a presentation choice

Discriminative vs generative is a substantive modelling decision, not a packaging decision. The generative LDA and the discriminative logistic regression have different bias-variance trade-offs and asymptotic error rates (Ng and Jordan 2002): LDA dominates when the generative assumptions hold and $n$ is small; logistic regression dominates when assumptions fail or $n$ is large. The right choice depends on the assumptions and the data volume, not on which is more familiar.

A worked-example caution A common pattern in industry: a team fits an XGBoost model, achieves 0.85 AUC on a hold-out, declares victory, and submits for model risk review. Six months later the model is rejected because (a) the bank cannot articulate why borrower X was declined ("the model says so" is not a defensible answer in adverse-action notices), (b) the model's predictions shift unstably when a single feature is perturbed, and (c) the AUC is not reproducible on subsequent data because of unaccounted-for drift. Every one of these failures was predictable from the model-class choice. A logistic regression with carefully selected, monotonically constrained features would have produced an AUC of 0.83 and survived review on the first submission.
Step 8

Frontiers and what's beyond

Three modelling frontiers sit just past the syllabus.

Causal machine learning. Classical statistical modelling identifies associations; classical causal inference identifies treatment effects under strong assumptions (randomisation, ignorability). Modern causal ML (Chernozhukov et al.'s double machine learning, Athey-Wager's causal forests, Hill's Bayesian additive regression trees for treatment effects) combines the prediction power of algorithmic methods with the inferential apparatus of causal inference. The result is uncertainty-quantified causal effect estimates for high-dimensional confounders. The credit-risk relevance is direct: the effect of a credit-line increase on default behaviour, of a forbearance policy on cure rates, of a pricing change on take-up. These are causal questions that pure prediction cannot answer.

Distribution-free uncertainty quantification. Conformal prediction (Vovk, Shafer, Wasserman) supplies prediction intervals around any black-box model with guaranteed marginal coverage at level $1 - \alpha$, with no distributional assumption. Conformalised quantile regression extends this to heterogeneous prediction intervals. The result is the modern answer to "what is the uncertainty around this prediction?" for non-parametric and black-box models, where classical pillar-3 inference does not apply. Already in use in clinical risk scores and weather forecasting; emerging in credit scoring.

Foundation models and transfer. Large pre-trained models (LLMs, vision transformers, multimodal foundation models) blur the supervised / unsupervised distinction: they are pre-trained on huge unlabelled corpora using self-supervised objectives, then fine-tuned on small labelled tasks. The implications for credit risk are speculative but real: text-based risk analysis (annual reports, news), unstructured-document automation, and zero-shot classification of new product categories.

Interpretable ML as a research field. Rudin (2019), Lakkaraju (2020), and others argue that for high-stakes decisions, post-hoc interpretation of black boxes is a placeholder, not a solution, and that the field should develop inherently interpretable models that compete with black boxes on accuracy. EBMs, neural additive models, and risk-score generators (RiskSLIM, FasterRisk) are working examples. This is the active frontier where statistical modelling and machine learning will increasingly converge for regulated finance.

Step 9

Synthesis and what's next

You now have the map of the model zoo. Three axes (learning paradigm, parametricity, generative vs discriminative) classify every model in the course. Two transverse principles (bias-variance and flexibility-interpretability) govern the trade-offs within any cell. A decision flowchart calibrated to credit-risk practice maps a problem to a model class. Breiman's two cultures frame the standing tension between interpretability-first statistics and accuracy-first machine learning; the modern reconciliation runs through post-hoc interpretation, inherently interpretable algorithmic methods, and causal ML.

The course now turns to the cell that matters most for regulated practice: supervised, parametric, discriminative. That is the home of the generalised linear model, the workhorse of credit risk and the subject of the next lecture. The exponential-family framing in lecture 05 will unify OLS, logistic regression, Poisson regression, gamma regression, Tweedie regression, and beta regression as a single parametric template with different choices of distribution and link. The inferential apparatus from lecture 03 (MLE, Wald and likelihood-ratio tests, confidence intervals) carries over unchanged. The result is the model class that fits more financial data than any other and underwrites more regulated capital than any other.
