Lectures / Statistics

Inferential statistics

From a sample of n observations to a defensible claim about the population that produced them. Two halves, both built on the sampling distributions of the previous lecture: estimation answers "what is the population parameter, and how uncertain are we about it?", testing answers "is the data compatible with this specific claim about the parameter?". By the end of this lecture every confidence interval, every p-value, and every standard error in finance and credit risk should read as a single coherent statement, not a recipe.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Inferential statistics is the discipline of using a sample to make probabilistic claims about an unobserved population. It has two halves. Estimation produces a point estimate of the parameter plus an interval reflecting our uncertainty; testing decides whether the data are compatible with a specific value of the parameter. The two halves are duals: a 95 per cent confidence interval is precisely the set of null values that a level-5 per cent test would not reject. Both rest on the sampling distribution of the estimator, a probability object from lecture 02. There is no separate inferential machinery.

Around the two halves sit five companion ideas that every working analyst should hold. Maximum likelihood is the dominant estimation method because, under mild conditions, its estimators are asymptotically unbiased, consistent, and efficient. The bootstrap is the dominant interval-estimation method when analytical sampling distributions are unavailable or unreliable. The Neyman-Pearson lemma is the result that justifies the form of every classical test. p-values are widely misunderstood; the modern (ASA 2016) statement clarifies what they are not. Multiple testing turns reasonable individual tests into garbage in aggregate, and the corrections that fix this (Bonferroni, Benjamini-Hochberg) are non-optional in any practice that performs more than a few tests on the same dataset.

Feynman test An estimate is a guess at a number you cannot directly observe. A confidence interval is a region of guesses, one of which is the true value, with a quantified rate of being right. A hypothesis test is a yes/no question about a specific guess, answered by checking whether your data fall in a pre-declared rejection region. A p-value is the probability of seeing data this extreme if the hypothesis were true, and only that. None of these are statements about the truth of the hypothesis, just about the compatibility of the data with it.
Step 2

Where this sits in the stats world

Pillar 3 This is pillar 3 of the stats world map. Pillar 1 (descriptive) gave us sample summaries. Pillar 2 (probability) gave each summary a sampling distribution. Pillar 3 turns the sampling distribution into a probabilistic claim about the population: an interval estimate, a p-value, a power calculation. The pillar covers both estimation theory (what is the parameter, and how precise is the estimate) and hypothesis testing (is this specific value of the parameter compatible with the data). All of it assumes IID sampling from a fixed population and a known or estimable sampling distribution.

The dependencies are clean. From lecture 02 we inherit the sampling distribution of any reasonable statistic. From lecture 01 we inherit the choice of statistic (the sample mean, the sample variance, the sample correlation). Pillar 3 turns these into inference. The four immediate consumers are: lecture 04 (modelling taxonomy), which classifies models by the inferential question they answer; lecture 05 (GLMs), where every standard error and Wald test is a pillar-3 result applied to maximum-likelihood estimates of $\beta$; lectures 06-08 (time series, survival, Markov), which extend pillar-3 inference to non-IID data; lecture 09 (Bayesian), which replaces the entire frequentist inferential logic with a different one, but inherits the same likelihood objects.

One scoping note. Classical pillar-3 inference is frequentist: it conditions on the parameter being fixed and asks what would happen if the experiment were repeated. Lecture 09 reverses this, conditioning on the data and treating the parameter as random. The two paradigms answer different questions and use the same likelihoods to do so. For this lecture, "inference" means the frequentist version.

Step 3

Why this matters

Inferential statistics is the part of the course where the model meets the auditor. A bank's internal-ratings-based model rests on the claim that a borrower with score 720 has a one-year default probability of 1.2 per cent, plus or minus some interval. The plus-or-minus is an estimation statement; the question "is the realised default rate consistent with the model?" is a testing statement. Every model validation document, every back-testing exercise, every regulator challenge, comes down to pillar-3 mechanics. Getting the framework right is the difference between a paragraph that withstands scrutiny and a paragraph that does not.

Three concrete reasons to invest. First, the choice of inferential approach changes the answer. A 95 per cent confidence interval for a default probability computed by the normal approximation will be different from the same interval computed by Wilson's score method, the exact Clopper-Pearson method, or the bootstrap. For a low-default portfolio (small $p$, small $n$), the differences are not academic. The right choice depends on the sample size and the parameter range, and a working analyst should know which to use when.

Second, the replication crisis (medical research, social science, parts of machine learning) is a pillar-3 crisis. Studies that report $p < 0.05$ do not all replicate. The reasons are well-understood: selection effects, multiple testing without correction, insufficient power, and the widespread use of $p$-values without effect sizes. Credit risk modelling can fall into the same trap when many candidate predictors are screened without correction; a tighter understanding of the multiple-testing apparatus is the cure.

Third, classical inference and modern machine learning interact in ways that an analyst trained only in the former or only in the latter mishandles. A logistic-regression coefficient $\hat{\beta}$ has a standard error, a t-statistic, and a confidence interval; a gradient-boosted equivalent does not, by default. The bootstrap and conformal prediction are the modern tools that re-introduce uncertainty quantification to algorithmic models, and they live entirely inside pillar 3. The user who has internalised this lecture moves smoothly between the two cultures; the user who hasn't picks one and defends it badly.

Step 4

The mental model

The diagram below is the architecture of classical inference. It has two parallel tracks (estimation and testing) that meet at the same probabilistic object: the sampling distribution of the estimator. Every result in the lecture can be located somewhere on this diagram.

One slogan: a confidence interval is an inverted test, and a test is a confidence interval evaluated at one point. If a 95 per cent CI for $\theta$ is $[a, b]$, then a level-5 per cent test of $H_0 : \theta = \theta_0$ rejects exactly when $\theta_0 \notin [a, b]$. The two outputs are reformulations of the same probabilistic statement. Knowing this saves you from learning two parallel theories.

A second slogan: the parameter is fixed; the estimator is random. In the frequentist worldview, the population parameter $\theta$ is an unknown constant. The estimator $\hat{\theta}(X)$ is a function of the random sample, and is therefore random. The 95 per cent in "95 per cent confidence interval" refers to the long-run coverage rate of the procedure if applied to many samples, not to a probability that $\theta$ lies in this particular interval. The latter probability is zero or one (the interval either contains $\theta$ or it does not, and which we don't know). Lecture 09 inverts this convention; this lecture lives entirely inside the classical one.

Step 5

The detail

5.1 Point estimation: estimators, bias, variance, MSE

An estimator of a parameter $\theta$ is any function $\hat{\theta} = \hat{\theta}(X_1, \ldots, X_n)$ of the sample. The sample mean is an estimator of the population mean; the sample proportion is an estimator of the population proportion; the maximum-likelihood estimator (MLE) is an estimator of whatever the likelihood is indexed by. Estimators are random variables (lecture 02); they have a distribution, an expectation, and a variance.

Three properties any candidate estimator is judged on:

  • Bias. $\text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = E[\hat{\theta}] - \theta$. An estimator is unbiased if its expectation equals the parameter for every $\theta$. The sample mean is unbiased for the population mean; the sample variance with $n - 1$ denominator (Bessel correction, lecture 01) is unbiased for the population variance. The MLE is generally biased in finite samples; the bias goes to zero as $n \to \infty$.
  • Variance. $\text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) = E[(\hat{\theta} - E[\hat{\theta}])^2]$. Smaller is better, holding bias constant. Standard error is $\sqrt{\text{Var}(\hat{\theta})}$.
  • Mean squared error. $\text{MSE}(\hat{\theta}) = E[(\hat{\theta} - \theta)^2] = \text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) + [\text{Bias}(\hat{\theta})]^2$. The fundamental decomposition: total error is variance plus squared bias. Estimators that trade a little bias for a lot of variance reduction (ridge regression, Stein estimators) can dominate unbiased estimators in MSE.

Two asymptotic notions complete the toolkit. Consistency: $\hat{\theta}_n \to \theta$ in probability as $n \to \infty$. Almost any reasonable estimator is consistent; consistency is the floor, not the ceiling. Efficiency: the estimator attains the Cramér-Rao lower bound, $\text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) \ge 1 / [n \cdot I(\theta)]$, where $I(\theta) = -E[\partial^2 \log f(X; \theta)/\partial \theta^2]$ is the Fisher information per observation. An estimator that is unbiased and attains the bound is the best unbiased estimator (UMVUE) in that model. The MLE is asymptotically efficient under regularity conditions, which is the deepest reason it dominates practice.

5.2 Methods of estimation

Four standard methods cover almost every modelling problem. They are sometimes equivalent and sometimes not.

Method of moments (MoM). Set sample moments equal to population moments and solve. To estimate the mean of an exponential, set $\bar{X} = 1/\hat{\lambda}$ and solve for $\hat{\lambda} = 1/\bar{X}$. MoM is fast, consistent under mild conditions, and often the right starting point. It is rarely efficient when alternatives exist.

Maximum likelihood (MLE). Choose $\hat{\theta}$ to maximise the likelihood function $L(\theta; X) = \prod_{i=1}^n f(X_i; \theta)$, or equivalently the log-likelihood $\ell(\theta; X) = \sum \log f(X_i; \theta)$. The estimator solves the score equation $\partial \ell / \partial \theta = 0$. MLE has three optimality properties for large $n$: consistency, asymptotic normality with $\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta} - \theta) \to \mathcal{N}(0, 1/I(\theta))$, and efficiency. It is the dominant method in classical statistics and the engine of every GLM in lecture 05.

Least squares (LS / OLS). Choose $\hat{\theta}$ to minimise $\sum (Y_i - g(X_i; \theta))^2$. For a linear model with normal errors, OLS coincides with MLE; in general it does not. Least squares makes no distributional assumption and gives unbiased estimators in linear models, but its standard errors require an assumption (homoscedasticity, normality) to be valid analytically. The bootstrap (section 5.4) supplies the missing standard errors when the assumptions fail.

M-estimators. Generalisations of MLE and LS that minimise $\sum \rho(X_i; \theta)$ for some loss function $\rho$. Includes Huber's robust regression ($\rho$ quadratic near zero, linear in the tails) and quantile regression ($\rho$ asymmetric absolute-value). M-estimators are the bridge from classical estimation to modern robust statistics, and they are useful whenever the data contain outliers that MLE/OLS would chase.

5.3 Interval estimation: confidence intervals

A confidence interval for $\theta$ at level $1 - \alpha$ is a random interval $[L(X), U(X)]$ such that $P(L(X) \le \theta \le U(X)) \ge 1 - \alpha$ for every $\theta$. The probability is computed over repeated samples (the frequentist convention), with $\theta$ fixed. The standard reading: "if I repeated this procedure many times on fresh data, $(1 - \alpha) \times 100$ per cent of the resulting intervals would contain the true $\theta$." It is not the probability that the specific interval in front of you contains $\theta$.

Four standard methods of construction, in roughly increasing order of robustness:

  • Wald interval. $\hat{\theta} \pm z_{1 - \alpha/2} \cdot \widehat{\text{SE}}(\hat{\theta})$, justified by asymptotic normality of the estimator. The 95 per cent version uses $z = 1.96$. Fast, ubiquitous, and prone to undercoverage when $n$ is small or $\hat{\theta}$ is near a boundary (proportions near 0 or 1, variance components, hazard ratios).
  • Score interval. Invert the score test (Rao). For a proportion this is the Wilson score interval, $[\hat{p} + z^2/(2n) \pm z\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n + z^2/(4n^2)}] / (1 + z^2/n)$. It has much better coverage than the Wald interval for $\hat{p}$ near 0 or 1, and is the right default for default-rate intervals in low-default portfolios.
  • Likelihood-ratio interval. The set of $\theta_0$ for which $-2[\ell(\theta_0) - \ell(\hat{\theta})] \le \chi^2_{1, 1-\alpha}$. By Wilks's theorem the LR statistic is asymptotically $\chi^2$, and the interval is invariant under reparameterisation, which the Wald interval is not.
  • Bootstrap interval. Resample, recompute $\hat{\theta}$, build a sampling distribution empirically (section 5.4).

For specific parameters there are exact intervals available. The Clopper-Pearson interval for a binomial proportion uses the exact binomial distribution and is guaranteed to attain at least $1 - \alpha$ coverage (it is often conservative). The exact Poisson interval uses the chi-square representation $2\lambda \hat{n} \sim \chi^2$. For normal-data inference the Student's t and $\chi^2$ pivots give exact intervals on the mean and variance respectively.

5.4 The bootstrap

The bootstrap (Efron 1979) is the practical answer to the question "what is the sampling distribution of $\hat{\theta}$ when I cannot compute it analytically?" The procedure is mechanical. Treat the sample $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ as the population. Draw $B$ resamples of size $n$ with replacement; for each resample $b$ compute $\hat{\theta}^{(b)}$. The collection $\{\hat{\theta}^{(b)} : b = 1, \ldots, B\}$ approximates the sampling distribution of $\hat{\theta}$.

Three flavours of bootstrap CI from the resampled distribution:

  • Percentile. Take the $\alpha/2$ and $1 - \alpha/2$ quantiles of $\{\hat{\theta}^{(b)}\}$. Simplest; valid asymptotically when the sampling distribution is symmetric.
  • Basic (pivot). $[2\hat{\theta} - \hat{\theta}^*_{1-\alpha/2}, 2\hat{\theta} - \hat{\theta}^*_{\alpha/2}]$, using a pivot argument; better when the sampling distribution is skewed.
  • BCa (bias-corrected and accelerated). Corrects for bias and skewness; the gold-standard bootstrap interval.

The parametric bootstrap resamples from a fitted parametric model rather than the empirical distribution; useful when the analyst has a model in mind but wants finite-sample inference. Two important warnings. The bootstrap fails for non-smooth functionals (sample max, for instance) where standard subsampling or the m-out-of-n bootstrap is required. The bootstrap also fails to capture dependence; for time series the block bootstrap (resample blocks of length $\ell$ rather than single observations) preserves short-range dependence at the cost of breaking the longer-range structure.

Why the bootstrap is everywhere now Modern machine-learning models (random forests, gradient boosting, neural networks) typically have no analytical sampling distribution for their parameters. The bootstrap supplies confidence intervals and prediction intervals where formula-based inference is impossible. In regulated practice this is the bridge that lets ML models survive a model-risk review: the predictions are non-parametric but their uncertainty can be quantified via the bootstrap.

5.5 Hypothesis testing: the Neyman-Pearson framework

A hypothesis test is a decision procedure between two complementary claims about $\theta$. The null hypothesis $H_0 : \theta \in \Theta_0$ is the claim being tested; the alternative $H_1 : \theta \in \Theta_1$ is the complement (or a subset of it). The test produces a verdict "reject $H_0$" or "fail to reject $H_0$" based on a test statistic $T(X)$ computed from the sample.

Two errors are possible:

$H_0$ true$H_0$ false
Fail to rejectCorrect (prob $1 - \alpha$)Type II error (prob $\beta$)
RejectType I error (prob $\alpha$)Correct (prob $1 - \beta$ = power)

The significance level $\alpha$ is the type-I error probability, fixed by the analyst before seeing the data (conventionally 0.05 or 0.01). The power $1 - \beta$ is the probability of correctly rejecting $H_0$ when it is false; it depends on the true parameter value, the sample size, and the test. A test is a decision rule: declare a rejection region $R \subset \mathbb{R}$ and reject $H_0$ iff $T(X) \in R$. The level constraint pins $P_{\theta \in \Theta_0}(T \in R) \le \alpha$.

The Neyman-Pearson lemma states that for testing a simple null against a simple alternative, the likelihood-ratio test, which rejects when $L(X; \theta_1)/L(X; \theta_0) > k$ for some threshold $k$, is uniformly most powerful (UMP) at any level $\alpha$. For composite hypotheses, UMP tests do not generally exist, but the likelihood ratio statistic remains the workhorse: Wilks's theorem says $-2[\ell(\theta_0) - \ell(\hat{\theta})] \to \chi^2_q$ in distribution under $H_0$, where $q$ is the dimension of the parameter restriction.

The p-value is the probability, under $H_0$, of observing a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed: $p = P_{H_0}(T \ge T_{\text{obs}})$. By construction, $p \le \alpha$ iff $T_{\text{obs}}$ falls in the level-$\alpha$ rejection region. The p-value is therefore an alternative reporting convention: report $p$ and the reader picks $\alpha$. The American Statistical Association's 2016 statement on p-values makes six clarifications, of which three are essential to remember:

  • A p-value is not the probability that $H_0$ is true.
  • A p-value is not the probability that the observed effect is due to chance.
  • "$p < 0.05$" is not a finding of scientific importance, nor "$p \ge 0.05$" a finding of no effect; both depend on power and effect size.

The p-value is the conditional probability $P(\text{data this extreme} \mid H_0)$, and the prosecutor's-fallacy warning from lecture 02 applies in full force here.

Six specific tests are worth a slower look because they account for most of the day-to-day usage.

One-sample t-test. Tests $H_0 : \mu = \mu_0$ against $H_1 : \mu \ne \mu_0$ from a normal sample. Statistic: $T = (\bar{X} - \mu_0) / (s / \sqrt{n})$. Under $H_0$, $T \sim t_{n-1}$. Robust to mild non-normality for $n \ge 30$ by the CLT.

Welch's two-sample t-test. Tests equality of two means with possibly unequal variances. Preferred to the equal-variances version essentially always: Welch is robust when variances are equal (no power loss), Student is not robust when they differ.

One-way ANOVA. Tests $H_0 : \mu_1 = \ldots = \mu_k$. Statistic: F = (between-group MS)/(within-group MS), distributed as $F_{k-1, n-k}$ under $H_0$ with normality and equal variances. Rejection without post hoc correction tells you "the groups are not all equal" but not which differ; the Tukey HSD test handles all pairwise comparisons while controlling family-wise type-I error.

Pearson chi-square of independence. Tests whether two categorical variables are independent. Statistic: $\chi^2 = \sum (O_{ij} - E_{ij})^2 / E_{ij}$, distributed as $\chi^2_{(r-1)(c-1)}$ under $H_0$. Requires expected cell counts $\ge 5$ in at least 80 per cent of cells; otherwise use Fisher's exact test.

Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. One-sample form tests whether a sample comes from a specified continuous distribution. Two-sample form tests whether two samples come from the same continuous distribution. Statistic: $D = \sup_x |F_n(x) - F(x)|$. Distribution-free and quite general; less powerful than Shapiro-Wilk for testing normality specifically.

Likelihood ratio test. Tests $H_0 : \theta \in \Theta_0 \subset \Theta_1 : \theta \in \Theta$ via $\Lambda = -2 \log(L_{H_0}/L_{H_1}) \to \chi^2_q$ in distribution where $q = \dim(\Theta) - \dim(\Theta_0)$. The default tool for comparing nested models in GLM (lecture 05) and survival analysis (lecture 07).

5.7 Multiple testing and effect sizes

A single test at level $\alpha = 0.05$ has 5 per cent false-positive probability. Twenty independent tests at the same level produce, on average, one false positive even when every null is true. With 1,000 tests, you can expect 50 false positives. This is the multiple-testing problem, and it is the reason rote application of $\alpha = 0.05$ across many variables produces irreproducible results.

Two control regimes:

  • Family-wise error rate (FWER). $\text{FWER} = P(\text{at least one false rejection})$. The Bonferroni correction tests each hypothesis at level $\alpha / m$ for $m$ hypotheses; it controls FWER at $\alpha$ but is very conservative. The Holm-Bonferroni step-down procedure dominates Bonferroni at no extra cost: sort p-values in increasing order $p_{(1)} \le \cdots \le p_{(m)}$ and reject $H_{(i)}$ iff $p_{(j)} \le \alpha/(m - j + 1)$ for every $j \le i$.
  • False discovery rate (FDR). $\text{FDR} = E[\text{false discoveries} / \text{total discoveries}]$. The Benjamini-Hochberg (1995) procedure: reject $H_{(i)}$ iff $p_{(i)} \le i\alpha/m$ for the largest such $i$. Controls FDR at $\alpha$ under independent or positively dependent tests. Less conservative than FWER methods and the right choice for screening: large numbers of candidate variables where false positives are acceptable as long as their proportion is bounded.

Effect sizes complete the picture. A p-value can be made arbitrarily small by collecting enough data; it says nothing about whether the effect is large enough to matter. Cohen's d = $(\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2) / s_{\text{pooled}}$ is the standardised mean difference. Conventions: d = 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large. $\eta^2$ (eta-squared) is the ANOVA effect-size analogue, the fraction of variance explained by the grouping factor. Cramer's V rescales chi-square to $[0, 1]$ for nominal associations. Odds ratios and risk ratios are the standard effect sizes for binary outcomes. Always report a p-value and a relevant effect size; the former tells you whether the effect is detectable, the latter tells you whether it matters.

Worked example: regional default rates

A retail-lending portfolio has three regions: north, midlands, south. Observed one-year default counts and exposures are:

RegionLoans, $n_i$Defaults, $d_i$Default rate, $\hat{p}_i$
North20,0004002.00%
Midlands15,0002251.50%
South10,0001201.20%
Total45,0007451.66%

Three legitimate questions can be asked. The chi-square test answers "are the regional default rates the same?". Pairwise z-tests answer "which pairs differ?". A logistic regression with region as a categorical predictor answers "by how much do they differ, and how confident are we?". Three apparently competing tools that should give consistent answers.

Pass 1, chi-square test of independence.

Under $H_0$: regional rates are equal at the pooled rate $\hat{p} = 0.01656$. Expected defaults: $20{,}000 \times 0.01656 = 331.1$ (north), $248.3$ (midlands), $165.6$ (south). Chi-square contribution $\sum (O - E)^2/E$: $(400-331.1)^2/331.1 + (225-248.3)^2/248.3 + (120-165.6)^2/165.6 = 14.34 + 2.19 + 12.57 = 29.1$. Under $H_0$ with $(3-1)(2-1) = 2$ degrees of freedom, the critical value at $\alpha = 0.001$ is $\chi^2_{2, 0.999} = 13.82$. Reject $H_0$: $p \ll 0.001$. The regional rates differ statistically.

What you learn: the global test is conclusive, but it does not tell you which regions differ.

Pass 2, pairwise z-tests with Bonferroni correction.

Three pairwise comparisons; Bonferroni-adjusted alpha is $0.05/3 \approx 0.017$. For north-vs-south: pooled rate $0.01733$, pooled SE $\sqrt{0.01733 \cdot 0.98267 \cdot (1/20000 + 1/10000)} = 0.001602$, z = $(0.02 - 0.012)/0.001602 = 4.99$, p $\approx 6 \times 10^{-7}$. Significant. North-vs-midlands: similar arithmetic gives z $\approx 3.65$, p $\approx 0.0003$. Significant. Midlands-vs-south: z $\approx 2.04$, p $\approx 0.042$. Not significant at the Bonferroni-corrected level.

What you learn: north differs significantly from both other regions; midlands and south are not statistically distinguishable at the corrected level. The Bonferroni correction has reduced the apparent significance of the midlands-vs-south comparison from $p = 0.042$ (which would seem reportable in isolation) to "not significant at the family level".

Pass 3, logistic regression with regional dummies.

Fit $\text{logit}(p_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \text{midlands} + \beta_2 \cdot \text{south}$ with north as the reference category. The MLE gives $\hat{\beta}_0 = -3.892$ (logit of 0.02), $\hat{\beta}_1 = -0.302$ (midlands log-odds-ratio vs north), $\hat{\beta}_2 = -0.523$ (south log-odds-ratio vs north). Wald standard errors are about 0.084 and 0.106. Wald z-statistics: $-3.60$ and $-4.93$, both highly significant. The fitted odds ratios are $e^{-0.302} = 0.74$ (midlands odds is 74 per cent of north's) and $e^{-0.523} = 0.59$ (south odds is 59 per cent of north's). 95 per cent CIs on the odds ratios: midlands $[0.63, 0.87]$, south $[0.48, 0.73]$.

What you learn: the regression gives a quantified answer the chi-square and pairwise tests cannot. The south's default rate is roughly 40 per cent below the north's, with a 95 per cent CI that excludes "no difference". The midlands-vs-south comparison can be obtained by setting midlands as the reference and refitting, or by a Wald test on $\beta_1 - \beta_2$; either way the answer is consistent with the Bonferroni-corrected pairwise test.

All three passes are mutually consistent. The chi-square says "they differ"; the pairwise tests say "north differs from both, midlands vs south is borderline"; the regression says the same with effect sizes and confidence intervals. The right tool to report is the regression: it carries the effect sizes the chi-square lacks and avoids the multiplicity issue that the pairwise tests handled crudely with Bonferroni. The chi-square is the right omnibus first step; the pairwise tests are the right sanity check.

Step 7

Common pitfalls and cognitive traps

p-hacking and the garden of forking paths

A study with many specifications (which subset of covariates, which transformation, which subsample) and a single $p < 0.05$ reported looks compelling but is not: the family of decisions that produced the reported p-value is unrepresented in the analysis. Gelman and Loken's "garden of forking paths" is the formal statement. The cure is pre-registration of analyses (specify the plan before seeing the data) or a multiple-testing correction that accounts for the search.

Misinterpreting the p-value

The p-value is $P(\text{data this extreme} \mid H_0)$, not $P(H_0 \mid \text{data})$, not "the probability the result is due to chance", and not "the probability of replication". The replication probability under exact-replication conditions and the prior $P(H_0)$ both enter the Bayesian posterior, which the p-value does not approximate. The ASA 2016 statement is the canonical reference and worth reading once a year.

Failing to distinguish statistical and practical significance

With a large enough sample, any non-zero effect becomes statistically significant. A new pricing rule that increases default rates by 0.001 percentage points across a 10-million-loan book will produce a p-value below any conventional threshold and will still not be worth the IT cost of implementing. Report effect sizes, confidence intervals, and the business-level decision boundary.

Bonferroni when FDR is the right object

For exploratory analysis with thousands of candidate variables (gene expression, drug screening, credit-portfolio variable selection), FWER control via Bonferroni is too conservative: power collapses to near zero. FDR control via Benjamini-Hochberg accepts that some of the discoveries will be false and bounds the false-discovery fraction. Conversely, for confirmatory analysis with one or two pre-specified primary endpoints, FWER is the right object.

Treating a non-rejection as evidence for the null

"Fail to reject $H_0$" is not the same as "accept $H_0$". A non-rejection can mean the data are consistent with $H_0$, or it can mean the data are not informative enough to reject anything. Power calculations tell you which. The right report when the null is not rejected is the confidence interval: $\hat{\theta} = 0.02$, 95 per cent CI $[-0.08, 0.12]$, "consistent with values from $-0.08$ to $0.12$".

Why the replication crisis is a statistics crisis A 2015 paper in Science reproduced 100 psychology studies; only 36 per cent had a statistically significant result in the same direction as the original. Subsequent attempts in economics, cancer biology, and parts of machine learning find similar rates. The proximate causes are p-hacking, publication bias, and unaccounted multiple testing. The deeper cause is the field-wide use of $p < 0.05$ as the decision rule without paired effect sizes, power analyses, or pre-registration. Banking practice is not immune: model-development pipelines that screen hundreds of features at $\alpha = 0.05$ and report only the survivors are vulnerable to the same failure.
Step 8

Frontiers and what's beyond

Three directions sit just past the syllabus.

The replication crisis and proposed fixes. Benjamin et al. (2018) "Redefine statistical significance" propose moving the threshold from $\alpha = 0.05$ to $\alpha = 0.005$ for claims of new discoveries, with $0.005 < p < 0.05$ rebranded "suggestive". The Bayes-factor argument behind the proposal is interesting in its own right. Other fixes include pre-registration (the analysis plan is filed before the data are seen), registered reports (peer review is on the plan, not the result), and the move from null-hypothesis testing toward equivalence testing (does the effect fall inside a band of practical irrelevance?).

e-values and post-selection inference. The frequentist tradition has been re-examining its own foundations. e-values (Vovk and Wang, Shafer) generalise p-values to a measure of evidence that composes correctly across studies and sequential testing. Selective inference (Fithian, Sun, Taylor) builds confidence intervals that are valid after a model-selection step (lasso, forward selection), which classical CIs are not. Both are still research-grade tools but are spreading into industrial practice quickly.

Conformal prediction. A distribution-free framework for prediction intervals around any black-box model (random forest, neural net, gradient boosting). The interval has guaranteed marginal coverage at level $1 - \alpha$, even when the underlying model is completely wrong. Conformal prediction is the modern answer to "what is the uncertainty around this ML model's prediction?" and is being adopted in clinical risk scores, weather forecasting, and emerging in credit scoring. It sits beside the bootstrap as the second modern tool for non-parametric uncertainty quantification.

Robust inference under model misspecification. Classical standard errors assume the model is correctly specified. Sandwich (Huber-White) standard errors give valid inference under mild misspecification by replacing the inverse Fisher information with $A^{-1} B A^{-1}$, where $A$ is the observed information and $B$ is the outer product of scores. Cluster-robust and HAC (heteroscedasticity-and-autocorrelation-consistent) variants are the standard in econometrics and increasingly in regulated credit-risk inference. Lecture 06 returns to these for time series.

Step 9

Synthesis and what's next

You now have the frequentist inferential toolkit. Estimation produces a point estimate (MoM, MLE, LS, or M-estimator) and an interval around it (Wald, score, LR, or bootstrap). Hypothesis testing produces a verdict on a specific $\theta_0$ via a test statistic, a rejection region, a significance level, and a p-value, all consistent with the Neyman-Pearson framework. The two halves are duals connected by the sampling distribution from pillar 2. Around the duals sit the bootstrap (when analytic sampling distributions fail), multiple-testing corrections (when many hypotheses are tested), and effect sizes (when the size of an effect matters more than its detectability).

The bridge to the next lecture is direct. Inference asks "what is $\theta$?" Modelling asks "what is $\theta$ inside a structured relationship $Y = f(X; \theta) + \epsilon$?" Once $\theta$ becomes a vector indexing a regression function, all of pillar-3 machinery (MLE, Wald tests, confidence intervals, the bootstrap, multiple testing on the coefficients) carries over unchanged. Lecture 04 maps out the model zoo; lecture 05 works through the most important family (GLMs) in full. The estimation-and-testing apparatus does not change; only the dimensionality of $\theta$ grows.
