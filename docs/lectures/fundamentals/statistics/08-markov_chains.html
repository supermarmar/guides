Lectures / Statistics

Markov chains

The third lecture under pillar 5. Drop both the IID assumption and the continuous-time framing of lectures 06 and 07: observations are now discrete states, indexed by time, with the dependence carried entirely by the most recent state. The Markov property "the future is conditionally independent of the past given the present" is the simplest non-IID structure that still admits a tractable theory. Out of it falls a complete catalogue of long-run behaviour (stationary distributions, ergodicity, absorption), and a credit-risk application that has shaped reduced-form default modelling since Jarrow, Lando, and Turnbull's 1997 paper: the rating transition matrix.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A Markov chain is a sequence of random variables $\{X_t\}_{t \geq 0}$ taking values in a discrete state space $\mathcal{S}$, with the Markov property: the conditional distribution of $X_{t+1}$ given the entire history $X_0, X_1, \ldots, X_t$ depends only on $X_t$. The entire dynamic is captured by a transition matrix $P$ whose entry $P_{ij}$ is the conditional probability of moving to state $j$ from state $i$ in one step. Repeated multiplication of $P$ gives the $n$-step transition probabilities, the long-run state distribution, and (for finite irreducible aperiodic chains) the unique stationary distribution $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ that satisfies $\boldsymbol{\pi}^\top P = \boldsymbol{\pi}^\top$.

The theory partitions cleanly into three regimes by the structure of the chain. Irreducible and aperiodic on a finite state space: a unique stationary distribution exists and the chain converges to it from any starting state. Absorbing: at least one state cannot be left; the question becomes the probability of absorption into each absorbing state and the expected time to absorption. Continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC): time runs continuously, the dynamic is encoded in a rate (generator) matrix $Q$, and transition probabilities over a time interval $t$ are given by the matrix exponential $\exp(Q t)$. Each regime has a named credit-risk application: rating migrations are typically modelled as a CTMC; a defaulted account is an absorbing state; through-the-cycle migration matrices live in the finite-state-irreducible regime.

Feynman test A Markov chain is a memoryless random walk on a graph. The graph's nodes are the states; the edges carry the transition probabilities. Walk one step from state $i$ to state $j$ with probability $P_{ij}$; the past is irrelevant once you know where you are now. Out of this single assumption the entire theory unfolds. The transition matrix is the description of the graph; matrix multiplication advances time; eigenvectors give long-run behaviour; the fundamental matrix $N = (I - Q)^{-1}$ on the transient block gives expected hitting times. The whole subject is matrix algebra applied to a probability constraint.
Step 2

Where this sits in the stats world

Pillar 5, discrete state transitions This lecture is the third and final lecture under pillar 5 of the stats world map. Lectures 06 and 07 generalised the IID setup along a continuous time index (time series) and along the censoring mechanism (survival). This lecture generalises in a different direction: the state space itself becomes the carrier of dependence. The observations are categorical, indexed by time, and the dependence is summarised by a transition matrix rather than an autocovariance function or a hazard function. Lecture 09 (Bayesian statistics) will revisit each of these three lectures from a Bayesian angle, including Bayesian estimation of transition matrices.

Two pieces of structure tie this lecture to its neighbours. To lecture 07 (survival analysis): a multi-state survival model is mathematically a continuous-time Markov chain on a finite state space. The transition intensities $q_{ij}(t)$ between states are the hazard functions; the matrix exponential $\exp(Qt)$ supplies the transition probabilities over an interval $t$. The split between the two lectures is conventional rather than mathematical: survival analysis was developed in biostatistics around terminal events; Markov chain theory was developed in probability around state transitions. They meet in the middle at the multi-state model, the natural object for modelling loan accounts that move between current, delinquent, default, and cured states. To lecture 06 (time series): a Markov chain is a finite-state analogue of an AR(1) process. The autoregressive equation $X_t = \phi X_{t-1} + \epsilon_t$ has a continuous-state, additive-noise structure; the Markov chain has a discrete-state, conditional-distribution structure. Both share the property that one step of history is sufficient.

One scoping note. This lecture treats time-homogeneous Markov chains in discrete and continuous time, with a brief on hidden Markov models. Non-homogeneous chains (transition matrices that vary over time) are mentioned where relevant but not developed; Markov decision processes (the reinforcement-learning extension) belong in the machine-learning track; general-state-space Markov chains (the formal setting for MCMC) are previewed for lecture 09. The credit-risk application of rating transitions is given its own section because of its centrality to the F107 syllabus.

Step 3

Why this matters

Three reasons Markov chains deserve their own lecture for a credit risk modeller. First, the credit rating transition matrix is the central object of reduced-form credit modelling. Standard and Poor's, Moody's, and Fitch each publish annual rating-migration matrices estimated from their historical issuer-rating histories, and these matrices are the input to the Jarrow-Lando-Turnbull (1997) model, the original reduced-form credit risk model, and to almost every CreditMetrics-style portfolio-credit-risk model that has come after it. Understanding the matrix, its estimation, its through-the-cycle versus point-in-time interpretations, and the assumption of time-homogeneity that underwrites it is fundamental to credit portfolio modelling. For F107 specifically, the rating transition matrix is one of the named topics in the credit risk syllabus.

Second, the multi-state framework for loan accounts reduces, formally, to a continuous-time Markov chain. A retail account moves through states "current", "1-29 dpd", "30-59 dpd", "60-89 dpd", "90+ dpd default", "cured" (back to current after delinquency), and "written off" (absorbing). The transition intensities between adjacent states are credit-risk-driven hazards; the matrix exponential gives the probability that an account in state $i$ today is in state $j$ in $t$ months. This is the natural state-space for stage-2 IFRS 9 ECL on portfolios where delinquency stage carries predictive content, and it is the framework underlying many production roll-rate models in retail banking. Understanding the Markov chain machinery is essential to understanding what a roll-rate model is, why it works, and where it breaks.

Third, the failure modes of Markov chain models are distinctive. The Markov property itself is rarely tested in practice but often violated: a rating downgrade today carries more information than a rating downgrade six months ago, in violation of the memorylessness assumption (this is the "rating momentum" effect documented by Bangia, Diebold, Kronimus, Schagen, and Schuermann (2002)). Time-homogeneity (a single matrix for all calendar dates) is contradicted by the through-the-cycle versus point-in-time literature: transition matrices estimated in expansions look very different from those estimated in recessions. Sparse-cell estimation is a real problem: many cells of a rating transition matrix have zero or one observation over a typical historical window, producing unstable maximum-likelihood estimates and zero one-step transition probabilities that propagate to zero estimated default probabilities over long horizons. Each of these failure modes has a named correction (regime-switching Markov chains, Bayesian smoothing, the Aalen-Johansen estimator with non-Markov adjustments) and knowing them is what separates a working credit transition model from a textbook example.

Step 4

The mental model

The picture below shows the three regimes that organise the entire theory.

Two slogans pin the workflow. One matrix is everything. Given $P$ (discrete time) or $Q$ (continuous time), all questions about the chain reduce to matrix operations: $P^n$ for $n$-step transitions, eigendecomposition of $P$ for the stationary distribution, $(I - Q)^{-1}$ for the fundamental matrix of an absorbing chain, $\exp(Qt)$ for the CTMC transition probabilities. The art is in setting up the matrix; the rest is linear algebra. Long-run behaviour is determined by the chain's reachability structure. A chain is partitioned into communicating classes, each of which is either transient or recurrent. The long-run distribution is concentrated on the recurrent classes; the transient classes are visited only finitely often before the chain settles in a recurrent class. Once you know the class structure, the long-run analysis is largely determined.

Step 5

The detail

5.1 Definition and the Markov property

A discrete-time Markov chain on a discrete state space $\mathcal{S} = \{1, 2, \ldots, N\}$ is a sequence of random variables $\{X_t\}_{t = 0, 1, 2, \ldots}$ satisfying the Markov property:

$$P(X_{t+1} = j \mid X_0 = i_0, X_1 = i_1, \ldots, X_t = i) = P(X_{t+1} = j \mid X_t = i),$$

for every history $(i_0, i_1, \ldots, i)$ and every next state $j$. The conditional distribution of the next state depends only on the current state, not on how it was reached. The chain is time-homogeneous if these conditional probabilities do not depend on $t$; we assume time-homogeneity throughout this lecture unless stated otherwise.

The dynamic is captured by the transition matrix $P = (P_{ij})$ with $P_{ij} = P(X_{t+1} = j \mid X_t = i)$. By definition, every row of $P$ is a probability vector: $P_{ij} \geq 0$ and $\sum_j P_{ij} = 1$. Matrices with this property are called stochastic matrices. The chain is determined by its initial distribution $\boldsymbol{\mu}_0$ (a row vector with $\mu_{0, i} = P(X_0 = i)$) and its transition matrix $P$: the distribution at time $t$ is $\boldsymbol{\mu}_t = \boldsymbol{\mu}_0 P^t$.

The Chapman-Kolmogorov equations express the $n$-step transition probabilities. With $P^{(n)}_{ij} = P(X_n = j \mid X_0 = i)$, we have

$$P^{(n+m)}_{ij} = \sum_{k} P^{(n)}_{ik} P^{(m)}_{kj}, \quad \text{or in matrix form,} \quad P^{(n+m)} = P^{(n)} P^{(m)} = P^{n+m}.$$

The $n$-step transition matrix is the $n$th power of the one-step matrix. This is the central computational identity of the discrete-time theory.

5.2 Classification of states

A state $j$ is accessible from $i$ (written $i \to j$) if there exists $n \geq 0$ with $P^{(n)}_{ij} > 0$. States $i$ and $j$ communicate ($i \leftrightarrow j$) if $i \to j$ and $j \to i$. Communication is an equivalence relation, partitioning $\mathcal{S}$ into communicating classes. The chain is irreducible if there is exactly one communicating class.

A state $i$ is recurrent if, starting from $i$, the chain returns to $i$ with probability one; otherwise it is transient. An equivalent criterion: $i$ is recurrent iff $\sum_n P^{(n)}_{ii} = \infty$. On a finite state space, every irreducible chain is recurrent. On an infinite state space, things become subtler: a recurrent state is positive recurrent if the expected return time is finite, otherwise null recurrent. The symmetric random walk on $\mathbb{Z}$ is recurrent but null recurrent in one and two dimensions, and transient in three or more dimensions (Polya 1921; "a drunk man will always make it home, but a drunk bird may not").

The period of state $i$ is the greatest common divisor of the set $\{n \geq 1 : P^{(n)}_{ii} > 0\}$. A state is aperiodic if its period is 1. Aperiodicity is automatic if any state has a self-loop $P_{ii} > 0$. Within an irreducible chain, all states share the same period.

5.3 The fundamental theorem and the stationary distribution

A stationary distribution $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ is a row vector satisfying $\boldsymbol{\pi}^\top P = \boldsymbol{\pi}^\top$, with $\pi_i \geq 0$ and $\sum_i \pi_i = 1$. If the chain starts in $\boldsymbol{\pi}$, the distribution at every later time is also $\boldsymbol{\pi}$; the marginal distribution is "frozen" along the trajectory.

The fundamental theorem of Markov chains. Let $\{X_t\}$ be an irreducible, aperiodic Markov chain on a finite state space. Then a unique stationary distribution $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ exists, and from any starting state,

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} P^{(n)}_{ij} = \pi_j.$$

The columns of $P^n$ converge to identical columns equal to $\boldsymbol{\pi}^\top$. Two practical computations of $\boldsymbol{\pi}$: (1) solve the linear system $\boldsymbol{\pi}^\top (P - I) = \mathbf{0}^\top$ with the normalisation $\sum_i \pi_i = 1$; (2) the left eigenvector of $P$ corresponding to eigenvalue 1 (always an eigenvalue for a stochastic matrix), normalised to sum to one. The latter is more numerically stable for large matrices.

Detailed balance and reversibility. A chain is reversible with respect to a distribution $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ if the detailed-balance equations hold:

$$\pi_i P_{ij} = \pi_j P_{ji} \quad \text{for all } i, j.$$

Detailed balance is a sufficient condition for $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ to be stationary (summing over $i$ recovers $\boldsymbol{\pi}^\top P = \boldsymbol{\pi}^\top$), but not necessary. The Metropolis-Hastings algorithm and the Gibbs sampler (lecture 09) are constructed to satisfy detailed balance, which is why they target the correct stationary distribution. Reversibility means that running the chain forwards and backwards in time gives statistically indistinguishable trajectories, when started in the stationary distribution.

5.4 Ergodicity

The ergodic theorem for Markov chains is the deeper statement that lies behind the fundamental theorem. For an irreducible, positive-recurrent chain with stationary distribution $\boldsymbol{\pi}$, and any bounded function $f: \mathcal{S} \to \mathbb{R}$,

$$\frac{1}{n} \sum_{t=1}^n f(X_t) \to \sum_i \pi_i f(i) \quad \text{almost surely}.$$

The time-average of $f$ along the trajectory converges to its space-average under $\boldsymbol{\pi}$. This is the formal justification for MCMC: estimate an expectation $E_\pi[f]$ by simulating a long enough run of the chain and averaging $f$ over the trajectory. The aperiodicity assumption is not needed for the ergodic theorem (it is needed only for the fundamental theorem's stronger pointwise convergence of marginals); a periodic chain still has converging time-averages.

The rate at which the chain reaches its stationary distribution is controlled by the second-largest eigenvalue $\lambda_2$ of $P$ (in absolute value); the convergence to stationarity is geometric at rate $|\lambda_2|^n$. The quantity $1 / (1 - |\lambda_2|)$ is the mixing time of the chain. Chains with $|\lambda_2|$ close to 1 mix slowly; chains with $|\lambda_2|$ close to 0 mix in one or two steps. Designing fast-mixing chains is the central engineering problem of MCMC.

5.5 Absorbing chains and the fundamental matrix

A state $i$ is absorbing if $P_{ii} = 1$. Once entered, the chain remains there forever. An absorbing chain has at least one absorbing state, and every state can reach some absorbing state. The natural question is no longer the long-run distribution (which is supported entirely on the absorbing states), but rather: from a given starting state, with what probability does the chain end up in each absorbing state, and how long does it take to get there?

Partition the state space into transient states $T = \{t_1, \ldots, t_k\}$ and absorbing states $A = \{a_1, \ldots, a_r\}$. Re-order the transition matrix into canonical form:

$$P = \begin{pmatrix} Q & R \\ \mathbf{0} & I \end{pmatrix},$$

where $Q$ is the $k \times k$ transitions among transient states, $R$ is the $k \times r$ transitions from transient to absorbing, and the lower block is the absorbing-state submatrix (no transitions out). The fundamental matrix is

$$N = (I - Q)^{-1} = I + Q + Q^2 + \cdots.$$

$N_{ij}$ is the expected number of visits to transient state $j$ starting from transient state $i$ before absorption. The expected time to absorption starting from $i$ is $t_i = \sum_j N_{ij}$, the $i$th row sum of $N$. The absorption probability matrix $B = NR$ has entries $B_{ij}$ = probability of being absorbed in absorbing state $j$ starting from transient state $i$. Three matrix multiplications, all standard linear algebra; the entire absorption analysis follows from setting up $Q$ and $R$.

The credit-risk application is direct. A loan account moves among current, delinquent, default, cured states. Default (the analyst's interest) is absorbing; the analyst wants the probability that a given account, currently in some delinquency stage, ends up in default before being cured, and the expected time to absorption (default). The fundamental-matrix formula supplies both in closed form.

5.6 Continuous-time Markov chains

A continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) $\{X_t\}_{t \geq 0}$ takes values in a discrete state space, but the time index is continuous. The Markov property generalises: the future depends on the past only through the present. The dynamic is now characterised by a generator matrix $Q$ with entries

$$q_{ij} = \lim_{h \downarrow 0} \frac{P(X_h = j \mid X_0 = i) - \delta_{ij}}{h}.$$

The off-diagonal entries $q_{ij}$ ($i \neq j$) are non-negative transition rates from $i$ to $j$; the diagonal entries $q_{ii} = -\sum_{j \neq i} q_{ij}$ are negative and equal the total exit rate from state $i$. Every row of $Q$ sums to zero. The waiting time in state $i$ before any transition is exponentially distributed with rate $|q_{ii}|$; conditional on a transition occurring, the next state is $j$ with probability $q_{ij} / |q_{ii}|$.

The transition probability matrix over an interval $t$ is given by the matrix exponential:

$$P(t) = \exp(Q t) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{(Qt)^n}{n!}.$$

$P_{ij}(t)$ is the probability of being in state $j$ at time $t$ starting from $i$ at time $0$. Note that $P(t)$ is the discrete-time analogue we have been working with throughout, but parameterised by continuous $t$. The Chapman-Kolmogorov equations become $P(t + s) = P(t) P(s)$.

Equivalent formulations: $P(t)$ satisfies the Kolmogorov forward equation $\frac{d}{dt} P(t) = P(t) Q$ and the backward equation $\frac{d}{dt} P(t) = Q P(t)$, both with initial condition $P(0) = I$. The forward equation describes how the distribution evolves; the backward equation describes how expectations of functions of the future state evolve.

The embedded discrete chain is the sequence of states visited, ignoring waiting times. Its transition matrix is the jump matrix with entries $J_{ij} = q_{ij} / |q_{ii}|$ for $i \neq j$ and zero on the diagonal. The embedded chain is a Markov chain in its own right and the apparatus of sections 5.1 to 5.5 applies to it.

5.7 Estimation from data

Two distinct estimation problems arise depending on how the data is observed.

Cohort method (discrete-time observation). The data consists of state observations at fixed time-points (annual rating snapshots, monthly account status). For each $(i, j)$, let $n_{ij}$ = number of transitions observed from $i$ to $j$ in one period and $n_i = \sum_j n_{ij}$ = total time-periods spent in state $i$ across all subjects. The MLE of the transition probability is

$$\hat{P}_{ij} = n_{ij} / n_i.$$

Simple and intuitive. Two limitations. First, sparse cells: a rare transition with $n_{ij} = 0$ gives $\hat{P}_{ij} = 0$, which propagates to zero estimated probabilities at all horizons. Second, the cohort method does not use within-period transition information: a subject that transitioned twice within a period (say AA → A → BBB within a month) contributes only the start-to-end transition AA → BBB, losing information.

Duration / hazard method (continuous-time observation). If transition times within the period are observed, the natural model is a CTMC. The MLE of the off-diagonal generator entry is

$$\hat{q}_{ij} = N_{ij} / Y_i, \quad i \neq j,$$

where $N_{ij}$ = total number of $i \to j$ transitions over the observation window and $Y_i$ = total time spent in state $i$ across all subjects. The diagonal entries follow from the row-sum-zero constraint. The cohort-method transition probability for a one-year period is then $\hat{P}(1) = \exp(\hat{Q})$, which is a more efficient estimator than the direct cohort estimator and (importantly) does not suffer from the zero-cell problem in the same way: $\hat{q}_{ij}$ is zero only if no $i \to j$ transition was ever observed, which is a stronger condition than no $i \to j$ transition being observed within a single period.

Smoothing rare-cell estimates. Three standard remedies for sparse cells. (1) Bayesian smoothing: place a Dirichlet prior on each row of $P$ and use the posterior mean. The result is a regularised estimator that shrinks zero cells toward the prior; with a Jeffreys prior, the posterior mean is $(n_{ij} + 1/2) / (n_i + N/2)$. (2) Cell-pooling across similar rating grades, accepting some bias for variance reduction. (3) The generator-method estimator with subsequent matrix exponentiation, as above.

Confidence intervals. Bootstrap is the standard tool: resample subjects with replacement, re-estimate $\hat{P}$ or $\hat{Q}$, and use the empirical quantiles of the bootstrap distribution. Asymptotic-normality intervals on $\hat{q}_{ij}$ are also available and standard in software, but the bootstrap is the more reliable approach for sparse cells.

5.8 Hidden Markov models

A hidden Markov model (HMM) assumes that the underlying state $X_t$ is unobserved (latent); the analyst sees only an observation $Y_t$ whose distribution depends on $X_t$. Formally, $\{X_t\}$ is a Markov chain on $\mathcal{S} = \{1, \ldots, N\}$ with transition matrix $A$, and $Y_t \mid X_t = i \sim B(\cdot \mid i)$ for some observation distribution $B$. The HMM is fully characterised by $(\boldsymbol{\pi}_0, A, B)$ where $\boldsymbol{\pi}_0$ is the initial-state distribution.

Three classical algorithmic problems define the apparatus.

  • Forward-backward algorithm: compute $P(X_t = i \mid Y_1, \ldots, Y_T)$ (the smoothed marginal) for each $t$ in $O(N^2 T)$ time. The forward pass computes $\alpha_t(i) = P(Y_1, \ldots, Y_t, X_t = i)$ recursively from $\alpha_{t-1}$; the backward pass computes $\beta_t(i) = P(Y_{t+1}, \ldots, Y_T \mid X_t = i)$ recursively from $\beta_{t+1}$; their product gives the smoothed marginal.
  • Viterbi algorithm: compute the most likely state sequence $\arg\max_{x_{1:T}} P(X_{1:T} = x_{1:T} \mid Y_{1:T})$ in $O(N^2 T)$ time by dynamic programming on the joint log-probabilities.
  • Baum-Welch algorithm: estimate the HMM parameters $(\boldsymbol{\pi}_0, A, B)$ from the observed sequence, by EM. The E-step uses forward-backward; the M-step updates the parameters in closed form. Local optimum only; multiple random starts are standard.

The finance application is regime-switching. Hamilton (1989) modelled GDP growth as an HMM with two regimes ("expansion" and "recession") and Gaussian observation distributions; the inferred regime-state probabilities are widely used as macro-financial regime indicators. Markov-switching ARIMA and GARCH models extend the framework to time-series modelling with discrete regime changes.

5.9 Credit rating transition matrices in detail

The standard credit-risk application. Standard and Poor's, Moody's, and Fitch each publish annual rating transition matrices estimated from issuer-rating histories over multi-decade windows. The state space is the ordered set of rating grades (S&P: AAA, AA, A, BBB, BB, B, CCC, default), with default treated as absorbing. The matrix dimensions are $8 \times 8$ for S&P; finer granularity (adding +/- modifiers) gives $20 \times 20$. Each row of the matrix sums to one and the rows have a characteristic shape: the diagonal is large (most issuers retain their rating in any given year), the adjacent-grade entries are moderate, and the far-off-diagonal entries are small.

Three substantive issues.

Time-homogeneity. A single matrix for all calendar years is the strongest convenient assumption, but the literature documents that recession-year matrices have systematically higher downgrade probabilities than expansion-year matrices. The through-the-cycle (TTC) matrix is the long-run average across the business cycle; the point-in-time (PIT) matrix is the current-period estimate, conditional on the prevailing economic regime. Regulatory PD modelling under Basel IRB favours TTC (for capital stability); IFRS 9 ECL favours PIT (for reporting timeliness). Both interpretations are useful; they are answers to different questions.

The Markov property is empirically violated for credit ratings. Bangia et al. (2002) and Lando-Skodeberg (2002) document that recently-downgraded firms have higher subsequent downgrade probabilities than firms that have been at the same rating for several years, even though the Markov property says the time-in-state is irrelevant. This is "rating momentum" or "downward drift". Two practical responses: (1) include "time in rating" as a covariate in a Cox-style hazard model on each off-diagonal transition (taking the multi-state survival framing); (2) use a higher-order Markov chain (where $X_{t+1}$ depends on both $X_t$ and $X_{t-1}$), at the cost of a much larger parameter space.

The Jarrow-Lando-Turnbull model. JLT (1997) gave the first reduced-form credit risk model in which the rating transition matrix is the central object. Under a risk-neutral measure (used for pricing rather than the physical measure used for capital), the JLT framework specifies a generator $Q^*$ and recovers risk-neutral default probabilities at every horizon as the relevant entries of $\exp(Q^* t)$. The physical-to-risk-neutral transformation is a multiplicative adjustment to the off-diagonal generator entries, calibrated to bond-spread market data. The JLT framework remains the workhorse of credit-derivative pricing (CDS, CDOs) and the conceptual ancestor of the Markov-chain models used in CreditMetrics, KMV, and the various academic extensions (Lando 1998, Duffie-Singleton 1999).

Step 6

Worked example: rating-migration matrix and default probabilities

A concrete numerical example to anchor the apparatus. Take a simplified four-state rating system: A (high), B (mid), C (low), D (default, absorbing).

Step 1: estimated one-year matrix.

From a cohort of corporate issuers observed annually over 20 years, the maximum-likelihood transition matrix is

$$P = \begin{pmatrix} 0.90 & 0.08 & 0.02 & 0.00 \\ 0.05 & 0.85 & 0.08 & 0.02 \\ 0.01 & 0.10 & 0.79 & 0.10 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Read the rows: an A-rated issuer has a 90 per cent probability of remaining A, 8 per cent of moving to B, 2 per cent to C, and effectively zero of defaulting within the year. C-rated issuers have a 10 per cent annual default probability. The default state is absorbing (last row).

Step 2: five-year transition matrix.

Compute $P^5$ (powers of the one-year matrix). Numerically,

$$P^5 \approx \begin{pmatrix} 0.66 & 0.21 & 0.09 & 0.04 \\ 0.14 & 0.49 & 0.22 & 0.15 \\ 0.05 & 0.21 & 0.39 & 0.35 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

An A-rated issuer has a 4 per cent five-year cumulative default probability, a B issuer 15 per cent, a C issuer 35 per cent. The diagonal of $P^5$ is much smaller than that of $P$: at five-year horizons, the rating distribution has diffused substantially from the starting rating.

Step 3: absorbing-chain analysis.

The 3 × 3 transient block $Q$ is the upper-left of $P$ with the D-row and D-column removed:

$$Q = \begin{pmatrix} 0.90 & 0.08 & 0.02 \\ 0.05 & 0.85 & 0.08 \\ 0.01 & 0.10 & 0.79 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The fundamental matrix is

$$N = (I - Q)^{-1} \approx \begin{pmatrix} 39 & 33 & 16 \\ 18 & 41 & 17 \\ 11 & 25 & 18 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Read it as: starting in A, the issuer spends on average 39 years in A, 33 in B, and 16 in C before defaulting; total expected time to default $39 + 33 + 16 = 88$ years. Starting in C, the issuer spends on average 11 in A, 25 in B, 18 in C; total expected time 54 years. Long, because annual default probabilities are small per state, but the absorbing state is reached with probability one.

Step 4: continuous-time refinement.

If the underlying observation is continuous (transitions observable at any date, not just year-ends), fit a CTMC. The MLE generator $\hat{Q}$ has off-diagonal entries $\hat{q}_{ij} = N_{ij} / Y_i$; the one-year transition matrix is $\hat{P}(1) = \exp(\hat{Q})$ which differs from the cohort matrix by an "internal transitions" correction. Multi-step transition probabilities are $\hat{P}(t) = \exp(\hat{Q} t)$ for any non-integer horizon $t$. This is the natural framing for IFRS 9 monthly ECL computations: compute $\exp(\hat{Q} t)$ at $t = 1/12, 2/12, \ldots, T_{\max}$ months and read the last column (the default probabilities) at each horizon.

Step 5: business-cycle conditioning.

The matrices above are through-the-cycle (averaged across expansion and recession years). A two-regime extension uses two matrices, $P_{\text{exp}}$ and $P_{\text{rec}}$, with regime-transition probabilities forming an outer two-state Markov chain (an HMM with rating states as the inner chain). Estimated separately on expansion-year and recession-year data, the recession matrix typically has 1.5 to 2.5 times higher one-year default probabilities than the expansion matrix, with downgrade probabilities also elevated. The point-in-time PD for a given rating in a given month is the regime-mixture: $P_t(\text{default} \mid i) = p_t^{\text{exp}} P_{\text{exp}}(\text{default} \mid i) + p_t^{\text{rec}} P_{\text{rec}}(\text{default} \mid i)$, where $p_t^{\text{regime}}$ is the time-$t$ probability of being in each regime under the outer HMM.

Three sanity checks anchor the workflow. (1) Row sums of every matrix should equal one (to numerical precision); a row sum off by more than $10^{-10}$ flags a computational bug. (2) The matrix should be "weakly diagonally dominant" in the sense that the diagonal is the largest entry of each row; a violation suggests the matrix is being applied at the wrong time horizon (a one-month matrix used as a one-year matrix, or vice versa). (3) Comparing $\hat{P}^k$ (cohort-method raised to power $k$) against the empirical $k$-period transitions is a goodness-of-fit check on the time-homogeneity assumption; large deviations flag regime effects that need explicit modelling.

Step 7

Common pitfalls and cognitive traps

Mistaking the Markov property for "no dependence"

The Markov property does not say that the present is independent of the past, only that the present is a sufficient summary of the past for predicting the future. The joint distribution of $X_0, X_1, \ldots, X_t$ has all kinds of dependencies; only the conditional distribution of $X_{t+1}$ given the full history reduces to the conditional on $X_t$ alone. Confusion between "Markov" and "independent" leads to bad intuitions about long-run autocorrelations and mixing times.

Time-homogeneity assumed without checking

A single transition matrix for the entire observation window is convenient but typically wrong. Macroeconomic regimes shift, regulatory regimes shift, and behaviour shifts. The fix is to (1) test time-homogeneity by splitting the sample into sub-periods and comparing matrices via a likelihood-ratio test; (2) if rejected, fit a regime-switching or time-varying-parameter version. Production credit models almost always violate time-homogeneity, and the failure to test it produces miscalibration when the model is deployed in a different regime from the one it was estimated in.

Zero-cell estimates extrapolated to zero default probabilities

Sparse cells in a rating transition matrix produce $\hat{P}_{ij} = 0$ from $n_{ij} = 0$, which is the MLE but disastrous when used to compute $\hat{P}^n$ for large $n$: zero one-step probabilities propagate to zero long-horizon probabilities, contradicting the practical knowledge that defaults can occur from any rating given enough time. The fixes are Bayesian smoothing (Dirichlet prior, posterior mean), pooling across similar grades, or the generator-method approach (where the matrix exponential always produces strictly positive off-diagonal entries unless the generator is reducible).

Applying through-the-cycle matrices in point-in-time contexts

TTC matrices average out the business cycle; PIT matrices condition on it. Using a TTC matrix to compute IFRS 9 ECL during a downturn understates current default probabilities; using a PIT matrix to compute Basel IRB capital introduces unwanted cyclicality. The two frameworks answer different questions and the practitioner must be explicit about which they intend.

Rating momentum and the failure of memorylessness

Empirically, ratings show momentum: a recent downgrade predicts further downgrades, in violation of the Markov property. The standard Markov chain analysis ignores this and produces over-optimistic estimates of long-run survival for recently-downgraded names. The fixes are duration-augmented models (include time-in-state as a covariate in a Cox-style hazard on each transition), higher-order Markov chains (where the next transition depends on the last $k$ states, not just the last one), or non-homogeneous Markov chains (where the matrix evolves over time). Each has costs in parameter inflation and estimation stability.

Long-run analysis without checking reducibility

The fundamental theorem applies only to irreducible aperiodic chains. A reducible chain has multiple recurrent classes and the long-run distribution depends on the starting state; an aperiodic chain has $P^n$ oscillating rather than converging. The chain in section 6 is reducible (default is absorbing, so the transient block does not communicate with default), and the right analysis is the absorbing-chain machinery, not the stationary-distribution machinery. Mismatching the regime to the chain structure produces nonsense.

Markov modelling is matrix algebra plus regime structure Most failure modes above are not failures of the matrix algebra but failures to verify the structural assumptions on the chain. Irreducibility, aperiodicity, time-homogeneity, the Markov property itself, and the presence or absence of absorbing states each carry consequences for which apparatus applies. A Markov-chain model that does not explicitly state these structural assumptions is incomplete; verifying them is the bulk of the modelling work. The matrix-algebra computations are typically a few minutes; the assumptions are typically a few weeks.
Step 8

Frontiers and what's beyond

Four frontiers sit past the classical material.

General-state-space Markov chains and MCMC. The classical theory above is for finite or countable state spaces. The general-state-space theory (Meyn-Tweedie 1993) extends the framework to continuous state spaces, with the role of the transition matrix played by a transition kernel $P(x, A) = P(X_{t+1} \in A \mid X_t = x)$ for measurable $A$. The fundamental and ergodic theorems generalise, with technical conditions (Harris recurrence, geometric ergodicity). The MCMC algorithms covered in lecture 09 (Metropolis-Hastings, Gibbs, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo) all construct general-state-space Markov chains with a designed stationary distribution.

Markov decision processes (MDPs) and reinforcement learning. An MDP adds two ingredients to a Markov chain: at each state, a decision-maker chooses an action from an action set, and a reward is collected. The transition matrix depends on the action. The decision-maker's objective is to choose a policy (an action-selection rule) that maximises expected discounted reward over the trajectory. The Bellman equation, value iteration, policy iteration, and Q-learning are the classical algorithms; deep Q-networks and policy-gradient methods are the modern neural extensions. The framework is the foundation of reinforcement learning and is covered in the machine-learning track.

Continuous-time multi-state models in credit and actuarial work. Aalen-Johansen estimation of transition probabilities in non-homogeneous multi-state models; Markov reward models for valuing cash flows along trajectories; semi-Markov models that allow non-exponential waiting times. The R packages msm (Markov multi-state models) and mstate (general multi-state survival) implement standard workflows. The contingencies track of this guide uses these tools for joint-life and multi-decrement models in life insurance.

Hidden semi-Markov models and structured prediction. An HSMM relaxes the geometric waiting-time distribution of an HMM by allowing arbitrary state-dwell distributions; it is the natural framework for speech recognition, gesture recognition, and finance applications where regime durations are far from geometric. Conditional random fields (CRFs) and structured prediction models extend the framework to sequence labelling with discriminative training; they are the workhorse of named-entity recognition and part-of-speech tagging in natural language processing.

Step 9

Synthesis and what's next

You now have the classical Markov chain toolkit. Discrete-time Markov chains are summarised by a transition matrix $P$; matrix powers give multi-step transition probabilities; the fundamental theorem applies to irreducible aperiodic chains and yields a unique stationary distribution. Absorbing chains are analysed via the fundamental matrix $N = (I - Q)^{-1}$, which gives expected times to absorption and absorption probabilities in closed form. Continuous-time Markov chains are summarised by a generator $Q$; transition probabilities over an interval $t$ are $\exp(Qt)$. Estimation from data is cohort-method or duration-method, with the latter typically more efficient and the former simpler. Hidden Markov models extend the framework to latent-state models, with forward-backward, Viterbi, and Baum-Welch as the algorithmic triad. Rating transition matrices are the central credit-risk application, with the Jarrow-Lando-Turnbull (1997) framework as the canonical reduced-form credit pricing model.

The next and final lecture introduces the Bayesian framework. Where the previous eight lectures have all treated parameters $\theta$ as fixed unknowns to be estimated from data (the frequentist paradigm), the Bayesian framework treats $\theta$ as itself a random variable with a prior distribution; data updates the prior to a posterior, from which all subsequent inference flows. The Bayesian toolkit re-derives versions of much of what we have built: Bayesian estimation, Bayesian hypothesis testing, Bayesian GLMs, Bayesian time-series, Bayesian survival, Bayesian Markov chain estimation. The computational engine is MCMC, which is itself a Markov chain construction: the algorithms of this lecture re-emerge as the foundation for sampling from posterior distributions in lecture 09. The course closes by tying the entire programme together.
