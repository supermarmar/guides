Coordination, subordination and embedding

A sixty-word sentence can be effortless and a twenty-word sentence can be unreadable. The difference is not length, it is where the branches go, and there is a specific reason English readers can take one shape and not the others.

Lecture 5 of 7 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: Pinker (2014), The Sense of Style, ch. 4; Chomsky & Miller (1963)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Clauses combine in two ways. Coordination joins units of equal rank and names almost no relation between them. Subordination makes one clause depend on another and names the relation explicitly: cause, concession, condition, time. Everything about sentence architecture follows from which you use and, far more importantly, from where you put the subordinate material.

Three placements are possible. Right-branching puts the subordinate material after the main clause is complete. Left-branching puts it before the main clause starts. Centre-embedding inserts it into the middle of the main clause, so the reader has to hold an unfinished structure in memory while processing an unrelated one. English readers handle right-branching almost without limit, left-branching for a clause or two, and centre-embedding to a depth of about one before comprehension collapses.

That asymmetry is not a matter of taste and it is not English chauvinism. It follows from the fact that readers build structure incrementally, word by word, and must hold every incomplete constituent in working memory until it closes. Right-branching closes each constituent before opening the next, so nothing accumulates. Centre-embedding opens constituents inside constituents, so everything accumulates at once. Length is therefore the wrong variable to manage. The right one is how many dependencies are open at the hardest point in the sentence.

Feynman test Think of reading as carrying plates. Right-branching hands you one plate, lets you put it down, then hands you the next: you can do this all day. Centre-embedding hands you a plate, then before you can set it down hands you another, then another, and only at the end lets you put them down in reverse order. Three plates is the limit for most people, and the sentence that requires it is not long, it is just badly stacked.
What is beyond the source Pinker's account of branching and Chomsky and Miller's result on centre-embedding are both about comprehension, not composition, and neither offers a repair procedure. The three repairs in step 4, extract to the right, split, and front as a condition, are this course's addition, assembled from what the diagnosis implies. The claim that statutory drafting convention systematically produces centre-embedding is an observation about legal style, argued by the plain-language legal writing movement rather than by the psycholinguists.
Step 2

Why this matters

The advice most professionals have absorbed about sentences is to make them shorter. It is not wrong, exactly, and it is a blunt instrument aimed at the wrong variable, which is why applying it produces prose that is choppy without being clearer. A writer who understands branching can write long when the material wants length, and can fix the genuinely hard sentences instead of shortening the easy ones.

The stakes are highest in exactly the documents this collection is about. Regulatory text, contracts and technical specifications share a drafting convention with a specific structural consequence: conditions and qualifications are placed before the operative verb, so that no reader can reach the substantive provision without passing through everything that limits it. The intention is precision and the effect is centre-embedding, sometimes to a depth of three or four. When someone says a piece of regulation is impenetrable, they are almost never complaining about its vocabulary.

Professional writing inherits the convention without inheriting the reason for it. A validation report is not a statute; nobody will litigate the placement of a qualifier. Yet the same sentences appear, because the writer learned the register from the regulation. Recognising centre-embedding is therefore a way of noticing when you have imported a legal drafting constraint into a document that has no need of it.

There is a second stake, which is that coordination hides decisions. Two clauses joined by and assert no relation between themselves, so the reader supplies one, and readers supply causation with enthusiasm. A writer who joins with and when they mean because has made a claim they can later disown. Sometimes that is carelessness. Sometimes it is the point, and lecture 1 promised this course would say so when it was.

Step 3

The mental model

The canonical demonstration is a pair of sentences carrying identical information. One is a nursery rhyme. The other is perfectly grammatical and no English speaker can parse it. Chomsky and Miller established the underlying result in 1963, that self-embedding beyond a shallow depth exceeds what a human parser will do even though the grammar permits it without limit; the malt-and-rat illustration built on "This Is the House That Jack Built" has been standard in the literature since, and it remains the cleanest version available.

The mechanism generalises past the toy example. A reader who encounters a subject must hold it open until its verb arrives, and holding costs something that rises with the amount of intervening material. This is the finding formalised in Gibson's dependency locality theory: the cost of integrating a word with the structure it belongs to increases with the distance between them. Centre-embedding maximises those distances by construction. Right-branching minimises them.

Left-branching sits between the two and behaves differently from either. A sentence that opens with a subordinate clause, "if the exposure has migrated to stage 2, the impairment becomes lifetime", makes the reader hold a frame with nothing yet to attach it to, and that is a genuine cost. It is also a small one at shallow depth, and it buys something real: the condition is established before the claim, so the reader never has to retrofit it. One left branch is often the best available choice. Three stacked left branches is the same failure as centre-embedding under another name.

Coordination
Joining units of equal grammatical rank with one of seven coordinators: and, but, or, nor, for, yet, so. The coordinated units must be the same kind of thing, which is the requirement of parallelism.
Subordination
Making one clause a constituent of another, using a subordinator that names the relation. Unlike coordination, subordination is explicit about how the two clauses connect, which is its main advantage and the reason it exposes you to disagreement.
Right-branching
Subordinate material placed after the main clause completes. Each constituent closes before the next opens, so working memory load stays flat and the sentence can grow without becoming harder.
Centre-embedding
Subordinate material inserted inside the main clause, between elements that belong together. Comprehension degrades sharply past one level, independently of sentence length.
Step 4

The detail

The four sentence types, briefly

The traditional four-way classification is worth having as vocabulary and is the least useful thing in this lecture. A simple sentence has one main clause. A compound sentence has two or more main clauses coordinated. A complex sentence has a main clause and at least one subordinate clause. A compound-complex sentence has both. The classification is nearly useless for diagnosis because it says nothing about placement, and placement determines difficulty. A complex sentence can be trivial or impossible depending on where its subordinate clause sits.

Subordinators name relations, and the relation is information

Each subordinator commits you to a specific logical relation, and the commitment is a feature rather than a risk. A reader who meets because knows a cause is being claimed and can dispute it. A reader who meets and knows nothing and will guess, usually generously.

Parallelism, and the failure that follows from ignoring it

Coordination requires the coordinated units to be the same kind of thing. Break that and the reader's parser, which committed to a structure at the coordinator, has to back up and rebuild. Faulty parallelism is therefore a real error rather than a stylistic preference, because it imposes a reparse.

FAULTY PARALLELISM Bad: "The model was recalibrated, revalidated, and we documented the changes." At "and" the reader commits to a third past participle to match "recalibrated, revalidated". They get a new subject and a finite verb instead, and must reparse the whole list. Fixed: "We recalibrated, revalidated and documented the model." CORRELATIVE COORDINATORS ARE STRICTER These come in pairs and require the same kind of unit after each half: both...and, either...or, neither...nor, not only...but also. Bad: "The finding not only affects the mortgage book but also the unsecured portfolio." "not only" is followed by a verb phrase, "but also" by a noun phrase. Fixed: "The finding affects not only the mortgage book but also the unsecured portfolio." Both halves now followed by noun phrases. THE TEST Delete everything after the first half of the pair, then read the second half straight onto the stem. "The finding affects not only the mortgage book" "The finding affects ... the unsecured portfolio" Both work, so the parallelism holds. If the second reading is ungrammatical, you have found the break.

Diagnosing centre-embedding in your own prose

You will not meet the rat and the cat in a validation report. You will meet centre-embedding in three specific disguises, and they are worth learning to recognise by shape.

The first is the qualification wedged between subject and verb, which lecture 4 measured as subject-verb distance. "The revised assumptions, which the committee approved in March following the external review, understate the peak loss rate" holds the subject open for thirteen words. This is centre-embedding at depth one, and depth one is survivable, which is why the construction is so common and so tempting.

The second is the relative clause inside a relative clause. "The exposures that the segments that the model covers include are immaterial" is depth two, and most readers fail on it. In practice writers rarely produce this deliberately; they produce it by revising a sentence twice and inserting each qualification where it seemed to belong.

The third and commonest is the stacked condition. "If, where the obligor is classified as unlikely to pay, the exposure has not been transferred to stage 3, the reporting requirement in paragraph 4 applies." Here a condition has been embedded inside a condition before the main clause has begun. It is a left branch with another left branch inside it, and it is the signature of imported statutory register.

THREE REPAIRS, IN ORDER OF PREFERENCE ORIGINAL (depth 2, 34 words) "If, where the obligor is classified as unlikely to pay, the exposure has not been transferred to stage 3, the reporting requirement in paragraph 4 applies to the originating business unit." REPAIR 1 Extract to the right "Paragraph 4 requires the originating business unit to report any exposure that has not been transferred to stage 3 despite the obligor being classified as unlikely to pay." -> One left branch removed, everything after the verb. Peak open dependencies: 1. REPAIR 2 Split into two sentences "An obligor may be classified as unlikely to pay while the exposure remains outside stage 3. Paragraph 4 requires the originating business unit to report those cases." -> Best when the embedded condition is itself worth stating as a fact rather than as a qualifier. REPAIR 3 Front as a single shallow condition "Where an unlikely-to-pay obligor's exposure has not been transferred to stage 3, the originating business unit must report it under paragraph 4." -> One left branch, no nesting. Keeps the conditional framing when the condition genuinely governs. WHICH TO USE Repair 1 by default. Repair 2 when the sentence is carrying two claims. Repair 3 when the condition must precede the obligation for legal or logical reasons, which in non-statutory writing is rarer than people think.

Garden paths, and why they are a branching problem

A garden-path sentence leads the reader into a parse that turns out to be wrong. Pinker's examples are the standard ones: "the horse raced past the barn fell", where raced looks like the main verb until fell arrives and forces a reanalysis in which raced past the barn was a reduced relative clause all along. Or "the man who hunts ducks out on weekends", where ducks looks like the object of hunts until out reveals it as the main verb.

These are not tricks, and they belong in this lecture rather than in a chapter of curiosities, because they are what centre-embedding feels like from the inside at the moment of failure. The reader has committed to a structure, held it open, and been forced to discard it. The professional version is duller and just as costly: a sentence in which a participle could be either a reduced relative or a main verb, or in which a noun could be either an object or a new subject, and the reader has to read to the end before finding out. The repair is nearly always to restore the word that resolves the ambiguity, usually that or which, both of which writers delete in the belief that they are tightening the prose.

This gives a small practical rule that runs against the usual advice. If deleting that creates even a momentary ambiguity about whether what follows is a new clause, put it back. It costs one word and saves a reparse. "He said the model failed" is fine; "he told the committee the model owner had approved in March the assumptions were wrong" is not, and the missing that is why.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The long sentence is not the enemy

Everything in this lecture cuts against the most widely repeated piece of writing advice in professional life, which is to keep sentences short. The advice survives because it correlates with the right answer often enough to be useful and because it is easy to state. It is still aimed at the wrong variable, and the cost of aiming at the wrong variable is that you cannot tell a good long sentence from a bad short one.

A right-branching sentence of sixty words can be read at speed, because the reader closes each constituent as they go and never carries a load. Long sentences of that shape are one of the genuine pleasures of English prose and they do something no sequence of short sentences can: they hold a set of related things in a single frame, so the reader receives them as one thought rather than five. Module 5 devotes a lecture to how to build them. What matters here is the negative version of the claim: shortening a centre-embedded sentence does not fix it, and lengthening a right-branching one does not break it.

There is a real dissent, and it is worth taking seriously rather than dismissing. Klinkenborg argues in Several Short Sentences About Writing that the long sentence is a trap for most writers most of the time, not because readers cannot process it but because writers cannot control it, and that the discipline of the short sentence forces you to know what each one claims. That is an argument about the writer rather than the reader, which is why it does not contradict anything above. Module 5 gives him the last word on the subject; this lecture only insists that if you do write long, branching is what decides whether you get away with it.

Why English is like this

The asymmetry is partly a fact about English specifically. English is a strongly right-branching language: it puts heads before their complements, so modifiers naturally accumulate to the right, and its readers are practised at that shape from childhood. Languages with the opposite word order distribute the difficulty differently, and their readers tolerate left-branching structures that would defeat an English reader. Centre-embedding, though, is hard everywhere, because the constraint there is working memory rather than habit.

The practical consequence is that two different things have been bundled together in this lecture and should be separated when you apply it. The preference for right over left branching is a fact about English and about what its readers are trained on, so it is a strong default with real exceptions. The limit on centre-embedding is a fact about human memory, so it is not a preference at all. You can write a good left-branching sentence. You cannot write a good triply centre-embedded one, and no amount of skill will change that.

Common failure Using this lecture as a licence to write long. The material here explains why some long sentences work; it does not claim they are better. A document of uniformly long sentences is exhausting whatever its branching, because sentence length is also a rhythmic variable and uniformity in any variable reads as machine output. Module 5, lecture 4 handles variation. Until then, treat branching as a diagnosis for the sentences that are already too hard rather than as permission for the ones that are not.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take the three repairs in step 4 and run all three on a genuinely difficult sentence from a regulation or policy document you work with. Produce all three versions, then say which you would use and why. Producing the repair you would not choose is the part that teaches.
  2. Why is peak open dependencies a better predictor of difficulty than word count? Give the pair of sentences from step 3 as your evidence.
  3. Distinguish coordination from subordination in terms of what each one tells the reader about the relation between two clauses.
  4. Right-branching, left-branching and centre-embedding: state the working memory consequence of each in one sentence.
  5. Which of the three limits in this lecture is a fact about English and which is a fact about human memory? Why does the distinction change how strictly you should apply each?
  6. Find a sentence in your own writing with a subject-verb distance over ten words. Identify what is sitting in the gap, then move it after the verb. Note whether the meaning changed, and if it did, what that tells you.
  7. Take three sentences from your own work that join clauses with "and". For each, name the relation from the table in step 4 that you actually meant, then decide whether asserting it explicitly would expose a claim you are not ready to defend.
  8. Klinkenborg argues against the long sentence on grounds that have nothing to do with reader processing. State his argument in one sentence, then say why it does not contradict this lecture.
