Punctuation as a signalling system

Punctuation does not mark where you would pause if you read aloud. It marks grammatical structure, ranked by the strength of the break, and one comma can change what a sentence claims about the world.

Lecture 6 of 7 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: Pinker (2014), ch. 6; Quirk et al. (1985), Appendix III
Step 1

The 30-second version

English punctuation is a system for signalling syntax. Each mark tells the reader something specific about how the units around it relate: whether a clause has ended, whether what follows is subordinate, whether a modifier is defining or merely adding. The marks form a rough hierarchy of break strength, from the full stop down through the semicolon, colon and dash to the comma and finally to no mark at all.

The thing almost everyone believes about punctuation is that it represents pauses in speech. That belief is a survival from eighteenth-century elocution teaching, and it is the direct cause of most punctuation errors made by educated adults. Speech and writing carve up an utterance differently, so a reader who punctuates by ear will place commas where they would draw breath, which is frequently between a long subject and its verb, exactly where a comma is wrong.

One consequence matters more than all the rest and it is not a matter of correctness. The presence or absence of a comma around a relative clause changes the truth conditions of the sentence. "The models that failed validation were withdrawn" says some models failed and those were withdrawn. "The models, which failed validation, were withdrawn" says all of them were withdrawn and incidentally all of them failed. Those are different claims about the world, and in a document that anyone might rely on, the difference is the whole point of learning this.

Feynman test Punctuation is the set of joints in a sentence, and each joint has a rated strength. A full stop is a break. A semicolon is a hinge between two things that could stand alone. A colon is an arrow pointing forwards at what is coming. A comma is a light tack, and it cannot take the load of joining two whole sentences, which is why the comma splice fails: you used a tack where the job needed a hinge.
What is beyond the source Quirk gives the descriptive account, and Pinker gives the argument against the pause theory. Neither ranks the marks explicitly as a load-bearing hierarchy; that framing is this lecture's, and it is a teaching device rather than a claim about English. Its justification is that it predicts the errors: nearly every punctuation mistake in professional prose is a mark used below its rated strength, and the ladder makes that visible in a way a mark-by-mark list does not.
Step 2

Why this matters

Punctuation has an unearned reputation as the trivial end of writing, the domain of pedants and copy editors, and there is a decent reason for that reputation: most punctuation errors do no damage. A missing serial comma usually costs nothing. An apostrophe in the wrong place makes the writer look careless and leaves the meaning intact. If that were the whole story, this lecture would be twenty minutes of etiquette.

It is not the whole story, because a small number of punctuation choices are semantic. The restrictive and non-restrictive distinction is the largest of them, and it has produced real litigation. Contracts have turned on whether a comma made a qualifying phrase apply to one item in a list or to all of them. Regulatory text is punctuated with more care than it is written, precisely because drafters know this. A credit policy that says "exposures which are more than ninety days past due" rather than "exposures that are more than ninety days past due" has, on a strict reading, said something about all exposures.

The second reason is cumulative rather than dramatic. Punctuation is how a reader locates clause boundaries, and locating clause boundaries is the first step in the incremental parsing described in lecture 5. Mispunctuation therefore does not merely look wrong, it briefly misdirects the parser. A comma between a long subject and its verb tells the reader that the subject has ended when it has not, and they must back up. Individually the cost is a fraction of a second. Across a document it is the difference between prose that reads smoothly and prose that feels like hard going for reasons the reader cannot name.

The third reason is diagnostic and unexpected. Punctuation trouble is often a symptom of structural trouble. When you genuinely cannot decide how to punctuate a sentence, the usual reason is that its grammatical relationships are unclear, which is to say that you have not yet decided what depends on what. The sentence needs restructuring rather than a mark. Learning the system therefore gives you an early-warning instrument for a problem that has nothing to do with punctuation.

Step 3

The mental model

Arrange the marks by how strong a break they signal, then ask of each one what it is licensed to join. The hierarchy explains almost every common error in one move, because errors are nearly always a mark used below its rated strength: a comma asked to do a semicolon's work, or a semicolon asked to do a colon's.

Panel B is the reason punctuation belongs in a foundations module rather than in an appendix. A restrictive modifier is part of the identification of the thing: it tells you which models are being talked about, and without it you do not know. A non-restrictive modifier assumes the thing is already identified and adds a comment. The commas are the only signal English provides for that distinction in writing, which puts a great deal of semantic weight on a mark most people place by ear.

Restrictive modification
A modifier that narrows the reference of what it attaches to, so removing it changes which things are meant. Takes no commas. "Exposures that are past due" picks out a subset.
Non-restrictive modification
A modifier that comments on an already-identified reference, so removing it leaves the reference unchanged. Takes commas, dashes or brackets. "Exposures, which are past due" says all of them are.
Comma splice
Two independent clauses joined by a comma alone. An error because a comma signals a within-clause break, so it tells the reader the clause is continuing when it has ended. Fix with a full stop, a semicolon, or a coordinator.
Parenthesis
Any interrupting element set off from the main clause. English offers three strengths: commas are quietest, brackets are an aside the reader may skip, and dashes are the loudest and most emphatic.
Step 4

The detail

The marks, one at a time

MarkWhat it actually signalsThe error it invites
Marks that join clauses
Semicolon Two independent clauses of equal weight, with no relation named. Also separates list items that themselves contain commas. Used where a colon is wanted, when the second clause explains the first rather than balancing it.
Colon Forward reference. What precedes it should be a complete clause; what follows delivers on it, whether a list, an explanation or a single word. Placed after an incomplete clause, typically after "include" or "such as", which need nothing.
Comma plus coordinator Two independent clauses joined by and, but, or. The comma is optional when both clauses are short. Dropping the coordinator and keeping the comma, which produces the splice.
Marks that separate within a clause
Comma, paired Non-restrictive modification, or any interruption. Both commas are obligatory; one alone is an error. Opening the interruption and forgetting to close it, which strands the reader.
Comma, single After an introductory element, before a coordinator joining clauses, between items in a list. Inserted between a long subject and its verb, because that is where the writer would breathe.
Dash, paired An emphatic interruption. The most conspicuous of the three parenthesis strengths. Overuse, which flattens the emphasis it exists to provide. Also barred outright by many house styles.
Brackets An aside the reader is licensed to skip. Quieter than commas, not louder. Putting load-bearing content inside them, which tells the reader it does not matter.
Marks inside words and phrases
Hyphen Binds a compound modifier before the noun it modifies: "point-in-time PD", "a high-risk exposure". Omitted, creating genuine ambiguity: "a small business owner" against "a small-business owner".
Apostrophe Possession or contraction. Not plurality, ever. its against it's, which is the one error readers reliably notice and judge.

The comma splice, and the one respectable exception

A comma cannot join two independent clauses in standard written English. "The model was recalibrated, the override rate fell" is a splice, and the fix is a full stop, a semicolon, a coordinator, or a subordinator that names the relation, which lecture 5 argued is usually the best of the four because it makes you decide what you meant.

The exception is real and narrow. Short, closely parallel clauses are conventionally spliced for rhetorical effect: "I came, I saw, I conquered." The construction works because the parallelism itself signals the structure, so the comma is not being asked to carry the join. It is not available for two ordinary clauses of unequal shape, and it is not available at all in a document whose readers include a regulator.

The colon needs a complete clause in front of it

This is the error that most reliably distinguishes careful from careless professional prose, because it is invisible to anyone punctuating by ear.

THE COLON TEST Read only what comes BEFORE the colon. Can it stand alone as a sentence? If not, the colon is wrong. WRONG "The three drivers of the increase are: migration, new business, and the model change." Before the colon: "The three drivers of the increase are" -> incomplete. The colon is separating a verb from its own complement. RIGHT, three ways "The three drivers of the increase are migration, new business and the model change." -> no mark needed at all "The increase had three drivers: migration, new business and the model change." -> complete clause, then the colon delivers "Three drivers explain the increase. They are migration, new business and the model change." -> split, which is often best when the list is long THE SAME ERROR IN ITS COMMONEST DISGUISE "This includes:" -> "includes" needs its object "Such as:" -> not a clause at all "The requirements are:" -> incomplete Note that bulleted lists do not suspend the rule. A stem that would be wrong before a colon in running prose is still wrong before a colon introducing bullets.

That, which, and the convention that is not a rule

Many style guides instruct you to use that for restrictive clauses and which for non-restrictive ones. The instruction traces to a proposal by H. W. Fowler, who was explicit that he was recommending a tidying-up rather than describing English as it stood, and it has been adopted more firmly in American editing than in British.

The descriptive facts are that which has introduced restrictive clauses in good English writing for centuries and continues to do so, and that the distinction English actually marks is the commas, not the relative pronoun. So the that and which rule is a house convention: arbitrary, socially enforced in some houses, and worth following where it is enforced. Lecture 7 gives that category a name and puts several other familiar rules into it.

Which leaves a practical instruction with two halves. Punctuate the distinction correctly, because the commas are what carry the meaning and getting them wrong changes the claim. Follow your organisation's that and which convention as a matter of house style, and do not imagine that following it excuses you from the commas or that ignoring it is an error of grammar.

The serial comma, where it earns its keep

The comma before the final and in a list is optional in British practice and standard in much American practice. Most of the time it makes no difference, which is why the argument about it is disproportionate to the stakes. It earns its keep in exactly one situation, which is when the last two items could be read as a unit modifying or appositive to something earlier.

WHERE THE SERIAL COMMA MATTERS Ambiguous: "I would like to thank my parents, Ayn Rand and God." Two readings: three groups thanked, or the writer's parents are named as Ayn Rand and God. A professional version of the same trap: "The report was reviewed by the model owner, the validation lead and the CRO." Fine. Now: "The report was reviewed by the second line, the validation lead and the model risk committee." Is the validation lead an apposition explaining who the second line is? A serial comma resolves it: "...the second line, the validation lead, and the model risk committee." -> three reviewers THE RULE THAT ACTUALLY HELPS Do not adopt a global position. Use the serial comma whenever the last two items could be read as apposition to an earlier item, and otherwise follow your house style. The mark is a disambiguator, not a badge of allegiance.

Quotation and British convention

British practice places punctuation according to logic: a mark goes inside the closing quotation mark only if it belongs to the quoted material. American practice places commas and full stops inside regardless. This matters here because the collection is written in British English, and because the logical convention is the one that preserves information: a reader can tell whether the full stop was part of the original.

The related point is about quoting technical definitions, which professional writing does constantly. When you quote a regulatory definition, quote it exactly, including its punctuation, and put anything you have changed in square brackets. A silently repunctuated quotation of a legal definition is a misquotation, and given what the previous sections established about commas changing claims, it can be a substantive one.

The dash, and a live example of a house convention

The dash is the strongest interrupting mark English has and it does something the others cannot: it creates emphasis by breaking the sentence violently and then resuming. Used sparingly it is one of the best tools available. Used often it becomes a tic, and its emphasis decays to nothing.

Many house styles, including the one governing this collection, prohibit the dash as punctuation entirely, requiring commas, colons, full stops or brackets instead. That is worth examining rather than merely obeying, because it is a clean specimen of the distinction lecture 7 is about. The prohibition has no basis in English grammar. It is a convention, adopted for defensible reasons, that dashes are overused, that they encourage loose sentence construction by making any afterthought attachable, and that a consistent house voice is worth more than the occasional gain from a well-placed one. Knowing that it is a convention rather than a rule is what lets you follow it without confusion and argue about it on the right grounds.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Where the pause theory came from, and why it persists

Eighteenth-century English grammarians and elocutionists taught punctuation as a notation for reading aloud, assigning relative pause durations to the comma, semicolon and colon. That was a coherent system for a culture in which reading aloud was normal. It is the wrong model for silent reading, and it survives in schools because it is easy to teach and produces acceptable results most of the time.

The reason it persists among adults is more interesting. Punctuating by ear works because speech prosody and syntax are correlated: we do tend to pause at clause boundaries. The correlation is good enough that the heuristic rarely fails conspicuously, and it fails in a systematic direction. Speakers pause after a long subject, before the verb, to manage breath. Writers taught to punctuate by ear therefore insert a comma in exactly the place English forbids it, and they do it in precisely the sentences that were already hard because of subject-verb distance. The heuristic breaks worst where you can least afford it.

What punctuation cannot fix

There is a temptation, once the system is clear, to treat punctuation as a repair tool. It is not one. A sentence that needs a semicolon to hold it together is usually a sentence that should be two. A sentence with three sets of paired commas has an embedding problem that no arrangement of marks will solve. Punctuation makes structure visible; it does not improve structure.

This gives the most useful heuristic in the lecture, and it is a diagnostic rather than a rule. When you find yourself unable to decide how to punctuate a sentence, stop trying. The indecision is information: it means the grammatical relationships in the sentence are genuinely unclear, most often because a modifier could attach in two places or because you have not decided whether a qualification is restrictive. Resolve the structure and the punctuation resolves itself, every time.

The honest scope of the semantic claim

One qualification, since this lecture has leaned hard on the restrictive and non-restrictive distinction. Readers do not apply it with perfect consistency, and a sympathetic reader will often recover the intended meaning from context regardless of the commas. The claim is not that every reader parses every comma correctly. It is that the commas are the only signal available, that careful readers do use them, and that in documents where someone may later dispute what was meant, the only defensible position is to punctuate the distinction deliberately.

That is a narrower claim than the one usually made and it survives contact with real readers, which the stronger version does not. It also tells you where to spend effort. Punctuate the modifiers that carry conditions and definitions with real care. Everywhere else, be consistent and stop worrying.

Common failure Discovering the semicolon. Writers who learn what it does start using it constantly, because it lets two related thoughts stay in one sentence and that feels like sophistication. The effect on a reader is a document with no full stops, in which every idea is presented as connected to the next and none is allowed to land. The stress position that module 2 will teach you to exploit exists at the end of a sentence. A semicolon denies it to the clause before.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take the colon test from step 4 and apply it to every colon in a document you did not write, including the ones introducing bulleted lists. Produce the three repairs for one failing case, then write a failing case deliberately and explain to yourself why it fails.
  2. State what punctuation signals, and say why the pause theory produces errors in a predictable location rather than at random.
  3. Write one pair of sentences from your own domain in which adding two commas changes the claim. Say precisely what each version asserts about how many things are involved.
  4. Why is a comma splice an error in terms of signalling rather than in terms of etiquette? What are the four available repairs, and which does lecture 5 argue is usually best?
  5. Rank commas, brackets and dashes by how much prominence each gives an interruption. What does putting load-bearing content in brackets tell the reader?
  6. The that and which rule is a house convention rather than a rule of English. State the descriptive facts that make it a convention, then say what you must still get right regardless of which convention you follow.
  7. Find a sentence in your own writing where you could not decide how to punctuate it. Diagnose the structural problem underneath rather than choosing a mark.
  8. The ban on dashes in this collection's house style has no basis in grammar. Give the strongest case for the ban and the strongest case against it, and say which you find more persuasive.
