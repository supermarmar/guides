Module 1 · Writing track

Foundations

The raw material of prose: words, clauses, sentences, and the marks that hold them together. Seven lectures that give you the vocabulary to say what is wrong with a sentence rather than only feel that something is.

What this module is

Everything the rest of the course teaches is stated in grammatical terms. You cannot be told to move the action into a finite verb until you know what a finite verb is, and you cannot spot a nominalisation without knowing what a noun is doing in a clause. This module supplies that vocabulary and stops there.

It stops there on purpose. Graham and Perin's meta-analysis of writing instruction found that traditional grammar teaching, taught as a body of rules to be mastered, has an effect size of minus 0.32 on writing quality: it makes writing measurably worse, presumably by displacing time that would be better spent writing and by convincing students that correctness is the goal. So nothing here is a rule to obey. Every term is introduced as an instrument of diagnosis, and the final lecture is devoted entirely to separating the constraints that are real from the superstitions that have been passed off as rules for two centuries.

Prerequisites

None. This is the entry point to the track.

Module sequence

Part 1 · The premise and the word (lectures 1 to 2)

Why writing is hard in a way that speaking is not, and what a word carries beyond its dictionary sense.

  • builtLecture 1: what writing is for. Writing as a one-way channel with no feedback loop, and the reader-expectation premise that governs the whole course: meaning is what readers construct, not what writers intend.
  • builtLecture 2: words. Denotation, connotation and register. The Anglo-Saxon and Latinate layers of English vocabulary, and why "use" and "utilise" differ in effect where they agree in sense.

Part 2 · The clause and the sentence (lectures 3 to 5)

The syntactic machinery. Function before label throughout: what an element does in a clause matters, what it is called does not.

  • builtLecture 3: parts of speech as functions. Nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, prepositions, determiners and conjunctions defined by the work they do rather than by definitions you memorise. The prerequisite for spotting a nominalisation two modules from now.
  • builtLecture 4: the clause. Subject, verb, object, complement and adjunct. Finite against non-finite, main against subordinate, and the small set of clause patterns that English actually uses.
  • builtLecture 5: coordination, subordination and embedding. Simple, compound, complex and compound-complex sentences. Right-branching, left-branching and centre-embedding, and Pinker's explanation of why English readers tolerate the first far better than the other two.

Part 3 · Marks and myths (lectures 6 to 7)

Punctuation as syntax made visible, then a reckoning with the rules that are not rules.

  • builtLecture 6: punctuation as a signalling system. Comma, semicolon, colon, dash and full stop ranked by the strength of break each marks. Restrictive against non-restrictive modification, and why that one comma changes what the sentence claims.
  • builtLecture 7: prescriptivism and descriptivism. Split infinitives, sentence-final prepositions, "none", which against that, singular they. Pinker's three-way split between real constraints, house conventions and superstitions, with Pullum's attack on Strunk and White as the case study and the defences of it given a fair hearing.
