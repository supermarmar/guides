Words

English carries two vocabularies for most of what you want to say, one inherited and one borrowed. Choosing between them is not a matter of taste, and it is not a matter of length either, though almost everyone who gives advice about it thinks it is.

Lecture 2 of 7 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: Baugh & Cable, A History of the English Language; Joos (1961), The Five Clocks
Step 1

The 30-second version

A word carries three things at once. Its denotation is what it points at. Its connotation is the attitude it smuggles in alongside. Its register is the social situation it belongs to, and therefore the situation it implies you think you are in. Two words can share a denotation exactly and still be non-interchangeable, because the other two layers differ.

English makes this unusually visible, for a historical reason. The language absorbed a French vocabulary after 1066 and a Latin and Greek vocabulary during the Renaissance, and it kept the native Germanic words rather than discarding them. The result is a language with two or often three words for the same concept, stratified by origin: ask, question, interrogate. They denote the same act. Nobody would use them in the same sentence.

The advice usually attached to this fact is Orwell's: never use a long word where a short one will do. That advice is right about ninety per cent of the time and wrong in a way that matters, because it mistakes the cause for the effect. Latinate words are not worse because they are long. They are, on average, less frequent, and less frequent words take measurably longer to read. Length is a proxy for frequency, frequency is a proxy for processing cost, and processing cost is the thing you actually care about. Once you see the chain, you can also see when to break the rule, which Orwell's version of it never lets you do.

Feynman test English is a house where the family who built it and the family who bought it both still live, and neither threw anything out. So there are two of everything: two words for the animal in the field and the meat on the plate, two words for asking and for enquiring, two words for a house and a residence. When you pick one, you are telling the reader which room of the house you are standing in.
What is beyond the source The histories describe the stratification; the usage guides prescribe the short word; neither connects the two to the reading research. The joining claim in this lecture, that the Anglo-Saxon preference is really a frequency preference wearing a costume, is an inference from the word frequency effect in eye-movement studies rather than something Orwell or Baugh and Cable argued. It earns its place because it predicts the exceptions, and the length rule does not.
Step 2

Why this matters

Word choice is the only part of writing that most professionals believe they are already doing deliberately. They are usually doing something else: reaching for whichever word signals that they belong. Institutions run on this. A junior analyst writes "utilise" not because they have weighed it against "use" but because the papers they have read all say "utilise", and imitating those papers is how you demonstrate that you have read them. The vocabulary is a membership badge, and worn as a badge it stops being a choice.

The cost lands in two places. The first is comprehension, and it is small per word and large per document. Readers fixate low-frequency words for longer and regress to them more often, so a paragraph built from Latinate synonyms is genuinely slower to read than the same paragraph in the native vocabulary, and the slowdown is invisible to the writer because the writer already knows what the paragraph says. Multiply a hundred milliseconds by four hundred words and you have spent your reader's patience on nothing.

The second cost is precision, and it runs the other way. Sometimes the Latinate word is the correct word and the plain synonym is not, because the Latinate word has been captured as a term of art. In a bank, impairment is not a fancy way of saying loss. It denotes a specific accounting measurement under a specific standard, and a writer who simplifies it to "loss" in a financial statement has not written more plainly, they have written something else. Terms of art are the standing exception to every rule in this lecture, and knowing which words in your field are terms of art is part of knowing your field.

So the practical stake is a discrimination, not a preference. You need to be able to tell the difference between a Latinate word that is doing work no other word does, and a Latinate word that is there because everyone else's is. Nearly all institutional prose fails to make that distinction, in the direction of keeping both.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture the English lexicon as three layers laid down at three different times. The bottom layer is Germanic: the Old English core, which supplies almost all the grammatical machinery and the vocabulary of the body, the household, the weather and the farm. On top of it sits a French layer, deposited after the Norman Conquest of 1066, which supplies law, government, the army, the church hierarchy, cookery and courtesy, because those were the domains the new ruling class controlled. On top of that sits a Latin and Greek layer, borrowed directly and deliberately from the sixteenth century onwards to give English the technical and abstract vocabulary its writers felt it lacked.

Because each layer arrived without displacing the last, the same concept often surfaces at all three levels, and the three words are not synonyms in use even where they are synonyms in sense. Reading down a triplet from Germanic to Latin, three things happen together and they are easy to confuse. The word gets longer. The word gets rarer. And the word gets more formal, which is to say it gets more strongly associated with situations of distance and authority. It is the second of those, rarity, that costs your reader time.

The diagram invites one wrong conclusion, so it is worth blocking now. It is not a quality ranking with the good words on the left. It is a cost ranking, and cost is only worth paying when something is bought with it. What the right-hand column buys, when it buys anything, is precision: ascend specifies an unbroken upward movement in a way that rise does not, and interrogate specifies a hostile and formal questioning that ask flatly denies. When the right-hand word buys nothing, as utilise buys nothing over use, you have paid the reader's time for a badge.

Denotation
The class of things a word picks out. Two words with the same denotation are referentially interchangeable: swapping them leaves the truth conditions of the sentence unchanged.
Connotation
The evaluative and affective freight a word carries beyond its denotation. Thrifty and stingy denote the same disposition and disagree completely about whether it is a virtue.
Register
The variety of language appropriate to a situation, defined by the relationship between the participants rather than by the subject matter. Register errors are read as social errors, not as writing errors.
Term of art
A word whose meaning inside a discipline has been fixed by definition, so that a plain-language synonym is not a synonym. In these cases the technical word is the clear word.
Step 4

The detail

Connotation is not decoration

The standard demonstration is a set of words that share a denotation and differ in judgement. A government that reduces spending has made a saving, a cut, an efficiency, a reduction or an austerity measure, and every one of those describes the same arithmetic. The choice does not colour the fact; it argues about it. This is why the instruction to write neutrally is harder than it sounds. There is no neutral word for a contested thing, only words whose bias is currently invisible to you.

Osgood and his colleagues measured this in the 1950s and found that the affective content of a word factors into three dimensions with striking regularity across languages: evaluation, whether the word is good or bad; potency, whether it is strong or weak; and activity, whether it is fast or slow. That result is worth carrying around, because it tells you what to check. When you suspect a word of editorialising, ask which of the three it is doing. Robust smuggles in potency. Aggressive smuggles in evaluation and activity together, which is why it can praise a sales team and condemn a provisioning assumption in the same report.

Two connotative traps recur in professional writing. The first is the euphemism, which is not merely dishonest but unstable: a word adopted to avoid an unpleasant association acquires that association within a generation and has to be replaced, so the euphemism treadmill keeps turning and the reader learns to translate. If your document says a portfolio is challenged, every experienced reader converts that to bad and adds a note about the writer. The second trap is the dead intensifier. Significant, material, substantial and considerable have been used so often as padding that they now cost you credibility in the one situation where you need them, which is when the thing genuinely is significant.

Register: five settings, not two

Formality is usually discussed as a dial with two positions, formal and informal, and that is too coarse to be useful. Martin Joos proposed five, and the fivefold split has survived because it maps onto real situations. Frozen register is language fixed by ritual or statute, where the words cannot be varied at all: the wording of a contract, a liturgy, a warning label. Formal register addresses a group with no expectation of reply, which is the register of the published paper and the regulatory submission. Consultative register addresses one informed person and expects them to interrupt, which is the register of good professional email and of this lecture. Casual register assumes shared background and drops the explanations. Intimate register uses private shorthand that outsiders cannot parse at all.

The practical value of the split is that it exposes the mistake most professional writers actually make, which is not excessive formality but the wrong distance. A validation report written in frozen register, all defined terms and no visible author, reads as evasive because frozen register belongs to documents nobody is answerable for. A board paper written in casual register reads as presumptuous. Neither error is about vocabulary difficulty, and neither is fixed by shortening words. They are fixed by deciding who you are talking to and how far away they are.

Why the short word usually wins

Here is the mechanism the usage guides skip. Eye-tracking studies of reading have established the word frequency effect as one of the most reliable findings in the field: readers fixate high-frequency words for less time than low-frequency words of equal length, skip them more often, and regress to them less. The effect is on the order of tens of milliseconds per word, which sounds negligible and is not, because it compounds and because regressions are far more expensive than fixations.

Now put that beside a fact about English etymology: the native Germanic vocabulary is, on average, both shorter and vastly more frequent than the Latinate vocabulary, because it supplies the words for the things everyone talks about constantly. So when Orwell tells you to prefer the short word, he is giving you a rule of thumb that correlates well with frequency without mentioning frequency. It works for the same reason a rule about weight would work as a proxy for volume: usually fine, occasionally embarrassing.

The embarrassing cases are the point. Use is shorter and commoner than utilise, so both the length rule and the frequency rule agree, and you should write use. But begin is longer than ope would be if English had kept it, and shorter than commence, and the reason to prefer begin over commence is not that it saves three characters. Better: compare get with obtain. Get is shorter and far more frequent, and in a legal document obtain may still be right, because get is so frequent and so polysemous that it has become vague. Frequency helps until it tips into ambiguity, and then precision takes over. A rule stated in terms of length can never see that boundary.

The case of "utilise"

This one deserves its own treatment because it is the purest example of a Latinate word that buys nothing, and because the defence of it is instructive. Garner's judgement is blunt: utilize is a needless variant of use in almost every context in which it appears. The word does have a narrow legitimate sense, making practical or profitable use of something not obviously suited to the purpose, as in a factory that utilises waste heat. That sense is real and it is not what your colleague means when they write that the model utilises a logistic link function.

THE UTILISE TEST Ask one question of every "utilise" in a draft: Does replacing it with "use" change the meaning? If no -> it was a badge. Replace it. If yes -> you meant "put to profitable use for a purpose it was not designed for", which is rare and worth keeping. Check that the sentence makes that clear, because most readers will not catch the nuance. WORKED 1. "The PD model utilises twelve behavioural variables." -> "uses". Meaning unchanged. It was a badge. 2. "The bank utilises its surplus deposit funding to support the mortgage book." -> "uses" works, but here "deploys" is better still, because the real point is direction, not usage. 3. "The plant utilises waste heat from the smelter." -> keep. Something not designed as fuel is being made to serve as fuel. This is the word's actual job. Base rate, on any corpus of professional prose you like: roughly one legitimate "utilise" for every thirty printed.

The general lesson generalises past this one word. The question is never whether a word is Latinate, long or technical. The question is whether the plain alternative changes the claim. If it does not, the fancy word is costing your reader time and buying you nothing but the appearance of belonging. If it does, the fancy word is the clear word and the plain one is an error.

Terms of art, and the limit of plain language

Every regulated discipline defines some of its vocabulary, and the definitions are what make the discipline's documents mean anything. In banking, default is defined by regulation and not by ordinary usage, exposure at default denotes a specific quantity, and impairment is an accounting measurement under a named standard. Writing "when a customer stops paying" instead of "at default" is not plainer, it is a different and weaker claim, because the regulatory definition includes obligors who are ninety days past due and obligors deemed unlikely to pay, and the paraphrase captures neither boundary.

The discipline this imposes is worth stating as a rule, since it is the one rule in this lecture that has no exceptions. Define a term of art on first use, in its own sentence, and then use exactly that term every time thereafter. Do not vary it for elegance. Prose gains from synonym variation everywhere except here, where variation makes the reader wonder whether you meant something new. This is also why professional writing tolerates a degree of repetition that would be intolerable in an essay, and why writers trained on essays find technical writing so uncomfortable.

The two vocabularies of legal English

One historical curiosity has a practical payoff. Legal English is full of doublets: last will and testament, breaking and entering, free and clear, goods and chattels. Mellinkoff's account is that many of these pair a native English word with its Norman French equivalent, from a period when both languages were in use in English courts and a scribe who used only one risked being misunderstood by half the room. The pairing was insurance.

The insurance expired six centuries ago and the doublets remain, which makes them the clearest available example of vocabulary surviving as ritual after its function has gone. Look for the same pattern in your own field's boilerplate. Phrases like "adequate and appropriate", "monitor and oversee", "review and challenge" often turn out to be doublets in exactly this sense: two words for one idea, retained because the phrase has become the unit rather than the words. Sometimes the pair does distinguish two things, and then it stays. Frequently it does not, and cutting one word costs nothing at all.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Orwell was right for the wrong reason, and knew it

"Politics and the English Language" contains the most quoted demonstration in the whole literature of style. Orwell takes a verse from Ecclesiastes, "I returned, and saw under the sun, that the race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong", and rewrites it into modern institutional English: "Objective considerations of contemporary phenomena compel the conclusion that success or failure in competitive activities exhibits no tendency to be commensurate with innate capacity." The parody is devastating and it is also carefully rigged, since Orwell has changed the vocabulary, the syntax, the concreteness and the presence of a human speaker all at once, then presented the result as evidence about vocabulary.

Unpack what actually happened between the two versions. The Biblical verse has a speaker who says "I", a set of physical images, and a sequence of short parallel clauses. The parody has no speaker, no image, and one long clause with a nominalised subject. Vocabulary is the least of it. Almost all the damage was done by removing the human character from the subject position and packing the actions into nouns, which is Williams's diagnosis and the subject of the whole of module 2. Orwell's own list of rules puts the vocabulary rules first and the passive voice third, and the passive is not even really the problem either. He got the target right and the aim wrong.

This matters more than a point of intellectual history, because a rule aimed at the wrong mechanism cannot be applied intelligently. A writer who obeys "never use a long word where a short one will do" will convert ascertain to find out, which is an improvement, and will also convert impairment to loss, which is an error, and has no principle available for telling the two cases apart. A writer who understands that the goal is to reduce processing cost without losing precision can decide each case on its merits, and will get both right.

Where the anti-Latinate position becomes a pose

There is a countervailing failure, and it is fashionable enough to be worth naming. Plain-English enthusiasm can curdle into a performance of bluffness, in which short words are chosen for their shortness and the writer starts sounding like someone pretending they have not read a book. The tell is usually a monosyllabic vocabulary carrying an argument that needs distinctions the vocabulary cannot make. Anglo-Saxon words are excellent for concrete things and poor for abstract ones, which is precisely why English borrowed the abstract vocabulary in the first place. A course that argued otherwise would be recommending you write about capital adequacy with the word stock of a farm.

The honest position is less quotable than either extreme. Prefer the common word, because it is cheaper to read. Keep the rare word when it says something the common word cannot, and know what that something is. Keep every term of art exactly as defined. And notice, when you reach for the rare word, whether you are reaching for precision or for cover, because the two feel identical from the inside and the reader can tell them apart.

Common failure Running a thesaurus over a finished draft to vary the vocabulary. This does two kinds of damage at once. It replaces frequent words with rarer ones, which raises reading cost for no gain, and it varies terms of art, which makes readers hunt for a distinction you did not intend. Synonym variation is the wrong instinct in professional prose. Repeat the defined term and vary the sentence instead.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take the Ecclesiastes verse and Orwell's parody from step 5. Write your own pair on a subject from your own work: first the plain version with a human subject and concrete images, then the institutional version of the same claim. Change only what Orwell changed. Then list which of the four changes, vocabulary, syntax, concreteness, or the missing speaker, did the most damage in your version.
  2. Distinguish denotation, connotation and register in one sentence each, without using the word "meaning".
  3. Why is "prefer the short word" a proxy rule rather than a real one? What is the underlying variable, and what evidence supports it?
  4. Give a word from your own field that is a term of art, and write the plain-English paraphrase that a careless writer would substitute for it. State exactly what the paraphrase loses.
  5. Joos's five registers: which one does most professional email actually need, and which one do writers reach for when they want to avoid being answerable?
  6. Find a document you wrote this year and search it for "utilise", "commence", "endeavour", "ascertain" and "in respect of". For each hit, apply the utilise test from step 4. Count how many survive it.
  7. Osgood's three dimensions are evaluation, potency and activity. Take the word "robust" and say which dimension it loads on, then explain why that makes it a bad word to use about a model.
  8. Legal doublets pair an English word with its French equivalent for a reason that expired centuries ago. Find one doublet in your own institution's boilerplate and decide whether the two halves distinguish anything. Be honest about the answer.
