Parts of speech as functions

A noun is not a person, place or thing. It is a word behaving in a particular way, and the same word can behave differently on Tuesday. Getting this right is the difference between grammar as a memory test and grammar as an instrument.

Lecture 3 of 7 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: Huddleston & Pullum, The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language; Sword (2012)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Word classes are defined by grammatical behaviour, not by meaning. You identify a noun by what it can do in a clause, take a determiner, pluralise, head a subject, and not by asking whether it names a thing. This is not a technicality. The meaning-based definitions taught in schools fail on exactly the words this course cares about most.

Consider destruction. Is it a person, place or thing? It is an action. By the school definition it should be a verb, and it is not: you cannot say "the army destructions the city". It is a noun, formed from a verb, and that transformation is called nominalisation. It is the single most consequential thing that happens to a word in professional prose, and the school definition of a noun cannot see it at all.

So this lecture does two things. It replaces meaning-based definitions with behavioural tests you can actually run on a word in your own draft. And it sets up the diagnostic that module 2 is built on: find the actions in a sentence, check whether they are sitting in verbs, and if they are sitting in nouns instead, note what had to happen to the rest of the sentence to accommodate them.

Feynman test Do not ask what a word is. Ask what job it has today. The same person can be a passenger on Monday and a driver on Tuesday, and asking "but which is he really" is not a question about him, it is a confusion about jobs. Words are like that. Fast is an adjective in "a fast car", an adverb in "he drove fast", a verb in "they fast in Lent", and a noun in "she broke her fast". One word, four jobs, no contradiction.
What is beyond the source Huddleston and Pullum give the behavioural tests with far more precision than is reproduced here, and they reorganise the traditional eight classes substantially, treating because and although as prepositions rather than conjunctions and replacing "determiner" with a distinction between the class and the function. This lecture keeps the traditional eight, which is a deliberate simplification. The traditional taxonomy is imperfect and it is sufficient for diagnosis, which is the only use this course has for it.
Step 2

Why this matters

Most writers can feel that a sentence is heavy without being able to say why, and feeling is not enough, because you cannot systematically repair what you cannot locate. The whole apparatus of this course, the character and action principle, the topic and stress positions, the given-new contract, is stated in terms of what occupies which grammatical slot. Every one of those diagnoses is unavailable to a writer who cannot reliably identify a subject, a finite verb or a nominalisation.

The specific payoff is worth previewing, because it justifies the effort of the next twenty minutes. Institutional prose has one dominant failure mode, and it is structural rather than lexical. The writer takes an action, packs it into a noun, and is then forced by English syntax into three further moves: a weak verb has to be found to fill the vacated verb slot, the original actor has to be demoted into a prepositional phrase or dropped entirely, and the objects of the action have to be attached with a chain of prepositions. One nominalisation triggers all of it. That is why a single grammatical operation can wreck a sentence, and why learning to spot it repays more than any other piece of grammar.

There is a second reason, less obvious and more important in the long run. Naming things changes what you notice. A writer who knows the word "nominalisation" starts seeing nominalisations in their own drafts within days, and the ones they see are not new, they were always there. This is the honest defence of teaching any grammar at all in a course that opened by citing evidence that grammar teaching makes writing worse. Grammar taught as a set of rules to obey does damage. Grammar taught as a set of names for things you are already doing gives you access to your own habits.

Step 3

The mental model

Think of a clause as a small set of slots, and of word classes as the shapes that fit each slot. The subject slot takes a noun phrase. The verb slot takes a verb. Modifier slots take adjectives if they attach to nouns and adverbs if they attach to anything else. Prepositions open a slot of their own and require a noun phrase to fill it. Determiners sit at the front of noun phrases and mark whether the reader is meant to already know what is being referred to.

Now the crucial move. Nothing forces the action of a sentence into the verb slot. English will happily let you put the action in the subject slot as a noun and fill the verb slot with something almost contentless, and the result is grammatical, well formed, and much harder to read. The diagram below traces exactly what that costs.

The last consequence in the diagram deserves emphasis because it is the one that scales. A verb can take a direct object with no preposition at all. A noun cannot, so every argument that the verb would have taken directly must now be introduced by of, in, for, or the dreadful in respect of. Two nominalisations in one sentence therefore produce a chain of three or four prepositional phrases, and prepositional chains are the most reliable visible symptom of nominalisation in institutional prose. When you see four ofs in a row, you have found four buried verbs.

Nominalisation
A noun derived from a verb or an adjective. Derived by suffix (decide to decision, assess to assessment, able to ability) or by no change at all (a review, an increase, a change). The zero-derived ones are the hardest to spot and just as damaging.
Open and closed classes
Nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs are open: English gains new members constantly. Determiners, pronouns, prepositions, conjunctions and auxiliaries are closed, with fixed membership. You can invent a verb. You cannot invent a preposition.
Behavioural test
A substitution or inflection frame that a word either fits or does not. "Can it follow the and take a plural?" is a noun test. Tests replace definitions because they give the same answer for every speaker.
Step 4

The detail

The tests, one class at a time

Each test below is a frame. Put the word in the frame; if it fits without further changes, it belongs to that class in that use. Run the test on the word as it appears in your sentence, not on the word in the abstract, because the answer can differ.

ClassTest frameWhat it does in a clause
Open classes: new members admitted constantly
Noun "the ___", "two ___s", "the ___'s" Heads a noun phrase, which can be a subject, object or complement. Names anything at all, including actions.
Verb "they ___ed", "she ___s", "they can ___" Heads the predicate. Carries tense and agreement when finite. This is where the action belongs.
Adjective "a very ___ thing", "it is very ___" Modifies a noun. Gradable in most cases, so "more ___" and "the most ___" work.
Adverb "she did it ___", "___, the model failed" Modifies a verb, an adjective, another adverb, or a whole clause. The clause-modifying use is where hedges live.
Closed classes: fixed membership
Preposition "___ the table", "___ Q3" Takes a noun phrase and attaches it to something else. Multiplies wherever nouns have replaced verbs.
Determiner "___ model failed", and cannot stack with an article Opens a noun phrase and marks whether the reference is one the reader is assumed to already hold.
Conjunction "A ___ B", where A and B are the same kind of unit Joins. Coordinating conjunctions join equals; subordinating ones make one clause depend on another and name the relation.
Auxiliary "they ___ go", "___ they go?" Supports a main verb and carries tense, aspect, voice or modality. Modal auxiliaries are where uncertainty is expressed.

Why the school definitions fail

The definitions most people were taught are semantic: a noun names a person, place or thing, a verb is a doing word, an adjective describes. Each fails on cases that matter. Destruction, arrival and assessment name actions and are nouns. Honesty and redness name qualities and are nouns. Meanwhile seem, have and resemble are verbs that describe no action whatever, and in "he received a blow" the only real doing is done by whoever threw it, while the grammatical subject is the person it happened to.

The failures are not curiosities. They are concentrated exactly where writing goes wrong. Every nominalisation is a counterexample to "a noun is a thing", and every empty verb is a counterexample to "a verb is a doing word". A writer working with the school definitions is blind in precisely the region where the diagnosis needs to happen.

The nominalising suffixes, and the ones with no suffix at all

SUFFIXED NOMINALISATIONS (easy to spot, search for them) -tion / -sion calibrate -> calibration decide -> decision -ment assess -> assessment adjust -> adjustment -ance / -ence perform -> performance depend -> dependence -al approve -> approval withdraw -> withdrawal -ure fail -> failure depart -> departure -ity able -> ability valid -> validity -ness robust -> robustness aware -> awareness -ing monitor -> monitoring report -> reporting ZERO-DERIVED NOMINALISATIONS (no suffix; much harder) review, increase, decrease, change, use, need, impact, result, cause, drop, rise, test, check, override, transfer These are identical in form to their verbs, so no search finds them. Spot them by the article: "a review", "an increase", "the transfer" are nouns. "We reviewed", "it increased", "they transferred" are verbs. THE SEARCH THAT ACTUALLY WORKS Do not search for the suffixes. Search for " of ". Every preposition-heavy stretch is a nominalisation wearing a disguise, and the "of" count in a document correlates with its unreadability better than any readability formula does.

Sword calls the suffixed cases zombie nouns, on the grounds that they consume the living verbs around them, and the name has stuck because it captures the cannibalising effect. Her diagnostic is to circle every word ending in one of those suffixes and ask what verb it came from and who was doing it. Both answers are usually available, and the fact that they are available and absent is the point.

One caution, because module 2 will make it more carefully. Nominalisation is not a sin. It is the normal way English forms an abstract noun, and abstract nouns are indispensable: inflation, impairment, validation and regulation are all nominalisations and all irreplaceable. The problem is never the existence of a nominalisation, it is a nominalisation occupying the subject slot of a sentence whose real actor has been evicted to make room.

Adverbs, and where the hedging hides

Adverbs are the most heterogeneous class, and the useful split for a writer is by what the adverb attaches to. An adverb modifying a verb tells you how the action went: the model calibrated poorly. An adverb modifying a whole clause comments on the claim rather than the action: arguably, the model is miscalibrated. That second kind is where professional prose leaks.

Clause-level adverbs, and the near-adverbial phrases that behave like them, are the vehicle for almost all hedging: arguably, broadly, generally, largely, potentially, in principle, to some extent, it appears that. Each one weakens a claim slightly, which is legitimate when the uncertainty is real. The failure is accumulation. A sentence with three hedges has not expressed three degrees of caution, it has expressed a writer who will not commit, and readers discount the whole sentence rather than discounting it three times. Module 5 gives this its own lecture. The reason to notice it here is that you cannot count your hedges until you can see that they are all doing the same grammatical job.

Determiners carry more than they look like

Determiners seem like the dullest class and they do something this course cares about a great deal. The difference between a and the is not politeness or variety, it is an instruction about what the reader is assumed to know. A model introduces something new. The model asserts that the reader can already identify which model is meant, either from earlier in the text or from shared context. Get this wrong and the reader stumbles without knowing why: a definite article pointing at something never introduced sends them hunting backwards for an antecedent that is not there.

This is the given-new contract arriving early, in miniature, at the level of a single word. Module 3 develops it into the governing principle of passage-level cohesion. For now, the practical note is that a draft with many definite articles in its opening sentences is usually a draft written from inside the writer's own knowledge, assuming the reader shares a context they have not yet been given.

Conjunctions, and the logic that "and" conceals

The coordinating conjunctions are a closed list of seven: and, but, or, nor, for, yet, so. They join units of equal grammatical rank and they name almost no relation between them. The subordinating conjunctions are a much larger set and each one names a specific relation: because names cause, although names concession, if names condition, when names time, whereas names contrast.

The diagnostic value is this. When you find two clauses joined by and, ask what the relation between them actually is. Often there is a real one, and the writer joined them with and because they had not yet decided which. "The PD model was recalibrated and the override rate fell" invites the reader to supply a causal relation the writer has not claimed. If the causation is meant, because or so says it and can be challenged. If it is not meant, and is smuggling it in. A vague conjunction is frequently the visible residue of an undecided thought, which is the same observation lecture 1 made about tangled syntax generally.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The taxonomy is a tool, and it is not a very good one

Honesty about the eight parts of speech: they are an inheritance from Latin grammar, imperfectly fitted to English, and modern descriptive grammar has substantially reorganised them. Huddleston and Pullum argue that because, although and if are better analysed as prepositions taking clausal complements than as a separate class of subordinating conjunctions, since prepositions and these words behave alike in most respects. They separate the class "determinative" from the function "determiner", because a possessive noun phrase can perform the function without belonging to the class. They deny that participles and gerunds are distinct categories in English at all, treating both as the single -ing form doing different jobs.

Every one of those revisions is well argued and none of them changes anything a writer does. That is worth sitting with, because it clarifies what grammar is for in a writing course. If the purpose were describing English, you would want the best available taxonomy and the arguments for it. The purpose here is finding the actions and the actors in your own sentences, and the crude eight-way split locates them perfectly well. Using the traditional labels while knowing they are approximate is not sloppiness; it is choosing the resolution the task requires.

The same word in four slots

The deepest habit to build from this lecture is to stop asking what a word is. English has almost no morphological marking of word class, which is why the same form moves between classes so freely, and why function before label is not a slogan but a description of how the language works.

ONE FORM, FOUR JOBS "review" verb We reviewed the calibration in Q3. noun A review of the calibration was performed. modifier The review process took six weeks. (a noun doing an adjective's job) "running" verb The batch job is running. noun Running the model takes four hours. adjective A running total. "fast" adjective a fast car adverb he drove fast verb they fast during Lent noun she broke her fast THE POINT In every case the form is identical and the job differs. So "is 'review' a noun?" has no answer. "Is 'review' a noun in this sentence?" has exactly one, and it is the question the rest of the course asks.
Common failure Learning to spot nominalisations and then deleting all of them. This produces prose that reads like a children's book about capital adequacy, because the abstract vocabulary of every technical field is built from nominalisations and cannot be written without them. The target is not the noun, it is the specific configuration where the action sits in the subject as a noun, the verb slot holds "was performed" or "took place", and the actor has vanished. Module 2 defines the target precisely. Until then, notice them rather than removing them.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Copy the two-panel structure from step 3 using a sentence from your own work. Write the verb-centred version first, then perform the nominalisation deliberately and label all three forced consequences on your own sentence. Producing the damage on purpose is how you learn to recognise it by accident.
  2. Why do behavioural tests give more reliable answers than meaning-based definitions? Name two words that the school definition of "noun" gets wrong.
  3. State the three consequences that follow automatically when an action is moved from a verb into a noun in the subject slot.
  4. Give three zero-derived nominalisations not on the list in step 4, and say how you would spot each one in a draft given that no text search will find them.
  5. Why does counting occurrences of " of " find nominalisations more effectively than searching for "-tion"?
  6. Take a paragraph you wrote recently. Underline every clause-level adverb and hedging phrase. Count them, then count the claims. If the first number exceeds the second, decide which hedges are carrying real uncertainty.
  7. Find a sentence in a document you did not write in which two clauses are joined by "and". State the logical relation the reader is likely to infer, then say whether the writer actually claimed it.
  8. Huddleston and Pullum reclassify "because" as a preposition. Explain why accepting their argument changes nothing about how you would revise a sentence, and what that tells you about the role of grammar in this course.
