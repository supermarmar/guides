What writing is for

Speech comes with a repair mechanism: the listener frowns, and you try again. Writing has no such thing. Everything that follows in this course comes from taking that one difference seriously.

Lecture 1 of 7 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: Gopen & Swan (1990), The Science of Scientific Writing
Step 1

The 30-second version

The reader-expectation principle states that the meaning of a piece of prose is not what the writer intends but what the reader interprets. Readers do not decode sentences neutrally. They arrive with fixed structural expectations, they use those expectations to decide what a sentence is about and which part of it matters, and they do this the same way from one reader to the next.

That last clause carries the weight. If readers varied wildly, writing advice would be taste and nothing more. They do not vary wildly. Gopen and Swan showed that a small set of expectations about where information sits in a sentence predicts, reliably, which sentences readers find hard. Prose that satisfies those expectations reads as clear. Prose that violates them reads as heavy, or vague, or somehow off, and the reader almost never knows why.

So clarity stops being a virtue you either have or lack. It becomes a property of the fit between what you are trying to say and the structures you say it in, and a badly fitting sentence can be diagnosed and repaired the way a badly fitting joint can. That is the promise of the next sixty-seven lectures. This one establishes why the promise is credible.

Feynman test Talking is a conversation; writing is a delivery. When you talk, you watch the other person's face and repair the message in real time, several times a minute, without noticing you are doing it. When you write, you hand over a sealed box and walk away. Everything this course teaches is about packing the box so it survives the journey, because you will not be there to explain it.
What is beyond the source Gopen and Swan wrote for scientists publishing in journals. The argument transfers without modification to any document a reader is obliged rather than eager to read, which describes almost everything written inside an institution. A model validation report, a credit paper, an exam answer, and a research article all share the crucial feature: the reader cannot ask you what you meant, and would not bother if they could.
Step 2

Why this matters

Consider what actually happens to a badly written technical document. It does not get returned with a note asking for clarification. It gets skimmed, half understood, and approved anyway, because the reader has eleven other papers and a meeting at two. The cost surfaces later, when a decision turns out to have rested on a caveat that was in the document but that nobody extracted from it.

This is the part professionals underrate. We tend to treat writing quality as a matter of polish, something applied at the end if time allows, and we assume that a reader who genuinely needs the information will work to get it. Readers do not work. Readers allocate a fixed budget of attention to your document, and every sentence that makes them re-read costs you some of that budget, which then is not available for your argument. A reader who spends their attention parsing your syntax has none left for your conclusion.

There is a second cost, and it falls on the writer. Prose that resists a reader usually resists its author too. When you cannot make a paragraph work, the reason is frequently that you have not yet decided what it claims, and the tangled syntax is the visible symptom of an undecided idea underneath. Writers who take this seriously find that revising for clarity turns into revising for substance, because you cannot state a muddled thought clearly without first unmuddling it. Module 9 returns to this with the research behind it.

The third reason is the one nobody mentions in a writing course, so this course mentions it first. Obscurity is useful. A sentence with no visible actor cannot be traced to anyone. A conclusion buried in the fourth paragraph of section 6 has technically been disclosed. Institutions produce vast quantities of prose whose unreadability is not an accident, and once you can see the mechanisms, you can see who is using them. Williams closes his book on that point and this course closes on it too.

Step 3

The mental model

Pinker gives the cleanest account of why writing is intrinsically difficult, and it has nothing to do with vocabulary or effort. You hold an idea as a web: a mess of concepts connected in many directions at once, all present to you simultaneously. Language cannot transmit a web. It can only transmit a string, one word after another, in a single order. So you must first impose a tree on the web, deciding what subordinates to what, and then flatten the tree into a line. Your reader receives the line and has to run the whole operation backwards, rebuilding a tree from the string and then a web from the tree, using nothing but the structural cues you left behind.

Two consequences follow, and they run in opposite directions. The first is discouraging: some loss is guaranteed. No arrangement of words puts a web into someone's head intact, so the question is never whether the reader will lose something but which things they will lose. The second consequence is the useful one. Because the reader rebuilds from structural cues, and because those cues work the same way for nearly everybody, you can predict what they will lose and place the load accordingly.

Topic position
The opening of a sentence, up to the main verb. Readers use it to decide what the sentence is about and how it connects to the sentence before. Put the wrong thing here and the reader spends the rest of the sentence recovering.
Stress position
The point of syntactic closure, usually the end of the sentence or of a clause marked off by a colon or semicolon. Readers give whatever lands here extra emphasis, whether or not the writer wanted them to.
Reader expectation
A structural assumption a reader applies automatically and cannot switch off. Not a preference and not a convention: a processing habit, measurable in reading time.
Step 4

The detail

The seven expectations

Gopen and Swan set out seven expectations that readers bring to English prose. Each of the next thirty lectures elaborates one or more of them, so treat this as the map rather than the territory. Read them once now, and again after module 3, when they will mean considerably more.

1. One unit, one function
Readers expect every unit of discourse, whatever its size, to serve a single function and make a single point. A paragraph doing two jobs reads as a paragraph doing neither.
2. Subject and verb close together
Readers expect to reach the verb quickly, and they treat whatever sits between a subject and its verb as an interruption to be held in memory and discounted. Long qualifications in that gap cost the reader real effort and gain them nothing.
3. The topic position supplies context
Readers expect the opening of a sentence to link backwards to what they have just read and to frame what is coming. It is the anchor point, not a place to store your most important new fact.
4. The stress position carries the point
Readers give emphasis to whatever occupies the position of syntactic closure. If your point is not there, they will emphasise something else instead and you will have no say in what.
5. The story's protagonist appears as the subject
Readers expect to find whoever or whatever the sentence is about sitting in the grammatical subject. When an abstraction occupies that slot, they keep searching for the real character and read the sentence twice.
6. The verb articulates the action
Readers expect the action of a sentence to appear in its verb. When the action has been packed into a noun and the verb reduced to something like "conduct" or "undertake", the sentence still parses but the reader has to unpack it.
7. Old before new, context before conclusion
Readers expect familiar material to arrive first and new material to follow it. New material placed in a stress position must either conclude the passage or become the familiar material that opens the next sentence. Nothing else works.

The seven applied to a real sentence

The following is invented, but anyone who has read a model validation report will recognise the dialect. Watch how many of the seven it breaks in forty-one words, and note that not one word of it is wrong.

BEFORE (41 words, 3 sentences of content compressed into 1) "An assessment of the appropriateness of the downturn LGD calibration, which was performed in respect of the mortgage portfolio by the independent validation function during Q3, resulted in the identification of a material understatement." DIAGNOSIS Expectation 5 Subject is "An assessment". Nobody is doing anything. The real characters, the validation function and the calibration, are buried in prepositional phrases. Expectation 6 The action is "assessed", but the verb slot holds "resulted in". The two real actions, assessing and identifying, are both nouns. Expectation 2 19 words separate the subject from its verb. Expectation 4 The stress position holds "a material understatement", which is at least the right idea, arriving too late to be believed. Expectation 1 One sentence carries who did it, what they looked at, when, and what they found: four functions, one unit. AFTER (32 words, 2 sentences) "The independent validation function reviewed the downturn LGD calibration for the mortgage portfolio in Q3. It found the calibration materially understated." Lard factor: 22 per cent. Characters restored to subjects, actions restored to verbs, and the finding now lands where the reader is already looking for it.

Three points about that revision, because two of them are commonly missed. It is shorter, but shortness is a side effect rather than the goal; a version that cut ten more words by dropping "for the mortgage portfolio" would be worse, not better. It splits one sentence into two, which is what the single-function expectation demands, and the split does more work than any of the individual word choices. And it names the actor, which the original carefully avoided. That last change is grammatical in form and political in substance, a theme this course returns to in its final module.

What this does not claim

Two boundaries are worth marking now, because the reader-expectation approach gets overstated by its enthusiasts. It does not claim that all readers are identical. It claims that the structural processing habits described above are widely shared, which is a much narrower and much better evidenced assertion than any claim about taste. Specialists reading in their own field, for instance, tolerate far more compression than the seven expectations would suggest, because the chunking is already done in their heads. Module 6 treats that as its central problem.

Nor does the approach claim that satisfying the seven expectations produces good writing. It produces clear writing, which is a floor rather than a ceiling. Prose can honour every one of them and still be flat, ugly, badly argued or pointless. Modules 5 and 8 exist because clarity is where the interesting part starts.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Meaning is constructed, not transmitted

The transmission metaphor is so natural that it hides inside ordinary language. We say we get an idea across, we put it into words, the reader takes our meaning. Every one of those phrases describes moving an object from one place to another, intact. None of them describes what happens.

What happens is closer to instruction-following. You leave a set of cues on a page; the reader uses them to build something in their own head out of their own materials. Two readers with different materials build different things from identical cues, which is why a paper that your colleague finds obvious can be opaque to the committee, and why the reflex explanation, that the committee did not read it properly, is usually wrong and always useless.

Accepting this changes what revision is for. If meaning were transmitted, revision would be a search for errors: things you got wrong, which can be corrected. Because meaning is constructed, revision is a simulation: you are trying to run the reader's construction process on your own text and notice where it produces the wrong building. That is genuinely difficult, and it is difficult in a specific way that module 6 names as the curse of knowledge. You cannot easily simulate not knowing what you know.

The consequence nobody likes

If the reader constructs the meaning, then a document that is misread is your problem. Not entirely, and not always, but by default. The instinct to blame the reader is strong and occasionally justified, and it is also the single reliable marker of a writer who has stopped improving. Every writer who gets better does so by taking on more responsibility for the reader's experience than is strictly fair.

There is a version of this that curdles into something worse, and it is worth naming before anyone reaches it. Taking responsibility for the reader does not mean writing whatever they want to hear, or removing every difficulty from an argument that is genuinely difficult. Some ideas are hard and the honest presentation of a hard idea is hard to read. The obligation is to make sure the difficulty in your prose is the difficulty of the subject and not difficulty you added.

Common failure Writers who learn the reader-expectation approach often start applying it while drafting. Do not. Trying to satisfy seven structural expectations at the moment of composition slows a first draft to a crawl and produces cautious, dead prose. These are revision heuristics, and module 9 shows the evidence for why that distinction matters more than any individual technique in the course. Draft badly and fast; diagnose afterwards.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take the revised sentence pair from step 4 and write your own version of the same shape, using material from your own work: an actor in the subject, the action in the verb, the finding in a second short sentence. Match the structure, not the content. Then write the bad version, deliberately, and check it against the seven expectations. Producing the failure on purpose teaches more than avoiding it by luck.
  2. State the reader-expectation principle in one sentence without using the words "clear" or "clarity".
  3. Why does the principle make writing advice testable in a way that "omit needless words" is not?
  4. A reader gives extra emphasis to material in the stress position. What must you do with new material that lands there and is not the end of the passage?
  5. The web, the tree and the string: which of the three does the writer control, and what does the reader have to do with it?
  6. Find a paragraph you wrote in the last month. Read only the grammatical subject of each sentence, in order, and write the list down. Do those subjects name the characters of a single story? (You are running the module 3 diagnosis two modules early. Note what you find and come back to it.)
  7. Give one reason a writer might break expectation 5 deliberately, and one reason an institution might break it habitually. They are not the same reason.
  8. Why does this lecture insist that the seven expectations are revision heuristics rather than drafting rules? What goes wrong if you ignore that?
