Prescriptivism and descriptivism

Some writing rules describe real constraints on English. Some are arbitrary conventions that are socially enforced anyway. And some were invented by individuals who disliked something, and have been repeated for three hundred years by people who never checked. Telling the three apart is the last thing this module teaches, because everything after it depends on knowing which kind of rule you are being handed.

Lecture 7 of 7 Reading time, 40 minutes Source: Pinker (2014), ch. 6; Pullum (2009), 50 Years of Stupid Grammar Advice
Step 1

The 30-second version

Writing rules fall into three bins. Real constraints are shared by every competent speaker of the language, so violating one produces something no dialect of English says. Conventions are arbitrary choices that a community enforces: they have no basis in the structure of the language and real consequences for the writer who ignores them. Superstitions are inventions with neither basis nor benefit, usually traceable to one person who disliked something and wrote it down.

The bins matter because they call for different responses. Real constraints you obey automatically and do not need to be taught. Conventions you learn and follow where they are enforced, without confusing compliance with correctness. Superstitions you should discard, because each one costs you a construction English offers and gives you nothing in return, and because obeying them makes prose worse in a specific and documented way.

Two complications make this harder than a clean three-way sort. Nearly everyone who will correct your writing believes some superstitions are real constraints, so you sometimes have to obey one for the sake of a reader rather than for the sake of the language, and knowing that this is what you are doing is the difference between judgement and submission. And the descriptivist position is easily caricatured as "anything goes", which it never was: the standard written dialect is socially enforced whether or not it is arbitrary, and telling someone their prose is fine because language changes does not help them.

Feynman test Three kinds of rule, and a driving analogy for each. Driving on the correct side of the road for your country is a convention: entirely arbitrary, and you will be killed for ignoring it. Not driving into a wall is a real constraint, and nobody had to teach it to you. And never changing gear on a Sunday is a superstition somebody's uncle invented, which costs you a gear and protects you from nothing.
What is beyond the source The three-way classification is Pinker's, and the demolition of Strunk and White's grammatical claims is Pullum's. What this lecture adds is the asymmetry in how to respond: obey conventions, discard superstitions, and obey a superstition anyway when the reader who holds it has power over the outcome. Pinker gestures at that last position; nobody states it as a decision procedure, probably because it is uncomfortable.
Step 2

Why this matters

The immediate cost of the superstitions is that each one removes a tool. The taboo on splitting an infinitive forces adverbs into positions where they modify the wrong thing or read as afterthoughts. The taboo on ending a sentence with a preposition produces the stilted inversions that make formal English sound insincere. The taboo on the passive is the worst of the three, because the passive is the correct choice whenever the thing acted upon is the topic of the passage, and module 2 devotes a whole lecture to when to use it. A writer who has been trained to avoid it has lost the ability to manage what their sentences are about.

The larger cost is to judgement. A writer who cannot distinguish a convention from a constraint has no basis for deciding anything, so they default to obeying whatever they were told most firmly. That is why professional prose is so uniform and so bad: it is written by people applying half-remembered rules they were never given grounds to evaluate, and the rules they remember most firmly are usually the superstitions, because superstitions are stated as absolutes and real constraints never need stating at all.

There is a third stake, and it is the reason this lecture closes the module rather than opening it. Everything in modules 2 through 9 is advice, and you are about to be given a great deal of it. Some of that advice is grounded in evidence about reader behaviour, some is a defensible aesthetic position, and some is one writer's preference dressed as a principle. You now have the categories to sort it, and you should apply them to this course as readily as to Strunk and White. A course that taught the three bins and then asked to be exempted from them would not deserve the exemption.

Step 3

The mental model

Sort every rule you meet by asking two questions in order. First: would violating it produce something that no competent speaker of any dialect of English would say? If yes, it is a real constraint. If no, ask the second question: does a community you write for actually enforce it? If yes, it is a convention. If no, it is a superstition, and the honest thing to do is drop it.

Notice something about bin 1 that is easy to miss. Nobody teaches those rules, nobody needs to, and no writer breaks them by accident. That is the signature of a real constraint: it is invisible because it is never violated. Which means that any rule you were explicitly taught, and that you have to remember to follow, is almost certainly in bin 2 or bin 3. Explicit teaching is itself evidence that a rule is not a constraint on the language.

Prescriptivism
The position that some usages are correct and others incorrect, and that the job of a usage authority is to say which. Not inherently unreasonable: conventions do exist and are worth codifying.
Descriptivism
The position that the facts about a language are what its competent speakers do, established by observation rather than decree. Not the position that anything goes, which no linguist has ever held.
Standard written English
A prestige dialect with a codified written form, enforced in education and employment. Arbitrary in its particulars and consequential in its effects, which is why it is worth learning and worth not confusing with English itself.
Step 4

The detail

The superstitions, with their origins

The ruleWhere it came fromWhy it fails, and what it costs
Traceable to an individual
Never split an infinitive Objected to by Henry Alford in The Queen's English (1864), and by others through the nineteenth century, on the analogy of Latin. The Latin infinitive is a single word and therefore unsplittable; the English one is two words with a gap in it. The taboo forces adverbs away from what they modify, which is why "to boldly go" reads better than either alternative.
Never end a sentence with a preposition Dryden, in his 1672 criticism of Ben Jonson, and repeated in Lowth's grammar of 1762 as a matter of elegance rather than grammar. English has stranded prepositions in every period of its history. Avoiding it produces the inversions that make formal prose sound insincere: "the rule to which you are objecting".
"Less" only with mass nouns, "fewer" with countables Robert Baker, Reflections on the English Language (1770), who stated it as his own preference and said so. "Less" with countables is attested from Old English onwards. The rule is harmless to obey and worth knowing as a convention, and it is not a fact about English.
No identifiable source: pure school lore
Never begin a sentence with "and" or "but" Nobody can name an origin. It appears to be a teaching heuristic against sentence fragments that hardened into a rule. Sentence-initial coordinators are ubiquitous in good English prose from the King James Bible onwards, and they are one of the cheapest ways to vary sentence opening.
"None" is always singular Asserted by nineteenth and twentieth-century usage writers on the false etymology that it means "not one". It derives from "not any" and has taken plural agreement since Old English. Costs nothing to obey and is simply not true.
Singular "they" is an error Eighteenth and nineteenth-century prescriptivists, promoting "he" as the generic pronoun. Singular "they" is attested from the fourteenth century and appears in Chaucer, Shakespeare and Austen. Now explicitly accepted by the major style guides, including Chicago from its seventeenth edition.
"Hopefully" cannot modify a sentence A usage campaign that began in the 1960s and singled out this one adverb. English has dozens of sentence adverbs nobody objects to: frankly, clearly, mercifully, regrettably. The Associated Press dropped its objection in 2012.
The one that does real damage
Avoid the passive voice Orwell's fourth rule (1946), Strunk and White, and a century of style guides since. The passive is the correct construction whenever the thing acted upon is the topic. Banning it destroys your control over what a passage is about, which module 2 shows in detail. The advice also travels with widespread confusion about what a passive actually is.

Pullum against Strunk and White

In 2009, on the fiftieth anniversary of The Elements of Style, Geoffrey Pullum published an attack on it under the title 50 Years of Stupid Grammar Advice. The charge is not that its advice is unhelpful. It is that its grammatical claims are incompetent, and that generations of writers have been taught grammar by authors who could not reliably identify the constructions they were prescribing about.

The documented centrepiece is the passive voice. Strunk and White instruct the writer to use the active rather than the passive, and then illustrate the passive with examples that are not passive. "There were a great number of dead leaves lying on the ground" contains no passive verb at all; it is an existential construction with a participle. Pullum's point is that if the authors cannot recognise a passive, their readers, taught by them, will spend their careers avoiding a construction they cannot identify, and will avoid other things instead.

The second charge is that the book breaks its own rules throughout, which is straightforwardly demonstrable and slightly less damning than it sounds, since a good writer breaking a bad rule is behaving correctly. The third and most consequential is the treatment of which and that, which the book presents as a grammatical requirement when it is Fowler's proposed tidying-up, as lecture 6 set out.

The case for Strunk and White anyway

Pullum's technical charges are correct and the conclusion usually drawn from them is too strong. Three things can be said for the book, and a course that suppressed them would be doing the same thing it criticises.

It is about a hundred pages long, and that is why it was read. A correct grammar of English runs to seventeen hundred pages, and the writer who needs help at two in the morning will not open it. Brevity was the design, and books that are actually read have effects that better books do not.

Much of its advice is right, for reasons the authors got wrong. "Omit needless words" is the single most useful instruction in the literature, and module 5 spends a lecture unpacking it into six identifiable kinds of redundancy. The preference for the concrete and the specific is well supported. The advice to put the emphatic word at the end of a sentence anticipates the stress position that Gopen and Swan later formalised. Being wrong about why a piece of advice works does not make the advice bad, which is uncomfortable and true.

And the book performs a coordination function that has nothing to do with linguistic truth. A shared style manual lets a large organisation produce documents that read as though one mind wrote them, and the value of that is real regardless of whether the manual's grammatical claims survive scrutiny. This is exactly the argument for bin 2. Conventions do work, and they work by being shared rather than by being right.

Where that leaves the dispute: take Pullum's factual corrections, which are not in serious doubt, and keep the advice that stands on its own. What you must not do is what most readers do, which is to absorb the book's grammatical vocabulary along with its advice, and then spend thirty years avoiding constructions you have misidentified.

Descriptivism is not "anything goes"

The caricature is worth dismantling because it is used to dismiss the entire descriptive tradition. No linguist holds that any string of words is as good as any other. Descriptivism is a claim about method: the facts about a language are discovered by observing competent speakers rather than decreed by authorities. That method rules out a great deal. It rules out "these model" and "between we", it rules out dangling modifiers as producing readings the writer did not intend, and it certainly rules out prose that fails to communicate.

What it does not do is treat the standard dialect as inherently superior to the others, and this is where the honest complication lives. Standard written English is a prestige variety, and its prestige is a historical accident rather than a property of the grammar. Sociolinguists have documented at length how standard language ideology operates as a mechanism of exclusion, most thoroughly in Lippi-Green's work on how accent and dialect judgements function in hiring, housing and the courts.

Accepting all of that changes nothing about what you should do on Monday. The conventions are enforced. A validation report written in a non-standard variety will be judged, and the judgement will land on the author rather than on the enforcement. The descriptive facts tell you that the conventions are arbitrary; they do not tell you that the enforcement is imaginary. Holding both at once is the mature position, and it is more useful than either half.

When to obey a superstition

Here is the practical case the literature usually avoids. Suppose you know the split-infinitive taboo is baseless, and you also know that the person reviewing your paper believes it firmly and marks it. What should you do?

THE DECISION Obey the superstition when ALL THREE hold: 1. The reader who holds it has power over the outcome. 2. Obeying costs little in clarity for this sentence. 3. You have no standing, or no appetite, to argue. Push back when: 1. Obeying would actually damage the sentence, which is most common with the passive: the alternative forces the wrong topic into the subject slot. 2. You have the standing, and the correction is being applied to a whole document rather than a phrase. 3. The "rule" is being used to reject substance, which happens more often than anyone admits. WORKED "The committee agreed to substantially revise the methodology." <- split infinitive Reviewer objects. Obeying costs almost nothing here: "The committee agreed to revise the methodology substantially." Slightly worse rhythm, same meaning. Comply and move on. "The exposures were reclassified in March." <- passive Reviewer demands the active. The active version is "The credit risk team reclassified the exposures in March." which is better IF the team is the topic. If the passage is about the exposures, the active version breaks the topic chain and the paragraph stops cohering. Here you push back, and you can now say why in terms the reviewer can check. THE ASYMMETRY Style superstitions about word order cost you rhythm. The passive superstition costs you cohesion. Fight the second one; concede the first.

The distinction that makes this workable is that you now have reasons. A writer who says "the split infinitive is fine" has an opinion. A writer who says "moving that adverb attaches it to the wrong verb, and here is the ambiguity it creates" has an argument, and arguments survive contact with reviewers in a way that opinions do not.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Why superstitions are so durable

The obvious explanation is inertia, and it is insufficient, because plenty of usage advice has been abandoned. Something keeps this particular set alive, and the something is worth naming: usage superstitions are useful to their holders. They provide a low-cost way of distinguishing insiders from outsiders and of dismissing an argument without engaging it. Knowing that "hopefully" is objectionable requires no expertise in anything, and it produces the feeling of expertise, which is the most powerful incentive in professional life.

This explains their distribution. Superstitions cluster on surface features that are easy to check mechanically: a word at the end of a sentence, an adverb between two others, a pronoun's number. They never cluster on the things that actually determine whether prose works, because those require reading the passage and thinking about it. A rule you can enforce with a text search will always be more popular than a rule you have to understand.

The uncomfortable corollary applies to this course. Any advice in the next sixty-one lectures that can be applied without judgement should be treated with suspicion for exactly that reason, and the ones that require you to read the passage and decide are more likely to be doing real work. The subject-chain diagnostic in lecture 4 requires judgement. "Never use the passive" does not. That asymmetry is a reasonably reliable guide to which advice is worth anything.

What prescriptivism gets right

The descriptive tradition has won the intellectual argument so thoroughly that it is worth stating the strongest prescriptivist position rather than the weakest, because the strong version is correct and often lost.

Written standards are coordination devices, and coordination devices do not need to be optimal to be valuable. They need to be shared. A community that agrees on a convention gains something real: readers can allocate their attention to content instead of to decoding, editors can work quickly, and organisations can produce consistent documents. None of that requires the convention to be well founded, and pointing out that it is arbitrary does not touch the argument. This is precisely why bin 2 exists as a separate category rather than being collapsed into bin 3. Arbitrary and worthless are not synonyms.

The prescriptivist error is not prescribing. It is misclassification: presenting a bin 2 convention as a bin 1 constraint, which is what Strunk and White did with which and that, and what every teacher does who says a split infinitive is "wrong" rather than "disliked by some readers". The fix is not to stop prescribing. It is to prescribe honestly, saying which kind of rule you are handing over. That is a higher standard than most style guides meet and it is not a difficult one.

Turning the instrument on this course

Module 1 ends here, so it is worth being explicit about what you have been given and what standard it should be held to. The reader-expectation framework in lecture 1 is an empirical claim, supported by reading-time evidence, and it should be abandoned if the evidence turns. The claim in lecture 2 that the Anglo-Saxon preference is really a frequency preference is an inference, clearly labelled as one, and you should treat it as a hypothesis that explains the exceptions better than the alternative. The grammar in lectures 3 and 4 is a serviceable approximation, openly not the best available analysis, chosen for a purpose. The branching account in lecture 5 rests on a memory limit that is about as well established as anything in the field. The punctuation ladder in lecture 6 is a teaching device rather than a fact about English.

That inventory is the point of the module. You now have the vocabulary to state what is wrong with a sentence, and the categories to evaluate anyone who tells you something is wrong, including the next sixty-one lectures.

Common failure Becoming the person who corrects other people's superstitions. Knowing that the split-infinitive taboo is baseless is worth something in your own drafting and almost nothing in conversation, and deploying it makes you the mirror image of the pedant you are correcting. The knowledge is for deciding what to write and for defending a sentence when the defence matters. It is not a debating position, and treating it as one is how a useful distinction turns into a personality.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take the decision procedure in step 4 and run it on a real correction you have received on your own writing. State which bin the rule belongs in, apply the three tests, and decide whether you should have complied. Then write the case for the reviewer's position as strongly as you can.
  2. State the two questions that sort a rule into the three bins, in the right order. Why does the order matter?
  3. Why is being explicitly taught a rule evidence that it is not a real constraint on the language?
  4. Give the documented charge against Strunk and White on the passive voice, and say what the consequence is for a reader who learned grammar from them.
  5. Make the strongest case for Strunk and White that does not depend on any of its grammatical claims being correct.
  6. Why is the passive superstition more damaging than the split-infinitive superstition? Answer in terms of what each one costs the writer.
  7. Descriptivism is not the position that anything goes. State what it actually claims, and give two things it rules out.
  8. Standard written English is arbitrary in its particulars and consequential in its effects. Explain why accepting both halves of that changes nothing about how you should write a board paper, and why the first half is still worth knowing.
  9. Take three pieces of advice from lectures 1 to 6 and classify each as empirical claim, defensible aesthetic position, or teaching device. Say which one you would abandon first if the evidence turned.
