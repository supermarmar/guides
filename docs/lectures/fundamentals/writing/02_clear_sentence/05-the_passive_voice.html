The passive voice, properly understood

The most misunderstood construction in English, banned by people who cannot identify it, and indispensable for the one job this module has established as central: choosing which participant occupies the subject slot.

Lecture 5 of 8 Reading time, 40 minutes Source: Pullum (2009); Huddleston & Pullum (2002), ch. 16; Williams, Style, lesson 3
Step 1

The 30-second version

A passive clause has three properties together: the verb is a form of be or get plus a past participle, the grammatical subject corresponds to what would be the object of the active version, and the actor may optionally appear in a by phrase or may be omitted. All three matter. A sentence that merely lacks an actor, or merely feels impersonal, is not thereby a passive.

That definitional point is not pedantry, because the received advice about the passive is delivered by authorities who fail it. Of the four examples Strunk and White offer to illustrate bad passives, Pullum showed that three contain no passive at all. Generations of writers have therefore been taught to avoid a construction they cannot identify, and have avoided other things instead: existential sentences, copular sentences, impersonal constructions, and in some cases perfectly good active sentences that merely sounded formal.

The positive account is simple once lectures 2 and 3 are in place. The subject slot determines what the reader takes the sentence to be about. Sometimes the thing you want the sentence to be about is not the actor but the thing acted upon. The passive is the construction that puts it there. Far from being a way of being vague, the passive is the primary tool English gives you for controlling topic, and a writer who has banned it has given up control of what their sentences are about.

Which leaves a genuine problem in a different place from where the style guides put it. The dangerous construction is not the passive; it is the agentless passive, the one with no by phrase. "The calibration was approved by the model risk committee" names its actor. "The calibration was approved" does not. Those two are grammatically the same construction and ethically nothing alike, and almost every treatment of the subject conflates them.

Feynman test Active and passive are two camera positions on the same event. Active puts the camera behind the person doing something; passive puts it on the thing being done to. Neither angle is dishonest, and a director who refused ever to use the second one would be unable to make a film about a building being demolished. What is dishonest is cropping the person out of the frame and then saying the demolition happened.
What is beyond the source Pullum supplies the demolition of the received advice; Huddleston and Pullum supply the grammar; Williams supplies the topic-continuity argument. The sharp separation of the agented from the agentless passive, and the claim that the entire ethical weight of the construction sits on that distinction rather than on the passive as such, is this lecture's framing. It is a better target than the one the style guides aim at, because it can be searched for: a passive with no by is findable, and a passive is not a defect.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first cost of the received advice is that obeying it damages cohesion, and the damage is invisible to the writer obeying it. Module 3 will show that a passage holds together when consecutive sentences keep a consistent topic in their subjects. Sometimes maintaining that topic requires a passive, because the topic is the patient of the next action rather than its agent. A writer converting every passive to an active will break the topic chain, and the resulting passage reads as disjointed for reasons that have nothing to do with voice.

The second cost is credibility. A reviewer who objects to "the exposures were reclassified in March" on the grounds that it is passive has made a claim about grammar, and if you can show that the passive is correct here because the paragraph is about the exposures, you have both improved the document and established that you know what you are talking about. Module 1's lecture 7 argued that arguments survive contact with reviewers where opinions do not. This is the case where it matters most, because the passive superstition is the one that does real damage rather than costing rhythm.

The third reason is the one this lecture treats most carefully. Agentless constructions are how institutional prose evades accountability, and the passive is only one of the routes. Nominalisation is another, as lecture 4 showed. Existential there and anticipatory it are two more. A writer who polices only the passive will produce documents just as actorless as before, having moved the evasion into constructions the style guide does not name. Seeing the mechanism rather than the construction is what lets you see all four.

And a reason specific to this collection: regulatory and accounting text uses agentless passives constantly and correctly, because the obligation falls on anyone at all. "Impairment is recognised when..." has no actor because the standard applies universally. Reading that as evasion would be a misreading, and distinguishing it from "an error was made in the calibration" requires exactly the analysis in step 4.

Step 3

The mental model

The clearest way to see what the passive is for is to watch it rescue a passage that the active voice wrecks. Below are two versions of the same three sentences. The first uses the active throughout, as the style guides instruct. The second uses one passive. The second is better, and the reason is visible in the subject chains.

Work through why the active version fails, because the diagnosis is the whole argument. Each of its three sentences satisfies lecture 2: a character sits in every subject. What it violates is a passage-level requirement that module 3 will state formally, which is that consecutive sentences should keep a consistent topic. The active voice forced the subject to be whoever acted, and the actors changed, so the topic changed with them. The passive breaks that coupling. It lets you decide the topic first and let the syntax follow, which is the correct order.

Passive clause
Verb is be or get plus a past participle; subject corresponds to the active's object; agent optional in a by phrase. All three properties together. "Was performed" is passive; "became impaired" and "there were leaves lying" are not.
Agented passive
A passive retaining its by phrase. Names the actor, so it makes no accountability sacrifice. Costs a few words and buys topic control. Usually the right choice when you need a passive at all.
Agentless passive
A passive with the by phrase omitted. Legitimate when the actor is unknown, universally quantified, or genuinely irrelevant. This is the construction that carries the whole ethical weight of the subject.
Topic
What a passage is about, as opposed to what a single sentence asserts. Signalled by what occupies the subject slots across consecutive sentences. The reason the passive exists.
Step 4

The detail

What Strunk and White got wrong

Pullum's 2009 charge is specific and checkable, so it is worth checking. The Elements of Style, in the section instructing the writer to use the active voice, offers four pairs of examples: a bad version and its correction. Pullum's observation is that three of the four bad versions contain no passive.

STRUNK AND WHITE'S FOUR EXAMPLES, CLASSIFIED 1. "There were a great number of dead leaves lying on the ground." -> NOT PASSIVE. Existential "there" plus "were" plus a present participle. No past participle, no promoted object. The sentence is wordy; it is not passive. 2. "At dawn the crowing of a rooster could be heard." -> PASSIVE. "could be heard": form of be plus past participle, subject is what would be the object of "hear". This is the one genuine example. 3. "The reason that he left college was that his health became impaired." -> NOT PASSIVE. "became impaired" is a copular verb plus an adjective. Compare "became ill". Nothing was done to anything by anyone. 4. "It was not long before he was very sorry that he had said what he had." -> NOT PASSIVE. "was not long" and "was very sorry" are both copula plus complement. There is no participle anywhere in the sentence. SCORE: one passive out of four. WHY IT MATTERS RATHER THAN BEING A GOTCHA All four sentences are bad, and every correction Strunk and White offer is an improvement. So the advice appears to work, which is exactly why the misdiagnosis persists. What the reader learns is not "avoid the passive" but "avoid whatever those four sentences had in common", and since three of them were not passive, the lesson they actually absorb is a vague aversion to impersonal-sounding prose. That aversion then attacks the agented passive, which was never the problem, and leaves the agentless passive and the nominalisation untouched.

Note the honest part of that analysis. Strunk and White's corrections are better sentences. What they diagnosed was real: those four sentences bury their actions and pad their subjects, which is lectures 2 to 4 of this module. They just named the wrong construction, and naming the wrong construction is why the advice cannot be applied intelligently.

The four legitimate uses

UseTestAgent retained?
Uses that cost nothing
Unknown or universal agent Can you name the actor? If genuinely not, or if the obligation falls on anyone at all, the passive is correct. "Impairment is recognised when..." applies to every preparer. No, and correctly so.
Topic continuity Does the paragraph's topic appear as the patient of this action? If the active would change the subject and break the chain, use the passive. This is the figure in step 3. Yes, usually. Keep the by phrase.
End weight Is the agent a long noun phrase? Putting it in the subject would separate subject from verb, which lecture 6 shows is expensive. Move it to the end. "The change was approved by the committee that oversees retail model risk across all portfolios." Yes. That is the point.
The use where the ethics start
Deliberate suppression Are you omitting the actor because naming them would be unfair, unsafe or irrelevant, or because naming them would be inconvenient? The first is legitimate. The second is the failure this course keeps returning to. No, deliberately. Requires a reason you would state out loud.

The end-weight row is worth expanding, because it is the use professionals most often need and least often recognise. English handles heavy material better at the end of a clause than at the front, for the reasons module 1's lecture 5 gave. A long agent in the subject slot therefore creates the exact problem lecture 6 of this module is about: a wide gap between the subject's head noun and its verb. The passive solves it by moving the heavy phrase past the verb. This is not a compromise with clarity; it is two principles agreeing.

The other three routes to an actorless sentence

A writer policing only the passive will produce prose just as actorless as before. Here are the four routes together, because seeing them as one family is the point.

FOUR WAYS TO DELETE AN ACTOR The event: the model owner approved a bad calibration. 1. AGENTLESS PASSIVE "The calibration was approved in March." Style guides flag this one. 2. NOMINALISATION "Approval of the calibration took place in March." Style guides do not flag this. Same deletion. 3. EXISTENTIAL THERE "There was an approval of the calibration in March." Style guides do not flag this. Same deletion. 4. ANTICIPATORY IT "It was considered appropriate to approve the calibration in March." Style guides do not flag this, and it is the worst of the four: it deletes the approver AND the person who considered it appropriate. THE COMMON TEST, WHICH CATCHES ALL FOUR Ask of every clause describing an event: who did this? If the sentence does not say, and you know, say. This is why "avoid the passive" is a weak instrument. It catches one of four routes, and writers who obey it simply take one of the other three, usually route 2, and usually without noticing that nothing has changed.

Two constructions that look passive and are not

Two further cases are worth knowing because they defeat automated flagging and confuse careful readers.

The first is the get-passive: "the model got rejected", "the desk got fined". These are genuine passives with get in place of be, and they carry an informal register plus a suggestion that the subject was somehow implicated in what happened. They are rare in professional writing and worth recognising, because a reviewer objecting to one is objecting to register rather than to voice.

The second is more interesting and shows that agent-absence is not peculiar to the passive at all. English has verbs that take a patient as subject in the active voice: "the calibration understates the loss rate", "the door opened", "the position unwound", "the spread widened". No passive, no agent, nobody complains. If the objection to the passive were really about missing actors, these should attract the same objection. They do not, which suggests the objection was always about how the sentence sounded rather than about what it withheld.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The two-question test, and what it costs to apply

Everything ethical in this lecture reduces to two questions asked of any clause describing an event with no actor in it. Could you name the actor? And would naming them change who appears answerable?

If the answer to the first is no, you are done: the construction is legitimate and there is nothing to discuss. If the answer to the first is yes and to the second is no, naming them is a small improvement and you should probably do it. The case that matters is yes to both, and there the passive or the nominalisation is doing work that has nothing to do with prose. You are deciding whether a document will attribute an act to a person, and you should decide that on its merits rather than by default.

Applying the test honestly is expensive, which is why it is rarely applied. Naming an actor in a document that circulates has consequences for that person, and a writer who names them has taken a position. Sometimes the position is wrong: attributing a systemic failure to the individual who happened to sign the form is a real injustice, and the agentless passive is occasionally the fairer construction. The instruction is not "always name the actor". It is "know that you are choosing, and be able to say why".

Where impersonality is a genuine convention

A fair account has to concede that some agentless writing is a professional norm with defensible grounds, rather than evasion. Three cases deserve it.

Accounting and regulatory text imposes obligations on an indefinite class, so the actor is universally quantified rather than hidden. "Impairment is recognised when there is objective evidence of..." is addressed to every preparer, and inserting a specific one would falsify it.

Method sections in empirical work traditionally use agentless passives to signal that the procedure is reproducible independently of who ran it. That convention has weakened, and many journals now prefer the first person, which is a bin 2 convention shift in the sense of module 1's lecture 7 rather than a discovery. Both are defensible; what matters is knowing which your venue expects.

Findings that must protect a source are the third case, and the only one where suppression is not merely conventional but obligatory. A report that says "concerns were raised about the override process" may be protecting whoever raised them, and here the agentless passive is doing exactly the job it should.

What none of the three licenses is the general habit. An institution whose documents are agentless throughout is not applying three narrow conventions; it has adopted a register, and the register has an effect regardless of what any individual writer intended.

The claim to take away

The passive is not a defect, and the advice against it has done more damage than the construction ever did, because it taught writers to fear a shape rather than to notice a deletion. The shape is a topic-management device you need. The deletion is a choice with consequences, available through at least four constructions, only one of which the style guides name.

So the operational rule inverts the usual one. Do not ask whether a sentence is passive. Ask whether it has an actor, and if it does not, ask whether you could have supplied one. That question catches every route, requires no ability to parse a participle, and points at the thing that actually matters.

Common failure Reading this lecture as permission and going back to writing passives everywhere. The four legitimate uses are narrow, and the vast majority of passives in professional prose fall outside all of them: they are there because the register was learned from documents that used them, and they cost the reader a step for nothing. The claim here is that the passive is a tool with four jobs, not that it has no cost. If you cannot name which of the four a given passive is doing, convert it.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Reproduce the step 3 figure with a passage from your own work: write it all-active and note where the subject chain jumps, then rewrite using one passive to hold the topic steady. Keep the agent in the by phrase. Report both subject chains.
  2. State the three properties a clause must have together to be passive. Use them to classify "the reason he left college was that his health became impaired".
  3. Why does it matter that Strunk and White's corrections are all improvements, even though three of their four examples are not passives? What do readers actually learn from the section?
  4. Name the four legitimate uses. For which two should you normally keep the by phrase, and why?
  5. Explain why the end-weight use is two principles agreeing rather than a compromise. Which lecture supplies the other principle?
  6. Give the four routes to an actorless sentence. Which one do style guides flag, and which is the worst of the four?
  7. "The spread widened." No passive, no agent, and nobody objects. What does that suggest about what the objection to the passive is really about?
  8. Apply the two-question test to three actorless clauses from a document you did not write. For each, say whether the construction is legitimate, a small improvement is available, or a real choice was made. Be specific about which.
