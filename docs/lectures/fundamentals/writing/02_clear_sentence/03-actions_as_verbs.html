Actions as verbs

The second half of the principle, and the half with a consequence nobody teaches. A finite verb carries tense, aspect, modality and the scope of negation. A noun carries none of them. So when you move an action out of a verb, you do not merely make the sentence heavier: you delete the grammar that says when it happened and whether anyone has to do it.

Lecture 3 of 8 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: Williams, Style, lesson 2; Quirk et al. (1985), ch. 3 and 4
Step 1

The 30-second version

Every sentence has actions in it. English does not require them to sit in verbs, and professional prose routinely puts them somewhere else: in a noun (the assessment), in an adjective (is dependent on), in a participle (having reviewed), or in a prepositional phrase (in breach of). Whenever an action moves out of the verb slot, something contentless has to move in, and the sentence acquires an empty verb.

The empty verb is the load-bearing symptom. If you circle every verb in a paragraph and find only forms of be and have, plus conduct, perform, undertake, provide and result in, you have found a paragraph whose actions are all somewhere else. That diagnosis takes ten seconds and requires no judgement, which is why it works on your own writing.

The cost is not only readability, and this is the part the style literature underplays. A finite verb is obliged to carry tense, aspect and modality; it cannot decline. A noun is obliged to carry none of them. "Remediation of the calibration is required" does not say who must remediate, by when, or whether it has started. "The model owner must recalibrate before the March submission" says all three, because the verb forced them into the open. In a governance document that difference is not a matter of style. It is the difference between an action having an owner and a date, and not having them.

Feynman test A verb is the sentence's engine and it comes with instruments attached: a clock that says when, a dial that says must or might, and a switch that says did or did not. Pack the action into a noun and you have moved the engine into the boot, so the sentence still contains it and nothing is connected to the instruments. That is why nominalised prose feels inert. Nothing in it is happening at any particular time to anybody.
What is beyond the source Williams argues for actions in verbs on grounds of clarity, and the grammars document what finite verbs inflect for. Joining the two, and arguing that nominalisation is therefore a control weakness rather than a style defect because it discards the machinery for tense and obligation, is this lecture's construction. It is the argument most likely to persuade a reviewer who does not care about prose, which is a substantial practical advantage over the aesthetic case.
Step 2

Why this matters

Start with the comprehension cost, which is Gopen and Swan's sixth expectation. Readers expect the action of a sentence to appear in its verb, and when it appears elsewhere the sentence still parses while requiring an extra step: the reader reaches the verb, learns nothing from it, and must continue to the object or the complement to find out what happened. Multiplied across a document, the cost is significant and invisible.

Then the governance cost, which is larger and easier to argue in a professional setting. Consider what a nominalised action leaves undetermined. Recalibration does not say whether it is planned, in progress, complete or merely desirable. Non-compliance does not say whether the breach is continuing. Escalation does not say whether anyone escalated. Each of these appears in real remediation plans, and each one is a sentence that survives audit precisely because it has not committed to anything checkable.

Negation makes this sharper. "Non-compliance with the threshold was identified" is ambiguous in a way its verbal counterpart cannot be. Did the desk not comply, or did the monitoring fail to establish compliance? Was the non-compliance identified once, or is it ongoing? "The desk breached the threshold in February and remains in breach" answers all of it, and it answers because the verbs were forced to.

There is a third reason that applies directly to exam writing, and it is worth stating because it is the most immediately testable claim in the lecture. Examiners award marks for points made. A nominalised answer frequently fails to make the point, not because the candidate did not know it, but because the sentence never committed to a claim anyone could tick. "Consideration of the impact of the downturn on the LGD estimate is necessary" is not an answer. "A downturn raises LGD because recovery values fall while exposures stay fixed" is an answer, and the difference is entirely in where the actions sit.

Step 3

The mental model

Think of the finite verb as a slot with four instruments wired into it. Whatever you put there must set all four, because English inflection does not permit abstention. Move the action out and the instruments are still there, still set, but now they describe the empty verb instead of the action you cared about.

The claim in the caption deserves to be tested rather than accepted, so test it on the two versions. Is the first sentence false? No. Is it incomplete in a way a reader would notice? Also no, which is the problem: it reads as a complete statement of a requirement. The second version contains four commitments that the first does not, and every one of them arrived because English would not let the verb recalibrate appear without a tense, a modality and a subject. The grammar did the work that the writer had avoided.

Empty verb
A verb contributing almost no meaning, occupying the verb slot because the real action has moved elsewhere. The closed set is small: be, have, do, make, give, take, conduct, perform, undertake, provide, occur, exist, result in, involve.
Finite verb
A verb inflected for tense and agreeing with its subject, and therefore obliged to specify when the action happened and, through its auxiliaries, whether it is complete and whether it is required. Only a finite verb carries the four instruments.
Modality
The grammatical expression of necessity, possibility and permission, carried by the modal auxiliaries must, may, should, can, will. Nominalisation strips it, which is why remediation plans written in nouns cannot be held to.
Negation scope
Which part of a claim a negation applies to. A verb fixes the scope by position; a negative noun such as non-compliance or non-performance leaves it open, which is why such nouns are so common in documents describing failures.
Step 4

The detail

The four hiding places

An action can leave the verb slot in four directions, and each has a characteristic repair. Lecture 4 gives the noun case its own treatment because it is the commonest and the most consequential; the other three are handled here.

The adjective and preposition rows share a shape worth naming, because once you see it you can search for it. Each is a form of be, followed by a word derived from the verb you wanted, followed by a preposition to reattach the object the verb would have taken directly. The pattern is be + derived form + preposition, and its presence is close to conclusive evidence that a verb has been displaced. A text search for "is dependent", "is reflective", "is in breach" and their relatives will find most of them in any document.

The circle-the-verbs diagnostic

CIRCLE THE VERBS Take a paragraph. Mark every finite verb. Then classify. PARAGRAPH "The purpose of the review was to establish whether the downturn calibration is reflective of the 2008 experience. Analysis of the stress window indicated that exclusion of the peak quarter had occurred. This is in breach of the internal standard. Remediation is required." VERBS, IN ORDER was -> be. empty. is -> be. empty. indicated -> real, but its subject is "analysis", which is a nominalisation, so nobody indicated anything. had occurred -> empty. "occur" is what you use when you do not want to say who did it. is -> be. empty. is required -> be. empty. SCORE Six verbs. Five empty. One real verb with an abstract subject. Zero human actors anywhere in the paragraph. WHERE THE ACTIONS ACTUALLY ARE establish (infinitive, so not carrying tense) reflective (adjective) exclusion (noun) breach (noun, inside a prepositional phrase) remediation (noun) REPAIR: move each back into a finite verb "We reviewed whether the downturn calibration reflects the 2008 experience. It does not: the stress window excludes the peak quarter, which breaches the internal standard. The model owner must recalibrate before the March submission." VERBS NOW reviewed, reflects, does (not), excludes, breaches, must recalibrate Six verbs, six actions, one empty verb ("does" in the negation, which is doing real work). 60 words down to 44, and the last sentence now has an owner and a deadline that the original did not contain.

Notice the third line of the diagnosis, because it is the subtlest failure in the paragraph. Indicated is a real verb carrying a real action, and the sentence is still broken, because its subject is analysis. This is the two halves of Williams's principle failing independently, as lecture 1 warned: you can satisfy the action half and fail the character half. The repair had to fix both, and fixing the subject was what forced indicated to become excludes, with the stress window doing the work.

Where "be" is the right verb

A lecture that treated be as an error would be giving bad advice, so here is the boundary. Be is the correct verb whenever the sentence's job is to assert identity, classification or a property, because those are not actions and there is no action to relocate.

Definitions need it: "A default is an event in which an obligor is more than ninety days past due or is assessed as unlikely to pay." Classification needs it: "The exposure is in stage 2." Attribution of a genuine property needs it: "The model is well calibrated but poorly discriminating." In each case be is carrying the claim rather than standing in for a displaced verb, and replacing it would falsify the sentence.

The test that separates the two cases is mechanical. Ask whether a verb exists that means what the complement means. In "the model is in breach of the threshold", the complement contains breach, and breach is a verb, so be is standing in for it. In "the exposure is in stage 2", the complement contains stage 2, which is not a verb and names no action, so be is doing its own job. Apply that test and the distinction stops being a matter of feel.

The exam application

This is the shortest section and the most immediately useful if you are writing an exam answer under time pressure. Marks attach to claims. A claim needs a subject and a verb that commits. So the fastest available improvement to a technical answer is to check that each sentence has a real verb, because a sentence without one has usually gestured at a point rather than made it.

EXAM ANSWERS: GESTURED AGAINST MADE Question: why does a downturn increase LGD? GESTURED (likely to score little or nothing) "Consideration of the impact of an economic downturn on the LGD estimate is necessary, as there is a relationship between economic conditions and recovery outcomes." Verbs: is, is. Zero actions. The candidate has said that the question is worth asking. MADE (scores the point) "A downturn reduces collateral values, so recoveries fall while the exposure at default stays fixed, and LGD therefore rises. Downturn LGD must reflect the worst recovery experience rather than the long-run average, because losses and defaults peak together." Verbs: reduces, fall, stays, rises, must reflect, peak. Six actions, each one a claim a marker can tick. THE HABIT TO BUILD Under time pressure, writing "consideration of X is necessary" feels like progress because it fills a line. It commits to nothing. If you catch yourself opening with a nominalisation, the sentence you meant to write starts with the subject of that noun and the verb it came from.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Why institutions prefer nouns, stated without moralising

It would be easy to conclude that nominalised prose is written by people avoiding accountability, and sometimes it is. The more common explanation is duller and more useful, because it suggests a different remedy.

A noun is safe under uncertainty. When you do not know whether the recalibration has started, remediation is required is true and the model owner recalibrated might not be. When you do not know who decided something, the nominalisation lets you write the sentence anyway. So writers reach for nouns at exactly the moments when they lack information, and the resulting prose is an accurate map of what the writer did not know. The reason it reads as evasive is that it is indistinguishable from prose written by someone who did know and declined to say.

That reframing gives you a better diagnostic than suspicion. When a draft is dense with nominalisations, ask what the author could not establish. Frequently the answer is something worth chasing: nobody knows who owns the fix, nobody has a date, nobody has established causation. The prose was not concealing the gap so much as papering over it, and converting the nouns to verbs is what exposes it. This is the same observation lecture 2 made about restoring actors, and it generalises: the revision fails where the underlying work is incomplete, which makes the revision a useful test of the work.

The strongest case for the nominalisation

Halliday's account, developed with Martin for scientific writing specifically, is that nominalisation is not a defect of technical language but its central resource. The argument runs like this. A discipline advances by taking established findings and building new claims on top of them. To build on a finding you must be able to refer to it compactly, as a thing, so that it can occupy a subject slot while the new claim occupies the predicate. The observed drift in the PD distribution packages an entire prior result into six words, and it does so precisely by turning processes into nouns.

On this view the nominalising suffixes are how English grew a technical vocabulary at all. Inflation, impairment, regulation, derivation, distribution, correlation: every one is a nominalisation, none is replaceable, and a field could not discuss them without them. Halliday calls this grammatical metaphor and treats it as an achievement rather than a symptom.

The case is correct and it does not conflict with this lecture, once the two claims are stated precisely. Halliday's claim is that nominalisation is indispensable for packaging established knowledge so that new claims can be made about it. Williams's claim is that the new claim itself should be expressed in a verb. Both can hold at once, and the good sentence usually does both: an established finding, nominalised, in the subject; the new assertion, verbal, in the predicate. "The observed drift in the PD distribution invalidates the through-the-cycle assumption" is exactly that shape, and it is better prose than either a fully nominalised or a fully verbal alternative.

So the real target is narrower than "avoid nominalisation". It is a nominalisation naming the action the sentence is currently asserting, with the verb slot filled by something empty. Where the nominalisation packages something already established and the verb asserts something new, the sentence is working as designed. Lecture 4 turns that distinction into a decision procedure.

Common failure Converting every nominalisation to a verb and producing a document with no abstractions in it. A technical field's nouns are its concepts, and a lecture on IRRBB that refused to write duration, convexity or repricing would be unreadable in a new way. The instruction is not to eliminate abstract nouns. It is to make sure that the action the sentence is asserting sits in a finite verb, so that the sentence declares when it happened, whether it is done, and whether anyone must do it.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Run the circle-the-verbs diagnostic from step 4 on a paragraph of someone else's professional prose. Classify every finite verb as empty or real, list where the actions actually are, then repair it. Report the verb count before and after, and note whether your repair had to add a fact.
  2. Name the four things a finite verb necessarily carries and a noun need not. Which of the four matters most in a remediation plan, and why?
  3. Why is "nominalisation is a way of saying less while appearing to say the same thing" a stronger claim than "nominalisation is heavier"? Use the two sentences in step 3 as evidence.
  4. Give the be + derived form + preposition pattern and three instances of it from your own field. What makes the pattern searchable?
  5. State the mechanical test that distinguishes be doing its own job from be standing in for a displaced verb. Apply it to "the exposure is in default".
  6. In the step 4 diagnostic, one verb was real and the sentence was still broken. Explain why, in terms of the two halves of Williams's principle.
  7. Halliday argues nominalisation is the central resource of technical language. State his argument, then state precisely why it does not contradict Williams. Give a sentence that satisfies both.
  8. Take a remediation action from a real plan you have seen, written in nouns. Convert it to a verb and list every commitment the verb forced you to make. If you cannot make one of them, say what that tells you about the plan.
