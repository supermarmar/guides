Module 2 · Writing track

The clear sentence

The highest-leverage material in the course. A small number of principles about where characters and actions sit in a clause accounts for most of what makes institutional prose impenetrable, and for most of what fixing it involves.

What this module is

Joseph Williams observed something that ought to be obvious and is not: readers judge prose clear when the main characters of the story appear as grammatical subjects and their actions appear as finite verbs. Almost every sentence that people call unclear violates that fit, usually by turning an action into an abstract noun and then propping the noun up with a weak verb and a chain of prepositions.

The consequence is that clarity is diagnosable. You do not have to read a sentence back and wait to see whether it feels right. You can ask two questions of it, find out who the characters are and where the actions went, and revise on the answers. This module teaches those questions, the four legitimate uses of the passive that survive them, the two positional principles that govern emphasis, and then hands you Lanham's procedure for running the whole thing as a drill.

Nothing here is about length. Long sentences are not the problem and short ones are not the cure, a point the first lecture spends its time establishing because almost every reader arrives believing the opposite.

Prerequisites

  • Module 1, lecture 3: parts of speech as functions. You need to recognise a noun formed from a verb.
  • Module 1, lecture 4: the clause. Subject, verb and the finite/non-finite distinction are used constantly here.
  • Module 1, lecture 6: punctuation. Lecture 7 uses the colon and the dash as emphasis tools.

Module sequence

Part 1 · The principle (lectures 1 to 4)

Williams's character and action principle, stated, then applied to the single commonest failure mode in professional writing.

  • builtLecture 1: why prose feels unclear. Clarity as a reader's judgement about the fit between story and syntax, not a property of word length or sentence length. Why the readability formulas cannot see it.
  • builtLecture 2: characters as subjects. Readers look for the main character in the grammatical subject. Abstract subjects, missing subjects and delayed subjects, and what each does to comprehension.
  • builtLecture 3: actions as verbs. The other half of the principle. Put the action in a finite verb rather than in a noun, an adjective or a participle hanging off the end.
  • builtLecture 4: nominalisation and the zombie noun. How the suffixes convert actions into abstractions, and what the abstraction costs. Sword's zombie-noun test and the five-part Writer's Diet diagnostic, taught alongside the reason no surface metric can be trusted alone.

Part 2 · Voice and position (lectures 5 to 7)

The passive, properly understood rather than banned, and the two positions in a sentence that carry weight.

  • builtLecture 5: the passive voice, properly understood. What the passive actually is, why most alleged examples are misdiagnosed (including three of the four in Strunk and White), and its four legitimate uses: unknown agent, topic continuity, end-weight, and deliberate suppression of the actor. The last is where the ethics start.
  • builtLecture 6: subject-verb proximity and the fast opening. Readers treat anything sitting between a subject and its verb as an interruption to be held in memory. Start fast, and move long qualifications out of the gap.
  • builtLecture 7: the stress position. Emphasis attaches to the point of syntactic closure. Earning emphasis by placement rather than by italics or intensifiers, and using the colon and the resumptive, summative and free modifier to build towards it.

Part 3 · The drill (lecture 8)

The whole module converted into a procedure you can execute on a page of someone else's prose.

  • builtLecture 8: diagnosis workshop, the Paramedic Method. Lanham's procedure end to end, including the three rhythm steps that most summaries of it drop. The Official Style as a recognisable dialect, and the lard factor as a measure of how much of a draft was never doing any work.

Where this reappears

The stress position
Introduced in lecture 7 as a sentence-level effect. Module 3 turns it into the engine of cohesion: new material in a stress position must become the old information of the next sentence.
Agent suppression
Flagged in lecture 5 as a legitimate use of the passive. Module 9, lecture 7 returns to it as the central mechanism by which prose evades accountability.
The lard factor
Introduced in lecture 8 as a diagnostic number. Module 5 supplies the taxonomy of what the lard is actually made of.
