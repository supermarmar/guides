Lectures / Writing / The clear sentence

Subject-verb proximity and the fast opening

Readers hold an unfinished clause in memory until the verb arrives, and everything sitting in the gap is held with it. This is the one principle in the module that comes with a number attached, and the number is easy to measure on your own prose.

Lecture 6 of 8 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: Gopen & Swan (1990), expectation 2; Gibson (2000); Williams, Style, lesson 5
Step 1

The 30-second version

When a reader meets a subject, they open a structure and must hold it open until the verb closes it. Everything between the two is held in working memory alongside the incomplete clause, and the cost of holding rises with the amount of material. So the distance between a subject's head noun and its verb is a measurable property of your sentence, and it predicts difficulty better than length does.

The positive instruction is to start fast: get the reader to a character and then to a verb within the first seven or eight words. That is not a measured threshold and should not be treated as one, since the cost is continuous rather than a cliff. It is a working heuristic tied to the same eight-word diagnostic lecture 2 used, and its value is that it flags candidates mechanically.

Three things create the gap, and each takes a different repair. The subject noun phrase can simply be long, with modifiers piled onto its head. A relative clause can interrupt between subject and verb. Or an adverbial or parenthesis can sit in the gap. The repairs are to move material after the verb, to move it in front of the subject as a shallow left branch, or to split the sentence. Almost never is the repair to delete anything, which is why this lecture is not about concision.

Feynman test Reading a clause is like being handed the first half of a sentence and told to wait. You can wait comfortably for a moment. If someone spends the wait handing you thirteen more things to hold, you are still waiting for the verb and now your hands are full. The fix is not to hand over fewer things. It is to let the verb arrive first, and then hand them over.
What is beyond the source Gopen and Swan state the expectation, Gibson supplies the cost mechanism, and Williams gives the start-fast advice. None of them ranks the three sources of the gap or pairs each with its repair, which is what step 4 does. The one claim worth flagging as this lecture's own is that the repair almost always converges with end weight: moving material rightward serves both principles at once, so the two never conflict in practice.
Step 2

Why this matters

This is the most mechanically checkable principle in the module and therefore the one you can apply fastest. Counting the words between a subject's head and its verb requires no grammatical judgement beyond identifying the two, which module 1's lecture 4 reduced to three mechanical tests. A writer can audit a whole document for this in twenty minutes, and the sentences it surfaces are reliably the sentences worth rewriting.

It also explains a category of complaint that otherwise has no explanation. Readers describe institutional sentences as "dense" or "heavy" far more often than they describe them as long, and the density they are reporting is frequently this: a subject held open across a mass of qualification. The sentence may be perfectly ordinary in length. What it demands is that the reader carry an unresolved dependency while processing material that cannot yet be attached to anything.

The professional stake is the same one module 1's lecture 5 identified from the branching side, and it is worth restating because this is where it bites hardest. Legal and regulatory drafting places qualifications before the operative verb by convention, so that no reader reaches the provision without passing through its limits. That convention produces exactly this gap, deliberately. A validation report or a credit paper inherits the shape without inheriting the reason, and the result is prose that imposes a statutory reading cost on a document nobody will litigate.

And there is a reason that matters for anything read under time pressure, including an exam script. A reader skimming reads sentence openings. If your first eight words deliver a character and a verb, a skimming reader gets your point. If they deliver a subject and the beginning of a qualification, the skimmer gets nothing and moves on, and the point you buried at word thirty is not in the document as far as they are concerned.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture the gap as a period during which the reader is carrying something. The subject's head noun opens the carry; the verb ends it. Whatever sits in between is additional load, and the load is worse than the same material placed after the verb, because after the verb the clause is complete and the reader can attach each new piece as it arrives rather than holding it unattached.

The mechanism behind the cost is the one module 1's lecture 5 introduced from the branching side. Gibson's dependency locality theory holds that integrating a word with the structure it belongs to costs more the further apart the two are, so the subject-verb dependency is expensive in exact proportion to the gap. Notice that this makes the principle continuous: there is no threshold at which a sentence becomes hard, and the eight-word heuristic is a convenience for finding candidates rather than a line the language enforces.

Subject-verb distance
The number of words between the head noun of the subject and the finite verb that agrees with it. Measured on the head, not on the start of the noun phrase, because the head is what the reader must hold.
Fast opening
A sentence that delivers a character and then a verb within its first several words. Williams's formulation. Its practical value is that skimming readers read openings, so a fast opening is the difference between being read and being passed over.
End weight
The English preference for placing heavy material late in a clause. Converges with subject-verb proximity: moving a long constituent rightward satisfies both, which is why the two principles never conflict in practice.
Step 4

The detail

Choosing among the four repairs

RepairUse it whenWhat it costs
Ranked by how often they are the right answer
Move the material right, after the verb The material is adjunct: when, where, for which portfolio, under what conditions. This is the default and handles most cases. Nothing. It also satisfies end weight, so the sentence improves twice.
Split into two sentences The interrupting material is making an assertion of its own, which a non-restrictive relative clause always is. Two claims were sharing one sentence. One extra sentence, and you must supply the link that the relative clause was providing implicitly.
Front it as a left branch The material is a condition or a frame that must be established before the claim: "If the exposure has migrated to stage 2, ...". One branch only. A small holding cost at the front, which buys the reader a frame they would otherwise have to retrofit.
Shorten the subject by promoting the head The head noun is a container word: set, range, number, series, proportion. Delete the container and the real noun becomes the head. Nothing. "The set of assumptions" almost always means "the assumptions".

The fourth repair deserves emphasis because it is the cheapest and the least known. Container heads are endemic in professional prose: a number of exposures, a range of scenarios, the set of assumptions, a series of adjustments, the proportion of accounts. Each one puts an abstraction in the head position and pushes the real noun into an of phrase, which both widens the gap and, as lecture 4 showed, is the tell for a nominalisation habit. Deleting the container fixes the gap, removes a preposition, and puts a concrete noun where the reader is looking.

Measuring it

Gap: 2. Words: 21 before, 20 after. SENTENCE B "Management, having considered the findings of the independent review and the representations made by the first line, concluded that no restatement was required." Subject head: "Management" Verb: "concluded" Gap: 18 Source: a participial interruption (source 3) Repair, fronting the branch: "Having considered the independent review's findings and the first line's representations, management concluded that no restatement was required." Gap: 1. Words: 27 before, 22 after. SENTENCE C "The proportion of accounts in the mortgage book that migrated to stage 2 during the quarter exceeded the appetite threshold." Subject head: "proportion" Verb: "exceeded" Gap: 13 Source: container head plus a restrictive relative clause that CANNOT move, because it identifies which accounts Repair: the relative clause is restrictive, so moving it would change the claim. Split instead. "Two fifths of the mortgage book migrated to stage 2 during the quarter. That exceeds the appetite threshold." Gap: 1 and 1. The restriction survives as the main clause of the first sentence. WHAT THE AUDIT SHOWS Word counts barely moved in any of the three. C is the instructive one: the obvious repair was unavailable because the interrupting material was restrictive, and module 1 lecture 6 is what tells you that.

When the gap is fine

Short interruptions are unremarkable and hunting them is a waste of attention. "The committee, however, disagreed" has a gap of one and reads perfectly. "The model, unusually, passed every test" is fine. A brief appositive is fine: "Basel III, the post-crisis framework, tightened the definition of capital."

The threshold question has no clean answer, and pretending otherwise would contradict what step 3 said about the cost being continuous. The practical guidance is that gaps of one to three words are invisible, gaps around five to eight are worth a glance if the sentence is otherwise complicated, and gaps above roughly ten are worth measuring and almost always worth repairing. Treat those as bands for allocating attention rather than as rules.

One further case is genuinely fine and looks bad: a long subject with a short gap, where the length is in the head noun's own compound rather than in modifiers after it. "Point-in-time probability of default estimates vary with the cycle" has a long subject and the verb arrives immediately after the head. The reader was not holding anything unresolved, because nothing intervened. This is why the measurement is taken from the head rather than from the start of the phrase.

Why writers create the gap

The habit has a rational explanation, which is worth knowing because it tells you when you are about to do it. A writer adding a qualification puts it next to the thing being qualified. That is locally correct: the qualification belongs to the subject, so it goes with the subject. And it is globally wrong, because the reader has not yet been given the verb and cannot use the qualification for anything.

Which yields the useful reformulation. The question is not where the qualification logically belongs but when the reader can use it. A reader can use "for the secured retail portfolio" only after they know what is being claimed, so it belongs after the claim. This is the same reasoning that makes the given-new contract work in module 3, arriving early and at sentence scale: material is placed by when it becomes usable, not by what it attaches to.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The convergence, and why it should reassure you

Four principles from four different lectures all recommend the same physical move, which is to shift heavy material rightward. Subject-verb proximity wants the gap closed. End weight wants long constituents late. Right-branching wants each constituent to close before the next opens. And the passive's end-weight use, from lecture 5, exists precisely to move a heavy agent past the verb.

That convergence is worth noticing because it is unusual. Writing advice mostly consists of principles that trade off against each other, and a writer applying several at once is normally balancing. Here they agree, which means the move is close to free: shifting a long qualification from before the verb to after it improves the sentence on four independent counts and worsens it on none.

The single exception is a restrictive modifier, as sentence C in step 4 showed. A restrictive relative clause identifies which things you mean, so moving it changes the claim, and no amount of positional tidiness justifies that. When you meet one, the repair is a split rather than a shift. Recognising the case requires module 1's lecture 6, which is why punctuation turned out to matter for a lecture about position.

The skimming argument, stated properly

Step 2 claimed that skimming readers read openings, and the claim deserves scrutiny rather than assertion, because it is doing a lot of work. What is well established is that readers of professional documents do not read them linearly or completely; they sample. What is less precisely established is exactly what they sample, and the honest position is that sentence and paragraph openings are the best available guess rather than a measured fact.

Even on that weaker footing the conclusion holds, and here is why. A sampling reader must decide, quickly and repeatedly, whether to keep reading a given sentence. Whatever governs that decision, it cannot be information the sentence has not yet delivered. So a sentence whose first eight words contain no claim is being judged on nothing, and a sentence whose first eight words contain a character and a verb is being judged on its content. You do not need to know precisely how readers sample to conclude that front-loading the claim is the dominant strategy.

Module 4 scales this argument to whole documents, where it becomes the Pyramid Principle: a reader who will not reach the end must be given the conclusion at the start. The sentence-level version here is the same argument at a smaller scale, and noticing that they are the same argument is part of why module 4 places the pyramid late rather than first.

Common failure Treating this as a licence to strip qualifications out. Almost nothing in this lecture involves deleting anything: the three repairs move material, and the fourth deletes only a container word. A writer who reads a proximity audit as a mandate for concision will remove the conditions that made a claim accurate, which is a far worse error than a wide gap. If your repair loses a qualification, you have applied the wrong repair.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Run the step 4 audit on five sentences from a document you did not write. For each, record the subject head, the verb, the gap, the source, and the repair you chose. Report word counts before and after, and identify at least one sentence where the obvious repair was unavailable.
  2. Why is the distance measured from the head noun rather than from the start of the subject noun phrase? Give a sentence with a long subject and a small gap.
  3. Name the three sources of the gap and the repair each one calls for. Which source requires a split rather than a shift, and why?
  4. What is a container head? Give four from your own field, and explain why deleting the container fixes two problems at once.
  5. Explain why subject-verb proximity, end weight, right-branching and the passive's end-weight use all recommend the same move. What is the single exception?
  6. The cost of the gap is continuous rather than a threshold. Why does that mean the eight-word heuristic should be treated as a way of allocating attention rather than as a rule?
  7. Writers put qualifications next to the thing qualified, which is locally correct and globally wrong. State the reformulation that resolves it.
  8. The skimming argument rests on a weaker empirical claim than step 2 implies. State the weaker claim, and show that the conclusion still follows from it.
