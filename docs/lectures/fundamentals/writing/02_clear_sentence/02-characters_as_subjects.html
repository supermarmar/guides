Characters as subjects

Readers look for the story's protagonist in the grammatical subject, and they look there first. Which means deciding what goes in your subjects is not a stylistic tidy-up performed at the end. It is deciding what your document is about.

Lecture 2 of 8 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: Williams, Style, lesson 3; Gopen & Swan (1990), expectation 5
Step 1

The 30-second version

Readers treat the grammatical subject of a clause as the answer to "who or what is this about". They form that judgement on arrival, before the verb, and they proceed on it. Put a character there, meaning whoever or whatever the story concerns and which performs the actions, and the sentence reads as being about something. Put an abstraction there, particularly one that names the action itself, and the reader keeps searching for the real protagonist while processing the rest.

Three things go wrong in the subject slot, and they need different repairs. The abstract subject puts the action there as a noun: "an assessment of the calibration was performed". The missing subject deletes the actor entirely: "the calibration was found to be inadequate", where nobody found anything. The buried subject keeps a real character but hides its head noun under a mass of modifiers, so the reader cannot tell which word is the subject until they have read twenty more.

The part that makes this interesting rather than mechanical is that "who is the character" usually has more than one defensible answer, and the answers are not equivalent. A set of facts about a model review can be written with the validation team as protagonist, or with the calibration as protagonist, or with the model owner as protagonist. All three can be perfectly clear. They leave the reader with materially different impressions of what happened and who is answerable for it, which is why this lecture belongs to a course that ends with a module on ethics.

Feynman test Every sentence is a tiny scene, and the subject slot is the doorway the reader watches. Whoever walks through it is the one the scene is about. If you send an abstraction through the door, the reader waits for the real character to arrive, and keeps waiting, and eventually decides the scene has no one in it. Which, if you wrote "errors were made", is exactly what you were hoping.
What is beyond the source Williams gives the diagnosis and the repair, and treats the choice of character as though it were usually obvious. It is not. The three-protagonist figure in step 3 is this lecture's addition, and the claim it supports, that selecting a protagonist is a substantive editorial decision with consequences for perceived accountability rather than a stylistic preference, goes beyond what Williams argues. He reaches it eventually, in his final lesson on ethics. This lecture puts it at the front because in professional writing it is the decision that actually matters.
Step 2

Why this matters

The comprehension argument comes first because nobody disputes it. Gopen and Swan's fifth reader expectation is that the story's protagonist appears as the grammatical subject, and violating it costs measurable effort: the reader holds the abstraction provisionally, scans forward for a candidate actor, and either finds one in a prepositional phrase or gives up and accepts the abstraction. Either way they have done work the sentence did not need to impose.

The accountability argument matters more in institutional writing, and it is not really about clarity at all. The grammar of English lets you describe an event without naming anyone who caused it, and it offers two distinct routes: make the action itself the subject, or use a passive and drop the by phrase. Both leave a sentence that is true, complete, grammatical and impossible to trace to a person. This is not a hypothetical abuse. It is the dominant register of institutional prose, and once you can see the mechanism you will notice that the documents most careful to avoid actors are reliably the documents describing things that went wrong.

The third reason is practical. Restoring characters to subjects is the highest-yield revision available, because it forces the other repairs. You cannot put the validation team in the subject slot and keep "was performed" in the verb slot; the sentence will not have it. So one decision about the subject cascades into fixing the verb, deleting the light-verb construction, and unwinding the preposition chain the nominalisation required. Module 1's lecture 3 showed damage propagating outwards from a single nominalisation. Repairing the subject runs that cascade backwards.

And there is a reason specific to technical documentation, which cuts the other way and is worth stating now so the lecture is not read as a blanket instruction. In a methodology document the appropriate protagonist frequently is not a person. A document about how a PD model works is legitimately about the model, and "the model" belongs in its subjects. The principle is not "write about people". It is "decide who the story is about, and let the grammar agree".

Step 3

The mental model

Take one set of facts. A validation team looked at a downturn calibration in the third quarter; the calibration understated the peak loss rate; the affected book is residential mortgages; the model owner had signed the calibration off in March using a stress window that excluded 2008. Now write it three times, each with a different protagonist in the subjects. All three versions below are clear by the standards of this module, and none is dishonest. They are not the same document.

The figure does two jobs. The first is to show that a coherent subject chain is what makes a passage feel connected, which is module 3's subject and is visible here in miniature: each version repeats or pronominalises its protagonist, and that repetition is the cohesion. The second is to block a misreading of Williams. His principle tells you that whoever your protagonist is should sit in the subject slot. It does not tell you who the protagonist should be, and pretending the choice is dictated by the facts is how writers end up making it by default.

Making it by default has a predictable direction. The version an institution produces without deciding is a fourth one, in which the protagonist is the process: "a review of the downturn calibration was undertaken in Q3, and a material understatement of the peak loss rate was identified". That is not a different editorial choice. It is the absence of one, and it is what happens when nobody asks the question.

Abstract subject
A subject slot occupied by a nominalisation naming the sentence's own action: the assessment, the implementation, the identification. The test is whether the thing can perform the verb. An assessment cannot assess.
Missing subject
A clause from which the actor has been deleted, usually by a passive with no by phrase. Grammatical, complete, and untraceable. Legitimate when the actor is genuinely unknown or irrelevant, which is rarer than its frequency suggests.
Buried subject
A real character whose head noun is hidden inside a long noun phrase, so the reader cannot identify the subject until they reach the verb. Diagnosed by the three subject tests in module 1, lecture 4.
Subject chain
The grammatical subjects of a passage read in order, ignoring everything else. A chain naming one or two consistent characters reads as connected prose; a wandering chain reads as incoherent even when every sentence is individually fine.
Step 4

The detail

The diagnostic: read the first seven or eight words

Williams's instruction is mechanical, which is the point: it works on your own prose, where critical reading does not. Take each sentence and look only at its opening seven or eight words. Two things should have happened by word eight. You should have met a character, and you should have reached a verb. If neither has happened, you have found a sentence to fix, and you found it without exercising any judgement about whether it read well.

THE EIGHT-WORD TEST Sentence 1 "An assessment of the appropriateness of the downturn ..." 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8 Word 8, no verb, no character. Subject is "assessment", which is the action itself. FAILS both checks. Sentence 2 "The revised set of downturn assumptions applied to ..." 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8 Word 8, no verb. Subject is "set", a container rather than a character. FAILS the verb check; the character check is marginal at best. Sentence 3 "The validation team reviewed the downturn calibration ..." 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 Word 4 is the verb. Character in the subject. PASSES both. Sentence 4 "Because the stress window excluded the 2008 peak, the model owner understated the peak loss rate." Word 8 is still inside a subordinate clause and the main subject has not arrived. This is a single shallow left branch, which module 1 lecture 5 said is acceptable and often preferable. PASSES on judgement. WHAT THE TEST IS FOR It finds candidates, not verdicts. Sentence 4 fails the literal test and is fine. The test's value is that it surfaces every sentence worth a second look without requiring you to trust your ear on your own writing.

Is it a character? The performance test

The word "character" invites a wrong test, which is concreteness. Concreteness is not the criterion. The criterion is whether the candidate can perform the verb you have given it, and more broadly whether it is the thing the passage is about.

This admits a wide range of non-human protagonists. A model can fail, discriminate, converge and misrank. A portfolio can migrate, deteriorate and concentrate. A calibration can understate. A yield curve can invert. In each case the noun names something in the world that behaves, and readers have no difficulty treating it as a protagonist, because they can attach behaviour to it. What fails the test is a noun naming the action of the sentence itself, because then the subject and the verb compete to express the same event and the verb loses.

Restoring a deleted actor

When the diagnostic finds a clause with no actor, ask who did it, and accept one of exactly three answers. The discipline is in refusing a fourth.

The first answer is a name or a role: the model owner, the credit risk committee, the second line. Use it. This is the case institutional prose most often has available and most often declines.

The second answer is a generic actor that is honest about being generic: we, the team, the bank. Many house styles bar the first person, which is a convention in the sense of module 1's lecture 7, and where it is barred a role name usually substitutes without loss. Where it is permitted, we is frequently the most honest subject available, and writers avoid it from a misplaced sense that impersonality is more rigorous. Impersonality is not more rigorous. It is less specific.

The third answer is that the actor is genuinely unknown or genuinely irrelevant. "The exposures were reclassified in March" is fine if the passage is about the exposures and nobody cares who moved them, and it is the correct construction if mentioning the actor would break the topic chain. This is one of the four legitimate uses of the passive, and lecture 5 sets all four out properly.

The fourth answer, which must be refused, is that naming the actor would be awkward. That is not a reason about the reader. It is a reason about the writer, or about the writer's colleagues, and recognising it as such is most of what this course has to offer on the subject of professional integrity.

A worked paragraph

BEFORE (98 words; subject of each sentence in brackets) "The purpose of this section is to summarise the outcome of the annual review. [purpose] A number of considerations were taken into account in the determination of the adequacy of the downturn calibration. [considerations] It was concluded that a material understatement of the peak loss rate exists. [it] There are three drivers of the understatement. [there] The most significant of these is the exclusion of the 2008 peak from the stress window. [the most significant] Remediation of the calibration is required prior to the next reporting cycle. [remediation]" SUBJECT CHAIN purpose -> considerations -> it -> there -> the most significant -> remediation Six subjects, no human being, two dummies, four nominalisations. The reader cannot say who did anything or who must now do anything. AFTER (76 words) "This section summarises the annual review. [section] The validation team assessed whether the downturn calibration was adequate, and concluded that it understates the peak loss rate materially. [team] Three drivers explain the understatement, of which the largest is that the stress window excludes the 2008 peak. [drivers] The model owner must recalibrate before the next reporting cycle. [model owner]" SUBJECT CHAIN section -> validation team -> drivers -> model owner Four subjects, two of them people who act, one of them the explanatory content itself. Every verb carries an action. WHAT CHANGED, AND WHAT DID NOT Not shortened for its own sake. Twenty-two words fell out as a by-product of moving actions into verbs. Two facts were ADDED that the original lacked: who assessed, and who must remediate. Both were known to the original author. Neither appeared. That is the pattern worth remembering. The original was not vague about the technical content at all. It was vague about people, and only about people.

The buried subject, briefly

The third failure mode needs less space here because module 1's lecture 4 gave it the three mechanical tests and lecture 6 of this module gives it a full treatment. The short version: a subject can be a perfectly good character and still fail, if its head noun is buried under enough modification that the reader cannot identify it before the verb arrives.

"The revised set of downturn assumptions applied to the secured retail portfolio during the 2024 cycle understates the peak loss rate" has a defensible protagonist in assumptions, and the reader cannot get to it, because the actual head noun is set and thirteen words separate it from its verb. The repair is not to change the character. It is to move the modifiers after the verb, which lecture 6 treats as a positional problem rather than a character problem. Keep the two diagnoses separate: this one is about which word is the subject, that one is about how far it sits from its verb.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Choosing a protagonist is choosing an argument

The three-panel figure is the most important thing in this lecture and it is easy to read as a demonstration of stylistic range. It is not. It is a demonstration that a grammatical decision carries an argumentative payload, and that a writer who does not make the decision consciously will still have made it.

Work through what each version commits you to. Putting the validation team in the subjects produces a document about the adequacy of the review, which is what second line wants to be assessed on. Putting the calibration in the subjects produces a document about a technical defect requiring a technical fix, which is what everyone involved prefers, because a defect has no author. Putting the model owner in the subjects produces a document about a person's judgement, and note what happened in the figure when that choice was made: the version had to supply a cause, because a sentence with a human subject and an active verb invites the question "why did they do that" in a way that "the calibration understates" does not.

That is the mechanism, and it is worth stating flatly. Human subjects with active verbs generate demands for explanation. Abstract subjects absorb them. An institution that writes in abstractions is not merely writing badly; it is choosing a grammar in which the question does not arise. Module 9's final lecture takes this up as an ethical matter. Here the point is narrower and more useful: you cannot avoid making the choice, so make it on purpose.

Where the abstraction is the right answer

A course that only pushed towards human subjects would be giving bad advice, so here is the countervailing case, stated as strongly as it deserves.

In mathematics, the protagonists are objects. "The function converges", "the estimator is consistent", "the series diverges" are not evasions and could not be improved by naming a mathematician. The subject is the thing the claim is about, and the claim is about the object. The same holds through most of the quantitative material in this collection: a lecture on the Basel risk weight function should have the function in its subjects, because the function is what behaves.

In accounting and regulatory description the same applies with a twist. "The provision is recognised when..." has no actor because the standard imposes the treatment on anyone at all; naming a preparer would falsely particularise a general rule. The absent actor is not hidden, it is universally quantified, and readers correctly understand it that way.

So the honest rule has two clauses. Where the story is about a thing and the thing behaves, the thing is the character and belongs in the subject. Where the story is about what somebody decided, did or concluded, the somebody is the character, and putting the abstraction there instead is a choice with consequences. The failure is not abstraction; it is abstraction standing in for a person whose identity you knew.

The trap on the other side

Writers who take this lecture seriously often overcorrect in a specific way, and the result is worth naming so you can recognise it in your own next draft. They insert we into every sentence. The document becomes a chronicle of the authors: we assessed, we found, we concluded, we recommend. Every subject is a character, every verb is an action, and the prose is now about the wrong thing, because a reader of a validation report wants to know about the model rather than about the authors' week.

The tell is a subject chain of forty consecutive first-person pronouns. Module 3 will give you the proper diagnosis, which is that a good passage usually alternates between two characters rather than hammering one, and that the choice of which one occupies a given subject slot is governed by what the reader already holds. For now the practical guard is simple: when you restore a character, ask whether it is the character the paragraph is actually about, or merely the nearest available human.

Common failure Treating this as a search-and-replace exercise. Restoring characters to subjects frequently requires you to find out a fact you do not have: who approved the calibration, who decided the window, who is accountable for the fix. That is not a writing task and it cannot be done at the keyboard. When the revision stalls because you genuinely do not know who the actor was, you have discovered a gap in the work rather than in the prose, and the honest move is to go and find out rather than to reach for a construction that conceals the gap.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Reproduce the three-panel figure from step 3 with facts from your own work. Write the same event three times with three different protagonists, extract each subject chain, and state in one sentence what impression each version leaves. Then write the fourth version, the one with the process as protagonist, and say what it conceals.
  2. Name the three ways a subject slot goes wrong, and give the distinct repair each one needs.
  3. State the performance test for whether a candidate is a character. Why is concreteness the wrong test?
  4. What are the three acceptable answers to "who did it", and what is the fourth answer that must be refused? Why is the fourth one not a reason about the reader?
  5. In the worked paragraph in step 4, two facts were added rather than merely rearranged. What were they, and what does it tell you that the original author knew both?
  6. Why do human subjects with active verbs generate demands for explanation that abstract subjects absorb? Use the third panel of the figure as your evidence.
  7. Give an example from your own field where an abstraction is the correct protagonist, and state the general condition that makes it correct.
  8. Run the eight-word test on the opening sentences of a document you wrote this month. Count how many reach word eight without a verb. Fix the worst one, and note whether the fix required information you had to look up.
