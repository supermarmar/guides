Lectures / Writing / The clear sentence

Why prose feels unclear

Clarity is not a property of words or of sentence length. It is a judgement readers make about the fit between the story being told and the syntax telling it. This matters practically, because the metrics everyone uses to measure readability are blind to exactly the thing that determines it.

Lecture 1 of 8 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: Williams, Style, lessons 1 to 3; Flesch (1948); Kincaid et al. (1975)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Joseph Williams's central claim is that readers judge a sentence clear when its main characters appear as grammatical subjects and their actions appear as finite verbs. When the story and the syntax line up that way, prose reads as transparent. When they come apart, when the action has been packed into an abstract noun and the subject slot holds that abstraction instead of an actor, readers report the sentence as heavy, vague or difficult, and they almost never identify the cause.

Notice what this does to the problem. Clarity stops being an aesthetic quality that some writers have, and becomes a checkable relationship between two things you can both inspect: what your sentence is about, and what its grammar says it is about. That is why this module is the highest-leverage part of the course. It converts an unanswerable question, is this well written, into two answerable ones.

The negative half of the claim is just as important and much less welcome. If clarity is a matter of that fit, then it is not a matter of word length or sentence length, which means every readability formula in common use is measuring the wrong thing. Flesch Reading Ease, Flesch-Kincaid, Gunning Fog and the rest are functions of two variables only: how long the words are and how long the sentences are. They cannot see word order at all. Scramble a sentence into gibberish and the score does not move.

Feynman test A readability formula is a bathroom scale being used to assess an athlete. It measures something real, and heavier does correlate loosely with slower, so the reading is not pure noise. But it cannot see technique, and if you make it the target the athlete will lose weight by cutting off a leg. Splitting a bad sentence in half improves your Flesch-Kincaid score exactly the way an amputation improves the scale reading.
What is beyond the source Williams makes the positive claim and does not attack the formulas; the readability literature defends the formulas on their own terms and does not engage Williams. Putting them in contact, and in particular the permutation argument in step 3, is this lecture's construction. The permutation point is not a subtle criticism: it is arithmetic, since every formula's inputs are counts that are invariant under reordering. Nobody in the readability tradition disputes it, which is itself worth noticing.
Step 2

Why this matters

The formulas are not a curiosity confined to schoolbook selection. They are embedded in the tooling that professionals actually use. Word processors report them. Content management systems enforce thresholds on them. Consumer disclosure regimes impose comprehensibility duties, and firms discharge those duties by demonstrating a score, because a score is auditable and a judgement is not. So the wrong measure is not merely available; it is the one with institutional backing.

What follows is a textbook case of a measure becoming a target and ceasing to measure. If the score is what gets checked, the cheapest way to improve it is to split long sentences at arbitrary points and swap Latinate words for shorter ones without regard to whether the shorter word means the same thing. Both moves improve the number. The first destroys the connective tissue between clauses, replacing subordination that named a relation with a full stop that names nothing, which is precisely the damage lecture 5 of module 1 warned about. The second is the error module 1's lecture 2 examined, where a term of art gets replaced by a plain synonym that makes a weaker claim.

There is a second cost, subtler and worse. Believing that clarity is about length teaches you to look in the wrong place when a sentence fails. You shorten it, the score improves, the sentence remains difficult, and the only conclusion available is that the material is inherently hard or that you lack some talent you cannot name. Thousands of competent professionals conclude exactly that about their own writing. The diagnosis in this module is available to anyone, and it is available specifically because it does not depend on talent.

The third reason to take this seriously is that the fit between story and syntax is not only a comprehension question. Deciding which character occupies your subjects is deciding what your document is about, and that is a substantive editorial choice with consequences for what the reader concludes. Lecture 2 shows the same facts written three ways with three different protagonists, all clear, all true, and all leaving the reader with a different impression of where responsibility lies.

Step 3

The mental model

Every readability formula in common use takes the same shape: a weighted sum of a sentence-length term and a word-length term. Flesch Reading Ease subtracts 1.015 times words per sentence and 84.6 times syllables per word from a constant. Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level adds 0.39 times words per sentence to 11.8 times syllables per word and subtracts 15.59. Gunning Fog takes four tenths of words per sentence plus the percentage of words with three or more syllables. Coleman-Liau uses letters instead of syllables. The details differ and the inputs are the same two quantities.

Those quantities are counts. Counts do not change when you reorder the things being counted. That single observation is the whole argument, and the figure below makes it concrete.

Someone will object that the scrambled sentence is a straw man because nobody writes like that. The objection misses the point, which is not that formulas fail on gibberish but that they are formally incapable of registering the difference. A measure blind to the distinction between a sentence and its permutation is blind to every ordering effect, and every principle in this module is an ordering effect: which element sits in the subject, where the action sits, how far apart the subject and verb are, what occupies the end. The formulas cannot see any of it. The next section shows what that costs on realistic prose.

Character
Whoever or whatever the story is about, and which does the actions. Need not be a person: a model, a portfolio or a bank can be a character. What disqualifies a candidate is naming an action rather than performing one.
Action
What happens in a sentence, as distinct from where English has put it. The whole diagnosis rests on the fact that an action can sit in a verb, a noun, an adjective or a participle, and only one of those positions is cheap for the reader.
Readability formula
A weighted sum of average sentence length and average word length, calibrated in the mid-twentieth century to predict reading grade level for text selection. Validated for that purpose. Not a measure of clarity, and not capable of being one.
Step 4

The detail

The formulas, and what they were built for

FormulaWhat it computesWhat it was built for
Length-only formulas: blind to order by construction
Flesch Reading Ease (1948) 206.835 - 1.015(words/sentences) - 84.6(syllables/words). Higher is easier. Grading magazine and schoolbook prose for general adult audiences.
Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level (1975) 0.39(words/sentences) + 11.8(syllables/words) - 15.59. Output is a US school grade. Commissioned for the US Navy, to match technical manuals to the reading level of enlisted personnel.
Gunning Fog (1952) 0.4[(words/sentences) + 100(words of 3+ syllables / words)]. Business writing, as a rough screen for over-complication.
SMOG (1969) A function of polysyllable count over a 30-sentence sample. Health communication, where it is still widely required.
Coleman-Liau (1975) Letters per 100 words and sentences per 100 words. Avoids syllable counting. Machine scoring, when counting syllables reliably was hard.
The one that sees slightly more
Dale-Chall (1948, revised 1995) Percentage of words outside a list of familiar words, plus sentence length. Also grade-level matching, but the word list makes it sensitive to vocabulary familiarity rather than to mere length, which is closer to the frequency effect module 1 described.

Two things follow from the right-hand column. These formulas were built to solve a real problem and they solved it: given a corpus of continuous prose and a population of readers, they predict grade-level comprehension well enough to be useful for selecting texts. That is a genuine achievement and the criticism here is not that they were badly made. The criticism is that they were built to choose between documents and are now used to improve them, which is a different task that the tools were never validated for.

Dale-Chall deserves the exception the table gives it. Because it consults a list of familiar words rather than counting syllables, it is picking up something closer to word frequency, and module 1's lecture 2 argued that frequency is the variable that actually drives lexical processing cost. It remains blind to every ordering effect, so it is a better instrument aimed at the same wrong target.

The demonstration on realistic prose

The permutation test proves the formulas are blind to order. This next one shows that the blindness has consequences on prose someone might actually write, and that the two most widely used formulas do not even agree with each other about it.

VERSION A (clear, one sentence, 25 words) "The validation team reviewed the downturn loss calibration for the mortgage book in the third quarter and found that it understated the peak loss rate." Words 25 Sentences 1 Syllables 36 (counted by hand) Words/sentence 25.0 Syllables/word 1.44 Flesch Reading Ease = 206.835 - 25.375 - 121.82 = 59.6 Flesch-Kincaid Grade = 9.75 + 16.99 - 15.59 = 11.2 VERSION B (nominalised, chopped up, 27 words) "An assessment was undertaken. The scope was the downturn loss calibration. The portfolio was the mortgage book. The period was the third quarter. An understatement was identified." Words 27 Sentences 5 Syllables 49 (counted by hand) Words/sentence 5.4 Syllables/word 1.81 Flesch Reading Ease = 206.835 - 5.48 - 153.13 = 48.2 Flesch-Kincaid Grade = 2.11 + 21.36 - 15.59 = 7.9 WHAT THE NUMBERS SAY Flesch-Kincaid: B is easier by 3.3 grade levels. Flesch Reading Ease: A is easier by 11 points. The two most-used formulas disagree about which passage is harder. That alone should end the practice of citing a single score as evidence of clarity. WHAT A READER SAYS Version B has no actor in any of its five subjects. Four of the five verbs are forms of "be". The two real actions, reviewing and finding, are both nouns. Nothing is connected to anything: the reader gets five disconnected assertions and has to assemble the relationship between them without help. It is worse in every respect that matters, and Flesch-Kincaid, the formula built into your word processor, prefers it. Note also that B is LONGER than A in total words. The chopping did not save anything. It only moved the words into more sentences.

The disagreement between the two formulas is worth dwelling on, because it is not a coincidence. Flesch-Kincaid weights sentence length heavily relative to word length; Flesch Reading Ease weights word length heavily relative to sentence length. Version B improves on the first variable and worsens on the second, so the formulas split. Any writer optimising against a single score is therefore optimising against an arbitrary weighting of two things that trade off, and could have got the opposite verdict by choosing the other formula.

What the formulas are actually good for

Discarding them entirely would be an overcorrection, so here is the honest scope. They work as an outlier screen at scale. If you have ten thousand customer letters and want to find the fifty worst, ranking by Flesch-Kincaid will surface documents with genuinely pathological sentence lengths, and those documents probably do need attention. The formula is not telling you they are unclear; it is telling you they are extreme, and extremity is correlated with trouble.

They also serve as a crude tripwire on your own drafting. A section that scores far worse than the rest of a document has usually gone wrong somehow, and it is worth rereading. What the score cannot do is tell you what went wrong or whether your fix helped, and the temptation to treat an improved score as evidence of an improved document is the failure this lecture exists to prevent.

The rule that follows is narrow and worth stating flatly. Use a readability score to decide what to read again. Never use it to decide whether a revision worked.

The positive claim, stated properly

Williams's principle has two halves and this module devotes a lecture to each. Readers judge prose clear when the characters of the story occupy the grammatical subjects, and when the actions those characters perform occupy the finite verbs. Both halves are needed, because either can fail alone. A sentence can have a proper character in the subject and still bury its action in a noun: "the team conducted an assessment of the calibration" has an actor and no action in the verb slot. A sentence can have a live verb and no character: "the calibration was found to understate losses" has an action and no actor at all.

Two further remarks, both of which head off predictable misreadings. Nothing in this principle says anything about length; a sixty-word sentence with characters in its subjects and actions in its verbs reads easily, as module 1's lecture 5 established from the branching side. And the principle is about the fit rather than about a fixed target: it does not say to write about people, it says to write about whatever your story is about and to let the grammar agree with you.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Why you cannot feel your own unclear prose

The most disorienting fact about this subject is that unclear prose does not feel unclear to the person who wrote it. This is not carelessness or vanity. When you read your own sentence you already possess the web that the sentence is a flattened trace of, so you supply the missing structure without noticing, and the sentence goes down smoothly because you are not actually parsing it. You are recognising it.

That is why the diagnosis in this module is stated as a set of mechanical questions rather than as an instruction to read your work critically. Reading critically does not work on your own prose, because the faculty you would be reading with is contaminated by knowing what you meant. Asking "is the grammatical subject of this clause a character" works, because it is a question about marks on a page rather than about your impression of them. Module 6 gives this its proper name, the curse of knowledge, and module 9 shows what expert revisers do about it.

The uncomfortable relationship between clarity and thought

Williams makes a claim that is easy to read as a platitude and is not. When you cannot make a sentence clear, the usual reason is that you have not decided what it says. The tangled syntax is not obscuring a clear thought; it is an accurate rendering of an unfinished one, and the reason the revision is hard is that finishing the thought is the actual work.

This is testable against your own experience and most people find it holds. The sentences that resist revision hardest are the ones where you were hedging between two claims, or where you had not established causation and did not want to say so. The syntax was doing something for you: it was letting you write the sentence without deciding. Repairing it forces the decision, which is why revision for clarity so often turns into revision for substance and why it takes longer than expected.

There is a version of this claim that goes too far, and it should be marked. Not all difficult prose conceals muddled thinking. Some subjects are genuinely hard, and their honest exposition is hard to read; a proof, a piece of regulation, a derivation of the Basel risk weight function. The distinction module 1's lecture 1 drew still holds: your obligation is to ensure that the difficulty in your prose is the difficulty of the subject and not difficulty you added. What this module gives you is a way to tell the two apart, which is exactly what the person insisting their subject is inherently complex usually cannot do.

Common failure Reading this lecture as an argument against measurement. It is not. It is an argument against a specific measure that is invariant under a transformation that destroys meaning. The properties listed in the figure's right-hand panel are all measurable, several of them mechanically: subject-verb distance is a word count, the proportion of subjects that are nominalisations is a ratio, the subject chain can be extracted automatically. A better metric is possible and this module is largely a specification for one. Reject Flesch-Kincaid, not counting.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Reproduce the version A and version B comparison in step 4 using a passage from your own work. Write the clear version, then produce the nominalised and chopped version deliberately, count the inputs by hand, and compute both formulas. Report whether the two agree.
  2. State Williams's principle in one sentence. Then explain why both halves are needed by giving a sentence that satisfies one and fails the other.
  3. Why is every readability formula blind to word order? Answer in terms of the mathematical property their inputs share.
  4. Version B in step 4 is longer than version A in total words, and Flesch-Kincaid still prefers it. Explain how both facts can be true.
  5. Give the one legitimate use of a readability score and the one use this lecture forbids. What distinguishes them?
  6. Why can you not detect unclear prose in your own draft by reading it carefully? What kind of question works instead, and why?
  7. Williams claims that a sentence you cannot make clear is usually a thought you have not finished. Find a sentence in your own recent work that resisted revision, and say honestly whether the claim holds for it.
  8. Dale-Chall consults a word list rather than counting syllables. Explain why that makes it a better instrument aimed at the same wrong target, using the frequency argument from module 1, lecture 2.
