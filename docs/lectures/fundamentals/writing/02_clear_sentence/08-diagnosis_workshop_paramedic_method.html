Diagnosis workshop: the Paramedic Method

Everything in this module, compressed into a procedure you can run on a page of someone else's prose in ten minutes. Including the three steps at the end that nearly every summary of Lanham's method leaves out, which are the three that require you to read the passage aloud.

Lecture 8 of 8 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: Lanham, Revising Prose
Step 1

The 30-second version

Richard Lanham's Paramedic Method is eight steps applied in order to a passage that is not working. The first five are mechanical: circle the prepositions, circle the forms of is, ask who is doing what to whom, put that action into a simple active verb, and cut the slow wind-up. The last three are about rhythm, and they are the ones summaries drop, because they cannot be done at a keyboard: mark the rhythmic units, mark the sentence lengths, and read the passage aloud with emphasis.

Its companion measure is the lard factor: the words you removed as a proportion of the words you started with. Lanham's expectation for the prose he calls the Official Style is that half of it can go. That number is worth having in mind before you start, because it recalibrates what a revision is. You are not tidying. You are removing the half of the passage that was never doing anything.

What makes the method worth a lecture rather than a checklist is that it targets a dialect rather than a set of errors. Lanham's Official Style is a recognisable variety of English with consistent features: nouns where verbs should be, is as the main verb, strings of prepositional phrases, no visible actor, a slow wind-up before any content, and a flat unvarying rhythm. Once you can hear it as a dialect, you stop diagnosing sentences one at a time and start recognising the register, which is faster and more reliable.

Feynman test Lanham's name for it is exact. A paramedic does not treat elegantly; they do the small number of things that keep the patient alive, in a fixed order, without stopping to think. That is what this is: five mechanical moves that will rescue almost any paragraph, applied without judgement, before anyone starts discussing whether the prose sings.
What is beyond the source Lanham gives the eight steps and the lard factor. The mapping in step 3, which pairs each step with the lecture of this module that explains why it works, is this lecture's construction, and it is the reason to teach the method last rather than first. Lanham's steps are a procedure without a theory: they work, and he does not explain the reader-side mechanism. Having the mechanism first is what lets you tell when to depart from the procedure.
Step 2

Why this matters

The diagnoses in lectures 2 to 7 are individually sound and collectively unusable at speed. Nobody revising a forty-page document is going to ask seven separate questions of every sentence. What you need is one pass that catches most of it, and that is what a procedure buys: it converts seven principles into a sequence you can execute without deciding anything until step 3.

The second reason is that the method works on other people's prose, which is where most professional revision actually happens. You review papers, you comment on drafts, you mark up submissions. A procedure gives you something to say beyond an impression, and, more usefully, it gives you something the author can act on. "Your preposition count is 22 in 90 words and four of your five main verbs are forms of is" is actionable. "This reads heavily" is not.

The third reason is the lard factor, and it is worth being blunt about why the number matters. A writer who believes their draft is roughly right will make small changes. A writer who expects to remove half will make structural ones. The measure is not valuable as a target, and Goodhart's law applies here exactly as it did to the readability formulas in lecture 1: optimising for the number produces prose that is short and gutted. Its value is as a calibration before you begin.

And there is a reason specific to the rhythm steps, which is why this lecture insists on including them. The five mechanical steps produce prose that is correct and can be lifeless, because every sentence has come out the same shape: actor, active verb, object, adjunct. Uniform sentence length and uniform construction read as machine output, and the only way to detect it is to hear the passage. That is what steps 6 to 8 are for, and dropping them is why so much plain-English revision produces documents that are clear and unreadable.

Step 3

The mental model

The eight steps divide cleanly into two groups with different characters. Steps 1 to 5 are mechanical, executable without judgement, and each corresponds to a diagnosis this module has already justified. Steps 6 to 8 require you to hear the prose, cannot be automated, and point forward to material module 5 covers properly. Seeing the division is what stops you treating the second group as optional.

Step 8 deserves a note, because it is the one people skip and the one that catches what the others cannot. Reading aloud with emphasis forces you to locate an emphatic word in each sentence, and where your voice has nowhere to land, the stress position is holding an adjunct. Lecture 7 gave the analysis; reading aloud is the detector. It also catches the failure the mechanical steps introduce, which is a paragraph of identically shaped sentences, because your voice will fall into a chant.

The Official Style
Lanham's name for the institutional dialect: nouns for verbs, is as the main verb, prepositional strings, no visible actor, slow wind-ups, flat rhythm. A register rather than a set of errors, which is why recognising it is faster than diagnosing sentence by sentence.
Lard factor
Words removed as a proportion of words you started with. Lanham's expectation for Official Style prose is around half. Useful as a calibration before revising, and corrupting if used as a target.
Slow wind-up
An opening that delays content: "The purpose of this section is to...", "It should be noted that...", "In considering the question of...". Deletable in almost every case, and its deletion is step 5.
Step 4

The detail

The full procedure, run once

THE PASSAGE (94 words) "The purpose of this section is to provide a summary of the conclusions of the annual review of the downturn calibration. A number of factors were taken into consideration in the assessment of the appropriateness of the stress window that is currently in use. It should be noted that the exclusion of the 2008 peak from the window is not in accordance with the requirements of the internal standard. Remediation of this deficiency is required prior to the next submission of the capital return." STEP 1 Circle the prepositions of, of, of, of, of, in, in, of, of, of, in, of, from, in, with, of, of, of, to, of, of = 21 21 prepositions in 94 words is 22 per cent. Fourteen of them are "of". Three consecutive "of" chains. STEP 2 Circle the "is" forms is (the purpose), were (taken), is (currently in use), is (not in accordance), is (required) = 5 Five finite verbs in the passage. All five are forms of be. Zero actions in any verb slot. STEP 3 Who is doing what to whom? Not stated anywhere. Reconstructed from context: - somebody reviewed the calibration (the reviewer) - the window excludes the 2008 peak (the window) - that breaches the internal standard (the window) - somebody must recalibrate (the owner) Note that two of the four actors had to be supplied. This is lecture 2's point: the revision stalls where the work is incomplete, and here it stalled twice. STEP 4 Put each action in a simple active verb reviewed / excludes / breaches / must recalibrate STEP 5 Cut the slow wind-up "The purpose of this section is to provide a summary of" -> "This section summarises" "A number of factors were taken into consideration in" -> delete entirely; it announces thinking, not content "It should be noted that" -> delete entirely RESULT AFTER STEP 5 (48 words) "This section summarises the annual review of the downturn calibration. The validation team assessed whether the current stress window is appropriate. It is not: the window excludes the 2008 peak, which breaches the internal standard. The model owner must recalibrate before the next capital return." Prepositions: 5 (from 21). "of" count: 2 (from 14). be-verbs: 2 of 6 finite verbs (from 5 of 5). Actors: two, both named. LARD FACTOR (94 - 48) / 94 = 49 per cent. Almost exactly Lanham's expectation for this register. STEP 6 Mark the rhythmic units "It is not: / the window excludes the 2008 peak, / which breaches the internal standard." Three units, short then medium then medium. This sentence has a shape. The others are single-unit and flat, which is acceptable but worth knowing. STEP 7 Mark the sentence lengths 11 / 12 / 17 / 11 words. Range 11 to 17. Adequate variation, though clustered. Nothing here reads as a chant, so no repair needed. STEP 8 Read aloud with emphasis Sentence 1: emphasis on "downturn calibration". Fine. Sentence 2: emphasis on "appropriate". Fine. Sentence 3: emphasis on "internal standard". Good, this is the finding. Sentence 4: emphasis on "capital return". WRONG. The point is that the owner must recalibrate; the deadline is an adjunct sitting in the spotlight. Repair, from lecture 7: "Before the next capital return, the model owner must recalibrate." Now the emphasis falls on the obligation. FINAL (48 words, lard factor 49 per cent, four actions in four verbs, two named actors, and the last sentence emphasising what it should.)

The instructive moment in that run is step 3, where two of the four actors had to be reconstructed from context because the passage did not contain them. That is the recurring finding of this module: the mechanical steps are mechanical right up to the point where they demand information the original author withheld or never had. Lanham's procedure cannot supply it, and its failure to supply it is diagnostic.

The second thing worth noting is that step 8 caught something steps 1 to 5 had introduced. The mechanical revision produced a final sentence ending on "capital return", which is worse in one specific respect than the original's ending on "capital return", because the revised sentence is short enough that the misplaced emphasis is conspicuous. Correcting prose can create new positional problems, and only the rhythm steps find them.

Recognising the Official Style

FeatureHow it soundsWhich step catches it
Lanham's dialect, feature by feature
Nouns doing verbs' work "the determination of", "the implementation of", "the identification of" Steps 1 and 2 together: the prepositions cluster and the verbs empty.
Prepositional strings Three or more "of" phrases in sequence. Step 1, and it is the fastest signal in the whole method.
No visible actor You cannot say who did anything without leaving the document. Step 3, which will stall rather than fail. The stall is the finding.
Slow wind-up Ten words before any content: "In considering the question of whether..." Step 5. Almost always a clean deletion.
Euphemism and abstraction "challenges", "issues", "areas for enhancement", "sub-optimal outcomes" No step catches this. It needs a reader who knows the subject.
Flat rhythm Every sentence 22 to 26 words, every one built the same way. Steps 6 and 7, which is why dropping them leaves this untouched.

The euphemism row is the honest gap in the method and worth stating plainly. Lanham's steps are syntactic, and euphemism is lexical. A passage can pass all eight steps and still say "areas for enhancement were identified" instead of "the model is wrong", and no procedure will catch it, because catching it requires knowing what the truth was. Module 9's final lecture is where that problem gets addressed, and it is not addressed by a technique.

What the method cannot do

Three limits, all of them structural rather than incidental.

It is a sentence-level and paragraph-level tool, and it is blind to cohesion. A passage in which every sentence has been paramediced can still fail to hold together, because the subjects wander and old information arrives after new. Module 3 supplies the diagnosis, and it is a different diagnosis rather than a refinement of this one.

It is blind to document structure. A report whose conclusion sits in section 6 will not be improved by revising section 6's sentences. Module 4 handles that, and the ordering is worth respecting: fixing the architecture first is usually better value than fixing the prose, because architectural failures cost the reader whole sections rather than fractions of a second.

And it cannot tell you whether the content is right. This ought to be obvious and is worth saying because the method is satisfying to apply, and satisfaction is a poor guide to value. A beautifully paramediced paragraph asserting a wrong conclusion about a stress window is worse than a clumsy one asserting the right conclusion, and no amount of revision changes that ranking.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Why the rhythm steps get dropped

Nearly every summary of the Paramedic Method online and in style guides presents five steps. The three that vanish are the three that require you to write the sentence out by hand, slash it into units, and speak it aloud. It is worth asking why, because the answer generalises to a great deal of writing advice.

They vanish because they resist compression. The first five steps can be stated as instructions and executed silently at a keyboard, which makes them summarisable and shareable. Steps 6 to 8 require a physical act and produce a judgement rather than a mark-up, so they cannot be turned into a checklist item that anyone can verify you performed. Advice that can be verified mechanically spreads; advice that requires you to exercise judgement does not.

Module 1's lecture 7 made the same observation about usage superstitions, and it is the same mechanism. A rule you can enforce with a text search will always be more popular than a rule you have to understand. The rhythm steps are the part of Lanham that requires understanding, so the culture has quietly discarded them, and what remains is a method that reliably produces correct, clear, monotonous prose.

The lard factor and Goodhart's law, one more time

This module opened by arguing that readability formulas fail because they became targets. The lard factor is vulnerable to exactly the same corruption and deserves the same warning, more sharply because it is a better measure.

Optimising for a high lard factor produces prose that has lost its qualifications. The conditions under which a claim holds, the portfolio it applies to, the period it covers: all of these are cuttable, all of them raise the number, and every one of them may be load-bearing. Lanham's own examples are drawn from prose so padded that nothing true was lost by halving it, which makes the fifty per cent figure a description of a particular corpus rather than a target for yours.

The honest use is diagnostic and one-directional. A passage with a lard factor near zero after a genuine attempt was probably already fine. A passage yielding half was in the Official Style. A revision that achieved seventy per cent should be checked for lost content rather than celebrated. Treat it as a thermometer, and never as a thermostat.

Where module 2 leaves you

Seven lectures of diagnosis and one procedure, and the honest summary of what they add up to is narrower than it feels. You can now take a sentence that is not working and say what is wrong with it in terms of where the characters and actions sit, how far the subject is from its verb, what occupies the stress position, and whether the nominalisations are packaging or hiding. You can run a procedure that catches most of it fast. That is a genuine and unusual capability, and it is entirely confined to sentences and paragraphs.

What you cannot yet do is make a passage cohere, and it is worth being clear that this is a separate skill rather than more of the same. Every sentence in a paragraph can pass this module and the paragraph can still drift, because coherence is about the relationship between sentences and nothing in module 2 looked at a relationship. Module 3 is about the seams.

Common failure Running the method on your own first draft while writing it. Lanham's steps are revision steps, and applying them during composition slows drafting to a crawl and produces cautious prose, for the reasons module 1's lecture 1 gave and module 9 evidences. Draft badly and fast. The Paramedic Method is what you do on the second pass, ideally on a different day, and ideally on a printout where you can circle things.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Run all eight steps on a 90 to 120 word passage from a document you did not write, exactly as step 4 does. Report the preposition count, the be-verb ratio, every actor you had to reconstruct, the lard factor, the sentence-length series, and at least one problem that only step 8 caught.
  2. List the eight steps in order. Which five are mechanical, and at which step does the method first require you to know the subject matter?
  3. Why is "circle the prepositions" the highest-yield single move, given that it says nothing about nominalisation directly?
  4. In the step 4 run, two actors had to be reconstructed. What does a stall at step 3 tell you, and why can no procedure fix it?
  5. Name Lanham's six features of the Official Style and say which step catches each. Which feature is caught by no step at all, and why not?
  6. Explain why the three rhythm steps get dropped from summaries. What does module 1's lecture 7 say about the same mechanism?
  7. The lard factor is a thermometer and not a thermostat. Give a concrete way that optimising for it damages a technical document.
  8. Name the three things the Paramedic Method cannot do. Which of the three should be fixed before the prose, and why?
