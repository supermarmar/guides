Nominalisation and the zombie noun

The commonest single defect in professional prose, and the one that cannot be fixed by a rule against it, because a technical field's concepts are nominalisations and always will be. What you need is not a prohibition but a test for whether a given one is earning its place.

Lecture 4 of 8 Reading time, 40 minutes Source: Williams, Style, lesson 2; Sword, The Writer's Diet (2007)
Step 1

The 30-second version

A nominalisation is a noun made from a verb or an adjective. Helen Sword calls the worst of them zombie nouns, because they consume the living verbs around them: one nominalisation empties the verb slot, evicts the actor into a prepositional phrase or out of the sentence, and forces every remaining argument to attach with a preposition of its own. The damage is structural and it propagates.

The instruction usually attached to this is to avoid nominalisations, and it is unusable, because inflation, impairment, regulation, distribution, correlation and duration are all nominalisations and none of them is replaceable. Every technical field builds its concepts this way. A rule that forbids the construction forbids the vocabulary.

What works instead is a test with five ways to pass. Keep a nominalisation when it packages something the reader already holds, when it is a term of art, when it replaces an awkward the fact that, when it names what would be the object of a verb like know or explain, or when it refers back to the previous sentence to build the link that module 3 is about. Convert it when it names the action the sentence is currently asserting, because then it is competing with the verb slot and the verb slot loses.

Feynman test A nominalisation is a verb that has been freeze-dried into a box so you can carry it around and stack it. Boxes are how a field accumulates: last year's result goes in a box, you write the box's name, and you build on top. The failure is freeze-drying the thing you are doing right now. You end up holding a box labelled "assessment" and telling the reader an assessment was performed, when what happened is that you assessed something.
What is beyond the source Williams gives four or five conditions under which a nominalisation is useful, scattered through lesson 2 rather than collected. Sword gives a measurement instrument and a memorable name. Neither offers a single decision procedure, and neither addresses the false-positive problem directly: Sword's test flags impairment and regulation as abstract nouns, which they are, and as defects, which they are not. The decision tree in step 3 and the false-positive analysis in step 4 are this lecture's attempt to make the instrument usable in a technical field.
Step 2

Why this matters

The reason to give nominalisation a lecture of its own, rather than a paragraph, is that it is the mechanism behind almost everything else in this module. The abstract subject of lecture 2 is a nominalisation in the subject slot. The empty verb of lecture 3 is what fills the gap a nominalisation leaves. The preposition chains that make institutional sentences unreadable are the arguments a noun cannot take directly. One grammatical operation, three symptoms, and they always arrive together.

That gives the construction an unusual property as a diagnostic target: fixing it fixes other things without being asked to. Convert the nominalisation to a verb and the empty verb has nowhere to sit, the actor has to be named to fill the subject slot, and the prepositions become unnecessary because the verb takes its objects directly. Three repairs for one decision, which is why this is the highest-yield edit available and why it is worth learning properly rather than as a rule of thumb.

The second reason is that the wrong version of this advice does real damage. A writer told to avoid abstract nouns in a field made of abstract nouns will either ignore the advice, which is correct, or obey it, which produces prose that cannot state its own subject matter. Both outcomes are worse than understanding the distinction. The professionals who write worst about credit risk are not the ones with too many nominalisations; they are the ones who have never asked which of theirs are doing work.

The third reason is measurement. Sword's Writer's Diet test is a genuine improvement on the readability formulas of lecture 1, because it can see nominalisation, prepositions and be-verbs, all of which are invisible to Flesch. It is still a surface metric, and understanding exactly what it can and cannot see is the clearest available illustration of a general point: better instruments do not become oracles, and the residue they cannot measure is where the judgement lives.

Step 3

The mental model

Every nominalisation in a draft is either packaging knowledge or hiding an action. The five keep-conditions below are the ways of packaging; anything that fails all five is hiding. Run the tree on the nominalisation, not on the sentence, and run it on each one separately, because a single sentence often contains one of each.

Conditions 1 and 5 are the ones professionals under-use, and they are worth separating clearly because together they explain why good technical prose is full of nominalisations. Condition 1 is about cohesion: a nominalisation is the neatest way to pick up what the previous sentence just said and make it the starting point of this one. Condition 5 is about accumulation: a nominalisation lets a finding become a thing you can assert something about. In both cases the noun holds old information and the verb carries the new claim, which is the shape module 3 will call the given-new contract.

Zombie noun
Sword's term for a nominalisation that has consumed the verbs and actors around it. Diagnostic value lies in the image: the noun is animate at the expense of everything else in the sentence.
Zero-derived nominalisation
A nominalisation identical in form to its verb: a review, an increase, the transfer, a change, the override. No suffix search will find these. The determiner in front is the tell.
The cascade
The three consequences that follow automatically from one nominalisation: an empty verb fills the vacated slot, the actor is demoted or deleted, and each remaining argument needs its own preposition. Repairing the noun undoes all three.
Writer's Diet test
Sword's five-part surface diagnostic, counting be-verbs, abstract nouns, prepositions, adjectives and adverbs, and the waste words it, this, that and there. Sees far more than Flesch-Kincaid, and still cannot tell a term of art from a zombie.
Step 4

The detail

The cascade, quantified

Module 1's lecture 3 asserted that one nominalisation forces three consequences. Here is the arithmetic, because the ratio is a usable diagnostic on its own.

THE PREPOSITION ARITHMETIC A verb takes its objects directly: "The team assessed the calibration." verb: assessed. prepositions: 0. A noun cannot, so each argument needs one: "an assessment of the calibration by the team" prepositions: 2 (of, by) Stack two nominalisations and the arguments multiply: "An assessment of the appropriateness of the calibration of the downturn parameters was undertaken by the team." nominalisations: 3 (assessment, appropriateness, calibration) prepositions: 4 (of, of, of, by) real verbs: 0 THE RULE OF THUMB In institutional prose the preposition count runs at roughly one more than the nominalisation count. So counting prepositions finds nominalisations without any grammatical analysis, and it catches the zero-derived ones that a suffix search misses. THE THREE-OF RULE Three or more consecutive "of" phrases is close to conclusive. There is almost no legitimate construction in English that requires it, and each "of" is an argument that a verb would have taken for free. WHAT TO SEARCH FOR, IN ORDER OF YIELD 1. " of " highest yield, catches everything 2. " by the " finds demoted actors 3. "-tion of" finds the classic stack 4. " in respect of " finds nothing else, and should go

The preposition heuristic is Lanham's, and it is the practical core of the Paramedic Method that lecture 8 sets out in full. Its value is that it requires no ability to identify a nominalisation: you count a function word that a text editor can find. The nominalisations are wherever the prepositions cluster.

Running the Writer's Diet test by hand

Sword's test counts five things. Verbs, meaning the proportion that are forms of be. Nouns, meaning abstract nouns and particularly nominalisations. Prepositions. Adjectives and adverbs. And what she calls waste words, the four items it, this, that and there, which cluster in prose that is pointing at things it has not named. The tool reports each category on a scale from lean through fit and trim, needs toning, and flabby, to heart attack territory.

Counting by hand is worth doing once, because it shows you what the instrument is actually looking at, and because it makes the false positives visible in a way an automated score does not.

HAND-COUNTED, ON A REALISTIC PARAGRAPH "The determination of the appropriateness of the downturn calibration is dependent on the identification of a representative stress period. It is the view of the validation function that the exclusion of the 2008 peak from the stress window is not in accordance with the requirements of the internal standard. Remediation of this deficiency is required prior to the next submission." TOTAL WORDS: 66 1. BE-VERBS is (dependent), is (the view), is (not in accordance), is (required) = 4 Total finite verbs = 4. So 100% be-verbs. -> heart attack territory. No sentence in the paragraph contains an action in a verb. 2. ABSTRACT NOUNS / NOMINALISATIONS determination, appropriateness, calibration, identification, exclusion, accordance, requirements, remediation, deficiency, submission, view = 10 or 11 -> roughly one in six words. Flabby at best. 3. PREPOSITIONS of, of, on, of, of, of, from, in, with, of, of, to = 12 -> 18% of all words. Note 7 of them are "of". Predicted by the arithmetic above: 10 nominalisations, 12 prepositions. 4. ADJECTIVES AND ADVERBS appropriate(ness is nominal), downturn (attributive noun), representative, internal, next = 4 -> lean. This paragraph's problem is not modifiers, which is exactly what a length-based formula would have missed. 5. WASTE WORDS (it, this, that, there) It (anticipatory), that (complementiser), this (deficiency) = 3 -> the anticipatory "it" is deleting the viewer, and "this deficiency" is the only real cohesive link in the paragraph. NOW THE FALSE POSITIVES Of the 10 or 11 abstract nouns, three are terms of art that must stay: calibration, requirements, submission. A fourth, "stress period", is a defined concept. Converting them would change the claims. So the honest count is 6 or 7 zombies, not 11. The instrument found them and could not grade them, which is the whole limitation in one line. REPAIRED (44 words, from 66) "Whether the downturn calibration is appropriate depends on choosing a representative stress period. The validation function considers that the stress window excludes the 2008 peak and therefore breaches the internal standard. The model owner must remediate before the next submission." be-verbs: 1 of 6 finite verbs. prepositions: 4. "of" count: 0. Terms of art: all four retained. Actors: two, both named.

Three observations about that repair, because two of them run against the usual advice. The word count fell by a third and no content was removed; the reduction is entirely accounted for by prepositions and empty verbs that existed only to support the nominalisations. The adjective count was already lean, so a writer working from an instinct that unclear prose means too many adjectives would have found nothing to fix. And the four terms of art survived unchanged, which is the test of whether a repair was surgical or merely enthusiastic.

What the instrument cannot see

The Writer's Diet test is a real improvement on Flesch-Kincaid and it is worth being precise about the residue, since the residue is what this course teaches.

It cannot distinguish a term of art from a zombie, as the worked example showed. It cannot see whether a nominalisation is packaging given information, so it penalises exactly the construction that conditions 1 and 5 of the decision tree call for. It cannot see the subject chain, so a paragraph with six different abstract subjects scores the same as one with a coherent protagonist. It cannot see subject-verb distance. And it cannot see whether old information precedes new, which module 3 argues is the single largest determinant of whether a passage coheres.

Which yields the same conclusion lecture 1 reached about the readability formulas, now applied to a better tool. Use it to find candidates. Never use it to certify a revision. The improvement over Flesch-Kincaid is that its candidates are the right candidates: a be-verb count of 100 per cent is genuinely diagnostic, in a way that an average sentence length of twenty-five words is not.

The suffixes, and the ones with no suffix

Module 1's lecture 3 listed these and they are worth having in front of you while revising, so they are repeated here in the form of a search strategy rather than a table. The suffixed cases, -tion, -sion, -ment, -ance, -ence, -ity, -ness, -al, -ure, -ing, are findable mechanically and are therefore the easy half.

The zero-derived cases are the ones that survive a search: review, increase, decrease, change, use, need, impact, result, cause, drop, rise, test, check, override, transfer, breach, default. Each is identical to its verb, so the only signal is grammatical: a determiner or a possessive in front makes it a noun. "The override rate rose" has override as a noun modifier and rose as the verb, which is fine. "An override of the score was applied" has the action in the noun and applied doing nothing, which is not.

This is where the preposition count earns its keep, because it finds both kinds without distinguishing them. A stretch of prose with an of every six words has nominalisations in it whether or not they carry suffixes.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Nominalisation as the engine of a discipline

Lecture 3 introduced Halliday's argument and this is the place to take it seriously, because it is the strongest objection to everything in this module and it is correct.

Scientific and technical English developed nominalisation as a resource for a specific reason. A field advances by asserting new things about established results, and to do that you must be able to name a result compactly enough to put it in a subject slot. Every step of that accumulation converts a process into a thing. The observed migration of exposures into stage 2 is a nine-word noun phrase encoding an entire empirical finding, and a sentence can now say something new about it. Without the machinery, each new claim would have to restate its predecessors as full clauses, and a technical literature would be impossible.

Halliday and Martin's historical case is that this is how the register was built, tracing the increasing nominal density of scientific writing from the seventeenth century onwards as the accumulated content grew. On that account, high nominal density is a signature of a mature field rather than a defect in its writers.

Reconciling this with Williams takes one sentence and it is worth getting exactly right. The reconciliation is that old information belongs in nouns and new claims belong in verbs. Halliday describes what happens to the old; Williams prescribes what should happen to the new. The good technical sentence does both: a nominalised subject packaging what the reader already has, and a live verb asserting what they do not. "The observed migration invalidates the through-the-cycle assumption" is the shape, and it would be worse either fully nominalised or fully verbal.

So the target narrows to something defensible. Not nominal density, which is a property of the subject matter. Not abstract nouns, which are the concepts. The target is a nominalisation naming the event the sentence is currently asserting, with an empty verb behind it. That construction has no defence in Halliday's account either, because it is not packaging anything: the content is new, and it has been packaged anyway.

Why specialists do not notice

One further complication, and it explains why nominalised prose survives peer review in every technical field. Specialists reading in their own field genuinely find it easier than non-specialists do, and not because they are tolerant. The chunking is already done in their heads. A credit risk modeller reads downturn LGD calibration as one unit, so the noun phrase costs them nothing like what it costs a generalist, and the prose that a reviewer waves through is prose their expertise is subsidising.

This has a practical consequence that cuts against the natural instinct. The documents that most need this module's treatment are the ones with mixed readerships: a board paper, an ICAAP submission, a regulatory response, a paper going to a committee that includes non-modellers. Among specialists writing for specialists the cost is real but modest. Where the audience straddles expertise levels, the same prose becomes genuinely impenetrable for half the room, and the half that struggles is usually the half making the decision.

Module 6 gives this its proper name, the curse of knowledge, and treats the general problem of writing for a reader who does not have your chunks. Here the narrow lesson is that your own sense of whether a paragraph is too dense is calibrated on your expertise and is therefore worthless for exactly the documents where density matters most.

Common failure Applying the decision tree to a whole sentence rather than to each nominalisation in it. Most institutional sentences contain both kinds: a term of art or a packaged finding that must stay, and an action-naming zombie that must go. A writer who asks "is this sentence nominalised" gets a useless yes and either rewrites everything or nothing. Test the nouns one at a time, and expect a typical sentence to keep half of them.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Run the hand-counted Writer's Diet test from step 4 on a paragraph of your own professional writing. Report all five counts, then separate the abstract nouns into terms of art and zombies. Repair it and report the counts again, confirming that every term of art survived.
  2. State the five keep-conditions for a nominalisation. Which two are about packaging prior material, and why does that make them the ones professionals under-use?
  3. Why is "avoid nominalisations" an unusable instruction in a technical field? Give three nominalisations from your own domain that cannot be converted.
  4. Explain the preposition arithmetic. Why does counting prepositions find zero-derived nominalisations that a suffix search misses?
  5. The step 4 paragraph scored lean on adjectives and adverbs. What does that tell you about a writer whose instinct is that unclear prose means too many modifiers?
  6. Name three things the Writer's Diet test cannot see. Which of the three does module 3 argue matters most?
  7. State Halliday's argument for nominalisation as the engine of a discipline, then give the one-sentence reconciliation with Williams. Write a sentence from your own field that satisfies both.
  8. Why is your own sense of whether a paragraph is too dense worthless for the documents where density matters most? Name a document type from your own work where this applies.
