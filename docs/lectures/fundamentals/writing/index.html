Fundamentals

Writing

Sixty-eight lectures on how prose works and how to make it work for you. The centre of gravity is professional expository writing, the model documentation, validation report, board paper and exam answer, because that is what most of us write and what almost nobody is taught. Craft and rhetoric arrive later, once the machinery of clarity is in place. Every principle here is drawn from a named source and most of them are testable claims about reader behaviour rather than matters of taste.

How the modules fit together

The course has one governing premise, borrowed from Gopen and Swan: the meaning of any prose is not what the writer intends but what the reader interprets. Readers make interpretive decisions from structural cues, and they make them the same way from one reader to the next. That premise turns most writing advice from opinion into engineering. It also fixes the order of what follows.

Modules 1 to 3 are strictly cumulative. Module 1 supplies the grammatical vocabulary that every later diagnosis is stated in, so you can name what is wrong with a sentence rather than only feel it. Module 2 uses that vocabulary to explain why prose feels unclear, and it is the highest-leverage material in the course: a handful of principles about where characters and actions sit in a clause accounts for most of the impenetrability of institutional writing. Module 3 moves up a level, because sentences that are individually clear can still form a passage that drifts.

Modules 4 and 5 handle scale and finish. The Pyramid Principle sits in module 4 rather than at the front, which is a deliberate departure from how consulting firms teach it. Taught first, the pyramid reads as an arbitrary house convention. Taught after the given-new contract, it reads as what it actually is: the document-scale version of the same reader expectation, applied to a reader who will not reach the end. Module 5 is subtraction and shaping, the work that separates prose that is correct from prose that is good.

Modules 6 to 8 are about choice. Module 6 asks which style, and it cannot be asked earlier, because the question is meaningless until you can execute more than one. Module 7 restores the classical framework that treats a text as an intervention in a situation, with audiences who resist and genres that carry their own obligations. Module 8 is craft: image, narrative, diction, and the music of a sentence.

Module 9 is last because it reinterprets everything before it. Almost nothing in modules 1 to 8 is a composition rule. It is all revision heuristics, and the research on how expert writers actually work explains why that distinction matters more than any single technique.

How to use this

All sixty-eight lectures are built. Each module folder carries its own landing page with the authoritative lecture list; this page is the map, not the detail. If you are starting, take modules 1 to 3 in order. If you are returning for one thing, take module 9's revision protocol, which is the only part of the course that is a procedure rather than an idea.

This track is the only one in the collection with no source notes behind it. Nothing in docs/wiki/ covers writing, and docs/raw/ holds no source text. Every lecture is therefore written directly from the primary literature and cites it, rather than summarising an ingested note. Treat the bibliography at the foot of this page as the syllabus's actual reading list.

Two findings from the research on writing instruction shape how the material is delivered, and they are worth stating up front because they cut against how writing is usually taught. Graham and Perin's 2007 meta-analysis, over 154 controlled comparisons, puts traditional decontextualised grammar instruction at an effect size of minus 0.32. Teaching grammar as a body of rules to be learned makes writing measurably worse. Module 1 therefore frames its grammar purely as diagnostic vocabulary: you learn what a nominalisation is so that you can find one in your own draft, and for no other reason. The second finding is older and comes from the classical tradition. The progymnasmata, the graded composition exercises that trained writers for fifteen centuries, began with close imitation of models and only later permitted original composition, with each exercise longer and more demanding than the last. The recall exercises across this course follow that pattern: imitate first, compose second.

Modules

Clarity

The cumulative core. Take these three in order; nothing later makes sense without them.

  • builtModule 1: foundations (7 lectures). The raw material of prose. Words, parts of speech as functions, the clause, sentence building, punctuation as a signalling system, and an honest account of which rules are real. All seven built.
  • builtModule 2: the clear sentence (8 lectures). Williams's character and action principle, nominalisation, the passive voice properly understood, subject-verb proximity, the stress position, and Lanham's Paramedic Method as a workshop. All eight built.
  • builtModule 3: cohesion and the paragraph (7 lectures). The given-new contract, topic strings, theme and rheme, paragraph design, connectives and the curse of knowledge, and a repeatable passage-level diagnosis. All seven built.

Structure and shape

Scale and finish. Module 4 is the professional writer's module; module 5 is where correct becomes good.

  • builtModule 4: architecture (8 lectures). Framing a document, Pinker's arcs of coherence, the Pyramid Principle vertically and horizontally, MECE, SCQA, McPhee on long-form structure, and the visual layer. All eight built.
  • builtModule 5: concision, rhythm and grace (7 lectures). Six kinds of redundancy, metadiscourse and hedging, the shape of the long sentence, sentence variety, sound, elegance, and Klinkenborg's argument that the whole preceding order is backwards. All seven built.

Choice and effect

Technique is now sufficient. These three supply the framework for deciding which techniques to deploy, on whom, and to what end.

  • builtModule 6: style as a stand (7 lectures). Thomas and Turner on style as prior commitment, classic style and its alternatives, the curse of knowledge, register, voice, and what readability metrics can and cannot see. All seven built.
  • builtModule 7: rhetoric (9 lectures). The rhetorical situation, the three appeals, the five canons, invention and stasis theory, arrangement, figures, genre as social action, audience analysis, and Toulmin on honest argument. All nine built.
  • builtModule 8: craft (7 lectures). Concreteness, metaphor as thought, narrative in nonfiction, point of view, exposition without tedium, diction, and reading as apprenticeship. All seven built.

Mastery

Where knowledge becomes skill, and the only honest place to answer the question the course is really about.

  • builtModule 9: revision, practice, ethics and greatness (8 lectures). Flower and Hayes on how writing actually happens, knowledge-telling against knowledge-transforming, how experts revise differently, a revision protocol, feedback, deliberate practice, the ethics of clarity, and an argued answer to what makes a writer great. All eight built.

What is and is not covered

The course covers English prose written to be read once, by a reader who is busy, sceptical, and free to stop. It does not cover poetry, screenwriting, or fiction beyond the narrative techniques that nonfiction borrows. It does not teach a house style, a citation convention, or a word processor. Grammar appears only as a diagnostic instrument, so anyone wanting a reference grammar should reach for Huddleston and Pullum rather than module 1.

Two things the course does that most writing courses avoid. It teaches its disagreements as disagreements: Klinkenborg against Minto on whether structure precedes sentences, Pullum against Strunk and White on whether the canonical style manual knows what a passive is, and the standard critique of Orwell's rules alongside the rules themselves. Where the authorities conflict, the lecture says so and argues a position rather than presenting a consensus that does not exist. And it grounds its claims in evidence where evidence exists, which is more often than the field's reputation suggests.

Source material

The spine is Joseph M. Williams, Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace, whose twelve-lesson order shapes modules 2, 3 and 5, and whose closing lesson on the ethics of clarity closes module 9. George Gopen and Judith Swan's 1990 paper The Science of Scientific Writing supplies the reader-expectation framing used throughout. Steven Pinker's The Sense of Style contributes the curse of knowledge, the tree-to-string account of why sentences are hard, and arcs of coherence. Richard Lanham's Revising Prose supplies the Paramedic Method and the Official Style. Barbara Minto's The Pyramid Principle governs module 4. Francis-Noël Thomas and Mark Turner's Clear and Simple as the Truth governs module 6. Helen Sword's Stylish Academic Writing and The Writer's Diet supply the zombie-noun diagnostic.

On craft: Ursula K. Le Guin, Steering the Craft; Verlyn Klinkenborg, Several Short Sentences About Writing; John McPhee, Draft No. 4; Stephen King, On Writing; Gary Provost, 100 Ways to Improve Your Writing. On rhetoric: Aristotle's Rhetoric, Quintilian's Institutio Oratoria, Aphthonius's progymnasmata, Lloyd Bitzer on the rhetorical situation, Carolyn Miller on genre as social action, John Swales on move analysis, Stephen Toulmin on argument. On document design: Karen Schriver, Dynamics in Document Design, and Janice Redish, Letting Go of the Words.

The empirical base sits behind module 9 and is cited throughout: Herbert Clark and Susan Haviland on the given-new contract (1977), Linda Flower and John Hayes on the cognitive process model (1981), Nancy Sommers on revision strategies (1980), Carl Bereiter and Marlene Scardamalia on knowledge-telling and knowledge-transforming (1987), and Steve Graham and Dolores Perin's Writing Next meta-analysis (2007). Geoffrey Pullum's 50 Years of Stupid Grammar Advice (2009) and George Orwell's Politics and the English Language (1946) both appear as texts to argue with rather than defer to.
