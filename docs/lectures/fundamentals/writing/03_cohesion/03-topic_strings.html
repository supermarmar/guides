Lectures / Writing / Cohesion

Topic strings

Read down the left-hand edge of a paragraph and write out what each sentence is about. That list is the paragraph's real subject matter, and if it does not form a recognisable set the paragraph drifts no matter how well the individual sentences are made.

Lecture 3 of 7 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: Williams, Style, lesson 5; Gopen & Swan (1990), expectations 1 and 3
Step 1

The 30-second version

A topic string is the sequence of things a paragraph's sentences are about, read in order. You extract it by taking the first five or six words of each sentence and identifying what the sentence is announcing itself as concerning. The result is a short list, and the list is diagnostic: if it names a recognisable set, the paragraph coheres, and if it names four unrelated things, the paragraph drifts.

The subtlety, and the reason the method gets misapplied, is that a recognisable set does not mean an identical word. A paragraph whose topics run the mortgage book, its 2022 vintage, collateral values on that vintage, the LGD floor has four different topics and passes, because all four belong to one story about a portfolio's credit quality. A paragraph whose topics run the mortgage book, June, the origination system, the technology plan also has four different topics and fails. The test is membership of a set, not repetition of a string.

This lecture also resolves something lecture 1 left as a puzzle. Why do so many well-joined paragraphs drift? Because the sequential chain that lecture 2 recommended for cohesion works by making each sentence's new information the next sentence's topic, and new information moves. Chaining is therefore the engine of drift as well as of cohesion, and the topic string is the constraint that keeps it honest.

Feynman test Cover the whole paragraph except a strip down the left-hand side, about six words wide. Now read the strip. That is roughly what your reader gets when they skim, and it is entirely what they get when deciding whether the paragraph is about the thing they are looking for. If the strip is incoherent, the paragraph is incoherent to everyone who does not read it closely, which is most people.
What is beyond the source Williams gives the method and the advice to keep topics consistent. Gopen and Swan give the underlying reader expectations. The claim that sequential chaining is the mechanism of drift, so that lecture 2's cohesion technique actively produces lecture 1's dangerous quadrant unless the topic string constrains it, is this lecture's argument. It matters because it means the two properties are not merely independent, as lecture 1 said: one of the standard cohesion techniques attacks coherence, and you need both diagnoses running to notice.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this is the only coherence check in the course that works. Cohesion can be checked by reading, because a broken join produces a stumble. Coherence cannot, for the reason lecture 1 gave: it is a property of the sequence rather than of any point in it, and your voice processes one sentence at a time. Extracting the topic string is the operation that makes a sequence-level property visible, and there is no substitute for it.

The second reason is that it is fast and it works on other people's documents at scale. Reading a forty-page submission properly takes a day. Extracting topic strings from its paragraphs takes an hour and tells you which sections have no argument in them. For anyone who reviews documents for a living, this is the highest-leverage reading technique in the course, and it is more valuable as a reading tool than as a writing one.

The third reason is the skimming argument, made concrete. Module 2's lecture 6 claimed that readers of professional documents sample rather than read linearly, and that sentence openings are the best guess at what they sample. If that is even roughly right, then the topic string is not a diagnostic abstraction: it is approximately the document your skimming reader actually receives. A paragraph whose left edge reads a number of considerations, it, there, various factors has delivered nothing to that reader, whatever its full text contains.

And a reason particular to documents written by more than one person. A section drafted by three people will have three topic strings spliced together, and the splice is invisible in continuous reading because each author wrote cohesively. Extracting the string exposes the seams immediately, which makes this the right first check on any document that has been through a review cycle.

Step 3

The mental model

Four paragraphs, four topic strings. Two pass and two fail, and the pair that matters is the third and fourth, because they are equally cohesive, equally well written sentence by sentence, and differ only in whether their topics belong to one story.

The lesson from the C and D pair is that cohesion technique is not self-limiting. Sequential chaining will connect anything to anything, so a writer applying it faithfully can produce a perfectly joined paragraph about four different subjects. What stops that is knowing, before you write the paragraph, what set its topics are allowed to come from. Which means the topic string is a planning constraint as much as a checking tool, and paragraph D was easier to write than paragraph C was to fix.

Topic string
The sequence of what each sentence in a passage is about, read in order. Extracted from the first five or six words of each sentence. Usually the grammatical subjects, but a fronted element can carry the topic instead.
Recognisable set
The pass condition. The topics need not repeat; they need to belong to one identifiable story, typically as parts, aspects or consequences of a single thing. Four topics from one set pass; two topics from two sets fail.
Topic against subject
The topic is what the sentence announces itself as about; the subject is the grammatical element the verb agrees with. Usually the same. When an adverbial is fronted, "In Q3, the model failed", the topic is arguably the time frame and the subject is the model.
Step 4

The detail

The extraction, done properly

EXTRACTING A TOPIC STRING 1. Print the passage, or view it so you can cover text. 2. Take the first five or six words of each sentence only. 3. Within that window, identify what the sentence announces itself as being about. Usually the subject. If an adverbial has been fronted, note both. 4. Write the list in order, nothing else. 5. Apply two tests. TEST 1 Do the topics form a recognisable set? Pass: all are the same thing, or parts, aspects, components or consequences of one thing. Fail: you cannot name the set without using a word as vague as "issues" or "matters". TEST 2 Are there more than two or three distinct topics in a paragraph of five sentences? More than three is almost always drift or a paragraph that should be split. WORKED, ON A REAL SHAPE Passage: "The purpose of this section is to summarise the annual review. A number of considerations were taken into account. It was concluded that the calibration understates the peak loss rate. There are three drivers. The most significant is the exclusion of the 2008 peak. Remediation is required before the next submission." Topic string: the purpose of this section a number of considerations it (anticipatory, refers to nothing) there (existential, refers to nothing) the most significant (of what? antecedent two back) remediation TEST 1: fail. The only set that contains all six is "things mentioned in this paragraph". TEST 2: fail. Six topics in six sentences. Note what the string reveals that reading did not. Two of the six topics refer to nothing at all, and the paragraph never once announces itself as being about the calibration, which is what it is actually about. REPAIRED, topic string chosen first Chosen set: the calibration and its stress window. "This section summarises the annual review of the downturn calibration. The calibration understates the peak loss rate, for three reasons of which one dominates. Its stress window excludes the 2008 peak, so the loss distribution has no severe tail. That exclusion must be corrected before the next submission." Topic string: this section the calibration its stress window that exclusion TEST 1: pass. All four belong to one story. TEST 2: pass. Four topics, five sentences, one set.

The instructive part of that repair is the order of operations. The topic string was chosen before the paragraph was rewritten, and everything else followed: once you have decided the paragraph is about the calibration and its window, the subjects are determined and the sentences almost write themselves. Attempting the reverse, rewriting the sentences and hoping a topic string emerges, is what produces the original.

Where the repair needs a passive

This is the payoff of module 2's lecture 5, and it is worth making explicit because it is the clearest case where two lectures combine into something neither could do alone.

Suppose your chosen topic is the mortgage book, and one of the things you need to say is that the validation team reclassified two thirds of it. The active sentence has the team as subject, which puts the team in the topic slot and breaks your string. The passive has the book as subject, which keeps the string intact and pushes the team into a by phrase at the end, where new information belongs anyway. So the topic string determines the voice, and a writer who has banned the passive cannot hold a topic string when the topic is the patient of the action.

That is the strongest available argument against the passive superstition, and it is stronger than the one lecture 5 of module 2 gave, because it shows the ban costing a coherence property rather than merely a stylistic option. If you can only write actives, your topics are whoever happens to be acting, and whoever happens to be acting changes.

Two legitimate reasons to break a string

A paragraph boundary is a licensed topic shift and requires no apology. That is what paragraph boundaries are for, and lecture 5 will treat the paragraph as the unit within which a topic holds. So a string that changes at every paragraph break is working correctly; a string that changes mid-paragraph is the thing to look at.

The second case is a deliberate contrastive shift, marked as such. "The retail book has stabilised. The wholesale book has not." Two topics, one sentence apart, and the shift is the point, because the sentences exist to be compared. What makes this legitimate rather than drift is that the two topics are being held in a single frame, the comparison, and a reader who can see the frame reads the shift as structure. The tell for legitimacy is that you could name the frame in one word.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Cohesion technique attacks coherence

Lecture 1 presented the two properties as independent, which is true and understates the problem. This lecture shows they interact adversarially: the standard technique for building cohesion, chaining each topic from the last sentence's new information, is the mechanism by which topics drift. Apply lecture 2's advice diligently and without a topic constraint and you will reliably produce lecture 1's dangerous quadrant.

That has a consequence for how the two diagnoses should be run, and it is not the obvious one. You cannot check cohesion first and coherence afterwards, because the cohesion repairs you make will introduce coherence failures. The checks have to be run together, or at least the topic string has to be settled before the joins are worked on. In practice this means deciding what a paragraph is about is a planning act, not a revision act, and the revision act is only checking that you held to it.

The string as a reading tool

The most valuable use of this method is not on your own writing. It is on documents you must evaluate, and it changes what reviewing means.

Take the executive summary of any substantial submission and extract the topic string of the whole thing, one topic per paragraph, using each paragraph's first sentence. You now have a list of six or eight items which is what the document says it is about. Compare that list to what the document was commissioned to address. The gaps are the interesting part, and they are usually invisible to a reader going through linearly, because linear reading supplies continuity that the document does not have.

Module 4 turns this into an architectural tool, where the topic string of first sentences becomes a test of whether the document has a structure at all. The technique is the same one, applied a level up, which is worth noticing because it means the effort spent learning it here pays twice.

Choosing the string is choosing the argument

Module 2's lecture 2 showed that selecting a protagonist for a sentence carries an argumentative payload. The same applies here, more strongly, because a topic string spans a passage and therefore commits you for longer.

A paragraph whose topics are the calibration, its window and the exclusion is a paragraph about a technical defect. A paragraph covering the same facts whose topics are the model owner, their stress window choice and their failure to revisit it is a paragraph about a person's judgement. Neither is dishonest, both can be fully accurate, and they will be received completely differently. The choice is not available for evasion, since you must choose something, and it is available for consideration, which is all this course can usefully ask.

Common failure Enforcing a topic string mechanically by starting every sentence with the same noun phrase. A paragraph whose left edge reads "the calibration, the calibration, the calibration, the calibration" satisfies the test and reads as a stuck record. The pass condition is a recognisable set, and paragraph D in the figure shows that four distinct topics from one story is usually better prose than one topic repeated four times. Vary within the set, and never outside it.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Reproduce paragraphs C and D from the figure using content from your own work: same chaining technique, one drifting and one held within a set. Extract both topic strings. Then state, in one sentence, the constraint that made D possible.
  2. Give the extraction procedure and the two tests. What is the pass condition, stated precisely?
  3. Why is "recognisable set" the right test rather than "same word repeated"? Use the figure's paragraph D as your evidence.
  4. Explain how sequential chaining causes drift. Why does that mean the cohesion and coherence checks cannot be run one after the other?
  5. Show how holding a topic string can require a passive. Why is this a stronger argument against the passive superstition than the one module 2 gave?
  6. Name the two legitimate reasons to break a topic string mid-passage, and the test that distinguishes the second from drift.
  7. Extract the topic string of a document's executive summary, one item per paragraph. Compare it with what the document was asked to address, and report the gaps.
  8. Take one paragraph of your own and rewrite it twice with two different topic strings, so that the same facts produce a paragraph about a technical defect and a paragraph about a person's judgement. Say which you would send and why.
