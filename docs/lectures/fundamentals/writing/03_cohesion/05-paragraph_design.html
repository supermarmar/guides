Paragraph design

A paragraph is a unit of function, not a unit of length. It opens by establishing an issue and stating a point, then develops it, and the single most consequential decision is where the point sentence goes. The default is early, and the cases for delaying it are narrower than most writers assume.

Lecture 5 of 7 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: Williams, Style, lesson 5; Gopen & Swan (1990), expectation 1
Step 1

The 30-second version

A paragraph has two parts. The issue is the opening, usually one or two sentences, which establishes what the paragraph is about and states its point. The discussion is everything after, which develops, evidences or qualifies that point. Williams's observation is that readers expect the point at the end of the issue, which means early, and that a paragraph whose point arrives later forces the reader to hold evidence they cannot yet evaluate.

Governing all of it is Gopen and Swan's first reader expectation, which is the strictest rule in this course: every unit of discourse, at every scale, serves a single function and makes a single point. A paragraph doing two jobs is two paragraphs. The test is mechanical: state the paragraph's function in one clause, and if you need the word and, split it.

The delayed point is legitimate and rare. Three cases justify it: the point is unwelcome and the reader will reject it before hearing the evidence, the point is genuinely the conclusion of an argument that must be built to be understood, or the paragraph is closing a section and functions as its conclusion. Outside those three, a delayed point is usually a writer who worked out what they thought while writing and did not go back.

Feynman test Tell the reader what the paragraph is for before you use their attention on the details. A reader given the point first knows what each subsequent sentence is doing: this one is evidence, this one is a qualification, this one is the mechanism. A reader given the details first is holding a pile of facts and waiting to be told what they are a pile of, which is work you could have saved them with one sentence at the top.
What is beyond the source Williams supplies issue and discussion and the argument for the early point. Gopen and Swan supply the single-function rule. What this lecture adds is the narrow enumeration of the three delay cases in step 4 and the argument that a fourth apparent case, the paragraph that builds to a discovery, is usually a drafting artefact rather than a design choice. That last claim is the contestable one and it is stated so you can disagree with it.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the point sentence is what a skimming reader gets. Module 2's lecture 6 argued that professional readers sample rather than read, and lecture 3 of this module showed that extracting first sentences is a diagnostic. Turn that round and it becomes a design instruction: if your first sentences are what gets read, then your first sentences must carry your argument. A document whose paragraphs all open with orientation and close with conclusions is a document whose argument is invisible to most of its readers.

The second reason is that the single-function rule is the only defence against a document growing by accretion. Lecture 1 described the mechanism: a paragraph is drafted, a query prompts an addition, a risk prompts another, and each addition attaches cohesively so nothing looks wrong. The single-function rule catches this at the point of addition, because the moment you cannot state the paragraph's function in one clause you know a new paragraph is required. Applied consistently it makes accretion produce more paragraphs rather than worse ones.

The third reason concerns the paragraph as a visual unit. A reader forms an impression of a page before reading a word of it, from the density and the break pattern, and a page of five uniform blocks reads as undifferentiated whatever it contains. Paragraph length is not a rule and it is a signal: a one-sentence paragraph after four long ones is emphatic without any lexical emphasis at all, which is module 2's lecture 7 operating at a scale above the sentence.

And a reason particular to exam answers under time pressure. If the point goes first, a marker who reads only your opening sentences has still met your argument, and a partially completed answer still scores. If the point goes last and you run out of time, the paragraph contains evidence for a claim you never made.

Notice what the failing column is not. It is not vague, it is not badly written sentence by sentence, and it is not missing content. Each paragraph presumably reaches its point somewhere. It is visible in about ninety seconds if you extract the first sentences.

Issue
Williams's term for the opening of a paragraph, typically one or two sentences, which establishes the topic and states the point. The reader treats it as a promise about what the rest of the paragraph will do.
Discussion
Everything after the issue: evidence, mechanism, qualification, consequence. Every sentence in it should be doing an identifiable job for the point stated in the issue.
Point sentence
The sentence stating the paragraph's claim. Default position is the last sentence of the issue. The three cases in step 4 license moving it to the end of the paragraph, and nothing licenses hiding it in the middle.
Single-function rule
Gopen and Swan's first expectation: every unit of discourse serves one function and makes one point. Applies to the sentence, the paragraph, the section and the document. Tested by stating the function in one clause.
Step 4

The detail

The three cases for delaying the point

CaseWhy the delay worksThe risk
Legitimate delays
The point is unwelcome A reader who meets a conclusion they will resist stops reading to argue with it, and evaluates the evidence as an advocate. Building the evidence first means each step is accepted on its own terms before its destination is visible. A reader who suspects where you are going resents the construction. Works once per document, not four times.
The point is genuinely unintelligible in advance Some conclusions cannot be stated until their terms exist. A claim about the interaction between a downturn window and a long-run average calibration needs both concepts established first. Writers overestimate how often this applies. Usually the point can be stated loosely first and precisely later.
The paragraph closes a section A concluding paragraph's function is to land the accumulated argument, so its point belongs at the end, where the stress position of the whole section falls. Only the last paragraph of a section qualifies. Applying it to every paragraph produces the failing column in the figure.
The case that looks legitimate and usually is not
"The paragraph builds to a discovery" It feels like a design choice because the writing genuinely happened that way: you worked out what you thought as you drafted, and the paragraph records the sequence of your thinking. The reader does not need your route, only your destination. This is the commonest reason points end up last, and it is a drafting artefact. Module 9 has the evidence that expert revisers restructure where novices polish.

The fourth row is the contestable claim in this lecture and it deserves the objection stated fairly. Sometimes the sequence of discovery is genuinely the clearest exposition, particularly in teaching material, where watching a conclusion emerge is how a reader learns to derive it themselves. This course's own lectures do it, in the step 3 sections. The distinction is between writing where the reader's task is to learn a method and writing where the reader's task is to make a decision, and professional documents are almost always the second.

Applying the single-function test

THE ONE-CLAUSE TEST Write: "This paragraph ______." Fill it in. PASSES "...explains why the stress window understates the peak loss rate." "...sets out the two available remedies." "...states which remedy the model owner has chosen." "...records the committee's objection to that choice." FAILS "...explains why the window understates the peak loss rate AND sets out the remedies." -> two paragraphs. "...covers the stress window." -> "covers" is not a function. If you cannot say what the paragraph DOES, you have not designed it. This is the commonest failure and it is not detected by the "and" test, so run both. "...provides background." -> background to what claim? A background paragraph with no claim it is background TO is usually deletable, and deleting it is a real improvement. A CAUTION ON THE "AND" TEST "...explains why the window understates the rate and therefore why the LGD floor no longer binds." This passes despite containing "and", because the second clause is a consequence of the first rather than a second job. The test is whether the paragraph has one function, not whether the sentence describing it has one verb.

Paragraph length, and what it signals

There is no correct paragraph length and there is a relationship worth understanding between length and function. A paragraph is as long as its job requires, which in professional prose usually means three to six sentences, because a point plus its evidence and a qualification comes to about that. Consistently longer suggests paragraphs doing two jobs. Consistently shorter suggests an argument broken into fragments the reader must reassemble.

The one-sentence paragraph deserves defending, because writers avoid it as though it were an error. It is a legitimate unit: a single sentence doing a single job, and its brevity against surrounding paragraphs makes it conspicuous. That is module 2's lecture 7 operating a level up, where contrast in length produces emphasis without a single intensifier. Used once in a document it lands hard. Used throughout it becomes the register and stops working, which is the same decay every emphasis device suffers.

The visual argument is worth taking more seriously than writers usually do. A reader sees the shape of a page before reading it, and a page of uniform blocks signals undifferentiated content, which is a signal about your argument's structure whether or not you intended one. Varying paragraph length is therefore not decoration; it is the only pre-verbal information your reader receives about where the emphasis lies.

Where paragraph boundaries go

Lecture 3 established that a paragraph boundary is a licensed topic shift, and this is the place to say what licenses it. A new paragraph is correct when the function changes: from stating a problem to proposing a remedy, from evidence to qualification, from one member of a set to the next. It is not correct merely because the paragraph has become long, and breaking a long paragraph at a convenient sentence produces two paragraphs neither of which has a point.

The practical procedure is to identify the functions first and let the boundaries fall where they change. If you find yourself looking for somewhere to break a paragraph, you have the order of operations wrong: the paragraph is long because it is doing several things, and the several things are where the breaks belong.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The single-function rule is the only rule that scales

Almost everything in this course applies at one level. The character and action principle is about clauses. Topic strings are about paragraphs. The pyramid, coming in module 4, is about documents. The single-function rule applies unchanged at every level, and that is unusual enough to be worth noticing.

A clause makes one claim. A sentence serves one function in its paragraph. A paragraph makes one point. A section answers one question. A document establishes one thing. Each level is the same rule, and the diagnostic is the same at every level: state the function in one clause and see whether you can. That means the effort of learning it at paragraph scale pays four times, and it means a document that fails the test at one level usually fails at others, because the failure is a habit of not deciding what a unit is for.

It also explains why module 4 places the Pyramid Principle late rather than at the front. Taught first, the pyramid reads as a consulting house convention. Taught after the single-function rule and the given-new contract, it reads as what it is: the same two ideas applied to a reader who will not reach the end of your document.

What paragraph design cannot fix

A well-designed paragraph with a wrong point is worse than a badly designed one, because good design makes the wrong claim more persuasive. This is the same warning module 2's lecture 8 gave about the Paramedic Method, and it deserves repeating at paragraph scale because the temptation is larger. Putting the point first is a strong rhetorical move: it commits you, it reads as confident, and it makes the following sentences look like support. If the point is not one you can defend, all of that is working against your reader.

The specific failure to watch for is a point sentence that overstates what the discussion establishes. "The calibration understates the peak loss rate" followed by a discussion showing only that the window is short is a paragraph whose issue promises more than its discussion delivers, and a reader checking will notice. The honest version weakens the point sentence to match: "the stress window may be too short to support a downturn estimate". Less satisfying, and accurate, and the discipline of matching the two is where paragraph design becomes intellectual honesty rather than technique.

Common failure Turning the early point into a template and opening every paragraph with a bald assertion. A document of forty paragraphs each starting with a flat claim reads as hectoring, and the uniformity flattens the emphasis for the same reason uniform sentence length does. The issue is one or two sentences, and using the first to establish the topic before the second states the point is usually better prose than leading with the claim, because it honours the given-new contract at the paragraph seam.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a paragraph of your own and write it twice, once with the point at the end of the issue and once delayed to the end. State which of the three delay cases would justify the second version, and if none applies, say so.
  2. Define issue, discussion and point sentence. Where does the point go by default, and why?
  3. Run the first-sentences test on a section of a document you did not write. Report whether the column reads as a summary, and if not, where the points actually are.
  4. Give the three legitimate reasons to delay a point. For each, state the risk of overusing it.
  5. The fourth row of the table calls the building-to-a-discovery paragraph a drafting artefact. State the strongest objection to that claim, and the distinction that answers it.
  6. Apply the one-clause test to four of your own paragraphs. Which failed on "and", and which failed because you could not name a function at all?
  7. Why does the single-function rule scale to four levels when almost nothing else in this course does? What does that imply about a document that fails it at paragraph level?
  8. Find a paragraph whose point sentence overstates what its discussion establishes. Weaken the point to match, and note whether the paragraph is now less useful or merely less satisfying.
