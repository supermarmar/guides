Connectives and the curse of knowledge

Lecture 1 argued that adding connectives to a drifting paragraph makes it worse. Pinker argues that prose is far more often confusing for having too few. Both are right, and reconciling them produces the only rule about connectives worth carrying: readers impose a default relation on adjacent sentences, so you must mark the relations that depart from it and leave the ones that do not.

Lecture 6 of 7 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: Pinker (2014), chs. 3 and 5; Mann & Thompson (1988); Camerer et al. (1989)
Step 1

The 30-second version

When two sentences sit next to each other with nothing between them, the reader does not treat the relation as unspecified. They impose one. The default relations are elaboration, meaning the second sentence says more about the first, and sequence, meaning the second follows the first in time or as its consequence. Readers apply these automatically and without noticing.

That single fact resolves the apparent conflict in this module. If your actual relation is elaboration or consequence, you need no connective, and adding one is noise. If your actual relation is contrast, concession, or a cause running backwards from effect to explanation, you must mark it, because otherwise the reader will impose a default that is wrong and read your paragraph as claiming something you did not claim. Under-marking a departure is not merely unhelpful; it produces a misreading.

The reason writers under-mark is the curse of knowledge, named by Camerer, Loewenstein and Weber in an economics context and applied to prose by Pinker. You know why your second sentence follows your first, so the relation is present to you whether or not you wrote it down. This is the same failure lecture 2 described for referents, one level up: a missing connective is a bridging inference at the level of relations rather than of things.

Feynman test Put two sentences side by side and the reader assumes the second is either explaining the first or following from it, because that is what two adjacent sentences usually are. So if your second sentence is actually a counterexample, and you have not said so, the reader has just read your counterexample as supporting evidence. The word but costs three letters and prevents that.
What is beyond the source Pinker argues for more connectives and gives the curse of knowledge as the cause. Mann and Thompson supply a principled taxonomy of the relations. Neither frames the decision in terms of default relations, and neither reconciles the argument with the over-marking failure this module's first lecture described. The default framing in step 3 is this lecture's construction, and it is the piece that makes the advice operational: it tells you which connectives to add rather than telling you to add more.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that an unmarked departure is a misreading rather than a slowdown. Every other failure in this module costs the reader time: a bridge takes a fraction of a second, a drifting topic string costs attention. An unmarked contrast costs correctness. The reader finishes the paragraph believing something different from what you wrote, and they will not know it, and neither will you, because you both read the relation you intended.

The second reason is the one Pinker emphasises and professionals resist. Writers under-supply connectives because supplying them feels laborious and slightly patronising, as though the reader could not work it out. That instinct is wrong in a specific way: the reader can work it out, and working it out costs them attention they would otherwise spend on your argument, and in the cases that matter they will work out the wrong thing. Being explicit about a relation is not condescension. It is refusing to make the reader guess.

The third reason is that this is where the two halves of the module meet. Lecture 3 established that coherence requires a hypertheme, and connectives are how you tell the reader that a sentence belongs to it. A paragraph whose topics form a set and whose relations are unmarked reads as a list of related facts rather than as an argument, because the reader can see that the items belong together and cannot see how they bear on each other. Set membership makes a paragraph about something; connectives make it claim something.

And a stake specific to technical documents with mixed readerships. The relations an expert infers instantly, that a short stress window implies a compressed loss distribution which implies an understated downturn estimate, are three inferential steps for a non-specialist. Those are exactly the joins that get omitted, because they are the ones most obvious to the author. So the documents most needing explicit relations are the ones whose authors are least able to feel them missing.

Step 3

The mental model

Three panels. The first shows the default working correctly, so no connective is needed. The second shows the default imposed on a relation that is not the default, producing a misreading. The third shows the repair, which costs one word.

Panel 3's note about position deserves emphasis because it is a separate point from whether to include the connective at all. A connective sets up a frame the reader uses to interpret what follows, so it has to arrive before what it governs. However at the end of a sentence is nearly useless: the reader has already processed the clause on the wrong assumption and must now revise. Front position, or early-mid position immediately after the subject, both work. End position does not.

Default relation
The relation a reader imposes on two adjacent sentences when nothing marks one. Elaboration, meaning the second says more about the first, and sequence or consequence, meaning the second follows from it. Applied automatically and below awareness.
Departure
Any relation other than the defaults: contrast, concession, backward-running cause, exception, alternative. Must be marked, because an unmarked departure is read as a default and produces a misreading rather than a delay.
Curse of knowledge
The difficulty of simulating not knowing what you know. Named by Camerer, Loewenstein and Weber in 1989 for economic settings, applied to writing by Pinker. The reason a writer cannot feel a missing connective: the relation is present to them regardless of the page.
Step 4

The detail

Which relations to mark

The taxonomy below draws on rhetorical structure theory, which enumerates the relations that can hold between spans of text. The column that matters is the third.

RelationTypical markersMark it?
Defaults: usually leave unmarked
Elaborationin particular, specifically, that is No, normally. The reader assumes it. Mark only when the elaboration could be mistaken for a new point.
Sequence in timethen, next, subsequently No, if the order on the page matches the order of events. Mark when it does not.
Result, running forwardsso, therefore, as a result Often unnecessary, since the reader defaults to it. Mark when the causal step is long or contestable.
Exemplificationfor example, for instance Usually mark, because an unmarked example can be read as a further general claim, which overstates your case.
Departures: always mark
Contrastbut, by contrast, whereas Yes. Unmarked contrast is read as elaboration, so your counterexample becomes supporting evidence.
Concessionalthough, even so, nevertheless, admittedly Yes. This is the highest-cost omission: the reader loses the fact that you know about the objection.
Cause, running backwardsbecause, since, the reason is Yes. Effect-then-cause reverses the default direction, so the reader will read the second sentence as a consequence of the first.
Exception or qualificationexcept where, unless, other than Yes, and this is where the professional cost is highest, because an unmarked exception reads as part of the rule.
Alternativealternatively, or instead Yes. Two unmarked options read as two sequential steps, which changes a choice into a procedure.
Restatement for emphasisin other words, put differently Yes, because an unmarked restatement reads as a second, distinct claim, and the reader then looks for a difference that is not there.

The last row is worth dwelling on because it catches a failure most writers do not know they are committing. If you say something twice in different words without signalling it, a careful reader assumes you meant two things and hunts for the distinction. In technical writing this is actively dangerous: two formulations of one requirement, unmarked, will be read as two requirements. Either mark the restatement or delete it, and deleting it is usually right.

The insertion test

THE BECAUSE-BUT-SO TEST Between each pair of consecutive sentences, try inserting each of these in turn: because / but / so / for example / even so / in other words Then classify what happened. CASE 1 One fits, and it is "so" or nothing The relation is a default. Leave it unmarked. CASE 2 One fits, and it is "but", "even so", "because" or "for example" The relation is a departure. Mark it, at the front. CASE 3 Several fit equally well You have not decided what the relation is. This is the module 2 finding again: the syntax was letting you avoid a decision. Decide, then mark. CASE 4 None fits The two sentences may not belong in the same paragraph. Check the topic string: this is usually a coherence failure surfacing as a connective problem, and adding a connective would conceal it. That is lecture 1's warning. WORKED, ON FOUR SENTENCES A "The window spans 2011 to 2023." B "Arrears never exceeded 1.4 per cent in that period." C "The calibration passed the benchmark test." D "The benchmark uses the same window." A-B "so" fits, "because" fits weakly. Default. -> leave unmarked. B-C "but" fits, "so" fits, and they mean opposite things. CASE 3: the writer must decide. Intended relation is concession. -> mark: "Even so, the calibration passed..." C-D "because" fits. Backward-running cause: D explains C rather than following from it. CASE 2. -> mark: "...passed the benchmark test, because the benchmark uses the same window." Result: four sentences, two connectives, both marking departures, neither marking a default. Note that the B-C join was the one where the writer had a decision to make and the unmarked version had quietly made it wrongly.

Reconciling this lecture with lecture 1

Lecture 1 took a drifting paragraph, added notably, moreover and in turn, and argued that the result was worse. This lecture argues connectives are under-supplied. The two positions are compatible and the reconciliation is exact.

A connective asserts that a relation holds. There are therefore two ways to get it wrong: assert a relation that does not hold, or fail to assert one that does. Lecture 1's paragraph committed the first, and the reason the added connectives made it worse is that each one made a false claim about how its sentences related, which is a substantive error rather than a stylistic one. This lecture's paragraphs commit the second. Both are failures of correspondence between the markers on the page and the relations in the argument.

Which yields a single rule covering both: mark the relation you have, do not mark one you do not have, and know which relations the reader will supply for free. The diagnostic difference is that over-marking is detectable by asking whether each connective's claim is true, and under-marking is detectable only by the insertion test, because the curse of knowledge hides it.

The metadiscourse boundary

Connectives are not the same as signposting, and the difference is worth marking because module 5 will tell you to cut the second. "However" names a relation between two claims. "It is worth noting that" names nothing; it talks about the document. "In this section we will consider" is pure navigation. Pinker's argument for more connectives is not an argument for more metadiscourse, and conflating them produces prose full of announcements and still missing its joins.

The test is whether the phrase would survive translation into a logical relation. But becomes a contrast; because becomes a cause. It should be noted that becomes nothing at all, which is why module 5's lecture 2 treats it as deletable. Add connectives freely and signposting sparingly, and be clear which one you are adding.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The two curses, and why they produce the same document

The curse of knowledge has now appeared twice in this module in structurally identical forms. In lecture 2 it explained why you cannot feel the bridging inferences you impose: the referent is in your head whether or not it is on the page. Here it explains why you cannot feel the missing connectives: the relation is in your head whether or not it is on the page.

Both are instances of the same thing, and stating it generally is worth the sentence. Comprehension requires the reader to reconstruct a structure from cues, and every element of that structure which you possess independently of the cues is an element whose absence you cannot detect. Referents and relations are simply the two kinds of element. That is why the checks in this module are all mechanical, all involve covering up or extracting from the prose, and none of them asks you to read carefully.

It also explains a pattern in professional documents that otherwise looks like laziness. Expert-written prose in every technical field tends to be simultaneously dense with referents that lack antecedents and sparse in connectives, and both properties have the same cause. The author's model of the subject supplies the missing material so completely that the draft reads as finished. The more expert the author, the more it supplies, which is why the most knowledgeable person on a team frequently produces the least usable document.

Why the resistance to connectives is not irrational

Writers resist adding connectives, and the resistance deserves a fair hearing rather than a lecture, because it has a real basis.

Explicit relations do change the register. A paragraph with a connective at every join reads more explanatory, more careful, arguably more junior, and in some professional cultures signalling that you have worked something out for the reader is signalling that you thought they needed it. That is a genuine social cost and it is not imaginary.

The answer is not to dismiss it but to note what it trades against. The cost of the register shift is to how you are perceived. The cost of the omission is that a reader draws a wrong conclusion from your document. Those are not commensurable, and in any document that supports a decision the second dominates. Where they genuinely conflict, in an internal note to a peer who shares your model, omitting the connective is defensible, and it is defensible precisely because you can name why.

The limit of the default framing

The framing in step 3 is a simplification and its edges are worth marking. Readers' defaults are not fixed by grammar; they are shaped by genre and by what the surrounding text has established. In a methods section, adjacent sentences default strongly to temporal sequence. In an argument, they default to elaboration or support. In a list, they default to co-ordination. So "the default relation" is really "the default relation given what the reader thinks they are reading", and a writer who has established the genre clearly has more license to leave relations implicit.

The practical consequence favours explicitness rather than undermining it. If defaults depend on the reader's model of the genre, then a document whose genre is unclear, which describes a great many internal papers that are part analysis and part recommendation, has no reliable default at all. In those documents, marking the departures is not optional, because you cannot predict what the reader will assume in their absence.

Common failure Adding connectives without checking the topic string, and thereby applying case 2 of the insertion test to a paragraph that is in case 4. If two consecutive sentences take no connective comfortably, the likely diagnosis is that they do not belong together, and forcing a relation onto them papers over a coherence failure with something that looks like an argument. Run the topic string first, then the connectives. In this module the order of the checks is part of the method.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Run the because-but-so test on a paragraph of your own, join by join, and classify each into one of the four cases. Report which joins were case 3, where you had a decision you had not made, and what you decided.
  2. Name the default relations. Why does an unmarked departure cost correctness rather than time?
  3. Why can neither the writer nor a rereading detect an unmarked contrast? Answer in terms of the curse of knowledge.
  4. Give three relations that must always be marked, and state what the reader concludes if each is left unmarked.
  5. Why does an unmarked restatement create a problem in technical writing specifically? What are the two repairs, and which is usually right?
  6. Reconcile this lecture with lecture 1's argument that added connectives made a paragraph worse. State the single rule that covers both.
  7. Distinguish a connective from metadiscourse, and give the test. Which does module 5 tell you to cut?
  8. Step 5 concedes that resisting connectives has a real basis. State the strongest version of that objection, and say what it trades against.
