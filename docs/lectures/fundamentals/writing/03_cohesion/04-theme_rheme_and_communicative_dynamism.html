Theme, rheme and communicative dynamism

The theory underneath everything modules 2 and 3 have taught as practice. It comes from Prague rather than from any style manual, it gives the three chaining patterns their proper names, and it explains something the practical lectures could only assert: why end-weight and end-focus point the same way.

Lecture 4 of 7 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: Firbas (1992); Daneš (1974); Halliday, Functional Grammar; Quirk et al. (1985)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Every clause divides into a theme, the point of departure, what the clause is about and where the reader is starting from, and a rheme, what is said about it. Firbas added that the elements of a clause carry differing degrees of communicative dynamism, meaning the extent to which each pushes the communication forward, and that dynamism rises towards the end of the clause. That is the theoretical statement of what module 2's lecture 7 called the stress position.

Daneš then showed that clauses connect into passages by a small number of thematic progressions. There are three: the rheme of one clause becomes the theme of the next, the same theme persists across several clauses, or a series of themes all derive from one superordinate topic. Those three are exactly lecture 2's sequential chain, lecture 2's constant chain, and lecture 3's recognisable set. The practice came first in this course; here are its names and the reason it forms a system.

The theory earns its lecture by explaining one thing the practical lectures could only assert. English prefers new information late and heavy constituents late, and these look like two separate preferences that happen to agree. They are not separate. Given information can be compressed to a pronoun; new information must be described in full. So old-before-new produces light-before-heavy automatically, and end-focus and end-weight are one fact seen from two directions.

Feynman test A clause is a run-up and a delivery. The run-up tells the reader where you are standing, and it should be ground they already know, so it can be short. The delivery is what you came to say, and it needs room. Everything in this module is a consequence of the fact that you cannot deliver first and then explain where you were standing.
What is beyond the source The sources supply the framework. What this lecture adds is the mapping in step 4, which shows that five traditions describe the same distinction under different names, and the argument in step 5 that the Anglo-American writing literature keeps rediscovering Prague School results without attribution. Gopen and Swan's expectations 3, 4 and 7 are functional sentence perspective restated for practitioners, and they cite no Prague linguist. That is worth knowing because it tells you where to look when a piece of writing advice seems to lack a theory.
Step 2

Why this matters

A reasonable objection is that you already have the practice, so why learn the theory. Three answers, in ascending order of usefulness.

The first is that the theory unifies advice you currently hold as separate rules. Old before new, put the point at the end, keep your topics consistent, prefer heavy constituents late: four instructions from three lectures, which turn out to be one principle about how information is distributed across a clause and a passage. Holding one principle rather than four is cheaper and more reliable, particularly under time pressure, because you can derive the four from it rather than remembering them.

The second is that a theory tells you what the exceptions are. Rules give you no guidance when they conflict; a mechanism does. When you need to violate old-before-new, and there are cases, knowing that the principle exists because compressed given information is short and described new information is long tells you immediately what the cost will be and whether it is worth paying.

The third and most practical is that Daneš's three progressions are a complete enumeration. Modules 2 and 3 have described two chaining patterns and a set condition without saying whether there might be others. There are three, and knowing that the list is closed means a passage that fits none of them is definitely broken rather than possibly novel. That is a genuinely useful thing to know when reviewing prose you find hard and cannot yet fault.

Step 3

The mental model

The figure has two halves. The top gives Daneš's three thematic progressions, each mapped to the lecture in this course that taught it as practice. The bottom gives the convergence argument, which is the piece of genuine explanation the theory contributes.

The convergence argument deserves one further turn, because it also explains the passive. Module 2's lecture 5 listed end-weight as one of the passive's four legitimate uses: you use a passive to move a long agent past the verb. Why are agents ever long? Because a newly introduced agent must be described in full, whereas a given one is a pronoun. So the end-weight use of the passive is the end-focus use of the passive, and the reason the construction exists at all is to let a clause put its given element first when that element is not the actor.

Theme
The point of departure of a clause: what it is about and where the reader starts. In Halliday's account, simply the first constituent. In the Prague account, the element with the lowest communicative dynamism, which is usually but not always the same thing.
Rheme
What is said about the theme. Carries the higher communicative dynamism, and the point of the clause sits at its end, which is the stress position.
Communicative dynamism
Firbas's notion that clause elements differ in how much they push the communication forward, and that dynamism generally increases from beginning to end. A gradient rather than a two-way split, which is its advantage over given and new.
Hypertheme
A superordinate topic from which the individual themes of a passage derive. The formal name for what lecture 3 called a recognisable set, and the thing you choose when you decide what a paragraph is about.
Step 4

The detail

Five traditions, one distinction

The same division of a clause has been named at least five times by people who were largely not reading each other. Having the map prevents you thinking you have learned five things.

TraditionFront of clauseEnd of clause
The same distinction under five sets of names
Prague School (Mathesius, Firbas) Theme: lowest communicative dynamism Rheme: highest communicative dynamism
Systemic functional (Halliday) Theme: the first constituent, defined by position Rheme: everything after it
Psycholinguistic (Clark & Haviland) Given: has an antecedent in memory New: the information being added
Practitioner (Gopen & Swan) Topic position: supplies context, links backwards Stress position: carries the point
Practitioner (Williams) Topic: what the sentence is about Emphasis: what the reader stresses

The rows are not quite interchangeable and the difference matters in one place. Halliday defines Theme positionally: whatever comes first is the Theme, full stop. The Prague tradition defines theme by information status, so the theme is the element the reader already holds. These usually coincide, because writers usually put given material first, and they come apart precisely when a writer breaks the contract. In "A 22 per cent fall in values invalidates the floor", the Hallidayan Theme is the fall, because it is first, and the Prague theme is arguably the floor, because that is the known element. Both analyses are describing the same defect from different ends.

Which gives the practical value of the distinction. When the positional theme and the informational theme are different elements, you have found a sentence that violates the given-new contract, and you have found it by a purely structural test. That is a more precise diagnostic than lecture 2's audit, and it is what the theory buys you.

Marked and unmarked themes

In an English declarative clause the unmarked theme is the subject: it comes first by default, so putting it there signals nothing. Anything else in first position is a marked theme, and marking carries meaning.

MARKED THEMES AND WHAT THEY SIGNAL UNMARKED (subject first, no signal) "The calibration understates the peak loss rate." MARKED: fronted adverbial of time or place "In Q3, the calibration understated the peak loss rate." Signals: a new time frame is being established. Use when the passage is moving between periods and the reader needs the frame before the claim. MARKED: fronted conditional "Where the obligor is unlikely to pay, the exposure moves to stage 3." Signals: the claim holds only inside this condition. Module 1's lecture 5 called this a shallow left branch and said one is cheap. MARKED: fronted complement, for contrast "That exclusion the committee accepted without challenge." Signals: strong contrast with something adjacent. Rare in professional prose and conspicuous when used. MARKED: fronted "as for" or "with respect to" "As for the wholesale book, no migration was observed." Signals: an explicit topic switch. Honest, slightly heavy, and the right choice when you are deliberately changing subject mid-passage rather than drifting. THE RULE OF THUMB Marked themes are a limited currency. One per paragraph reads as deliberate framing; four reads as a writer who has not decided what their topics are. Note also that every fronted adverbial pushes the subject rightward, so a marked theme costs subject-verb proximity: module 2's lecture 6 and this lecture are in mild tension, and the resolution is that one marked theme is worth the cost and a habit of them is not.

Where the principle can legitimately be broken

The theory tells you the cost of a violation, which the rule alone could not. Three cases are worth knowing.

The first is the deliberate cold open, at the start of a section or a document, where there is no prior text to be given information. Here the first sentence must introduce something new in the theme position, because nothing is available to start from. This is not a violation; it is the boundary condition. What makes it work is that the reader knows a section has begun and expects orientation.

The second is dramatic reversal, where you front the new information precisely to disorient. "Nothing in the monitoring pack showed the drift." That is a marked theme carrying new content, it costs the reader a moment, and the moment is the effect. Use it once in a document and it lands; use it four times and it becomes the register.

The third is genuine list structure, where several items are being enumerated and each is new. "Three drivers explain the shortfall. Migration accounts for half of it. Model change accounts for a third. New business accounts for the remainder." Sentences two to four all open with new information, and the passage works because sentence one established the frame that licenses it. This is Daneš's derived-theme pattern with the hypertheme stated explicitly, and stating the hypertheme is what buys you the licence.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

A tradition the style guides do not cite

Functional sentence perspective was developed in Czechoslovakia from the 1930s, formalised by Firbas over four decades, and taken up in Britain by Halliday in a related form. It is standard material in European linguistics departments. It is almost entirely absent from Anglo-American writing instruction, and the consequence is visible: the practical literature keeps rediscovering its results and naming them afresh.

Gopen and Swan's expectations 3, 4 and 7 are functional sentence perspective restated for scientists, and their 1990 paper cites no Prague linguist. Williams's topic and emphasis are theme and rheme with different labels. This is not plagiarism and it is not really a criticism of anyone; it is what happens when a practitioner tradition and a theoretical tradition do not read each other. But it does mean that a writer who has only read the style guides holds a set of rules whose underlying system was worked out sixty years earlier and is more general than the rules.

The practical lesson generalises past this instance. When a piece of writing advice works reliably and its source offers no explanation for why, there is usually a descriptive tradition somewhere that has the explanation. Module 1's lecture 3 made the same point about Halliday's grammatical metaphor sitting underneath Sword's zombie nouns. Looking for the theory is worth the effort because the theory tells you the exceptions, and the rules never do.

What the theory does not settle

Two genuine disputes should be flagged rather than smoothed over, because a lecture that presented this as settled would be doing what step 5 just criticised.

The first is the definitional split described in step 4. Halliday's positional definition of Theme and the Prague informational definition are not reconcilable, and specialists have argued about it for decades. The pragmatic position taken here, that the divergence between them is itself a useful diagnostic, is a way of making the dispute productive rather than resolving it.

The second is whether communicative dynamism is a real gradient or a useful fiction. Firbas treats it as a genuine graded property determined by the interplay of linearity, semantics and context, and critics have questioned whether it can be measured independently of the intuitions it is meant to explain. That objection has force. For a writer it matters little, because the gradient framing earns its place by being more useful than a two-way given and new split: it predicts that the very end of a clause is more emphatic than the middle of the rheme, which is exactly what module 2's lecture 7 needed and could not derive from a binary distinction.

Where this leaves the module

Lectures 1 to 4 have given you two properties, one experimental result, one extraction method and one framework, and they now fit together in a way worth stating in one place. Coherence requires a hypertheme, chosen before you write. Cohesion requires that consecutive themes relate to each other by one of three progressions. Within each clause, the theme is what the reader holds and the rheme is what you are adding, with the point at the very end. And the reason the rheme is heavier is the reason it goes last.

Lecture 5 turns to the unit inside which all of this operates, which is the paragraph, and asks the question the module has been deferring: where does the point sentence go, and what licenses the rare decision to delay it.

Common failure Using this vocabulary in feedback to colleagues. "Your thematic progression is neither linear nor constant" is correct and useless; "each of these four sentences is about a different thing, and here is the list" is the same observation and can be acted on. The terminology is for your own thinking, where its precision earns its cost. In review comments, describe what you extracted rather than naming the framework.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a paragraph of your own and diagram its thematic progression in the notation of the figure: label each theme and rheme, draw the links, and identify which of Daneš's three types it uses. If it uses none, say what would be needed to make it type 3.
  2. Define theme, rheme and communicative dynamism. Why is a gradient more useful to a writer than a binary given and new split?
  3. Name Daneš's three progressions and map each to the lecture in this course that taught it as practice. Why does it matter that the list is complete?
  4. Explain why end-focus and end-weight are one fact rather than two. Then use the same argument to explain why the passive exists.
  5. Halliday and the Prague School define theme differently. Give a sentence where the two definitions pick out different elements, and say what that divergence tells you about the sentence.
  6. What is a marked theme? Give four kinds with the signal each carries, and state the tension between marked themes and module 2's lecture 6.
  7. Give the three cases where breaking old-before-new is legitimate. What does the third case require you to supply first, in Daneš's terms?
  8. Gopen and Swan's expectations 3, 4 and 7 restate Prague School results without citing them. What general lesson does step 5 draw from that, and where else in this course has the same pattern appeared?
