Module 3 · Writing track

Cohesion and the paragraph

Sentences that are individually clear can still form a passage nobody can follow. Seven lectures on what happens between sentences, and why a paragraph can be locally faultless and globally about nothing.

What this module is

Module 2 fixed the sentence. This one fixes the joins, and the first thing it does is separate two properties that are usually confused. Cohesion is the local sense that each sentence follows from the last. Coherence is the passage-level sense that the whole thing is about something. They are independent. A paragraph can flow smoothly from sentence to sentence and still leave the reader unable to say what its point was, and a paragraph with a perfectly clear point can jolt at every transition.

Each has its own mechanism. Cohesion is managed by what you put at the start of a sentence: readers comprehend by locating information they already hold and attaching the new to it, a finding Clark and Haviland demonstrated experimentally and named the given-new contract. Coherence is managed by what your subjects have in common across a passage, which Williams calls the topic string. Both are checkable from the page without asking anyone to read it.

Prerequisites

  • Module 2, lecture 7: the stress position. This module is largely an argument about what happens to material after it lands in a stress position.
  • Module 2, lecture 2: characters as subjects. Topic strings are read off the subjects, so you need to be able to find them.

Module sequence

Part 1 · The two properties (lectures 1 to 3)

The distinction, then the mechanism behind each half of it.

  • builtLecture 1: cohesion versus coherence. Two independent properties with two separate cures. Worked pairs showing each present without the other.
  • builtLecture 2: the given-new contract. Clark and Haviland's finding that readers locate given information first and attach new information to it, with the experimental evidence that truth-equivalent paragraphs differ in processing time depending on whether they honour it. Old before new; the topic position as anchor.
  • builtLecture 3: topic strings. Read down the left-hand edge of a paragraph and list the subjects. If they do not form a recognisable set, the paragraph reads as drift no matter how good the individual sentences are.

Part 2 · The linguistics and the unit (lectures 4 to 5)

What sits underneath the practice, and how the paragraph itself is built.

  • builtLecture 4: theme, rheme and communicative dynamism. Functional Sentence Perspective from Mathesius and Firbas, and Halliday's theme and rheme, as the theory behind what modules 2 and 3 teach as practice. Why end-weight and end-focus coincide in English rather than by accident.
  • builtLecture 5: paragraph design. Issue, discussion, point. Where the point sentence goes and the narrow set of cases for delaying it. The single-function rule: every unit of discourse serves one function, and a paragraph doing two jobs is two paragraphs.

Part 3 · Joining and checking (lectures 6 to 7)

The connectives that most writers under-supply, and a diagnosis you can run on any page.

  • builtLecture 6: connectives and the curse of knowledge. Pinker's argument that prose is far more often confusing for having too few connectives than pedantic for having too many, because the writer can already see joins the reader cannot. A taxonomy of connective relations and which ones English leaves implicit.
  • builtLecture 7: passage-level diagnosis. A repeatable checklist combining Gopen and Swan's seven reader expectations with topic-string mapping, run start to finish on a genuinely bad page of professional prose.

Where this reappears

The given-new contract
A sentence-to-sentence principle here. Module 4 scales it to the document: the Pyramid Principle is the same expectation applied to a reader who will not reach the end.
The curse of knowledge
Introduced in lecture 6 as a cause of missing connectives. Module 6, lecture 4 treats it as the general diagnosis of bad expert prose.
The single-function rule
Applied to the paragraph in lecture 5. Module 4 applies it to sections and to whole documents.
