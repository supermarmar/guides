The given-new contract

The most solidly evidenced claim in this course. Readers comprehend a sentence by finding the part they already hold, locating its antecedent in memory, and attaching the new part to it. When the antecedent is missing they build a bridge, and the bridge costs time that can be measured.

Lecture 2 of 7 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: Haviland & Clark (1974); Clark & Haviland (1977)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Clark and Haviland proposed that comprehension runs in three steps. The reader divides the incoming sentence into given information, which they are meant to already possess, and new information, which is the point. They search memory for the antecedent of the given part. Then they attach the new part to that antecedent. The given-new contract is the writer's implicit undertaking to package sentences so that step two succeeds.

When it fails, readers do not stop. They bridge: they construct an inference that supplies the missing antecedent, and then proceed. Haviland and Clark demonstrated in 1974 that bridging takes measurably longer than direct matching, using pairs of passages that are equally true, equally grammatical and equally long, differing only in whether the second sentence's given information had appeared in the first.

The practical rule that falls out is old before new, and it applies at two scales. Within a sentence, put familiar material at the front, in the topic position, and new material at the end, in the stress position. Across sentences, whatever you put in the stress position must either end the passage or become the given information opening the next sentence. That second half is the engine of cohesion, and it is why module 2's lecture 7 could not finish its own argument.

Feynman test Every sentence you write is a parcel you are handing over, and it has a hook on one end. The hook has to catch something the reader is already holding. If it does, they attach the parcel and carry on. If it does not, they have to reach behind them, find something plausible, and improvise a fastening. They will manage, and it costs them a moment, and after twenty parcels they are tired and holding a structure you did not design.
What is beyond the source Clark and Haviland's contract is a claim about comprehension, framed for psycholinguists and stated in terms of single sentence pairs. Extending it to the two chaining patterns in step 3 is standard in the writing literature, notably in Williams, though the terminology varies. The observation in step 5, that a writer always possesses a direct antecedent in their own head and therefore cannot feel the bridge they are imposing, is not in either source and is the reason this principle is so hard to self-apply.
Step 2

Why this matters

This is the principle with the best evidence behind it, and that is worth something specific in a field where most advice is assertion. Module 1's lecture 7 asked you to classify claims as empirical, aesthetic or teaching device. The given-new contract is empirical: it makes a prediction about reading time, the prediction was tested, and it held. If you are going to trust one thing in this course over the others, trust this.

The practical stake is that it converts cohesion from an impression into an operation. You do not have to feel whether a paragraph flows. You read the opening few words of each sentence and ask whether they contain something the previous sentence supplied. That is a mechanical check on marks on a page, and it is the only reliable way to audit your own prose, because the impression of flow is exactly what your knowledge of the subject contaminates.

The third reason is that it explains a failure that otherwise looks like carelessness and is not. Technical writers routinely open sentences with new information because the new information is what they are excited about, and the topic position is the first place their hands land. "A 22 per cent fall in collateral values now invalidates the LGD floor" opens with a number the reader has never seen. The sentence is true, clear by every module 2 test, and it makes the reader hold an unattached fact until the end of the clause. Reversing it costs nothing and repairs it.

And there is a stake for anyone whose documents are read by people less expert than themselves. Bridging is cheap for a reader who has the domain knowledge to build the inference and expensive for one who does not. A paragraph that reads smoothly to the model risk team can be genuinely hard for the committee, and the difference is entirely in how many bridges each reader has to construct. That is not a matter of simplifying the vocabulary. It is a matter of supplying antecedents.

Step 3

The mental model

The figure has two halves. The top is Haviland and Clark's experimental contrast, which is the evidence. The bottom is the two ways a legitimate chain can be built across a passage, plus the version that breaks.

The constant pattern is worth flagging because writers avoid it out of a misplaced fear of repetition. Three consecutive sentences opening with the model, it, it look monotonous on the page and read as tightly focused, because the reader is being told repeatedly that the topic has not changed. Varying the opening for elegance is precisely what breaks the chain, and module 1's lecture 2 already forbade varying a defined term. Here the same discipline extends to the topic itself.

Given information
The part of a sentence the writer treats as already available to the reader, whether from earlier text or shared context. Belongs at the front. Its job is to locate the sentence, not to inform.
New information
The part that constitutes the point. Belongs at the end, in the stress position. Becomes given information for the following sentence, or ends the passage.
Bridging inference
The inference a reader constructs when the given information has no direct antecedent in memory. Measurably slower than direct matching. Not an error: bridges are how prose avoids stating everything, and the question is only whether this reader can build this bridge.
Chaining
The sequential pattern, in which each sentence's new information becomes the next sentence's given. The other legitimate pattern is constant, holding one given across several sentences. Using neither is what a broken chain means.
Step 4

The detail

Four ways the contract breaks

FailureWhat the reader doesRepair
Ranked by how often they occur in professional prose
New before old within the sentence Holds an unattached fact until the clause supplies something to attach it to. Reverse the order. "A 22 per cent fall in values invalidates the LGD floor" becomes "the LGD floor no longer holds, because values have fallen by 22 per cent".
Bare demonstrative with no noun Searches for what this refers to. Often several candidates, so they pick one, sometimes the wrong one. Never write a bare this or these at the start of a sentence. Add the noun: this exclusion, these two findings. Costs one word, removes the ambiguity entirely.
Antecedent too far back Bridges, or scrolls up. Both cost more than the writer imagines, because the writer never lost the thread. Re-name the thing rather than pronominalising it. After two intervening sentences, it is no longer safe.
No antecedent at all Constructs a bridge from domain knowledge, if they have it, or accepts the sentence without integrating it, if they do not. Supply the missing step. Usually one clause, and usually a fact the writer thought too obvious to state.

The second row is the highest-value single habit in this lecture, so it is worth stating as a flat rule with no exceptions offered: do not begin a sentence with a bare this. The word is doing reference work, reference is the most fragile of Halliday and Hasan's five devices, and a demonstrative with two plausible antecedents will be resolved by the reader in whichever direction their expectations point rather than yours. Adding the noun converts the fragile device into a robust one for the price of a word.

Worked: the same paragraph, contract honoured and broken

BROKEN (every sentence opens with new material) "A 22 per cent fall in residential collateral values has been recorded since June. Peak-valuation underwriting in 2022 accounts for most of the affected exposure. The LGD floor of 10 per cent no longer binds for this cohort. Recalibration before the next capital return is required." Every one is true and every one is clear by module 2's tests. The reader assembles four unconnected facts and constructs the argument themselves, which they may do correctly or not at all. HONOURED (each opening picks up the previous close) "Residential collateral values have fallen 22 per cent since June. Most of that fall sits in exposures underwritten at peak valuations in 2022. For that 2022 cohort, the 10 per cent LGD floor no longer binds, so the floor overstates recoveries rather than protecting against them. The model owner must therefore recalibrate before the next capital return." Sentence openings, in order: "Residential collateral values ..." given by context "Most of that fall ..." given: the fall "For that 2022 cohort ..." given: the cohort "The model owner must therefore ..." given: the whole preceding argument WHAT CHANGED Word count: 44 -> 62. It got LONGER. Nothing was added except joins: "that fall", "that 2022 cohort", "therefore", and the clause explaining what the floor failing actually means. Every one of those is given-information scaffolding. THE POINT WORTH TAKING Honouring the contract usually costs words. This module is not about concision and at several points it is in tension with it. Module 5 will make you cut; this lecture makes you add. The two are reconciled by asking what the words are doing: scaffolding earns its place, padding does not.

That word count going up is the honest and slightly awkward finding of the lecture, and it is worth confronting rather than hiding. Module 2's Paramedic Method removed half a passage. This lecture added 40 per cent to one. Both are correct, because they target different things: Lanham removes words that carry no information, and the given-new contract adds words that carry no new information but do carry attachment. A join is not padding, and telling them apart is a matter of asking whether deleting the phrase would leave the reader to infer something.

The mechanical audit

Two minutes per page, and it requires no judgement about quality.

Take a printed page and cover everything except the first five or six words of each sentence. Read down that column. For each entry, ask whether it contains a word, a pronoun, or a demonstrative phrase referring to something in the sentence immediately before. Mark the ones that do not. Those are your cold starts, and each is either a deliberate topic shift, which belongs at a paragraph boundary, or a break.

The reason to cover the rest of the page is not theatrical. Reading the full sentences reactivates your knowledge of the subject, and your knowledge supplies every missing antecedent instantly, which is exactly the faculty the audit is trying to bypass. Looking only at the openings is the closest you can get to reading your own prose without knowing what it says.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Why you cannot feel the bridges you impose

Here is the structural reason this principle is harder to self-apply than anything else in the course. Bridging happens when the given information has no antecedent in the reader's memory. When you read your own draft, the antecedent is always present, because it is in your head whether or not it is on the page. You wrote the sentence knowing that picnic supplies include beer. So step two of the comprehension process always succeeds for you, on every sentence, and you never experience the cost you are imposing.

This is not carelessness and no amount of care fixes it, because the faculty you would use to check is the faculty that is compromised. It is why the audit in step 4 is mechanical and why it involves covering up the prose. And it is the sharpest available illustration of the curse of knowledge, which module 6 treats generally: the specific thing an expert cannot simulate is not knowing a fact, and the specific damage that does to prose is a document full of bridges its author cannot see.

The corollary is about feedback. A reviewer from your own team shares your knowledge, so they bridge cheaply too and will report the document as clear. A reader from outside will find it hard and will report that it was "quite technical", which is a description of vocabulary rather than of the actual defect. So neither reviewer tells you what is wrong, and the only reliable detector is the audit.

Bridging is not the enemy

A course that treated every bridge as a defect would be recommending prose that states every connection explicitly, and that prose is unreadable in its own way: tedious, condescending, and four times the necessary length. Bridges are how writing stays economical. "We checked the picnic supplies. The beer was warm." is a perfectly good pair of sentences, and spelling out that picnic supplies include beer would insult the reader.

So the question is never whether you are imposing a bridge but whether this reader can build it cheaply. That reframes the whole principle as an audience judgement rather than a rule, and it gives you a workable criterion: a bridge is acceptable when the inference required is one any member of your intended readership makes automatically from general or shared professional knowledge. It is not acceptable when the inference requires something only you know, such as which meeting a decision was taken at, or why two figures in your document differ.

Applied to a mixed-readership document, that criterion has an uncomfortable implication. The bridges that a board paper can safely leave implicit are far fewer than those in a paper for the model risk team, and the difference is not vocabulary. It is that the committee lacks the domain knowledge to build the technical bridges, so joins that are invisible scaffolding for one audience are missing steps for the other. Writing the same content for two audiences is largely a matter of deciding how many bridges to build, which is why it takes longer than it looks.

Common failure Turning "old before new" into a rule that every sentence must open with a backward reference, and producing a paragraph in which every sentence begins "This...", "That...", "Such...". The chain is then so conspicuous that it reads as laboured, and the constant repetition of demonstratives has its own cost. The contract requires that the reader can attach the sentence, not that you signpost the attachment every time. Lexical repetition of the topic, a pronoun, or simply a shared frame will often do it without a demonstrative at all.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Reproduce the step 4 pair with content from your own work: write a four-sentence version where every sentence opens with new material, then the version that honours the contract. Report both word counts, and identify every word you added that is pure join.
  2. State Clark and Haviland's three-step model of comprehension. Which step fails when the contract is broken?
  3. Describe Haviland and Clark's 1974 contrast. Why does it count as evidence rather than assertion, and what is controlled between the two conditions?
  4. Name the two legitimate chaining patterns and say what each is good for. Why do writers avoid the constant pattern, and why is that avoidance a mistake?
  5. Give the flat rule about bare demonstratives and the reason for it in terms of Halliday and Hasan's five devices.
  6. Honouring the contract made the worked paragraph 40 per cent longer, while module 2's method cut a passage in half. Reconcile the two, and give the test that distinguishes a join from padding.
  7. Why can you not feel the bridges you impose on a reader? Why does care not fix it, and what does that imply about feedback from your own team?
  8. Run the covered-page audit on a page you wrote. Report how many cold starts you found, and for each one say whether it was a deliberate topic shift or a break.
