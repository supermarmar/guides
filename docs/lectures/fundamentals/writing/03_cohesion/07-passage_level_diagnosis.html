Lectures / Writing / Cohesion

Passage-level diagnosis

Seven checks in a fixed order, run on a genuinely bad page. The order is the substance of the lecture: structural failures make sentence repairs wasted work, so the cheapest checks go last.

Lecture 7 of 7 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: Gopen & Swan (1990); Williams, Style, lessons 4 and 5
Step 1

The 30-second version

Modules 2 and 3 have supplied a dozen diagnostics. This lecture puts seven of them in an order and runs them once, end to end. The order runs from most structural to most local: find out what the passage is about, then what it claims, then where the claim sits, then how the sentences join, then what relations the joins assert, and only then how each sentence is built.

The reason for that direction is economic rather than aesthetic. A coherence failure invalidates the sentence-level work: if a paragraph is about four unrelated things, polishing its sentences is effort spent on prose you are about to delete. Running the checks in the other direction, which is what most people do because sentence-level problems are the ones you notice while reading, means repairing sentences that should not exist.

The lecture also settles something the module has been assuming. Gopen and Swan's seven reader expectations, introduced in module 1's opening lecture as the map of the course, are all covered by these seven checks. The mapping in step 3 shows which check discharges which expectation, which is both a completeness argument and a way of finding what you have not checked.

Feynman test Diagnose a building from the outside in. Is it in the right place, is it the right kind of building, does it have the rooms it needs, do the doors connect, and only then whether the skirting is straight. Nobody sands the skirting before checking whether the room is meant to be there, and yet in prose that is the normal order, because bad skirting is the thing you notice from inside.
What is beyond the source Neither source gives an ordered procedure. Gopen and Swan give seven expectations as a list, and Williams gives methods scattered through several lessons. The ordering argument in step 5, that most-consequential-first beats cheapest-first because structural repairs obviate local ones, is this lecture's, and so is the admission in step 5 that running all seven on every paragraph of a long document is unrealistic and that the honest practice is the first three always and the rest selectively.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that a procedure survives fatigue and a set of principles does not. You have twelve diagnostics and you will remember four of them at five o'clock on the day a paper is due. A fixed order, written down, gets applied; a body of understanding gets applied on good days.

The second is that the order itself prevents a specific and expensive mistake. Writers revising their own work start with the sentence they are unhappy with, which is nearly always a local problem, and work outwards only if the local repair fails. That produces documents in which every sentence has been worked over and the argument still does not land, because nobody asked what the section was for. Reversing the direction feels wrong and saves hours.

The third is that this is the last diagnostic lecture that operates below document scale, so it is worth knowing exactly where its ceiling is. Everything here works within a passage, meaning a paragraph or a run of a few paragraphs. None of it can tell you that section 4 should have been section 2, or that a twelve-page paper needed three pages. Module 4 handles that, and the boundary between the two is worth respecting: if a document's architecture is wrong, this checklist will happily certify each of its paragraphs.

Step 3

The mental model

Seven checks, in order, with the reader expectation each discharges and the reason each blocks the next.

The note under check 5 is a real trap worth naming. When two sentences do not connect, the symptom is the same whether the cause is a missing antecedent or a missing connective, and the repairs are different: one supplies a referent, the other names a relation. Running check 4 first separates them, and running 5 first leads you to insert a connective where the actual problem was that this pointed at nothing.

Step 4

The detail

The page

THE PASSAGE (145 words, as received) "The purpose of this section is to set out the findings of the annual review of the downturn LGD calibration for the residential mortgage portfolio. A number of considerations were taken into account. The stress window currently in use spans the period from 2011 to 2023. It should be noted that arrears in this period did not at any point exceed 1.4 per cent. The 2008 downturn saw peak arrears of 3.1 per cent. The benchmark test which is performed on an annual basis was passed by the calibration. This test utilises the same window. There are three drivers of the understatement which has been identified. The most significant of these relates to the exclusion referred to above. Remediation of the deficiency is required prior to the next submission of the capital return. The technology plan approved by the board in November provided for an upgrade to the origination system, which went live in June."

Checks 1 to 3: the structure

CHECK 1 TOPIC STRING the purpose of this section a number of considerations the stress window it (anticipatory; refers to nothing) the 2008 downturn the benchmark test this test there (existential; refers to nothing) the most significant (of what? antecedent two back) remediation the technology plan Eleven topics in eleven sentences. Two refer to nothing. The last belongs to a different story entirely. Can you name the set? Only as "things mentioned here". RESULT: FAIL. CHECK 2 STATE THE POINT IN ONE CLAUSE "This passage ______." Attempt: "...reports that the calibration understates the peak loss rate, and that the benchmark test did not catch it, and that remediation is needed, and something about a technology plan." Three "and"s and a trailing fragment. RESULT: FAIL. STOP CONDITION REACHED. Do not proceed to checks 4 to 7. DECISIONS REQUIRED BEFORE REWRITING Hypertheme: the downturn calibration and its stress window. Point: the calibration understates the peak loss rate because the window excludes 2008, and the benchmark test cannot detect this. Out of set: the technology plan sentence. It fails set membership. If it matters, it is a separate paragraph with its own point; it is not a sentence in this one. Note what check 2 exposed that reading did not. The passage never states the understatement as a claim. It refers to "the understatement which has been identified" as though already established, and to "the exclusion referred to above" when no exclusion was ever stated. The argument is assumed rather than made. CHECK 3 LOCATE THE POINT SENTENCE In the original: nowhere. Restart from the decisions above.

Checks 4 to 7, on the rewrite

REWRITTEN (118 words) "This section reports the annual review of the downturn LGD calibration for the residential mortgage book. The calibration understates the peak loss rate, because its stress window excludes the 2008 downturn. The window spans 2011 to 2023, a period in which arrears never exceeded 1.4 per cent. In the 2008 downturn they reached 3.1 per cent. The window therefore contains no severe outcome, so the calibration produces a downturn estimate close to the long-run average. Even so, the calibration passed the annual benchmark test, because that benchmark uses the same window. No other control would have caught the defect. The model owner must recalibrate against a window that includes 2008 before the next capital return." CHECK 1 Topic string this section / the calibration / the window / they (arrears) / the window / the calibration / no other control / the model owner Set: the calibration, its window, and who must fix it. PASS. CHECK 2 "This passage reports that the calibration understates the peak loss rate because its window excludes 2008." One clause. PASS. CHECK 3 Point sentence is the second sentence, at the end of the issue. PASS. CHECK 4 Sentence openings "This section..." opens the passage "The calibration..." given: named in S1 "The window..." given: named in S2 "In the 2008 downturn..." given: named in S2 "The window therefore..." given "Even so, the calibration..." given "No other control..." given: the test in S6 "The model owner..." new, and licensed: this is the closing paragraph and the owner is the news PASS. CHECK 5 Because-but-so, join by join S2-S3 "so" fits. Default. Unmarked. Correct. S3-S4 contrast between periods. Marked by "In the 2008 downturn", which fronts the shift. Adequate. S4-S5 "therefore" present and needed: the causal step from two arrears figures to "no severe outcome" is an inference, not a restatement. S5-S6 CONCESSION. Marked with "Even so". This is the join the original got wrong: unmarked, the reader reads the passed test as reassurance. S6-S7 backward cause, marked with "because". PASS. CHECK 6 Characters and actions Prepositions: 11 (was 24). "of" count: 2 (was 9). be-verbs: 1 of 9 finite verbs (was 6 of 11). Verbs now carrying actions: understates, excludes, spans, exceeded, reached, contains, produces, passed, uses, caught, must recalibrate. PASS. CHECK 7 Gaps and stress positions Largest subject-verb gap: 2 words (was 8, in "the benchmark test which is performed on an annual basis was passed"). Stress positions, read aloud: "...excludes the 2008 downturn." the cause. good. "...close to the long-run average." the consequence. good. "...uses the same window." the reason the control failed. good. "...before the next capital return." a DEADLINE in the spotlight, where the obligation should be. ONE FAILURE FOUND. Repair, per module 2 lecture 7: "Before the next capital return, the model owner must recalibrate against a window that includes 2008." Now the emphasis falls on the obligation. FINAL: 145 words to 118, four paragraphs from one, every Gopen and Swan expectation discharged, and one sentence deleted for failing set membership.

Three observations about that run. The stop condition did real work: checks 1 and 2 failed, and no amount of sentence-level repair would have produced the rewrite, because the rewrite required deciding a point the original never stated. Check 5 caught the concession that the original left unmarked, which was the one defect that would have made a reader draw the opposite conclusion. And check 7, the last and cheapest, still found something, which is the argument for running the whole list rather than stopping when it starts feeling pedantic.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Most-consequential-first, against the obvious alternative

There is a respectable argument for the opposite order. Sentence-level checks are cheap, mechanical and fast; structural checks are slow and require decisions. Cheapest-first is the standard heuristic in most diagnostic work, and it has the advantage that you make visible progress immediately.

It is wrong here for one reason: the repairs are not independent. Fixing a sentence in a paragraph you are about to delete is pure waste, and fixing a sentence whose subject is about to change because you chose a different topic string is worse than waste, because you will have to undo it. Structural decisions propagate downwards and local ones do not propagate at all. So the ordering follows the dependency graph rather than the cost.

The exception is worth stating because it is common. When you are reviewing someone else's document and cannot rewrite it, only comment on it, cheapest-first is defensible, because your output is a list of observations rather than a revised text and nothing is wasted. Even then, leading a review with sentence-level notes on a passage that has no point is a disservice, since the author will fix the sentences.

What the honest practice looks like

Running seven checks on every paragraph of a forty-page document is not going to happen, and a lecture that pretended otherwise would be giving advice nobody follows. The realistic version has three tiers.

Checks 1 to 3 on every section, always. They are fast, they catch the failures that matter most, and a section that fails them is a section to redesign rather than to edit. For a forty-page document this is perhaps ninety minutes and it is the highest-value ninety minutes available.

Checks 4 and 5 on the passages that carry your argument: the executive summary, the conclusions, the two or three paragraphs a decision will actually rest on. These are the places where an unmarked concession or a broken antecedent changes what the reader concludes.

Checks 6 and 7 on whatever you have time for, and on anything a reviewer has flagged as hard to read. This is the tier that most writers do first and exclusively, and the argument of this lecture is that it should be last and partial.

The ceiling

Every check in this lecture operates within a passage, and none of them can see across one. That has a specific consequence worth being clear about: a document can pass all seven checks on every paragraph and still be badly organised, because the checks never ask whether a paragraph belongs where it sits or whether the document needed it.

The technology plan sentence in step 4 makes the point in miniature. Check 1 correctly identified it as failing set membership and the right move was to remove it from the paragraph. What check 1 could not say is whether it belongs anywhere in the document, and that is not a defect in the check but a boundary. Module 4 asks the question this module cannot: given a set of things you must say, what shape should the document be?

Common failure Treating the checklist as a certification. Every check here is necessary and none is sufficient, and the passage in step 4 could be rewritten to pass all seven while asserting something false about the calibration. Module 2's lecture 8 gave the same warning about the Paramedic Method and it is worth repeating at passage scale, because a checklist creates a feeling of completion that is unrelated to whether the content is right.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Run all seven checks, in order and with written output at each, on a 120 to 180 word passage from a document you did not write. Report where the stop condition fired if it did, and which check found the defect you consider most serious.
  2. List the seven checks in order and name the Gopen and Swan expectation each discharges.
  3. State the stop condition and explain what it protects you from.
  4. Why must check 4 precede check 5? Give the symptom that looks identical under both failures and the two different repairs.
  5. Give the argument for cheapest-first ordering, then the argument that defeats it. What is the one situation in which cheapest-first is defensible?
  6. Describe the three-tier honest practice. Which tier do most writers do first, and where does this lecture put it?
  7. In the step 4 run, check 2 exposed something reading had not. What was it, and why does a one-clause statement of the point catch it?
  8. Check 1 correctly removed the technology plan sentence from the paragraph. What question could it not answer, and which module answers it?
