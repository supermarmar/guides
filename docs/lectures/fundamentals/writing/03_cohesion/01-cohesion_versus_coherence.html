Cohesion versus coherence

Two properties that everyone conflates and that vary independently. A paragraph can flow perfectly from sentence to sentence and be about nothing at all, and the cure for that is the opposite of the cure for a paragraph that jolts.

Lecture 1 of 7 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: Halliday & Hasan (1976); Williams, Style, lessons 4 and 5
Step 1

The 30-second version

Cohesion is the local property that each sentence connects to the one before it. It is created by devices on the page: a repeated word, a pronoun, a demonstrative, a connective. Coherence is the global property that a passage is about something and arrives somewhere. It is created by the subjects sharing a topic and by the passage having a point.

They are independent, and that is the whole lecture. All four combinations occur, and two of the four are common in professional writing. The dangerous one is cohesive but incoherent: every sentence links neatly to its predecessor, the prose reads smoothly, and the paragraph has walked from a credit problem to a board meeting without anyone noticing. A chain of valid links can lead anywhere, which is precisely the problem with a chain.

The practical consequence is that the two need opposite treatments, and applying the wrong one makes things worse. A jolting paragraph needs links, so you supply antecedents and connectives. A drifting paragraph needs a topic and a point, and supplying links to it makes it smoother and therefore harder to notice as broken. If you have ever added however and moreover to a paragraph and felt it improve without knowing why you were unsatisfied, that is what happened.

Feynman test Cohesion is whether each carriage is coupled to the next. Coherence is whether the train is going anywhere. You can couple twenty carriages perfectly and roll gently into a siding, and everyone on board will report a smooth journey. Checking the couplings tells you nothing about the destination, and most writing advice is about couplings.
What is beyond the source Halliday and Hasan supply the taxonomy of cohesive devices and treat coherence as largely outside their scope; Williams treats both and does not sharply separate the diagnoses. The claim here that the two failures need opposite treatments, and that treating a drift as a cohesion problem actively conceals it, is this lecture's framing. The consequence in step 5, that reading aloud detects cohesion failures and is blind to coherence failures, follows from it and is worth knowing because it limits the module 2 toolkit in a specific way.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is diagnostic economy. If you cannot tell which of the two properties has failed, you cannot choose a repair, so you will do what writers do by default, which is to fiddle with the sentences. Module 2 gave you seven ways to fiddle with sentences productively, and none of them touches either property in this module, because both are relations between sentences and module 2 never looked at a relation.

The second reason is that incoherence is invisible to the writer in a way that non-cohesion is not. A jolt is audible: you read the passage and something catches. Drift is not audible, because each transition is locally fine and you already know where the paragraph was going, so you supply the missing purpose without noticing. This is the curse of knowledge arriving at passage scale, and it means the coherence check has to be mechanical rather than impressionistic.

The third reason is specific to institutional documents, and it is the reason this distinction matters more here than in general writing instruction. A great deal of professional prose is written by accretion: a section is drafted, then a paragraph is added to answer a query, then another to cover a risk somebody raised. Each addition is attached to what preceded it, so cohesion survives. Nothing checks whether the section still has one topic, so coherence degrades with every edit. The characteristic result is a well-linked paragraph that answers four questions and states no position, and the process that produced it guarantees that shape.

And a reason for the reader on the other side. Readers work hard to impose coherence on prose that lacks it, constructing a purpose for the paragraph out of whatever is available. That effort is largely invisible to them, so their feedback will be that the section was "a bit dense" rather than that it had no point. You will not be told about a coherence failure. You have to find it.

Step 3

The mental model

Two properties, two states each, four quadrants. Below is the same opening sentence developed four ways, one for each quadrant, so the difference is not a definition but something you can see.

That last observation in the caption is the mechanism of the dangerous quadrant, and it is worth stating separately. Cohesion is created by picking up the previous sentence's content, so the writer picks up whatever was last, and whatever was last is not necessarily on topic. A chain built by always attaching to the most recent item will follow the most recent item wherever it goes. Coherence requires attaching to the paragraph's topic instead, which is a different and less automatic thing.

Cohesion
The property that consecutive sentences are linked by devices on the page. Local, checkable one join at a time, and audible: you can hear its absence when you read aloud.
Coherence
The property that a passage has a single topic and arrives at a point. Global, checkable only by looking at the whole passage, and inaudible: reading aloud will not detect its absence.
Drift
The characteristic failure of the cohesive but incoherent paragraph: each sentence attaches to the previous one's most recent content rather than to the paragraph's topic, so the topic migrates without any single join looking wrong.
Step 4

The detail

The five cohesive devices

Halliday and Hasan's taxonomy is worth knowing because it tells you what your options are when a join needs repairing. English offers five, and professional writers habitually use two of them and neglect the other three.

DeviceHow it worksNote
After Halliday and Hasan (1976)
Reference A pronoun or demonstrative points back: it, they, this, that, those. The cheapest device and the easiest to break. A this with no clear antecedent is the commonest cohesion failure in technical writing.
Substitution A placeholder replaces a repeated element: "the retail model failed; the wholesale one did not." Under-used in professional prose, which tends to repeat the full noun phrase instead and reads as heavy for it.
Ellipsis The repeated element is simply omitted: "the retail model failed; the wholesale model did not [fail]." Also under-used. Both substitution and ellipsis are how English avoids the deadening repetition that a defined-term discipline otherwise forces.
Conjunction A connective names the relation: however, therefore, in addition, by contrast. Over-used as a substitute for the other four, and simultaneously under-supplied where a genuine logical relation needs naming. Lecture 6 handles both failures.
Lexical cohesion Repeating a word, or using a related one from the same field: calibration, recalibrate, stress window, downturn. The workhorse in technical writing, and the one a terms-of-art discipline is obliged to use, since module 1's lecture 2 forbids varying a defined term for elegance.

The pattern in the right-hand column is worth naming. Technical writers lean on lexical cohesion because their vocabulary is fixed, and on conjunction because it is conspicuous and feels like signposting. They neglect substitution and ellipsis, which are the two devices that let you refer back without repeating, and the result is prose in which every sentence restates the full noun phrase. That is not a cohesion failure; the joins hold. It is a weight problem created by using one device for a job three could share.

The two checks, run separately

CHECK 1: COHESION (one join at a time) Read the first six or seven words of each sentence. Does each opening contain something the reader already has from the previous sentence? Sentence 1: [nothing required, it opens the passage] Sentence 2: "That deterioration ..." -> yes, from S1 Sentence 3: "Those valuations ..." -> yes, from S2 Sentence 4: "The assumption ..." -> yes, from S3 A sentence whose opening contains nothing from before is a jolt. It may be a deliberate topic shift, which is fine at a paragraph boundary and jarring mid-paragraph. CHECK 2: COHERENCE (the whole passage at once) Two questions, and both must pass. (a) List the grammatical subjects in order. Do they form a recognisable set? Good: book -> deterioration -> valuations -> assumption All belong to one story about a credit portfolio. Drift: book -> June -> system -> plan Four items, each linked to the last, belonging to no single story. (b) Can you state the paragraph's point in one sentence using only material the paragraph contains? Good: "The 2022 vintage's valuation fall has made the LGD assumption stale." Drift: [cannot be done. There is no claim.] WHY RUN THEM SEPARATELY Check 1 passes on the drift paragraph. Check 2 fails on it. If you run only check 1, which is what reading aloud amounts to, you will certify the worst quadrant as fine.

Why adding connectives conceals a drift

Take the drift paragraph and do what a writer dissatisfied with it usually does, which is to add signposting. "The mortgage book has deteriorated since June. Notably, June was also when the new origination system went live. This system, moreover, was procured under the 2023 technology plan, which in turn was approved by the board in November."

It now reads more confidently. Nothing has been fixed. The connectives assert relations that do not exist: notably claims the coincidence of dates is significant without arguing it, moreover claims the procurement adds to a case that has not been made, and in turn claims a chain of consequence between a technology plan and a credit deterioration. Each one is a promise the paragraph does not keep, and the reader either accepts the promises, in which case they have been misled, or notices, in which case the writer has lost credibility.

This gives a diagnostic worth having. A paragraph with a connective at the start of most of its sentences is more likely to be drifting than to be tightly argued. Genuinely coherent prose needs fewer connectives, because the topic does the work: when consecutive sentences are about the same thing, the reader does not need to be told they are related. Lecture 6 will argue the opposite case as well, that connectives are more often under-supplied than over-supplied, and both are true because they apply to different failures. Under-supplied where a real logical relation is left implicit; over-supplied where no relation exists to name.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Reading aloud is blind to the failure that matters

Module 2's lecture 8 gave reading aloud as the eighth step of the Paramedic Method, and it is a genuinely good detector. It is worth being precise about what it detects, because the answer bounds the whole module 2 toolkit.

Reading aloud detects cohesion failures reliably, because a missing antecedent produces an audible stumble: your voice reaches this and has nowhere to point. It detects rhythm failures, which is what Lanham intended. It detects an empty stress position, because your voice cannot find an emphasis. What it cannot detect is drift, and the reason is structural rather than a matter of insufficient care. Your voice processes one sentence at a time, and drift is a property of the sequence. A smooth chain reads smoothly aloud precisely because each join is sound.

So the coherence check has to be visual and mechanical: extract the subjects into a list, look at the list. That is the only method in this course that requires you to stop reading the prose in order to examine it, and the requirement is not an inconvenience. It follows from the fact that the property being checked does not exist at any point in the text, only across it.

Coherence is not a writing problem

The uncomfortable conclusion of this lecture, and the one that connects it to module 9, is that you cannot edit a paragraph into coherence. Cohesion is repairable by technique: supply the antecedent, name the relation, and the join holds. Coherence requires a topic and a point, and if the paragraph has neither, no amount of rearrangement will produce them, because they are not properties of the prose. They are properties of what you decided to say.

This is the same claim module 2 made about sentences you cannot fix, scaled up, and it holds more strongly at paragraph level. A drifting paragraph is a paragraph whose purpose was never settled, usually because it was written by accretion in the way step 2 described. The repair is to decide what the paragraph is for, discard what does not serve that, and write it again. Editing is the wrong operation and it feels like the right one, which is why drifting paragraphs survive so many rounds of review.

There is a version of the accretion problem worth flagging as unavoidable rather than a failure. Sometimes a document genuinely has to answer four unrelated queries, and the honest structure is four paragraphs, or four subsections, each with its own topic. The failure is not covering four things; it is covering four things in one paragraph and linking them, which produces the appearance of an argument where there is a list. Module 4 handles the architecture of a document that genuinely has several jobs.

Common failure Concluding that cohesion does not matter because coherence matters more. Both are needed and the coherent but non-cohesive quadrant is a real defect: a reader who has to build every link themselves is spending attention on reconstruction rather than on your argument, and by the fourth sentence some of them have stopped. The claim here is that the two are independent and need different repairs, not that one is a substitute for the other.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take one opening sentence from your own work and develop it into all four quadrants: cohesive and coherent, cohesive and drifting, coherent and jolting, and neither. The drifting one is the exercise that teaches, so make it genuinely smooth. Then state each quadrant's cure.
  2. Define cohesion and coherence, and say which one is audible and which is not.
  3. Explain the mechanism of drift: why does building cohesion by attaching to the previous sentence tend to move the topic?
  4. Name the five cohesive devices. Which two do technical writers over-use, which two do they neglect, and what does the neglect cost?
  5. Why does adding connectives to a drifting paragraph make it worse rather than better? Give one connective and state the promise it makes falsely.
  6. Lecture 6 will argue connectives are usually under-supplied. Reconcile that with step 4's argument that they are over-supplied.
  7. Why can reading aloud not detect drift? Answer in terms of where the property lives.
  8. Take a section of a document you did not write that has been through several rounds of review. Extract the subject list. Then try to state its point in one sentence using only its own material. Report honestly whether you could.
