MECE and the discipline of grouping

Mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive. The useful form of the idea is not the acronym but a diagnosis: overlap is never a property of your items, it is evidence that you cut on two dimensions at once. And real taxonomies, including the ones in Basel, are usually not strictly MECE at all.

Lecture 5 of 8 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: Minto, The Pyramid Principle; Basel II Annex 9
Step 1

The 30-second version

A group is mutually exclusive when no item belongs in two categories, and collectively exhaustive when every relevant thing belongs in one. Lecture 4's plural noun test checked that each member is an instance of the class. These two check the harder properties: that the members do not overlap, and that between them they cover the class.

The insight worth taking from this lecture is that overlap is never a defect in your items. It is evidence that you have cut on two dimensions at once. Split credit risk into retail, wholesale and IFRS 9 and the first two are a cut by counterparty while the third is a cut by accounting framework, so overlap is guaranteed before you look at a single exposure. Fix the dimension and the exclusivity follows for free, which means MECE is not a property to be checked item by item but a consequence of choosing one basis of division.

The second thing to take is a concession. Many real and successful taxonomies are not strictly MECE. Basel's seven operational risk event types cut on several different dimensions, and they work anyway, because their boundaries are settled by documented convention rather than by a clean division. That is a legitimate way to build a taxonomy and it costs something specific, which step 5 sets out.

Feynman test Sorting things into boxes only works if you decided in advance what question the boxes answer. Ask "what colour is it" and the boxes are red, blue, green, and everything lands in exactly one. Ask "what colour is it, and also is it heavy" and you have made boxes that overlap, and no amount of care about individual objects will fix it. Overlap is a fact about your question, not about your objects.
What is beyond the source Minto supplies the requirement and the failure modes. The dimensional diagnosis in step 3, which treats overlap as a symptom of a mixed basis of division rather than as a property to be inspected item by item, is this lecture's framing and it converts a check into a repair. The Basel example in step 5 and the distinction it supports, between strictly MECE and conventionally MECE, is also this lecture's, and it is the part that makes the idea usable in a field whose taxonomies were not built to satisfy it.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that in quantitative documents this stops being a writing question. If your four drivers of a shortfall are meant to sum to the shortfall, mutual exclusivity is arithmetic: overlap means double counting, and the numbers will not add up or, worse, will add up to the right total by cancelling errors. A great deal of attribution analysis in banking is a MECE problem wearing a spreadsheet, and the discipline is the same whether the output is a paragraph or a waterfall chart.

The second reason is that collective exhaustiveness is what licenses a reader to stop worrying. A set the reader believes is complete lets them accept the analysis; a set they suspect has gaps leaves them holding a question you never addressed. This is why the residual "other" category is so damaging out of proportion to its size: it is a written admission that the organising idea does not cover the ground, and a reader who sees it starts wondering what is in there.

The third reason is lecture 4's robustness argument, and the check it needs. That lecture recommended inductive sets partly because three independent reasons survive losing one. That only holds if the reasons are genuinely independent, and a writer who has split one argument into three overlapping restatements has manufactured the appearance of robustness while adding nothing. Mutual exclusivity is the test that separates three reasons from one reason stated three ways.

And a reason about your own analysis rather than your writing. A group you cannot make MECE is usually a sign that you have not worked out what question you are answering. The failure surfaces as a presentational problem and originates as an analytical one, which is the same relationship module 2 identified between tangled sentences and undecided thoughts.

Step 3

The mental model

The top panel is the dimensional diagnosis. The three beneath are the failure modes, each with the repair that follows from it.

The regrouping repair in the third panel is worth stating as a general habit, because it is the one that improves documents most. A list of seven is almost never seven parallel things: it is two or three groups whose organising idea has not been articulated. Finding it is analytical work rather than presentational, and the payoff is that the reader gets three things they can hold instead of seven they cannot.

Mutually exclusive
No item belongs in two categories. Achieved by cutting on a single dimension, not by adjudicating individual items. Overlap is therefore a diagnosis of the dimension.
Collectively exhaustive
Everything within a stated scope belongs to some category. Always relative to a scope, which is why the scope must be stated: exhaustive of what?
Basis of division
The dimension you are cutting on: by cause, by counterparty, by time, by severity, by owner. A group has one. Two bases in one group produce overlap before any item is examined.
Conventionally MECE
A taxonomy cut on several dimensions whose boundaries are fixed by documented convention rather than by a clean division. Most real regulatory taxonomies are this, and it works at a cost step 5 sets out.
Step 4

The detail

Naming the dimension, which is the whole method

THE DIMENSION TEST For any group, complete this sentence: "These are the categories of ______, divided by ______." If you cannot fill the second blank with a single thing, you have found the problem and you already know the repair. WORKED EXAMPLES GOOD "These are the categories of exposure, divided by credit deterioration since origination." -> stage 1, 2, 3. One dimension. Boundaries defined. Exclusive by construction, exhaustive within the scope of exposures in the standard's scope. "These are the drivers of the shortfall, divided by cause." -> migration, model change, new business. One dimension. Sums arithmetically. BAD "These are the categories of credit risk, divided by counterparty and by accounting framework." -> two dimensions. Repair: choose one for level 1 and use the other at level 2. "These are the findings, divided by ..." -> nothing fills the blank. "Findings" is not a divided class, it is a container. This is lecture 3's label problem reappearing as a grouping problem. THE SCOPE HALF, WHICH IS USUALLY OMITTED Collective exhaustiveness is meaningless without a scope. "Exhaustive" of what? "The three defects account for the whole of the 40 basis point understatement." -> scope stated. Checkable. If a fourth defect exists, the claim is false and can be shown false. "The three defects are the main issues." -> no scope. Unfalsifiable, and therefore worth nothing to the reader. Stating the scope is what converts a list into a claim.

Where the discipline is arithmetic

In any document that decomposes a quantity, the grouping rules stop being about prose. A shortfall attribution, a capital movement analysis, a profit and loss walk: each is an inductive group whose members are meant to sum to the parent. There, mutual exclusivity is the no-double-counting condition and collective exhaustiveness is the requirement that the components reconcile to the total.

Two consequences follow that are worth being explicit about. First, an "other" or "unexplained" line in a decomposition is the residual failure in its most visible form, and its size is the honest measure of how well you understand the movement. A three per cent residual is a rounding note; a thirty per cent residual means the organising idea is wrong and the other categories are not what is driving the number. Second, order matters more than in a prose group, because sequential attributions depend on the order in which effects are applied, and a document that does not state the order has not defined its own categories.

The general lesson is that when a group has to add up, MECE is not a writing convention that a careful author observes. It is a property the analysis either has or lacks, and the writing merely reveals which.

The long list, and how many siblings a reader holds

Minto's advice on lists of five or more is to look for a higher level of abstraction, and the underlying reason is about the reader rather than about elegance. A reader can hold two or three parallel items and use them; at seven they retain a subset, and you do not get to choose which subset. So a flat list of seven findings is a document that has delegated the selection of its own most important point to the reader's memory.

The regrouping is usually available and takes twenty minutes. Seven findings about a model are typically three about data quality, two about methodology and two about controls, and once grouped the reader holds three things and can navigate to the seventh if they want it. Note that nothing was cut: the tree got a level deeper, which is what regrouping means, and the same content is now retrievable rather than merely present.

The exception is a genuine enumeration where the items have no natural higher grouping and completeness is the point: a list of the fourteen exposures affected, or the seven Basel event types. There a flat list is correct, and the right presentation is a table rather than prose, which lecture 8 takes up. The distinction is whether the reader needs to hold the items or to look them up.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Basel's operational risk taxonomy is not MECE, and it works

The Basel framework classifies operational risk loss events into seven categories: internal fraud; external fraud; employment practices and workplace safety; clients, products and business practices; damage to physical assets; business disruption and system failures; and execution, delivery and process management. Anyone who has used them knows they are indispensable. It is worth noticing that they are not cut on a single dimension.

The first two divide by where the perpetrator sits. The third and fourth divide by who was harmed. The fifth divides by what kind of asset was affected. The sixth divides by which system failed. The seventh divides by which process stage went wrong, and it functions substantially as a residual: a large share of recorded losses in practice land there. By the test in step 4, this taxonomy has at least four bases of division and a residual category, and should therefore be unusable.

It is not unusable, and the reason is instructive. The boundaries are settled by convention, documented in detail, and applied consistently because everyone has agreed to apply them the same way. An internal fraud that also involved a process failure goes to internal fraud, not because the categories do not overlap but because a rule says so. That is what conventionally MECE means: exclusivity produced by agreement rather than by construction.

The cost is real and specific. A conventionally MECE taxonomy requires documentation, training and adjudication, because the boundaries are not derivable from the categories; someone has to be told where a mixed case goes. It does not survive being borrowed by someone who has not read the rules. And it cannot be used for causal analysis, because a category that mixes bases of division does not isolate a mechanism, which is why operational risk loss data is better for capital estimation than for telling you what to fix.

The lesson for a writer is a permission with a condition. You may use a conventionally MECE grouping when a shared taxonomy already exists and your readers know its boundary rules, and you gain the enormous benefit of borrowing a structure they already hold. You may not invent one, because a taxonomy with several bases of division and no documented conventions is simply overlap, and your reader will have to adjudicate every case themselves.

MECE as a test rather than a goal

Over-applying this produces its own failure, and it is worth naming because the acronym invites it. A writer determined to make every group MECE will invent distinctions that do not exist, split categories that were fine, and produce taxonomies whose tidiness is the only thing recommending them. The characteristic result is a paper with five perfectly exclusive categories, three of which contain nothing anyone cares about.

The honest position is that MECE is a diagnostic for groups you already have reason to believe in. When it fails, it has told you something: either the dimension is mixed, or the analysis is incomplete, or the grouping was never real. When it passes, it has told you nothing about whether the group was worth making. Lecture 3's question test is what establishes that, and the two together are what the plural noun test alone could not do.

Where the pyramid now stands

All three of Minto's rules are covered. Each level summarises the level beneath and answers the question above. Each group is a chain or a set and never both. Each set has one basis of division, does not overlap, and covers a stated scope, in the strict case, or borrows a documented convention, in the practical one.

What remains in the pyramid is the entry: how the reader gets from the top of the document into the top of the pyramid, which is what SCQA supplies and lecture 6 covers. After that the module leaves Minto for structures the pyramid cannot supply, and it is worth noting in advance that lecture 7's long-form material is a different tradition with different assumptions, not an extension of this one.

Common failure Using MECE as a review comment. "This isn't MECE" is correct, unhelpful, and slightly insufferable. The useful version names the dimension: "these three are causes and the fourth is a cohort, so which are we dividing by?" That comment can be acted on in a minute, and it locates the problem where the repair is rather than where the symptom is. The same applies to your own drafts: name the dimension rather than inspecting the items.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a group of four or more items from a document you wrote and run the dimension test: "these are the categories of ___, divided by ___." Report what you could not fill in, the repair, and whether your stated scope made the exhaustiveness claim falsifiable.
  2. Define mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive. Why is overlap a diagnosis of the dimension rather than a property of the items?
  3. Give the three failure modes and the repair for each. Which one is a written admission and what does it admit?
  4. Why is collective exhaustiveness meaningless without a scope? Give a scoped and an unscoped version of the same claim.
  5. In a quantitative decomposition, what do mutual exclusivity and collective exhaustiveness become? What does the size of an "unexplained" line tell you?
  6. Basel's seven operational risk event types have at least four bases of division and a residual. Explain why they work anyway, and name the three costs of a conventionally MECE taxonomy.
  7. State the permission and the condition that follow for a writer from the Basel example.
  8. Why is MECE a test rather than a goal? What does a passing group still not tell you, and which lecture supplies that?
