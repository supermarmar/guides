The opening as a contract

A reader opening your document holds nothing, so the first thing you owe them is the ground to stand on and the conclusion to attach to it. Once you have written an opening, the reader is entitled to four things, and each one you withhold costs something specific.

Lecture 1 of 8 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: Williams, Style, on global coherence; Minto, The Pyramid Principle
Step 1

The 30-second version

An opening does four jobs. It establishes shared context, the ground the reader and you both already stand on. It introduces a destabilising condition, the thing about that ground which is wrong, unknown or newly changed, and which is why the document exists. It states the point, meaning your conclusion. And it puts the key concepts into circulation, in the words you will use for them throughout.

Calling this a contract is not a metaphor for politeness. Once the opening exists, the reader proceeds on four assumptions: that the document answers the question the destabilising condition raised, that the point stated is the point argued, that the terms named are the terms used, and that nothing structurally important will arrive unforeshadowed. Each assumption is one you created, and breaking any of them imposes a cost the reader attributes to the document rather than to their own inference.

The reason this is module 4's first lecture rather than a note on introductions is that the opening determines the architecture. A point stated in the opening is a promise about what the sections must do; key concepts named in the opening are the topic strings the body must follow. Write the opening properly and the structure is largely decided. Write it as a table of contents in prose, which is the professional default, and the document has no structural commitments at all.

Feynman test Your reader is standing in a doorway with nothing in their hands. You have one paragraph to give them a floor to stand on, tell them what is wrong with it, and say what you are going to do about it. If instead you spend that paragraph telling them what order the rooms are in, they now know the layout of a building they still have no reason to enter.
What is beyond the source Williams supplies context, destabilising condition and point, and the observation that key concepts should appear at the end of the introduction and then recur. Minto supplies the answer-first argument. The contract framing, with four specific entitlements and a named cost for breaching each, is this lecture's, and so is the claim in step 5 that openings are written last. That last claim is practical rather than theoretical and it is the one most likely to change what you do tomorrow.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason follows directly from module 3. The given-new contract says readers attach new information to information they already hold. A reader opening a forty-page report holds nothing about it, so there is no antecedent for anything, and every sentence in the first paragraph is a bridge they must build unaided. The opening's job is to manufacture the given information that the whole document will attach to, and the point is the largest single piece of it. Everything after attaches to the conclusion, which is why stating it first is not a stylistic preference but the only way the rest of the document has anything to hook onto.

The second reason is that the alternative default is genuinely bad and genuinely universal. The professional opening states a purpose, describes a process, and lists the sections. Every element of that is about the document rather than about the subject, and none of it gives the reader anything to attach to. It is a table of contents written in sentences, and its persistence is explained by the fact that it can be written before you have done the work, which is exactly when people write openings.

The third is that an unstated point does not stay unstated. The reader constructs one, because they cannot read forty pages without a hypothesis about what they are for, and their construction will be built from whatever is most salient early on. So the choice is not between stating your conclusion and leaving it open. It is between stating your conclusion and having the reader invent one, then reading your evidence as support for theirs.

And a reason specific to documents that go to committees. A paper whose opening states its conclusion can be discussed. A paper whose opening states its purpose can only be received, and the discussion then happens over whatever the most senior person in the room decided it was about. If you want a decision made on your analysis rather than on somebody's summary of it, the opening is where that is won or lost.

Step 3

The mental model

The figure shows the four elements on the left, the key concepts threading into the body in the middle, and the four entitlements with their costs beneath.

The second entitlement deserves separating from the others because it is the one with consequences beyond irritation. A reader who has been given a conclusion in an opening reads the body as support for it, and remembers the conclusion rather than the support. So if your opening claims a 40 basis point understatement and your body establishes only that the window is short, you have put a number into your reader's memory that your evidence does not carry. That is not a writing failure. It is a misstatement that the architecture made easy.

Shared context
Ground the reader and writer both already occupy, stated so the document has an antecedent. Not background: background is everything you know, context is the specific thing this document departs from.
Destabilising condition
Williams's term for what is wrong with the shared context: something unknown, changed, contradicted or at risk. It is what makes the document necessary, and a document without one has no reason to exist.
Key concepts
The terms that will thread through the body, put into circulation in the opening in the exact words the body will use. Williams's advice is to place them at the end of the introduction, where the stress position gives them weight.
Step 4

The detail

The default opening, and what each of its parts is doing instead

THE PROFESSIONAL DEFAULT (68 words) "The purpose of this report is to document the annual review of the downturn LGD calibration for the residential mortgage portfolio. The review was conducted by the independent validation function during Q3 in accordance with the model risk policy. This report is structured as follows. Section 2 describes the methodology. Section 3 sets out the data. Section 4 presents the findings. Section 5 contains the recommendations." WHAT EACH SENTENCE DOES S1 states a purpose. Tells the reader the document exists, which they had inferred from holding it. S2 states a process and an authority. Establishes compliance, not content. S3 announces that a list follows. S4-7 a table of contents, in prose, which the actual table of contents already provides. ELEMENTS OF THE CONTRACT PRESENT Shared context absent Destabilising condition absent Point absent Key concepts "downturn LGD calibration" only WHY IT PERSISTS Every sentence can be written before the work is done. That is the whole explanation, and it is why openings written first look like this. WHAT THE READER DOES NEXT Constructs a hypothesis about the point from the section titles, which are the only content available. Section 4 is called "findings", so they turn to section 4, read the first finding they encounter, and treat that as the document's conclusion.
THE SAME REPORT, OPENING REWRITTEN (81 words) "The bank calibrates its downturn LGD for residential mortgages to a stress window spanning 2011 to 2023. Arrears in that window never exceeded 1.4 per cent, against a peak of 3.1 per cent in the 2008 downturn, so the window contains no severe outcome. The calibration therefore understates the peak loss rate by approximately 40 basis points, and the annual benchmark test did not detect this because the benchmark uses the same stress window. This report evidences both findings and recommends recalibration against a window that includes 2008 before the next capital return." CONTRACT Shared context sentence 1 Destabilising condition sentence 2 Point sentence 3, with a number Key concepts stress window, peak loss rate, benchmark test, recalibration Questions raised, and therefore promised: why is the window what it is? -> section 2 how large is the shortfall? -> section 3 why did no control catch it? -> section 4 what should be done? -> section 5 13 words longer than the default. It contains the entire argument, and it commits the author to four sections whose content is now determined. NOTE THE RISK IT CREATES "Approximately 40 basis points" is a number in the reader's memory from page one. If section 3 establishes only a range of 20 to 60, the opening has overstated. Answer-first structure raises the cost of overclaiming, which is a feature.

Where the point should not go first

Module 3's lecture 5 gave three cases for delaying a paragraph's point, and they scale up with one addition. The general answer-first default holds, and four situations genuinely qualify.

CaseWhy delay works at document scaleWhat still must go first
Legitimate at document scale
The conclusion will be rejected on sight A reader whose prior is strongly against your finding will read the evidence as an advocate. Building it first means each step is conceded before its destination is visible. Context and destabilising condition, always. Delaying the point is not licence to delay the frame.
The document presents options without recommending Some papers exist to lay out a choice for a body that will make it. Recommending would exceed the author's remit, and a stated point would prejudge. The question, explicitly, and the criteria by which the options will be compared. A genuine options paper still commits to those.
The conclusion is unintelligible without its terms Rare at document scale, because an opening can state a point loosely and the body can refine it. A loose version of the point. "The calibration is not conservative" costs nothing and orients the reader.
The genre forbids it Some formats are prescribed: a regulatory return, a standard-form legal opinion, a journal article with a fixed structure. You do not get to redesign them. Whatever the format permits. Most prescribed formats have an abstract or summary field that will take a point.
Not legitimate, however it is described
"I want the reader to follow the analysis" This is the drafting artefact from module 3's lecture 5, at document scale. The reader does not want your route. If they need to check the derivation, they will read section 3. Everything. This is the case that produces the default opening in the worked example above.

Summary against opening

An executive summary and an opening are different documents doing different jobs, and conflating them is why so many executive summaries are useless. A summary is a compressed version of the whole: it restates each section's conclusion in proportion, and it is read instead of the document. An opening frames and commits: it states the point once and raises the questions the body will answer, and it is read before the document.

The practical consequence is that a good summary makes a poor opening and vice versa. A summary that gives equal weight to five sections does not tell the reader which finding matters, and an opening that states one point does not serve a reader who will read nothing else. If a document needs both, write both, and write the summary last, from the finished sections, since a summary of a document you have not written is a set of intentions.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Openings are written last

This is the most practically consequential claim in the lecture and the one most likely to be resisted, because writing the opening first feels like the natural order and produces the immediate relief of having started.

The argument against it is simple. Three of the four contract elements cannot be settled in advance. You cannot state a point you have not established. You cannot name the key concepts until you know which ones the body actually needed, and drafting reliably changes that list. And you cannot know which questions the point raises until the point is fixed. Only the shared context is stable at the outset, and it is the element that matters least.

So the opening you write first is a set of intentions, and the professional default opening is exactly what a set of intentions looks like when written down: a purpose, a process, a list of sections. That explains its persistence without appealing to laziness. It is not a bad opening carelessly written; it is a faithful record of what its author knew when they wrote it, which was the plan rather than the findings.

The practical procedure is to write a placeholder, draft the body, and then write the opening properly from what the body established. Module 9 gives the evidence that expert writers restructure late where novices polish early, and this is the largest single instance of it: the opening is the part of the document most improved by being written after everything else, and most commonly written before.

What the contract framing gets you that a checklist does not

Four elements could have been presented as a checklist and the reason to frame them as entitlements is that entitlements have costs attached, and costs let you decide. A checklist tells you to include a destabilising condition. The contract tells you that omitting it means the reader has no reason to read on, which is a claim you can weigh against the situation. Sometimes you will decide the reader's motivation is not in doubt and omit it deliberately, and that is a legitimate call that a checklist could not license.

The framing also locates responsibility correctly, which matters for the third and fourth entitlements. When section 3 renames the stress window, the reader's confusion is not carelessness on their part; it is a reasonable inference from a signal you sent. When section 4 introduces an unforeshadowed finding, the reader's need to rebuild their frame is a cost you imposed. Module 1's lecture 1 argued that a document which is misread is by default your problem, and the contract is the mechanism by which that becomes concrete rather than pious.

The tension with honesty, stated plainly

Answer-first structure has a cost that the business writing literature does not much discuss. Stating a conclusion in an opening commits you before the reader has seen your evidence, and it makes the conclusion the thing they remember. Both of those are precisely why it works, and both make overclaiming easier and more damaging.

The worked example flagged this. "Approximately 40 basis points" in the opening is a number the reader carries from page one, and if the body supports only a wide range, the architecture has helped you mislead. The discipline that answers this is to write the opening from the body, which is the same discipline step 5 recommended for a different reason: an opening written after the evidence can only claim what the evidence carries. Written before, it claims what you expected to find.

Common failure Adding the four elements to an existing default opening rather than replacing it. The result is a paragraph that states the context, the condition and the point, and then explains that section 2 describes the methodology. The section list is not a fifth element; it is what you write instead of the four, and once you have the four it is redundant with the table of contents. Delete it.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take the opening of a document you wrote and label which of the four elements are present. Then rewrite it with all four, list the key concepts, and write down the questions your point now obliges the body to answer. Check whether the document actually answers them.
  2. Name the four elements and the four entitlements. Which entitlement is most dangerous to breach in a paper supporting a decision, and why?
  3. Why does the given-new contract make answer-first structure a necessity rather than a preference?
  4. Explain why the professional default opening persists, in terms of when it can be written rather than in terms of its author's care.
  5. An unstated point does not stay unstated. What does the reader do instead, and what do they then do with your evidence?
  6. Give the four legitimate cases for delaying the point at document scale. In each, what must still appear first?
  7. Distinguish a summary from an opening. Why does a good one make a poor other, and when should each be written?
  8. Step 5 concedes that answer-first structure makes overclaiming easier and more damaging. State the mechanism, and the discipline that answers it.
