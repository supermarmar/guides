Headings, lists, tables and the visual layer

The layer most writers treat as formatting carries meaning, and one of its devices is a machine for deleting logical relations. A bulleted list strips out exactly the connectives that module 3 established must be marked, which is why so many bullets destroy the argument they were meant to clarify.

Lecture 8 of 8 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: Schriver, Dynamics in Document Design (1997); Redish (2007); Tufte (2003)
Step 1

The 30-second version

The visual layer is not a presentation of the meaning; it is part of it. Karen Schriver's argument is that prose and visual elements stand in identifiable relations to each other, and that a document's design either carries those relations or breaks them. Janice Redish adds the reader's side: professional readers scan, and the visual layer is what they scan, so it is where most of your communication actually happens.

Three practical claims follow. Headings must be assertions, from lecture 3, and their levels must be visually distinguishable and no more than three deep, from lecture 2. A table beats prose whenever the content has two dimensions, because prose is linear and a table is not. And a bulleted list should be used only where the relation between items genuinely is co-ordination, because bullets delete every other relation.

That last claim is the strongest thing in the lecture and it follows directly from module 3. Bullets present items as parallel and independent. Module 3's lecture 6 established that readers impose a default relation when none is marked, and that departures must be marked or they are misread. A bulleted list marks nothing, so every contrast, concession and backward-running cause among its items has been deleted, and the reader will supply co-ordination instead.

Feynman test A bulleted list is a sentence with all the joining words removed. Say out loud: the window excludes 2008, and the arrears were low, and the test passed, and the test uses the same window. Four facts, all true, joined by nothing. Now say it properly: the test passed even though the window excludes 2008, because the test uses the same window. Same four facts, opposite conclusion, and the difference is entirely in the two words the bullets threw away.
What is beyond the source Schriver and Redish argue that design carries meaning; Tufte attacks the bulleted list on grounds of information density. The specific argument here, that bullets are a mechanism for deleting coherence relations and therefore inherit module 3's finding that an unmarked departure produces a misreading rather than a delay, is this lecture's, and it is a sharper charge than low information density. It says that bulleting a concession does not merely lose emphasis; it reverses the meaning.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this layer is where the argument goes to die in most organisations, and it dies by process rather than by decision. A template supplies the heading structure, a house convention supplies the bullets, and both are treated as formatting to be applied after the thinking. So the layer that most readers actually consume is the only layer nobody has taken responsibility for.

The second reason is the bullet argument, which has a specific and checkable consequence. If a bulleted list has deleted a concession, the reader draws the opposite conclusion from the one you hold, and they will not know and neither will you. That is not a stylistic cost. It is the same failure module 3 called the most expensive in the course, delivered by a formatting choice.

The third reason is that bullets conceal the structural failures the previous lectures taught you to find. A flat list of seven bullets looks orderly, and lecture 5 established that seven siblings usually means no organising idea has been found. In prose, seven parallel clauses are obviously unmanageable. In bullets they look like thoroughness, so the visual layer is actively hiding a grouping failure that a paragraph would have exposed.

And a reason about tables, which is the positive half. A great deal of professional content is two-dimensional: portfolios by metric, risks by control, scenarios by outcome, requirements by responsible party. Prose renders two dimensions badly because it must pick one to iterate and hold the other, and the reader has to reconstruct the grid. Recognising two-dimensional content and putting it in a table is one of the cheapest large improvements available to a technical writer.

The mental model

The top panel is the bullet demonstration. The lower two are the dimensional test for tables.

The note under the table is worth reading as an instruction rather than an aside. A table displays a grid and does not say what the grid means, so a table without a sentence stating its finding has delegated the conclusion to the reader. The pattern that works is a sentence making the claim, the table supporting it, and a caption that repeats the claim rather than describing the contents, which is Schriver's complementary relation: neither element is sufficient alone.

The visual layer
Headings, lists, tables, spacing, emphasis, captions. Not a presentation of meaning applied after the writing, but a carrier of meaning in its own right, and the layer scanning readers consume.
The dimensional test
Count the dimensions of your content. One dimension of related items is prose, one dimension of independent items is a list, two dimensions is a table. Prose renders two dimensions badly because it must iterate one and hold the other.
Relation deletion
What a bulleted list does to the connectives between its items. Since readers default to co-ordination when nothing is marked, every contrast, concession and backward cause among the bullets is not merely unstated but actively misread.
Step 4

The detail

When bullets are correct

The case against bullets is narrow, not general, and it follows exactly from module 3's taxonomy: bullets are right when the relation among items genuinely is co-ordination, and wrong otherwise. That gives a usable test with a short list of legitimate uses.

ContentBullets?Why
Bullets are correct
A genuine enumeration where order is arbitrary Yes The entities affected, the documents reviewed, the people consulted. The relation really is co-ordination, and a reader looks these up rather than following them.
Parallel requirements or criteria Yes Each must be satisfied independently, so co-ordination is the relation. Note that "each independently" should be stated, because bullets do not say whether the list is conjunctive or disjunctive.
A checklist meant to be worked through Yes The visual form matches the use: the reader ticks items rather than reading a claim.
Bullets destroy the content
An argument with a concession in it No The concession is deleted and the reader reverses your meaning. This is the case in the figure and the most damaging of the four.
A causal chain No Bullets present dependent steps as parallel items, which is lecture 4's mixed group rendered typographically. The reader cannot follow a chain laid out as a set.
Items of unequal weight No Bullets assert parallelism, so a list containing one major and three minor findings has told the reader they are comparable. Prose can subordinate; bullets cannot.
Anything you want emphasised No Each bullet ends, so each has a weak stress position and none has a strong one. Emphasis is diffused across the list, which is module 2's lecture 7 read backwards.

The unequal-weight row is the failure most likely to be present in your own last document. A findings list of four bullets, one of which is a forty basis point capital understatement and three of which are documentation gaps, has told the reader those are four comparable items. Prose would have subordinated the three, and the bullet form makes subordination unavailable, so the visual layer has flattened a judgement you had already made.

Headings, and the three-level limit

THE HEADING LAYER, THREE REQUIREMENTS 1. ASSERTIONS, not labels. (lecture 3) Test: read the headings alone. Do you get the argument? 2. VISUALLY RANKED, unambiguously. A reader must be able to tell a level 2 from a level 3 without counting numbers. If the only difference between your heading levels is the numbering, the hierarchy is not visible, it is merely recorded. Adequate: size, weight and space above, differing clearly at each level. Inadequate: all headings bold, same size, distinguished only by 4.2 against 4.2.1. 3. AT MOST THREE LEVELS. (lecture 2) Four levels exceeds what a reader will hold, and the numbering becomes an index rather than a structure. If you have four, one of two things is true: - the document should be two documents, or - the fourth level should be prose, introduced by a bold lead-in phrase rather than a numbered heading. THE BOLD LEAD-IN, which is the fix nobody uses Instead of a level-4 heading, open the paragraph with a bolded phrase: "Recovery lag assumptions. The current model sets recovery lags to the benign-period mean of 14 months, which understates..." This gives the reader a scannable entry point without adding a hierarchy level, and Redish's work on scanning readers suggests entry points are what they are looking for rather than depth.

Schriver's relations, and what a caption is for

Schriver's analysis of how prose and visual elements relate identifies several distinct relationships, and the useful ones for professional documents are three. In a redundant relation the visual repeats the prose, which is legitimate for emphasis and wasteful otherwise. In a complementary relation both are necessary and neither is sufficient, which is the target for most tables and figures. In a supplementary relation one dominates and the other supports, which describes a table of detail beneath a claim.

That taxonomy converts the caption question into an easy one. If the relation is complementary, the caption must say what the visual establishes, because the prose cannot carry it alone. If the relation is supplementary, the caption can describe the contents, because the claim is already made in the prose. What is never useful is a caption that names the visual, of the "Table 3: portfolio metrics" variety, because it repeats what the reader can see and delegates the finding to them.

The practical rule is to caption the conclusion rather than the contents. "Unsecured LGD is three times mortgage LGD, which is why the coverage gap is concentrated there" is a caption. "Portfolio metrics by segment" is a label, and lecture 3 already established what a label is worth.

White space and grouping

Visual proximity signals grouping, and the signal is strong enough to override content. Items placed close together read as belonging together, and a gap reads as a boundary, which means paragraph spacing and indentation are carrying structural claims whether or not you intended them.

Two consequences worth acting on. If a table's rows are grouped conceptually, the grouping should be visible through spacing or a banded row, because a reader who cannot see the groups will not infer them from the labels. And if two paragraphs belong together as one point with its evidence, the spacing between them should be smaller than the spacing to the next point, which most templates get wrong by applying uniform paragraph spacing. Uniform spacing asserts that every boundary is equally strong, which is the same flattening the bullet form performs.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The bullet is not a formatting choice

Tufte's attack on presentation software rests on information density: a slide of bullets carries a few dozen words where a page of prose carries several hundred, and the format therefore forces the fragmentation of any argument complex enough to be worth making. That charge is correct and it is not the strongest one available.

The stronger charge is the one this lecture has made. Bullets do not merely reduce how much you can say; they systematically delete a specific class of content, namely the relations between claims, and module 3 established that a deleted relation is not a gap but a misreading, because the reader supplies the default. So a bulleted argument does not communicate less than the prose version. In the concession case it communicates the opposite, which is a different and worse failure than thinness.

This reframes what looks like a matter of taste. A reviewer who asks for the bullets to be turned into prose is not expressing a preference for continuous text; they are asking for the logical relations to be restored. And a writer who bullets a paragraph to make it "clearer" has performed an operation whose effect is to remove the part of the paragraph that carried its reasoning.

The honest complication is that bullets are demanded. House styles require them, committees expect them, and a paper of unbroken prose reads in some organisations as a failure to summarise. The workable position is to bullet the genuine enumerations, write the arguments as prose, and where a bulleted argument is unavoidable, put the relations into the bullets themselves: begin the third bullet with "even so" and the fourth with "because". It is ugly and it preserves the meaning.

Why this layer is nobody's job

The visual layer is produced by a template, applied by whoever formats the document, and reviewed as presentation. That division of labour explains its state better than any claim about writers' skills, and it has a specific consequence: the layer that scanning readers consume is the one furthest from the person who understood the analysis.

The remedy is not to take over the formatting. It is to recognise which parts of the visual layer are content decisions and to make those yourself: the headings, because they are assertions; the choice between prose, list and table, because it follows from the dimensionality of the content; the captions, because they state findings; and the grouping of table rows, because it encodes a taxonomy. Everything else, fonts, margins, colour, genuinely is presentation and can be left to a template.

Where module 4 ends

Eight lectures have moved from the opening as a contract to the shape of the page. Two claims have recurred and are worth collecting, because they are the module's real content. The first is that structure is a reader-side property: hierarchy, arcs, spines and the visual layer all exist to manage what a reader can hold and find, and none of them is about elegance. The second is that every layer of a document carries meaning, so there is no layer that can be treated as presentation applied afterwards. Punctuation carried meaning in module 1, voice carried it in module 2, transitions in module 3, and the heading and table layer here.

Module 5 turns from structure to subtraction and shaping, and it begins by unpacking Strunk and White's best line into six identifiable kinds of redundancy. It is worth noting the tension it inherits: module 3 made a paragraph 40 per cent longer to honour the given-new contract, and module 5 will tell you to cut. The reconciliation was given there and holds throughout: scaffolding earns its place, padding does not, and the test is whether deleting a phrase leaves the reader to infer something.

Common failure Converting bullets to prose and stopping there. Prose with no connectives is a bulleted list with the dots removed, and it is worse, because it looks like an argument. If you unbullet a list, you must supply the relations, which means running the because-but-so test from module 3's lecture 6 on every join. The bullets were not the problem; the missing relations were, and the bullets were merely the form in which their absence was invisible.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a bulleted list of four or more items from your own writing and convert it to prose, running the because-but-so test on every join. Report which relations you had to supply, and whether any of them reversed the meaning a reader would have taken from the bullets.
  2. Why is "bullets delete relations" a stronger charge than "bullets carry too little information"? Use module 3's finding about unmarked departures.
  3. Give the dimensional test. What form does one dimension of independent items take, and what about three dimensions?
  4. Name three cases where bullets are correct and four where they destroy the content. Which of the four is most likely present in your last document?
  5. State the three requirements of a heading layer. What is the bold lead-in for, and which requirement does it help you satisfy?
  6. Distinguish Schriver's redundant, complementary and supplementary relations. What follows for what a caption should contain?
  7. Why does uniform paragraph spacing assert something false? What does it have in common with the bullet form?
  8. Which parts of the visual layer are content decisions you must make yourself, and which are genuinely presentation?
