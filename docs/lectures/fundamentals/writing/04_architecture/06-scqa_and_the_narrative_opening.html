SCQA and the narrative opening

Situation, complication, question, answer. Lecture 1 gave four elements; Minto's version adds the one that matters most, because the question is the hinge on which the whole pyramid turns. And the situation has a requirement nobody states: the reader must be willing to nod at it.

Lecture 6 of 8 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: Minto, The Pyramid Principle
Step 1

The 30-second version

Minto's introduction has four parts in a fixed logical relationship. The situation states something about the world the reader already accepts. The complication states what has changed, gone wrong or become unknown. Together those make a question inevitable. The answer is your response, and it is the apex of the pyramid that the rest of the document supports.

The question is what lecture 1's four elements were missing, and it is the load-bearing part. Situation and complication exist for one purpose, which is to make a particular question the obvious next thing to ask. If the question a reader would actually ask after your complication is not the question your document answers, the opening has failed regardless of how well each part is written, and you will not discover it by reading the parts.

The situation also has a requirement that is easy to miss and expensive to breach: it must be uncontroversial. Its job is to establish common ground, so if the reader might dispute it, it is not a situation but a claim, and it belongs in the body where you can support it. An opening that starts an argument in its first sentence has spent the reader's goodwill before reaching the point.

Feynman test It is a story with four beats. Here is how things were. Here is what went wrong. So what should we do? Here is what. The reason it works is not that it is a template but that it is the oldest shape there is, and a reader who recognises the shape supplies the question themselves, which means they are asking rather than being told.
What is beyond the source Minto supplies the four elements, the ordering variants and the argument for the answer at the top. The nod test for the situation in step 4 is this lecture's, and so is the diagnostic use in step 5: SCQA as a test of whether you have a document at all, since a missing complication means nothing has changed and a missing question means you do not know what you are answering. Both are cheap checks that can be run before any drafting.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the question determines the whole pyramid. Lecture 3 said each level answers a question its parent raises. The opening's question is the one the top level answers, so getting it wrong misplaces everything below. A document whose complication is "the calibration may understate losses" and whose sections answer "how our model works" has a broken join at the highest level, and every section beneath it is correctly placed under the wrong parent.

The second reason is that this is the fastest way to discover you do not have a document. Try to write the complication. If nothing has changed, gone wrong or become unknown, there is no reason for the paper to exist, and the honest outcome is a two-line note rather than a twelve-page report. That check takes a minute and prevents a great deal of work that produces documents nobody needed.

The third reason is the nod test, which catches a failure that otherwise survives all the way to a committee. If your situation is contestable, the first thing that happens in the room is a dispute about your premise, and the discussion never reaches your finding. Writers do this without noticing, because a claim they consider settled feels like common ground, and the distinction between what you accept and what your reader accepts is exactly the distinction the curse of knowledge hides.

And a reason about the ordering variants. Minto's observation that the four elements can be presented in different orders is more useful than it first appears, because it means answer-first is not a single technique but a family, and you can lead with the answer, with the problem or with the question depending on what the reader needs to be persuaded to keep reading. That is a real choice, and most professional writers do not know they have it.

Step 3

The mental model

The narrative shape on top, the four orderings beneath with what each is for.

The last observation in the caption is worth stating separately because it clears up a common confusion. The standard S-C-Q-A ordering is sometimes described as delaying the answer, and it does not: the answer is the last element of the introduction, which means it arrives within the first paragraph or two and before any of the body. The genuine delay cases from lecture 1 push the answer past the introduction entirely, which is a different and much larger move.

Situation
A statement about the world the reader accepts without argument. Its job is common ground, not information. Tested by asking whether they would nod.
Complication
What has changed, gone wrong, or become unknown about the situation. The reason the document exists. If you cannot write one, you do not have a document.
Question
What the reader asks once they have the situation and complication. Usually implicit in the prose and always explicit in your planning, because it is the question your top-level answer must answer.
Answer
Your response to the question, and the apex of the pyramid. Everything in the body exists to support it or to answer the questions it raises.
Step 4

The detail

Writing the four, in the order you should write them

THE PLANNING ORDER IS NOT THE PROSE ORDER Write them: Q, A, C, S. Present them: S, C, Q, A. 1. QUESTION first, in planning. What exactly is this document answering? One sentence, interrogative, and specific enough that a different document would answer it differently. Bad: "What is the state of the LGD model?" -> too broad; a hundred documents answer this Good: "Does the current downturn LGD estimate satisfy the downturn requirement?" -> answerable yes or no, and a no obliges you to say by how much 2. ANSWER second. If you cannot answer your own question in one sentence, you are not ready to write. "No: it understates the peak loss rate by approximately 40 basis points." 3. COMPLICATION third. Work backwards: what makes the question live? Something must have changed or been discovered. "Arrears in the calibration window never exceeded 1.4 per cent, against 3.1 per cent in 2008." 4. SITUATION last, and shortest. The minimum common ground needed for the complication to land. "The bank calibrates downturn LGD for residential mortgages to a stress window spanning 2011 to 2023." WHY THIS ORDER The situation is the element writers over-produce, because it is the only one that can be written without knowing anything. Writing it last, from the complication backwards, forces it to be exactly as long as it needs to be. A situation written first becomes background. THE LENGTH TEST Situation: one to two sentences Complication: one to two sentences Question: usually unwritten, always known Answer: one to three sentences If the situation runs to a paragraph, you are writing background. Background belongs in section 2 or an appendix, and the test for whether a sentence is situation or background is whether the complication would still land without it.

The nod test, and what fails it

A situation must be uncontroversial, and writers breach this constantly because a claim they hold firmly does not feel like a claim. Three kinds of sentence fail the test and each has a repair.

Fails becauseExampleRepair
Not common ground
It embeds a judgement "The bank's downturn calibration has always been conservative." Move it to the body as a claim with evidence. The situation version is the factual core: "the bank calibrates downturn LGD to a stress window spanning 2011 to 2023."
It is the complication in disguise "The stress window is too short to capture a severe downturn." That is what you are here to establish. Demote it to the complication or to the answer, and let the situation state the window neutrally.
The reader does not know it "Following the 2024 methodology change, the indexation basis moved from regional to national." If the reader does not hold this, it is not common ground, it is information. Either it is part of the complication, or the audience is wrong for this opening and needs more situation than one sentence can carry.

The third row exposes a real tension rather than a simple error. Sometimes a reader genuinely lacks the ground you need, and then the situation has to do more work than a sentence allows. The honest resolutions are to write a longer situation and accept the cost, or to write two documents for two audiences, or to put the ground in a preliminary section and keep the opening for readers who have it. What you cannot do is state unfamiliar material as though it were shared and hope, because the complication will land on nothing.

Choosing among the orderings

The four orderings in the figure are chosen on one question: what does this reader need before they will keep reading? A reader who already has the context needs the answer, so lead with it. A reader who must be persuaded there is a problem at all needs the complication early. A reader who commissioned the work wants to see their own question, so state it. A reader who has none of these needs the full narrative, which is why S-C-Q-A is the default.

One practical note on the problem-first ordering, since it is the one most often chosen for the wrong reason. Leading with the complication creates urgency, and urgency is not the same as importance. A document that opens on a problem before establishing the ground invites the reader to dispute the problem's significance, and an incident report that opens "a control failure has been identified" without saying what the control was for gets a first question about scope rather than about the failure. Use it when the reader already knows the ground and needs to know that something has gone wrong in it.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

SCQA as a test of whether you have a document

The most valuable use of this framework is not writing openings. It is deciding, before drafting, whether the document should exist and what it is. Three failures show up in the planning and each has a cheap resolution.

If you cannot write a complication, nothing has changed and there is no reason for the paper. The honest output is a short note confirming that the position is unchanged, which is a legitimate and welcome document, and is not twelve pages. A great deal of routine reporting is a complication-free document padded to the length the process expects, and recognising that is the first step towards writing the note instead.

If you cannot write a single question, you have more than one document. Two questions means two answers means two pyramids, and lecture 3's warning about a bland apex applies: inventing a top that contains both is how you get "several matters arise from the annual review". Splitting is almost always better, and where a genuine synthesis exists, finding it is real work that pays.

If you can write the question and not the answer, you are not ready to write and you have learned something valuable at low cost. This is the case where the temptation to start drafting is strongest, because drafting feels like progress and the structure can be filled in later. It cannot: lecture 3 established that the apex determines every section beneath it, so drafting without an answer produces sections placed under a parent that does not exist yet.

Why the situation is the element that goes wrong

Of the four, the situation is the one professional documents get wrong most often, and there is a structural reason worth naming. It is the only element that can be written with no knowledge of the findings. So it is the element that survives from a draft written before the work, and lecture 1 argued that openings written first are records of intentions rather than of conclusions.

The characteristic symptom is a situation that has grown into background: three paragraphs of history, governance and methodology before anything happens. Each sentence is true and each was easy to write, and their cumulative effect is that the complication arrives on page two, by which time a sampling reader has left. The repair is the length test in step 4, and the discipline is writing the situation last, from the complication backwards, so that it is exactly as long as the complication requires and no longer.

What SCQA does not give you

Two honest limits. The first is that SCQA is an introduction structure, not a document structure. It gets the reader into the pyramid; it says nothing about how the pyramid is built, which is lectures 3 to 5. A document with a flawless SCQA opening and an ungrouped body is a common and slightly worse failure than the reverse, because the opening has promised a structure the body does not deliver.

The second is that the framework presumes a decision-oriented reader. Situation, complication, question, answer is the shape of a problem being solved, and it fits a validation report, a board paper or a recommendation exactly. It fits a thematic review, a methodology manual or a reference document poorly, because those are not answering a question at all: they are describing something. Forcing SCQA onto a reference document produces a manufactured complication, and the tell is a first paragraph explaining that the absence of documentation is a risk. Lecture 7 turns to structures for documents the pyramid does not fit.

Common failure Manufacturing a complication because the template requires one. If the honest position is that nothing has changed, saying so is the document. A fabricated complication, of the "increasing regulatory focus" or "evolving best practice" variety, is worse than no opening at all: it is unfalsifiable, it signals that the paper has no news, and an experienced reader will read the phrase and stop.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Plan a document you need to write by filling in Q, A, C, S in that order, obeying the length test. Then write it in S-C-Q-A order, and separately in A-S-C order. Say which you would send and to whom.
  2. Name the four elements. Which one does the reader supply, and why does that make it the hinge?
  3. State the nod test. Give the three ways a situation fails it and the repair for each.
  4. Why should you write the four in the order Q, A, C, S rather than the order you present them?
  5. Give the four orderings and the reader each suits. Why is problem-first the one most often chosen for the wrong reason?
  6. What do you learn if you cannot write a complication? If you cannot write a single question? If you can write the question but not the answer?
  7. Why is the situation the element that most often goes wrong? Answer in terms of when it can be written.
  8. Name the two things SCQA does not give you. Which kind of document does it fit poorly, and what is the tell that it has been forced on one?
