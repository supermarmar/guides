Arcs of coherence

A document is a tree of sections and also a set of threads running across that tree. Outlining advice describes only the tree, which is why documents that outline beautifully can still read as a sequence of unrelated chapters.

Lecture 2 of 8 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: Pinker (2014), ch. 5, Arcs of Coherence
Step 1

The 30-second version

Pinker's model has two components that most structural advice conflates. A document has a hierarchy, a tree in which each section sits under a parent and contains its children. It also has arcs, continuities that run across the tree: a recurring key term, an actor the reader is tracking, a question opened in section 1 and closed in section 4, a running example. Hierarchy tells the reader where they are. Arcs tell them why they are still reading the same document.

The two are independent, and this is module 3's distinction at document scale. Hierarchy is the single-function rule applied to sections: each one does one job and belongs under its parent. Arcs are the given-new contract applied across sections: each section attaches to material the reader already holds. A document can have a clean tree and no arcs, in which case it reads as a set of separate memoranda bound together, or arcs and no tree, in which case it reads as continuous and undifferentiated.

The practical consequence is that an outline cannot check your document. An outline is a rendering of the tree, so it displays exactly the property that is easier to get right, and it is silent about the property that fails more often. This is why the outline stage feels productive and the resulting draft still reads badly.

Feynman test A building has rooms arranged in a plan, and it also has pipes and wiring that run through the walls from one end to the other. The plan tells you where you are. The services are what make it one building rather than four sheds sharing a wall. You can draw a beautiful plan and forget to connect anything, and the plan will not show you that you have.
What is beyond the source Pinker gives the tree-plus-arcs model and the argument that readers track both. The mapping in step 1, which identifies hierarchy with module 3's coherence and arcs with its cohesion, is this lecture's, and it earns its place by importing the diagnostics: the topic-string method and the sentence-opening audit both have document-scale versions, which is what step 4 sets out. The claim about three levels in step 5 is a working rule of thumb rather than a measured limit and is flagged as such.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this is the failure mode of documents written by committee, which describes most substantial professional documents. Sections are allocated to authors, each author writes a well-formed section, and the tree is correct because someone drew it first. What nobody owns is the arcs, because an arc by definition crosses a section boundary and therefore belongs to no single author. The characteristic result is a report in which every section is competent and the document has no through-line.

The second reason is that arcs are what make a long document readable at all. A pyramid works when the reader can hold the whole structure, which in practice means a document of up to perhaps twenty pages with three levels. Beyond that the hierarchy exceeds working memory and the reader stops holding it: they navigate instead, using headings as signposts. At that point continuity is the only thing carrying them, and a document with no arcs becomes a reference work whether or not you intended one.

The third reason is diagnostic and cheap. Arcs are checkable with a document-scale version of the methods in module 3, which means you can audit a fifty-page report's through-line in under an hour without reading it properly. Given that most review effort goes on sentences, this is a large gain in a place nobody looks.

And a reason particular to regulatory submissions and validation reports. Those documents are read in fragments by different people: one reader wants section 4, another the appendix, a third the summary. A document with strong arcs survives fragmentary reading, because each section carries the threads that orient it. A document with only a tree does not, because a section read out of order has lost the parent that gave it meaning.

Step 3

The mental model

The main panel shows a document with both properties. The two beneath show what each looks like alone.

The committee note under the first failure is the practically important part. An arc crosses a boundary, so it is owned by nobody in a document with allocated sections, and the standard remedy of appointing an editor to "make it flow" is aimed at the wrong thing: flow is what the sections already have internally. What is missing is that no term, actor or question survives a section boundary, and fixing that is a structural act rather than a stylistic one.

Hierarchy
The tree: sections nested under parents, each doing one job and summarising its children. What an outline displays. The document-scale form of the single-function rule.
Arc
A continuity running across the tree: a recurring key term, an actor being tracked, a question opened early and closed late, a running example. What an outline cannot display, and what makes a long document one document.
Open thread
An arc the reader is currently holding: a question you raised and have not answered, an actor whose fate is undetermined. Readers can hold a small number, and exceeding it produces the sense of being lost rather than any identifiable confusion.
Step 4

The detail

The four kinds of arc

ArcWhat the reader tracksHow to build it deliberately
Ranked by how reliably they carry a long document
Key terms The same named concepts recurring in the same words across sections. Name them in the opening, per lecture 1, and then never vary them. The cheapest arc and the one most often broken by a well-meaning editor removing repetition.
Open questions A question raised early and explicitly closed later. Strongest arc available, because the reader is actively waiting. Raise it in the opening, refer to it once in the middle, close it by name. "The question of why no control detected this is answered in section 4" and then section 4 says so.
Actors Who is doing things, followed across sections: the model owner, the validation team, the committee. Keep them in subjects, per module 2's lecture 2. An actor who appears only in section 3 is not an arc; an actor whose decisions are traced from 2 to 5 is.
A running example One concrete case carried through the whole document and returned to in each section. Choose it before drafting. Expensive to retrofit, and the strongest device available for genuinely long documents, because it gives every abstraction a place to land.

The second row deserves the emphasis it gets. An explicitly opened and explicitly closed question is the most powerful arc because it recruits the reader's own attention: they are holding the question, so they notice the answer. It is also the one professional documents almost never use, because raising a question in an opening feels like admitting you did not know something. That reluctance is worth overriding, since the alternative is a document whose sections the reader has no reason to connect.

The two checks, at document scale

CHECK A THE HIERARCHY (module 3 check 1, a level up) 1. List your section headings in order. 2. For each, name the question its parent raises that it answers. Lecture 3 formalises this. 3. Ask whether the set of children under each parent forms a recognisable group. Lecture 5 gives the MECE test. FAILS IF - a section answers no question the parent raised - two sections answer the same question - the children of a parent belong to no single set CHECK B THE ARCS (module 3 check 4, a level up) 1. Take the first paragraph of each section. 2. Underline anything in it that appeared in an earlier section: a key term, an actor, a reference to an open question. 3. Mark any section whose opening paragraph contains nothing from earlier. Those are your cold sections. A cold section is a section the reader arrives at with nothing to attach it to, and it will read as a separate document however good it is. WORKED, ON A FIVE-SECTION REPORT S1 opening [opens the document] S2 "The stress window was selected in 2019..." -> "stress window" from S1. WARM. S3 "Quantifying the shortfall requires..." -> "shortfall" from S1's point. WARM. S4 "The benchmark test is run annually by the second line..." -> "benchmark test" from S1. WARM. S5 "Three options exist for the observation period..." -> COLD. Nothing from earlier, AND it has renamed the stress window as the "observation period", which breaks the key-term arc as well. Two defects in one sentence, and both are invisible from the outline, because the outline says "Section 5: options", which is exactly where section 5 belongs.

The section 5 failure in that example is worth dwelling on because it is the commonest arc break in practice and it has a mundane cause. A different author wrote section 5, or the same author wrote it three weeks later, and used the term that was natural at the time. The tree is untouched. The arc is severed, and a reader arriving at section 5 has to work out for themselves that the observation period and the stress window are the same object.

Why outlines cannot catch this

An outline is a projection of the tree onto a list, and projections lose information. What it loses is precisely the arcs, because an arc is a relation between non-adjacent nodes and an outline represents only parent-child relations. So the outline stage is genuinely useful and structurally incapable of detecting the failure that this lecture is about.

Two practical consequences. First, do not treat an approved outline as evidence that a document will hold together, particularly a multi-author one, because the outline is silent on the property that multi-author documents fail. Second, if you want a planning artefact that shows arcs, you need something other than a list: a table with sections as rows and key terms, actors and open questions as columns, which lets you see at a glance which threads survive which boundaries. That table takes ten minutes and catches what an outline cannot.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

How deep a hierarchy can a reader hold?

Roughly three levels. That figure is a working rule of thumb rather than a measured limit, and it should be treated as such, but the reasoning behind it is sound and the practical implications are worth taking seriously.

A reader following an argument holds their current position in the tree, which means holding the chain of parents above them. At two levels that is trivial. At three it is manageable. At four, section 4.3.2.1 requires the reader to keep four claims in mind to know what the paragraph in front of them is supporting, and in practice they stop trying. They do not become confused in a way they could report; they switch from following to navigating, treating the document as a reference work and using headings to find what they came for.

Which gives a usable diagnostic. If your numbering reaches four levels, you have either a document that should be two documents, or a hierarchy that should be flattened by promoting the fourth level into prose. The numbering is not the problem; it is a symptom that the tree has outgrown what the reader will hold, and the fact that the numbering makes the structure visible does not make it followable.

There is a legitimate exception, which is documents designed for navigation rather than reading: standards, manuals, rulebooks, and the regulatory texts this collection covers. Deep numbering there is correct, because the reader is looking things up and the numbering is an index. The failure is applying that convention to a document meant to persuade, which is what happens when a validation report is drafted by someone whose reading diet is regulation.

Arcs against the pyramid

This lecture and the four that follow are in mild tension, and it is worth naming before Minto arrives rather than after. The pyramid is a hierarchy, and a strict pyramid says everything belongs in exactly one place under exactly one parent. Arcs cut across that. A key term recurring in sections 2, 3 and 4 is, from the pyramid's point of view, the same idea appearing in three places, which the pyramid would regard as a grouping failure.

The resolution is that they describe different things. The pyramid governs where ideas sit; arcs govern what the reader carries between them. A term recurring in three sections is not the same idea three times: it is one object about which three different claims are made, and those claims properly belong in three places. So the pyramid organises the claims and the arcs maintain the shared vocabulary the claims are about.

Where they genuinely conflict is over long documents. A pyramid presumes the reader can hold the whole structure, which fails past about twenty pages. Pinker's model does not presume that; it allows a text to be held as a sequence of local hierarchies stitched by arcs, which is how anyone actually reads a book. So for short documents use the pyramid and check the arcs; for long ones the arcs carry more of the load, and lecture 7 on long-form structure is where that becomes the primary mechanism.

Common failure Building arcs out of cross-references. "As discussed in section 2" is not an arc; it is an instruction to go and look. An arc is a continuity the reader experiences without navigating, created by the same term, actor or question appearing in the prose. A document held together by cross-references has admitted that its sections do not connect, and the reader who follows three of them has lost the thread they were following. Use cross-references for detail you deliberately excluded, not for the through-line.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Build the arc table for a document you wrote: sections as rows, key terms, actors and open questions as columns. Mark which threads survive which boundaries. Report every cold section and every renamed term.
  2. Distinguish hierarchy from arcs. Which module 3 property does each correspond to, and what does that let you import?
  3. Why is an outline structurally incapable of detecting a broken arc? Answer in terms of what an outline represents.
  4. Name the four kinds of arc. Which is strongest, and why do professional documents avoid it?
  5. Why do multi-author documents fail on arcs specifically? What does the usual remedy get wrong?
  6. State the three-level rule of thumb and the reasoning behind it. What happens to a reader at four levels, and what is the legitimate exception?
  7. Explain the tension between arcs and the pyramid, and the resolution. Where do they genuinely conflict?
  8. Why is "as discussed in section 2" not an arc? What is it appropriate for?
