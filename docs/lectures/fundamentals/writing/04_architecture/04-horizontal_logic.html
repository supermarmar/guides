Horizontal logic

Siblings in a pyramid relate to each other in exactly one of two ways: as steps in a chain or as members of a set. The reader checks the two differently, so a group containing both cannot be checked at all. There is also a robustness argument for preferring sets that Minto makes only in passing and that matters more than anything else in this lecture.

Lecture 4 of 8 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: Minto, The Pyramid Principle
Step 1

The 30-second version

Minto's second rule is that ideas in a grouping must be the same kind of idea. There are two kinds of grouping and no others. A deductive group is a chain: a statement, a second statement about it, and the implication that follows. Order is fixed, and each element depends on the one before. An inductive group is a set: several items of the same class, from which the parent draws a generalisation. Order is flexible within limits, and the items are independent.

The reader checks them differently, which is why mixing matters. A chain is checked by following it: accept step one, accept step two, accept the conclusion. A set is checked by testing each member against the class name and asking whether anything is missing. Put steps and members in the same group and the reader can do neither operation, because some elements need to be followed in order and others need to be compared with each other.

The claim in this lecture that goes furthest beyond the source is about robustness. A deductive chain is serially connected: reject the first step and the conclusion falls. An inductive set is parallel: reject one of four reasons and three remain. For any document that will be challenged, and a validation report or a board paper certainly will be, that difference is decisive and it is an argument for sets that has nothing to do with readability.

Feynman test Either you are telling the reader a story where each event causes the next, or you are handing them a list of things that are all the same sort of thing. Both work. What does not work is a list in which items two and three only make sense if you have read item one, because the reader is now holding a list and reading a story and cannot tell which they are supposed to be doing.
What is beyond the source Minto gives the two logics, the requirement that a group contain one kind, and a general preference for the inductive. The serial-against-parallel robustness argument in step 3 is this lecture's, and it is the reason to prefer sets in adversarial settings rather than merely in readable ones. Step 5's claim that most business writing which presents itself as deductive is actually badly ordered inductive is also not in Minto, and it is the more common failure by a wide margin.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the plural noun test, which falls straight out of this lecture, is the fastest structural check available. If your section headings under a parent can be summarised as "three reasons", "four drivers" or "two options", you have an inductive group and every item must be an instance of that noun. If one of them is not, you have found the misplaced section in ten seconds. There is no comparable test for a chain, which is one reason to prefer sets.

The second reason is the robustness argument, and it is worth stating in the language of the people who read these documents. A deductive argument has a single point of failure. If your paper says the window is short, therefore the loss distribution has no tail, therefore the estimate is understated, then a reader who disputes the first link has disposed of the whole paper without engaging the rest. An inductive paper saying the estimate is understated for three independent reasons survives losing one, and the reader who wants to reject it must do three times the work.

The third reason is that mixing the two is extremely common and nearly invisible. It happens naturally: you list three reasons, and while writing the third you realise it needs a preliminary point, so you add it as a fourth item rather than nesting it underneath. The group now has three members and a step, the plural noun no longer fits, and nobody notices because each item is individually sound.

And a reason specific to quantitative work. In a document that adds things up, an inductive group is usually also an arithmetic decomposition, and then the grouping discipline is not stylistic at all. If your four drivers of a shortfall are meant to sum to the shortfall, they must be mutually exclusive or you have double counted. Lecture 5 makes this its subject, and the point here is that the horizontal relationship determines whether the question even arises.

Step 3

The mental model

Three panels: the chain, the set, and the mixture. Each shows what the reader has to do, and the third shows why they cannot.

The robustness contrast between the first two panels deserves stating in one place because it is the practically decisive consideration and it is not a readability argument. A chain of three links each with a 90 per cent chance of surviving challenge survives as a whole about three times in four. Three independent reasons each with a 90 per cent chance leave you with at least one surviving essentially always. That is a crude calculation and the direction is right, and it is why papers that expect resistance should be built as sets.

Deductive group
A chain of dependent statements ending in an implication. Typically a situation, an observation about it, and what follows. Order is not negotiable, and the reader's operation is to follow.
Inductive group
A set of independent items of one class, summarised by a parent that generalises over them. Named by a plural noun. The reader's operation is to test membership and to ask what is missing.
The plural noun test
Name the group with a plural noun and check that every member is an instance of it. "Three reasons", "four drivers", "two options". If one member is not an instance, it is misplaced. If no plural noun fits, the group is mixed.
Step 4

The detail

Ordering an inductive set

Minto's third rule is that ideas in a grouping must be logically ordered, and for a set that leaves a real choice. Three orderings are defensible and a fourth is not.

OrderUse whenWhat it signals
Defensible orderings
Degree The items differ in size or importance and the difference matters. Largest first. That you have quantified them and that the reader should attend to the first. The default for findings.
Time The items happened or must happen in sequence, without depending on each other logically. A process. Distinguish carefully from a deductive chain: steps in time are independent, steps in an argument are not.
Structure The items map onto a structure the reader already holds: portfolios, legal entities, risk types, geographies. Completeness. Borrowing an existing taxonomy is the cheapest way to signal that nothing is missing.
Not an ordering
The order you found them in Never, and it is the commonest arrangement in professional documents. That the document records the investigation rather than the argument. The same drafting artefact module 3's lecture 5 identified in paragraphs.

The structural ordering row is worth a note for this collection's subject matter, because it is where writing and domain knowledge meet. If your four items are the four Basel risk types, or the three lines of defence, or the stages of an impairment model, you have borrowed a taxonomy your reader already holds, and that does two things at once: it fixes the order, and it makes the completeness question answerable. Lecture 5 is about what happens when no such taxonomy exists and you have to build one.

Diagnosing and repairing a mixed group

THE MIXED-GROUP REPAIR 1. Write out the group's members as a list. 2. Try to name the list with a plural noun. 3. If no noun fits, test each member: is it an instance of something, or a step towards something? 4. Demote the steps. A step belongs beneath the claim it supports, not beside it. 5. Re-run the plural noun test on what remains. WORKED BEFORE, four items under "points about the estimate" (a) The stress window spans 2011 to 2023. (b) So it contains no severe downturn. (c) Recovery lags use the benign-period mean. (d) Indexation is national, not regional. Step 2: no plural noun fits. "Points" is not a class. Step 3: (a) a fact. Not an instance of anything. (b) a step, following from (a). (c) an instance of "defect". (d) an instance of "defect". Step 4: (a) and (b) together constitute one defect, and (a) is the evidence for it. Demote both. AFTER Parent: "Three defects understate the estimate." 1. The stress window excludes every severe downturn. evidence: it spans 2011 to 2023, in which arrears never exceeded 1.4 per cent. 2. Recovery lags use the benign-period mean. 3. Indexation is national rather than regional. Plural noun: "defects". All three are instances. Order: by degree, largest first, once quantified. WHAT CHANGED STRUCTURALLY Four siblings became three, with one gaining a child. Nothing was deleted. The tree got one level deeper in one branch, which is what demotion means. THE TELL TO REMEMBER A group whose only available name is "points", "considerations", "matters", "aspects" or "issues" is mixed. Those words are not classes; they are what you write when no class exists.

When deductive is right

The preference for sets is a default rather than a rule, and three situations genuinely call for a chain.

The first is a genuinely surprising conclusion, where the derivation is what persuades. If your finding is counter-intuitive, a reader given it as one of three reasons will not believe it, and the only thing that will move them is being walked through the steps. The chain's serial fragility is the price of its persuasive force.

The second is a formal derivation, where the steps are the content. A capital calculation, a proof, an actuarial derivation: these are chains by nature and presenting them as a set would be a misdescription. Note that such material usually belongs in an appendix with an inductive summary above it, which is how you get both.

The third is where the audience will reject the conclusion, which is lecture 1's first delay case. Building the chain means each step is conceded before its destination is visible. This is the case where the chain's fragility is least dangerous, because the reader who has accepted every step has already given up the ability to reject the conclusion cheaply.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Most "deductive" business writing is badly ordered inductive

Writers who learn this distinction often conclude that their documents are deductive, because the sections seem to build on each other and the conclusion comes at the end. Examine one and the diagnosis is usually different. The sections do not depend on each other logically; they were arranged in the order the work happened, and the conclusion is at the end because that is where the writer arrived at it.

The test is to reorder them. Take a supposedly deductive document and swap sections 3 and 4. If the argument still holds, it was never a chain, because in a chain the order is load-bearing and a swap produces nonsense. If it still holds, you have an inductive set that has been arranged chronologically, and the repair is to add the class name, put the parent claim at the top, and order by degree.

This matters more than a taxonomic quibble because the two failures have different costs. A genuine chain presented as a chain is fragile but honest. An inductive set presented as a chain has inherited the fragility without gaining the persuasive force: a reader who disputes section 2 will assume the rest falls, when in fact sections 3 and 4 stood independently all along. You have given away robustness you actually had.

Why the robustness argument is not in the source

Minto prefers the inductive on grounds of summarisability: a set can be summarised by its class name, which lets you lead with the point, and a chain can only be summarised by its conclusion, which is at the end. That is a good argument and it is about the writer's convenience and the reader's speed.

The robustness argument is about what happens after the document is read, in a meeting, and it is worth separating because it points the same way for a different reason and applies where Minto's does not. A short document with a chain is perfectly summarisable, so Minto's objection is weak. The robustness objection still holds: the short chain still has one point of failure, and if the paper is going to a committee that contains someone motivated to reject it, the structure has handed them the cheapest possible route.

The honest limit is that robustness can be bought dishonestly. Three reasons are only genuinely independent if they are, and a writer who splits one argument into three restatements has manufactured apparent robustness while adding nothing. Lecture 5's mutual exclusivity test is the check on that, and it is why the two lectures belong adjacent: horizontal logic tells you to prefer sets, and MECE stops you faking one.

Where this leaves the pyramid

Three rules, now all covered in part. Vertical logic says each level summarises the level beneath and answers the question above. Horizontal logic says a group is a chain or a set and never both. The third rule, logical ordering, has been handled for sets in step 4 and is trivial for chains, where the order is given.

What remains is the question the plural noun test raises and cannot answer. The test tells you whether each member is an instance of the class. It does not tell you whether the members overlap, or whether they exhaust the class, and those are the two failures that make a set look complete when it is not. Lecture 5 supplies both.

Common failure Reaching for a chain because the material feels causal. A great deal of technical content involves causation, and causal content does not require a deductive structure: "three mechanisms transmit the shock" is an inductive set whose members are each causal. What makes a group deductive is that the elements depend on each other, not that their contents concern cause and effect. Confusing the two produces chains where sets would have been more robust and just as accurate.

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a group of sections from a document you wrote and run the five-step mixed-group repair. Report the plural noun you ended with, what you demoted, and whether the tree got deeper.
  2. Define deductive and inductive grouping. State the checking operation a reader performs on each.
  3. Give the plural noun test. What does it tell you when no noun fits?
  4. State the serial-against-parallel robustness argument. Why does it point the same way as Minto's argument for a different reason, and where does it apply when hers does not?
