Structure in long-form nonfiction

McPhee spends a large share of his time on structure before writing a word, and holds that the reader should never notice it. Minto insists the structure be visible in every heading. That is a real contradiction, not a difference of emphasis, and which one is right depends entirely on why your reader is reading.

Lecture 7 of 8 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: McPhee, Draft No. 4 (2017), especially "Structure"
Step 1

The 30-second version

John McPhee's account of structure has three claims that transfer to professional writing and one that does not. The three: decide the structure before drafting, sometimes literally by drawing it. Prefer chronology, because the reader already has it, and make any departure earn its way. And treat a structure as sound rather than clever, since a structure the reader admires is a structure that is intruding.

The claim that does not transfer is the last one taken to its conclusion, which is that the reader should not notice the structure at all. Minto's assertion headings exist precisely to make the structure conspicuous, so that a reader who reads nothing else still receives the argument. Both positions are correct for their own readers, and the difference is that McPhee's reader chose to read and yours did not.

What long-form supplies that the pyramid cannot is a spine for documents too long to hold as one hierarchy. Lecture 2 put that limit at about three levels; past it the reader stops holding the tree and starts navigating. A chronological spine solves this because the reader supplies the ordering themselves, at no cost to memory, which is why it is the default for anything long.

Feynman test Time is a free structure. Everyone already knows that March comes before April, so a document arranged in time costs the reader nothing to follow, and you have spent no attention teaching them the plan. Any other arrangement is one you have to teach, and every departure from time is a small debt: the reader has to be told where they are, and told again when you come back.
What is beyond the source McPhee is describing his own practice for New Yorker pieces and books, not offering a theory for professional documents. Extracting the three transferable claims and identifying the fourth as non-transferable is this lecture's work, and so is the direct comparison with Minto in step 5. McPhee does not mention business writing and would probably not recognise the use being made of him here.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the pyramid runs out. Minto's framework presumes a reader who can hold the whole structure, and a fifty-page thematic review, a methodology manual or a book-length model document exceeds that by a wide margin. At that length the choice is not which hierarchy but which spine, and the pyramid has nothing to say about spines.

The second reason is that some professional documents genuinely describe rather than answer, which lecture 6 identified as SCQA's limit. A model methodology document is not answering a question; it is setting out how something works. Forcing a pyramid onto it produces a manufactured complication, and the honest alternative is a structural spine borrowed from the thing being described: the model lifecycle, the calculation sequence, the data flow. Those are chronologies in disguise, which is why McPhee's preference applies directly.

The third reason is the structure-first discipline, which is the single most transferable thing in the lecture and the least practised. Most professional documents are drafted and then restructured, which is expensive because every restructuring invalidates prose. McPhee's order, structure first and at length, is cheaper, and module 9 supplies the evidence that this is what expert writers do generally rather than a peculiarity of one essayist.

And a reason about the invisible-structure claim, which is worth understanding even where you reject it. A reader who notices your structure is spending attention on your architecture rather than on your content. That cost is real in every genre. What differs is whether it is worth paying, and the answer for professional writing is usually yes, because a reader who never sees your structure also never finds section 4.

Step 3

The mental model

Two spines, and the cost of departing from the cheap one.

The technical documents in this collection make the point concretely. A model methodology document arranged by lifecycle stage, from data through feature engineering to estimation, validation and monitoring, is chronological: the stages happen in that order and the reader knows it. A document arranged by the same content grouped as data, methodology and controls is thematic, and each section then has to tell the reader which lifecycle stage it is talking about. The first arrangement is free and the second is not.

Spine
The organising sequence of a long document: time, a process, a geography, a hierarchy of themes. Distinct from the hierarchy, which nests; the spine runs.
Orientation cost
The attention a reader spends working out where they are. Zero for a chronological spine, charged once per section for a thematic one, and charged twice per departure when a chronology breaks.
Structure-first
McPhee's practice of settling the structure, often by drawing it, before drafting any prose. Cheaper than drafting and restructuring, because restructuring invalidates prose and structuring does not.

Does not transfer
The reader should not notice the structure at all It would mean no assertion headings, no signposting, no explicit summary of what each section establishes. McPhee's reader chose to read and will finish. Yours did not and will not. A reader who never notices your structure also never finds section 4, and lecture 3 established that headings are how most readers receive the argument.

The second row deserves the emphasis the table gives it, because it resolves a question this collection's readers face constantly. A model document, a validation report and a methodology manual all have a natural chronology available in the process they describe, and using it is free. The temptation is to organise by theme instead, because themes look more analytical, and the cost is that every section then has to establish its own temporal position before it can say anything.

The lead, and what it is for

McPhee's image for an opening is a flashlight shining down into the piece: it illuminates the material that follows, and its beam determines what the reader sees. The two properties he insists on are that it must be honest about what follows and that it must not be a trick. A lead that promises a piece the writer did not write is the worst thing an opening can do, because the reader spends the whole text waiting for something that is not coming.

Both properties map onto lecture 1's contract, and it is worth noticing that they were arrived at independently. Lecture 1 said the reader is entitled to assume the point stated is the point argued, and derived that from the given-new contract. McPhee arrives at the same requirement from the practice of writing narrative, where the cost of a false promise is a reader who feels cheated at the end. Two traditions, two arguments, one conclusion, which is reasonable evidence that the conclusion is right.

Where they differ is in what the lead delivers. Minto's opening delivers the answer. McPhee's lead delivers the terms on which the reader will read, and withholds the answer because the answer is the piece. That difference is the same one step 5 is about, and it is a difference about readers rather than about openings.

Departures, and the debt each incurs

THE DEPARTURE TEST Before breaking chronology, answer both: 1. Does this material deliver something the reader needs AT THIS POINT? 2. Could it have been delivered earlier, in its own place in time? If (1) yes and (2) no -> take the departure. Otherwise -> move the material. PAYING THE DEBT A departure costs two orientation sentences, not one. On the way out: "The window was set in 2019, and the reason matters here." On the way back: "Returning to Q3, the review then found..." Documents that take the departure and pay only on the way out leave the reader unsure when they have come back, which is worse than not departing. WORKED, A DEPARTURE THAT PASSES Chronology reaches Q3 2025, where the review finds the window excludes 2008. The reader now needs to know why anyone chose that window, and could not have used that information in 2019, because in 2019 it was not yet a problem. (1) yes (2) no -> depart. WORKED, A DEPARTURE THAT FAILS Chronology reaches Q3 2025, and the document flashes back to describe the model's governance history since 2015. (1) does the reader need governance history here? No. (2) could it have gone earlier? Yes, or nowhere. -> move it to the opening's situation, in one sentence, or delete it. THE PATTERN Most flashbacks in professional documents fail on (2). The material could have been placed in time and was not, because the writer discovered its relevance late. That is a drafting artefact, and the same one module 3's lecture 5 identified in paragraphs and lecture 1 in openings.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

McPhee against Minto, stated as a genuine conflict

This module has spent four lectures on a framework whose central device is the assertion heading, whose whole purpose is to make the structure of an argument visible to a reader who will not read the prose. McPhee's position is that the reader should not be aware of the structure at all. These are not compatible, and it would be dishonest to present them as complementary emphases.

The resolution is that they optimise for different reading modes. McPhee writes for a reader who has chosen the piece, will read it from beginning to end, and whose experience is the product. For that reader, visible scaffolding is an intrusion: they do not need to find anything, because they are going to read all of it, and being told what each section establishes deprives them of the experience of finding out.

Professional readers are the opposite on every count. They did not choose the document, they will not finish it, and their experience is not the product: a decision is. For that reader the scaffolding is the most useful part, because it is what lets them extract the argument without reading the prose, and lecture 3 argued that the heading layer is what most of them actually receive.

So Minto wins for the documents this collection is about, and knowing McPhee's position tells you what you are paying for the win. You are giving up the experience of a reader discovering something, which is a real loss in the small number of professional documents that are genuinely meant to be read: a thematic review that hopes to change how people think, a paper making an unfamiliar argument, a piece of internal writing that wants to persuade rather than to inform. In those, borrowing from McPhee is defensible, and it should be a decision rather than a default.

Structure-first, and why nobody does it

McPhee's practice of settling the structure before drafting is the most clearly correct advice in this lecture and the least followed, and the reasons are worth naming because they are not laziness.

Drafting produces visible output and structuring does not. An afternoon spent on a structural diagram yields one page that looks like nothing, and an afternoon spent drafting yields eight pages that look like progress, even when the eight pages will be discarded. In an organisation that reviews drafts rather than plans, the incentive points the wrong way, and the writer who structures first has nothing to show at the first check-in.

There is also a genuine intellectual objection. You sometimes do not know the structure until you have written enough to discover what you think, which is the exploratory case lecture 6 identified. Structure-first assumes the analysis is complete, and where it is not, drafting is a legitimate way of finding out. The honest version of McPhee's advice is therefore narrower: structure first when you know your findings, and where you do not, draft to discover them and then structure before writing the document anyone will read. What is never right is drafting the deliverable while still working out what it says.

Where this leaves the module

Seven lectures have supplied the opening as a contract, hierarchy and arcs as two independent properties, Minto's three rules, the entry into the pyramid, and now the spines for documents the pyramid cannot hold. One thing remains and it is the layer most writers treat as somebody else's job: headings, lists, tables and the shape of the page. Lecture 8 argues that this layer is part of the meaning rather than a presentation of it, which is the same claim this lecture just made about structure and the same one module 3 made about punctuation.

Common failure Adopting a thematic structure because the material has themes. All material has themes; that is what analysis produces. The question is whether the reader needs them as the spine, and the default answer is no, because a theme is something you found and a chronology is something they already have. If you choose themes, you have taken on an orientation cost in every section, and you should be able to say what the theme structure delivers that the process order would not.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a long document you have written or must write and draw both spines: the chronological one available in the process it describes, and the thematic one you would otherwise choose. For each section in the thematic version, write the orientation sentence it would need. Then decide.
  2. Name McPhee's three transferable claims and the one that does not transfer. What exactly does the fourth one cost a professional reader?
  3. Define orientation cost. What is it for a chronological spine, a thematic spine, and a departure?
  4. Why is a model lifecycle a chronology in disguise? Give another example from your own field.
  5. Give the two-part departure test. Which part do most professional flashbacks fail, and what does that failure indicate?
  6. State the McPhee and Minto conflict without softening it, then the resolution. On what four counts does a professional reader differ from McPhee's?
  7. What do you give up by choosing Minto? Name a document type where borrowing from McPhee is defensible.
  8. Why does nobody structure first? Give the incentive reason and the genuine intellectual objection, and state the narrower version of the advice that survives both.
