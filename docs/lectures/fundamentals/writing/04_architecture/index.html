Module 4 · Writing track

Architecture

Structuring documents and arguments. At document scale the reader's question changes from what does this sentence mean to why am I reading this and where is it going. Eight lectures on answering the second question, for a reader who will not read to the end.

What this module is

This is the professional writer's module, and the one most directly useful to anyone who produces validation reports, model documentation, board papers or committee submissions. Its centrepiece is Barbara Minto's Pyramid Principle, which is the most widely used structural framework in business writing and the most widely misunderstood.

The pyramid sits here rather than at the front of the course for a reason. Taught first, as consulting firms teach it, it arrives as an arbitrary house convention: put the answer at the top because that is how we do things. Taught after module 3, it arrives as something else entirely. The given-new contract says a reader attaches new information to information already held. A reader opening a fifty-page report holds nothing, so the first thing you owe them is the conclusion, which then becomes the given information that every subsequent section attaches to. The pyramid is the document-scale form of the same reader expectation. Everything else in Minto, the vertical question and answer dialogue, the split between deductive and inductive grouping, MECE, follows from that.

The module then widens. Pinker's arcs of coherence supply the model for texts too long to hold as a single pyramid, McPhee supplies the structural repertoire of long-form nonfiction, and the final lecture deals with the layer most writers treat as someone else's job: headings, tables, lists and the visible shape of the page.

Prerequisites

  • Module 3, lecture 2: the given-new contract. The whole justification for answer-first structure rests on it.
  • Module 3, lecture 5: paragraph design. The single-function rule scales up from the paragraph to the section here.

Module sequence

Part 1 · Openings and the shape of a whole text (lectures 1 to 2)

What the first page owes the reader, and how a text longer than a pyramid holds together.

  • builtLecture 1: the opening as a contract. Shared context, destabilising condition, point, and the key concepts that will thread through everything after. What the reader is entitled to assume once you have written an opening, and what breaking that assumption costs.
  • builtLecture 2: arcs of coherence. Pinker's model of a text as an ordered tree of sections crossed by arcs that track topics, points, actors and themes. Hierarchy and continuity as two separate problems that most outlining advice conflates.

Part 2 · The Pyramid Principle (lectures 3 to 6)

Minto in four parts: the two logics, the grouping test, and the narrative entry.

  • builtLecture 3: vertical logic. Every idea summarises those beneath it and every point raises a question the level below answers. The pyramid as a question and answer dialogue with the reader rather than a filing scheme.
  • builtLecture 4: horizontal logic. Deductive chains against inductive groupings, and why mixing them inside a single group produces an argument the reader cannot check.
  • builtLecture 5: MECE and the discipline of grouping. Mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive as the test for whether a group is a real group. The three failure modes: overlap, a residual "other" category, and the unexamined list of five or more items that turns out to have no organising idea.
  • builtLecture 6: SCQA and the narrative opening. Situation, Complication, Question, Answer. Why answer-first is the professional default, and the specific cases where it should be broken.

Part 3 · Beyond the pyramid (lectures 7 to 8)

Structures the pyramid cannot supply, and the layer of the document that is not prose at all.

  • builtLecture 7: structure in long-form nonfiction. McPhee on chronology against theme, the structural diagram drawn before drafting, the use of flashback, and the governing principle that readers are not supposed to notice the structure at all.
  • builtLecture 8: headings, lists, tables and the visual layer. Schriver and Redish on document design as part of meaning rather than decoration applied afterwards. Headings as a navigable outline, when a table beats a paragraph, and why bullet points so often destroy the argument they were meant to clarify.

Where this reappears

Answer-first
The default here. Module 5, lecture 7 gives Klinkenborg's argument that the whole outlining apparatus is a way of avoiding the work of writing sentences, and asks you to decide.
Arrangement
Treated pragmatically in this module. Module 7, lecture 5 sets the pyramid against the six-part classical oration and asks when each is the better instrument.
Structure as a discovery process
McPhee's structural diagram in lecture 7 assumes you know your material before you shape it. Module 9, lecture 2 challenges that with the evidence on knowledge-transforming.
