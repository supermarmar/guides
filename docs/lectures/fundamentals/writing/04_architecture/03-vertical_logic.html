Vertical logic

Minto's pyramid is not a filing scheme. It is a question and answer dialogue: every claim you make raises a question in the reader's mind, and the level below exists to answer it. Which means every heading in your document should be an assertion, and almost none of them are.

Lecture 3 of 8 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: Minto, The Pyramid Principle
Step 1

The 30-second version

Minto's pyramid has three rules. Ideas at any level must be summaries of the ideas grouped beneath them. Ideas in each grouping must be the same kind of idea. Ideas in each grouping must be logically ordered. This lecture is about the first, which governs the vertical relationship between a level and the level below it.

The mechanism is a dialogue. You assert something; the reader's mind produces a question, almost always why? or how?; the next level down answers it. That is what a pyramid is: not a hierarchy of topics but a controlled sequence of questions you have anticipated. It gives you a test that decides whether a section belongs where it sits, which is to name the question its parent raises that it answers. If you cannot name one, the section is in the wrong place or should not exist.

The consequence that changes documents most is about headings. If each level summarises what is beneath it, a heading must be an assertion, because a summary is a claim. "Findings" is not a summary of anything; it is a label for a container. The practical test is to read a document's headings and nothing else: with assertions you get the argument, and with labels you get a filing structure. Almost all professional documents use labels, which means their tables of contents convey nothing.

Feynman test Imagine the reader can interrupt you exactly once per claim, and always with the same two words: "why?" or "how?". Your document is your answer to the sequence of interruptions you expect. If a section answers a question nobody would have asked at that point, it does not belong there, however relevant it is to the subject.
What is beyond the source Minto supplies the three rules, the dialogue framing and the insistence on assertion headings. What this lecture adds is the argument in step 5 that the label habit is not laziness but risk aversion, since a label commits to nothing and an assertion can be wrong, and the observation that this makes the heading test a test of intellectual courage rather than of technique. The honest limits in step 5, that the pyramid presumes you have an answer and presumes there is one, are also not in Minto, who treats those as given.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that vertical logic supplies the only test that can tell you a section does not belong. Every other criterion you might use, relevance, importance, completeness, will justify almost any section: of course the data description is relevant to a validation report. The question test is stricter. If your point is that the stress window excludes severe downturns, the reader's question is how you know, and a data description that does not answer that question belongs in an appendix rather than in the argument.

The second reason is that the dialogue framing gives you the order as well as the contents. Once you know the question, you know what has to come first, because a question cannot be answered before it is raised. Documents that feel badly ordered usually contain a section answering a question the reader has not yet been given a reason to ask, which reads as a digression however well written it is.

The third reason is the heading test, and it is the highest-value single change available to most professional writers. A reader who reads only your headings should get your argument. With label headings they get a filing scheme, so the entire navigational layer of the document is doing no work, and every reader who skims, which is most of them, has received nothing. Converting labels to assertions costs an hour on a fifty-page report and changes what the document communicates to its largest group of readers.

And a reason about your own thinking. Writing an assertion heading forces you to know what the section concludes. Writing "Findings" does not, and a section headed "Findings" can be drafted before you have any. The label habit therefore permits a document to be structured before it is understood, which is the same failure lecture 1 identified in openings written first.

The mental model

The dialogue in the figure is worth reading in order rather than as a diagram, because the sequence is the point. The top claim raises why. Three answers appear. Each answer raises its own question, and the questions differ: section 2's answer invites how do you know, section 3's invites how was that computed, section 4's invites why not. Those are three different demands, and a document that answered all three with the same kind of material would have misread what its own claims provoke.

Vertical logic
The relationship between a level and the level beneath it: the upper summarises the lower, and the lower answers the question the upper raises. Minto's first rule, and the one that decides whether a section belongs.
Summary against label
A summary is a claim about what is beneath it and can be wrong. A label names a container and cannot. "Three defects understate capital" is a summary; "Findings" is a label.
The question test
For any section, name the question its parent raises that this section answers. No nameable question means the section is misplaced, belongs in an appendix, or should not exist.
Step 4

The detail

The questions a claim can raise

QuestionWhat the claim wasWhat the level below must supply
The two that account for most of it
Why? An assertion the reader might not accept: "the calibration understates". Reasons or evidence. Each child is a ground for believing the parent.
How? A recommendation or a process claim: "recalibrate against a 2008 window". Steps or components. Each child is part of doing the thing.
The three that catch the rest
How much? How do you know? A quantified claim: "40 basis points". The derivation, the data, the comparison. Sub-case of "why" with a numerical answer.
Which ones? Where exactly? A scoped claim: "the affected exposures". An enumeration or a definition of the boundary. Frequently the honest answer is a table rather than prose.
So what? A finding stated without consequence: "the window is short". The consequence. A claim that provokes only "so what" is usually a claim stated at the wrong level: it belongs beneath something else, not above anything.

The last row is a genuine diagnostic rather than a curiosity. If the only question a section heading raises is so what, the section is not a summary of anything: it is a piece of evidence that has been promoted above its station. "The stress window spans 2011 to 2023" is a fact, and a reader meeting it as a section heading has no question except why they are being told. Demote it under the claim it supports, and the pyramid corrects itself.

Building the pyramid from an existing draft

RETROFITTING A PYRAMID You have a draft with label headings. Procedure: 1. For each section, write one sentence stating what it concludes. Not what it covers. What it concludes. 2. Any section where you cannot write that sentence is one of three things: (a) evidence, which belongs under another section (b) background, which belongs in an appendix or in the opening's shared context (c) nothing, which belongs deleted 3. Lay the conclusion sentences out in a row. Ask what single sentence they collectively support. That is your top-level claim, and if you cannot write it, the document has no argument and you have found that out cheaply. 4. Now check the question links. For each conclusion, name the question the top claim raises that it answers. 5. Replace each label heading with its conclusion sentence. WORKED, ON THE FIVE-SECTION REPORT 2. Methodology -> concludes: "The stress window excludes every severe downturn." KEEP, answers "why". 3. Data -> concludes: nothing. It describes the data set. CASE (a): the arrears series belongs under section 2 as its evidence; the exposure data belongs under section 3's quantification. The section disappears and its content is redistributed. 4. Findings -> concludes two things: the 40bp understatement AND the control failure. SPLIT into two sections, each with its own assertion heading. 5. Recommendations -> concludes: "Recalibrate before the next return." KEEP, answers "how" / "what now". RESULT Sections: 5 -> 5, but not the same five. One dissolved, one split, and every heading now carries a claim. Note what step 3 does. If the conclusion sentences do not collectively support a single claim, you have a document with two arguments in it, and lecture 5 will tell you that is a grouping failure rather than a presentational one.

The heading test as a review tool

Reading only the headings is the fastest useful check on someone else's document, and it takes under a minute on a fifty-page report. Three outcomes, each diagnostic.

If the headings read as a connected argument, the document has vertical logic and you can review the evidence. This is rare enough to be worth remarking on when you find it.

If the headings are labels, you have learned nothing about the document and something about its author's process: the structure was fixed before the findings existed. The right review comment is not "improve the headings" but "what does section 4 conclude?", which is a question about content that the heading should have answered.

If the headings are assertions and do not connect, you have found the more interesting failure. Each section concludes something, and the conclusions do not add up to a single claim, which means the document contains two or more arguments presented as one. That is a structural problem no amount of editing will fix and it is precisely what lecture 5's grouping discipline addresses.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Why labels persist, and it is not laziness

The obvious explanation for label headings is that they are easier, and it is insufficient, because writing "The stress window excludes every severe downturn" is not harder than writing "Methodology" once you know it. Something else keeps the habit alive.

A label commits to nothing. An assertion can be wrong, and a wrong assertion in a heading is conspicuous: it sits in the table of contents, it appears in the navigation pane, and anyone who disagrees with it can say so without reading the section. A label is unfalsifiable, so it cannot be attacked, and in an organisation where being wrong in writing has consequences, that is a rational preference rather than a careless one.

Which reframes the heading test. It is presented as a technique and it functions as a test of whether you are prepared to put your conclusions where they can be challenged. That is worth saying explicitly, because it explains why the advice is so widely known and so rarely followed. Everyone who has read Minto knows headings should be assertions. The reason documents do not have them is not that the writers forgot.

The honest counterweight is that there are situations where a label is the right choice, and they are narrower than the habit suggests. A reference document that readers navigate rather than read should have labels, because the reader is looking for a topic rather than following an argument. An appendix should have labels. And a document whose conclusions are genuinely provisional, circulated for challenge before they harden, can legitimately use labels to signal that nothing is settled, though saying so in the opening would be better.

What the pyramid presumes

Two presumptions, both usually satisfied and both worth knowing, because when they fail the pyramid actively misleads.

The first is that you have an answer. A pyramid is built downwards from a claim, so a document with no claim cannot be pyramided, and forcing one produces a false top: a sentence that sounds like a conclusion and is really a topic. Genuinely exploratory work, a research note, a first look at a data set, an options paper of the kind lecture 1 described, does not have a claim at the top and should not pretend to. The right structure there is a question at the top rather than an answer, with the sections being what you found, and that is a legitimate variant rather than a failed pyramid.

The second is that there is one answer. A pyramid has a single apex, so a document with two genuinely independent findings does not fit: either you find a higher claim that both support, which is real intellectual work and often the right move, or you write two documents. What you must not do is invent a bland apex that technically contains both, of the "several matters arise from the annual review" variety, because that top is a label wearing a sentence's clothing and it puts the document's real claims a level lower than they deserve.

Where this leaves the module

Lecture 2 gave hierarchy and arcs as two independent properties. This lecture has formalised the hierarchy half: a well-formed tree is one in which every node summarises its children and answers a question its parent raised. Lecture 4 takes the horizontal relationship, meaning how the children of a single parent relate to each other, and lecture 5 gives the test for whether they form a real group at all.

It is worth noticing that the question test and the arc check can disagree, and when they do the question test wins. A section that answers no question raised above does not belong, even if it maintains three arcs beautifully. Continuity is not a licence to include material, and a document can be perfectly threaded and still contain a section that is there because someone asked for it.

Common failure Writing assertion headings that assert something the section does not establish. This is lecture 1's overclaiming problem moved into the navigation layer, and it is worse there, because a heading is read by everyone and its section by few. A heading claiming "the benchmark cannot detect this" over a section that shows only that the benchmark did not detect it has put a stronger claim into every skimming reader's memory than the evidence supports. Match the heading to the section, and if the section is weaker, weaken the heading.

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Retrofit a pyramid onto a document you wrote, following the five-step procedure in step 4. Report which sections dissolved, which split, and whether your conclusion sentences supported a single top-level claim.
  2. State Minto's three rules and say which one this lecture is about.
  3. Give the question test. What are the three possible diagnoses when a section answers no nameable question?
  4. Distinguish a summary from a label. What is the quick diagnostic for spotting a label heading?
  5. Take the five questions in step 4's table. Which one indicates that a section has been promoted above its station, and what is the repair?
  6. Run the heading test on a document you did not write. Which of the three outcomes did you get, and what does the third one tell you that the second does not?
  7. Why do label headings persist, if writing an assertion is not harder? What does that make the heading test a test of?
  8. Name the two things the pyramid presumes. For each, describe a document where the presumption fails and what should be done instead.
