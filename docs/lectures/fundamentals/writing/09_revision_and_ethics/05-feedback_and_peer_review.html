Feedback and peer review

A reader is the world's leading authority on their experience of your document and has no special standing on how to fix it. Ask for symptoms, not remedies, and ask at the pass where the answer can still be acted on.

Lecture 5 of 8 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: Graham and Perin (2007); Elbow
Step 1

The 30-second version

The distinction that makes feedback useful is between descriptive and evaluative. Evaluative feedback reports a judgement: this is good, section 3 does not work, the tone is off. Descriptive feedback reports an experience: I slowed down here, I re-read this sentence, at this point I expected the finding and got a table.

Descriptive wins for a specific reason rather than a diplomatic one. A reader is genuinely authoritative about their own experience of reading and has no special standing on what would fix it, so asking them to diagnose invites them to prescribe, and their prescriptions are typically worse than the problem they detected. Symptoms are not arguable; remedies are.

Timing matters as much as form, and lecture 1 said why. Feedback has to arrive at the pass that can still act on it, which means structural comment on a one-page outline before drafting, and local comment on the draft. Two gates with different names, because a reviewer who is handed a finished document will supply the cheap half whatever you asked for.

The empirical case for doing any of this is stronger than most writing advice can claim. In Graham and Perin's meta-analysis, collaborative arrangements score 0.75 and specific product goals 0.70, against minus 0.32 for traditional grammar instruction. Those are among the larger effects in the analysis, and the practical translation of the second is that the best feedback you can give is a specific product goal rather than an assessment.

Feynman test Do not ask whether it was clear. Ask where they slowed down, what they thought it was about, and what they would do about it. They know those things and they do not know how to fix your document. And ask before you have written it, because after is too late to change anything that matters.
What is beyond the source The descriptive-evaluative distinction is standard in the writing-workshop tradition and the effect sizes are Graham and Perin's, reported with the caveat in step 5 that they come from school-age studies. Three arguments are the lecture's own. The first is the authority argument: readers are authoritative on symptoms and not on remedies, which is why the question form matters more than the reviewer's skill. The second is that "is it clear?" is unanswerable, since a reader can only report whether they understood, and saying no means admitting confusion. The third is the permission move in step 5, which removes the reviewer's structural incentive to be kind.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that you cannot see your own document. Module 6's lecture 4 established that the curse of knowledge is not defeated by effort, and lecture 4 of this module added that you read your intention rather than your text until the intention has faded. A reader is the only instrument that measures what the document actually does, and using it badly wastes the one measurement available.

The second reason is that professional review is systematically the wrong shape, and lecture 3 gave the evidence: thirty-four wording changes and no structural comment. The cause is structural rather than personal, so the remedy has to be structural too, which means changing when you ask and what you ask for rather than hoping for better reviewers.

The third reason is that peer assistance is one of the best-supported interventions in the writing research, which is unusual for something so cheap. Fifteen minutes of a colleague's attention on an outline is not a courtesy or a compliance step; on the available evidence it is among the highest-yield things you can do to a document.

And a reason about receiving. Most people handle feedback badly in a predictable way, by defending the document against a report of somebody's experience, which is a category error: if they say they slowed down, they slowed down. Separating symptom from remedy makes the conversation tractable and removes most of what makes it uncomfortable.

Step 3

The mental model

What a reader can and cannot know, the two gates, and the effect sizes that justify bothering.

Descriptive feedback
A report of the reader's experience: where they slowed, re-read, lost the thread, or formed an expectation that was not met. Data rather than judgement, and the only kind of feedback the reader cannot be wrong about.
Evaluative feedback
A report of the reader's judgement about quality. Cheap to give, hard to act on, and systematically distorted upwards in professional settings by the reviewer's reasonable wish not to create work for a colleague.
Specific product goal
An instruction about what the finished document should contain or achieve, such as "state the finding in the first three sentences" or "add the two strongest objections". Scores 0.70 in Graham and Perin, and is the most actionable form feedback can take.
Step 4

The detail

The same review, asked three ways

WHAT YOU ASK DETERMINES WHAT YOU GET. ASK: "Any comments on this?" GET: "Looks good. A couple of small things in section 3." Cost to reviewer: 5 minutes. Value: nil. You have learned that a colleague is polite. ASK: "Is it clear?" GET: "Yes, I followed it." Unanswerable as posed. They can report that they understood; they cannot report whether a different reader would, and answering no means confessing confusion about a colleague's technical work. You have learned nothing and made them uneasy. ASK: "Three things. After page one, what did you think this was about? Where did you slow down or re-read? And what would you do as a result of reading it?" GET: "I thought it was about data quality until page four. I re-read the paragraph at the top of page three twice. And I would ask the model owner for a rebuild plan, though I am not sure by when." THREE FINDINGS, IN NINE MINUTES: - The document announces the wrong subject for three pages. That is a pass-one structural fault and the reader located it precisely. - One paragraph fails. Now diagnose it yourself, which you can and they cannot. - The ask has no date. The reader has told you the action is unclear without knowing that is what they told you. NOTE WHAT THE THIRD VERSION DID NOT ASK It did not ask for a diagnosis, a judgement, or a fix. Every question is about the reader's own experience, which is the one subject on which they cannot be mistaken.

The third answer illustrates why descriptive questions outperform expert reviewers asked badly. The reader did not know they had identified a structural fault, a paragraph-level fault and a missing deadline; they simply reported what happened to them. Diagnosis is the writer's job and this course is sixty-five lectures of equipment for it. Detection is the reader's, and they are better at it than you will ever be on your own document.

Giving feedback well

Instead ofSayWhy it works
Converting judgements into experiences and goals
"This section is confusing." "I re-read the second paragraph twice and still could not tell whether the override was authorised." Locates the fault and names what the reader failed to extract, which tells the author what the paragraph was supposed to do and did not.
"The structure needs work." "I reached your finding on page 11. Get it onto page 1." A specific product goal. The author knows exactly what success looks like and retains the choice of how.
"The tone is too strong." "Reading this as the model owner, I would come to the meeting prepared to defend myself rather than to fix it." Reports a predicted reaction from a named position rather than asserting a property of the text. Arguable in a productive way.
"You should cut section 2." "I skipped section 2 and did not miss it later." Evidence rather than a prescription. If the author disagrees they now know the cost of keeping it.
"Good paper, well written." "I could restate your finding from memory afterwards: forty basis points, short window, rebuild before the capital return." Reports that the document achieved its purpose, with the evidence. Praise that names what worked is repeatable; praise that does not is noise.

Receiving feedback

One rule handles most of it: you may argue about remedies and you may not argue about symptoms. If a reader reports slowing down at a paragraph, they slowed down, and no explanation of what the paragraph was meant to do changes the datum. What is open is whether the cause is the paragraph, the one before it, or a structural decision three pages earlier, and that is a diagnostic question you are better placed to answer than they are.

That rule defuses most of the discomfort, because it removes the implied criticism. A report that a reader re-read a sentence is not a claim that the writer is careless; it is an instrument reading. Writers who take feedback badly are usually responding to an evaluation that was not offered, and asking descriptive questions makes that misreading harder for everybody.

The one case where a symptom can be set aside is when the reader is outside the document's audience. Module 7's lecture 8 built a reader model with four positions, and a colleague from a different function may slow down at a passage that its intended reader will not. That is a legitimate discount and it should be applied explicitly, by naming which reader you were writing for, rather than used as a general defence.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Reviewers are structurally incentivised to be kind, and you can remove the incentive

Lecture 3 attributed the wording-comment review to when the gate sits, which is most of the explanation and not all of it. The rest is that a structural comment on a colleague's finished draft creates work for them, possibly a great deal, and possibly to no purpose if the deadline makes the rebuild impossible. A reviewer weighing that will supply the useful cheap half, and they are behaving considerately rather than lazily.

Which means the fix is to remove the reason for kindness, out loud, and it takes one sentence. "I have a day in hand and I would rather rebuild this than ship it wrong, so please tell me if the structure is off." That changes the calculation, because the reviewer now knows the structural comment is affordable, and it also grants permission, which is the part people find hard to give and easy to receive.

The converse holds and should be said plainly. If you do not have a day in hand, do not ask for structural feedback, because you are asking a colleague to spend their attention producing something you cannot act on, and they will notice. Ask for the local pass and say so. Feedback requests should be honest about what the writer can still change.

There is a further move worth naming for anyone who reviews often. Ask the author, before reading, what they most want checked and what they can still change. Thirty seconds of that converts a generic review into a targeted one, and it saves the reviewer from guessing at the affordable scope, which is what produces the wording pass by default.

What the effect sizes do and do not license

The Writing Next figures are the strongest empirical support this course cites and they need their limits stated, since the course has claimed testability throughout and this is the place where over-reading would be easiest.

The studies are overwhelmingly of school-age writers, roughly grades four to twelve, measuring writing quality on rated scales in controlled comparisons. Transferring an effect size from that population to a credit risk professional writing a validation report is an inference and not a finding. The direction of the effects is considerably more transferable than their magnitude: it is reasonable to believe that structured collaboration helps and that teaching grammar as rules does not, and it is not reasonable to expect 0.75 from asking a colleague to read your paper.

A second limit matters more practically. The collaborative writing element covers structured arrangements with defined procedures and roles, closer to co-authoring and formal peer response than to a sign-off gate, and unstructured peer review performs considerably less well in the underlying literature. So the figure supports the two-gate practice with named questions rather than supporting review in general, which is fortunate, since named questions are what this lecture recommends.

And the product-goals result is the one to act on hardest, because it is both well supported and almost free. Giving a writer a specific target for the finished document, rather than an assessment of the current one, costs a sentence and appears to do more work than any amount of commentary.

When there is no reader available

The honest gap in this lecture is that the recommended instrument is another person, and sometimes there is not one. The material is confidential to a small team, the only qualified colleague is the person the finding concerns, or nobody has fifteen minutes before the deadline.

The substitutes are worth knowing and worth being pessimistic about. Lecture 4's cooling period plus the first-sentence sweep gives you a partial stranger's view of your own structure. Reading aloud catches what the ear notices and the eye forgives. Writing down, before rereading, what you expect the document to do, then checking, approximates the expectation questions. All three help and none of them measures a reader, because the thing you cannot simulate is not knowing your own content.

Which leaves one recommendation that solves the availability problem structurally. Find one person, outside your immediate team and inside your general field, and trade with them regularly. A standing arrangement to read each other's outlines for fifteen minutes is worth more than any amount of occasional review, because the reciprocity removes the imposition, and because a reader who has seen your last six documents knows which faults are yours.

Common failure Asking descriptive questions and then explaining. A reader reports slowing down and the writer says what the paragraph was meant to convey, at which point the reader has been told the answer and can no longer report a naive reading of anything downstream. The explanation is also almost always the thing the paragraph should have said. Take the report, write it down, say thank you, and diagnose it afterwards on your own.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. On your next document, ask the three questions from step 4's third version, and add the permission sentence from step 5. Report what the reader found and how long it took them.
  2. Then compose. Convert five evaluative comments you have given or received into descriptive ones or product goals, using step 4's table as the pattern.
  3. Distinguish descriptive from evaluative feedback and say why the first is preferable, in terms of authority rather than diplomacy.
  4. Why is "is it clear?" unanswerable? What does a reader actually report when asked it?
  5. Give four descriptive questions worth asking. Which is the most uncomfortable and why is it the most useful?
  6. Describe the two gates, and say why most processes have only the second.
  7. What is a specific product goal, what does it score, and why does it beat commentary?
  8. State the rule for receiving feedback. When may a symptom legitimately be discounted, and how should that be done?
  9. Why are reviewers incentivised to be kind, and what one sentence removes the incentive? What is the converse obligation?
  10. State the two limits on transferring the Writing Next effect sizes. Which travels better, direction or magnitude?
  11. Why does the collaborative-writing figure support named questions rather than review in general?
  12. What are the substitutes when no reader is available, and what can none of them simulate?
