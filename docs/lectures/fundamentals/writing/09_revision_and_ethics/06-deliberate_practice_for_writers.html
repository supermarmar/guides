Deliberate practice for writers

Professional writing has the worst feedback structure of any skill you practise daily: aggregate, delayed and confounded. That predicts a plateau regardless of volume, and it is why drills with a measurable target beat writing more.

Lecture 6 of 8 Reading time, 32 minutes Source: Ericsson et al. (1993); Macnamara et al. (2014); Graham and Perin
Step 1

The 30-second version

Deliberate practice, in Ericsson's sense, is not repetition. It requires a well-defined target, full attention, immediate informative feedback, repetition with refinement, and working at the edge of current ability. Ordinary writing supplies the second and the fifth occasionally and the third almost never.

That is the mechanism behind the plateau, and it is worth stating precisely because it is not about motivation. You write a document, it is approved, and you learn nothing about which of the several hundred decisions inside it were good ones. The feedback is aggregate, delayed by weeks, and confounded by everything else that determined the outcome. No skill improves on a signal like that, however much of it you receive.

The exercises that work therefore share one property: a feedback signal that is objective rather than a judgement. Lanham's lard factor is a number. Whether you can point at something is a yes or no. Whether a reader can restate your finding is a yes or no. Prefer drills whose result you can read off, because you do not have a coach.

And the design principle that organises them: each drill isolates one pass from lecture 4's protocol. Six passes, six families of exercise, fifteen minutes three times a week, on material that is not your real work, because a deadline defeats the requirement to operate at the edge of your ability.

Feynman test You cannot get better at something when the only feedback is "the report was approved". So practise one thing at a time, on something that does not matter, where you can measure whether you did it. Writing more documents mostly teaches you to write the documents you already write.
What is beyond the source Ericsson's criteria, the Macnamara critique and the Writing Next figures are all reported as they stand, and step 5 gives the professions result that is most damaging to the strong claim. Three arguments are the lecture's own. The first is the diagnosis of professional writing as a domain with an unusually poor feedback structure, which predicts a plateau from the mechanism rather than from a lack of effort. The second is the mapping of the six revision passes onto six drill families, so that practice is organised by what it isolates. The third is the filter in step 4 that a drill is only worth doing if its feedback is objective, because a self-coached writer cannot supply a judgement they do not already have.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that most professionals plateau in writing after a few years and attribute it to talent or to having no time. The mechanism in step 1 offers a better explanation and a more actionable one: the feedback signal available in normal work is too weak to support improvement, so improvement requires manufacturing a better signal, which is cheap.

The second reason is that this course has now supplied a great deal of diagnosis and one procedure, and neither addresses the fact that applying diagnosis is slow. Module 8's lecture 7 distinguished slow deliberate diagnosis from fast recognition and observed that only reading builds the second. Drills are the other route: repeated deliberate application is how a diagnostic check becomes automatic, which is the only way the protocol's passes get faster.

The third reason is the honest one about scope. Deliberate practice will make you measurably better at the specific thing you drill and will not make you a great writer, and the reason it cannot is lecture 8's subject. Being clear about which half of the problem drilling addresses stops it from becoming another form of the avoidance lecture 4 warned about.

And a reason for anyone who supervises. Sentence combining scores 0.50 in Graham and Perin, which is the highest of the content-focused elements they identify, and it is a drill rather than an instructional philosophy. If you are trying to improve a team's writing, the evidence points at exercises with targets rather than at style guides and courses.

Step 3

The mental model

Ericsson's five conditions against what ordinary writing supplies, and the six drill families that fill the gap.

Deliberate practice
Ericsson's construct: practice with a well-defined target, full attention, immediate informative feedback, repetition with refinement, at the edge of current ability. Distinguished from experience, which is repetition without the other four.
Lard factor
Lanham's measure: the proportion of words removed by revision, computed as words cut divided by original length. An objective feedback signal for a concision drill, which is what makes the exercise self-coachable.
Sentence combining
Joining several short sentences into one that retains all the information. The highest-scoring content-focused element in Graham and Perin at 0.50, and a drill rather than a doctrine.
Step 4

The detail

Two drills, worked

SENTENCE COMBINING · fifteen minutes START (five short sentences, deliberately flat): The estimation window runs from 2011 to 2023. Its worst arrears rate is 1.4 per cent. The model policy defines a downturn at 2.5 per cent. No year in the window reaches that level. The window therefore contains no downturn. TARGET: one sentence. All five facts retained. Subject adjacent to verb. The conclusion in the stress position. ATTEMPT 1 (35 words) "Because the estimation window running from 2011 to 2023 has a worst arrears rate of 1.4 per cent, which is below the 2.5 per cent the policy requires, the window contains no downturn." CHECK: subject "window" is 6 words from its verb. Conclusion is at the end. Good. But "which is below" is a slack relative clause. ATTEMPT 2 (28 words) "The estimation window's worst arrears rate, 1.4 per cent across 2011 to 2023, falls short of the policy's 2.5 per cent downturn threshold: the window contains no downturn." CHECK: subject and verb now separated by 9 words by the apposition. Worse on that test, better on length. ATTEMPT 3 (24 words) "Across 2011 to 2023 the estimation window peaks at 1.4 per cent arrears, below the policy's 2.5 per cent downturn threshold, so it contains no downturn." CHECK: subject "window" adjacent to "peaks". All five facts present. Conclusion last. 24 words from 5 sentences and 41 original words. SIGNAL: 41 -> 24 words, all information retained, both module 2 tests passed. Objective, and it took a quarter of an hour. WHY THREE ATTEMPTS RATHER THAN ONE Ericsson's fourth condition. One attempt is a task; three with a check between them is practice. THE PARAMEDIC DRILL · ten minutes START (institutional, 39 words): "It was determined by the review team that there was an absence of adequate documentation in relation to the justification for the selection of the stress window, which is a requirement under the provisions of the model policy." STEP 1 Circle prepositions: by, of, in, to, for, of, under, of. Eight. STEP 2 Circle forms of "to be": was, was, is. Three. STEP 3 Where is the action? "documented" and "requires". STEP 4 Make them the verbs, with real subjects. STEP 5 Cut the windup: "It was determined that". RESULT (16 words): "The model policy requires the stress window choice to be documented. It is not documented." LARD FACTOR: (39 - 16) / 39 = 59 per cent. SIGNAL: a number. Institutional prose commonly yields 30 to 50 per cent; anything above that means the original was mostly scaffolding. Note the split into two sentences, which module 8's lecture 5 predicted: the requirement and the failure are two claims and were fused into one clause.

The arithmetic in both drills is the point. Forty-one words to twenty-four, and thirty-nine to sixteen for a lard factor of fifty-nine per cent, are measurements rather than impressions, and a writer who does this weekly accumulates a record of their own improvement that does not depend on anybody's opinion.

Choosing drills, and the filter that matters

DrillFeedback signalWorth doing?
The filter: is the signal objective, or does it require a judgement you do not have?
Sentence combining Word count, plus the mechanical module 2 tests Yes. Best-supported drill available, and entirely self-coachable.
Paramedic method Lard factor: a percentage Yes. The only writing exercise with a genuine numerical outcome.
Franklin's reconstruction Difference from the original, which you can enumerate Yes, and it is the broadest of the drills. Module 8's lecture 7 has the method.
Descend the abstraction ladder Can you point at something? Binary. Yes, and it is the only drill that reaches the content space at all.
Deliberate constraint (word or length limits) Did you meet the constraint? Binary. Read aloud for the rest. Yes for the constraint, weakly for the quality. Forces search, which is its value.
"Write a paragraph in the style of X" Your own judgement of resemblance No, alone. You cannot grade this, and a wrong self-assessment trains the error. Do it as reconstruction against a model instead.
"Write for thirty minutes a day" None No, as practice. It has other virtues, and it is the volume approach whose plateau this lecture is about.

The last two rows are the ones people default to, and both fail the filter for the same reason. A self-coached writer can only learn from a signal they cannot fake, and both a resemblance judgement and a daily word count are signals about effort rather than about the thing being practised.

Scheduling it

Fifteen minutes, three times a week, on material that is not your work. The last clause carries most of the weight: a deadline forces you to write what you can already write, which violates Ericsson's fifth condition directly, so practice on live documents is not practice. Use published papers, old drafts of your own, or the prose in front of you for the combining and paramedic drills.

Rotate by pass rather than by mood, since the drills you enjoy are the ones addressing skills you already have. A four-week rotation of structure, paragraphs, sentences, concision covers the passes that matter most and takes three hours in total, which is less than one document's revision.

And keep the numbers. A lard factor log across twenty attempts tells you whether you are improving at concision, which is information nothing else in your working life supplies, and it takes a line of a notebook per session.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The deliberate practice literature is contested, and the professions result is the worst case

This lecture rests on a construct that has taken a serious empirical beating since 1993, and the honest thing is to lead with the damage rather than footnote it.

Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald's 2014 meta-analysis asked how much variance in performance deliberate practice accounts for across domains, and the answer was far less than the strong version of the theory implies. Their figures were roughly twenty-six per cent for games, twenty-one for music, eighteen for sports, four for education, and one for professions. Writing sits somewhere between the last two categories, which is the least favourable place it could sit.

Their proposed explanation is worth taking seriously because it applies to writing exactly. Domains where deliberate practice explains a lot are ones with stable, well-defined tasks, clear performance measures and repeatable conditions. Chess has all three. Professional writing has none: the task changes every time, quality has no agreed measure, and conditions never repeat. So the prediction is that structured practice should help less in writing than in chess, and it is probably correct.

What survives that critique is narrower and sufficient. The strong claim, that accumulated deliberate practice explains expertise, is not supported. The weak claim, that isolated practice with immediate feedback beats aggregate feedback for acquiring a specific sub-skill, is well established in learning research independently of the expertise debate, and it is all this lecture uses. The drills in step 4 do not promise expertise; they promise that you will get better at combining sentences and at cutting institutional prose, and both of those are measurable.

This is the third time this module has made the same move, which is deliberate. The weak version of a contested construct is often enough to act on, and stating which version you are relying on is the difference between using research and citing it.

The plateau is partly a reasonable choice

Professionals stop improving at writing, and the diagnosis so far has been about feedback structure. There is a second reason and it is not a failure.

A professional whose writing is adequate for their role has little to gain from improving it. The documents get approved, the findings get actioned at roughly the rate the institution actions findings, and the marginal return on a better paragraph is small and hard to observe. Adequacy is a defensible stopping point, and a course that treats every plateau as a deficiency is asking people to invest in a skill whose returns they have correctly assessed as low.

Which means the honest case for practising is conditional rather than universal. It is worth it if you write documents whose failure has consequences you care about, if you are frequently in the position of needing to move a reader who does not want to be moved, or if you find the activity itself interesting. Those are real conditions and they do not describe everybody, and a reader for whom none holds is not making a mistake by stopping here.

What drilling cannot reach

Five of the six drill families operate on the rhetorical problem space, and the sixth reaches the content space only at its edge, by asking whether you can point at something. That distribution is not an oversight in the exercise design; it reflects that the content space cannot be drilled.

You cannot practise having something worth saying. You can practise stating it, ordering it, compressing it and making it land, and those are the six passes, and a writer who has drilled all of them is a writer whose good ideas reach readers intact. The quality of the ideas is a function of the analysis, the subject knowledge and the judgement, none of which a writing exercise touches.

So the honest summary of what this lecture offers is that fifteen minutes three times a week will make you measurably better at specific, isolable, checkable sub-skills, and will not make you a great writer. The reason it cannot is that greatness in writing turns out to depend on something outside writing, which is the argument lecture 8 closes the course with, and which is why the course ends on a question rather than a summary.

Common failure Drilling on live documents. It feels efficient and it violates the fifth condition, because a deadline forces you to write what you can already write rather than attempt what you cannot. Worse, a failed attempt on a real document has to be repaired rather than analysed, so the learning is discarded to meet the deadline. Practice belongs on material where failure costs nothing, which is what makes the fifteen minutes separable from the working day.

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Run the paramedic drill on three sentences of institutional prose, computing the lard factor for each. Report the three percentages.
  2. Then compose. Run the sentence-combining drill with three attempts and a check between them, as in step 4. Report the word counts and which module 2 test each attempt failed.
  3. Give Ericsson's five conditions and say which three ordinary professional writing fails.
  4. Why is the feedback available in normal work an unusually bad learning signal? Name its three defects.
  5. What is the selection filter for drills, and why does a self-coached writer need it?
  6. Give the six drill families and the pass each isolates.
  7. Define the lard factor and give the typical range on institutional prose. What does a figure above it mean?
  8. Why do "write in the style of X" and "write for thirty minutes a day" both fail the filter?
  9. Why must practice be on material that is not your real work?
  10. State the Macnamara result, including the professions figure, and the explanation offered for the pattern. Where does writing sit?

  13. What can drilling not reach, and why?
