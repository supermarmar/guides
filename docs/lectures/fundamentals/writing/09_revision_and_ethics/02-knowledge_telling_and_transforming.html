Lectures / Writing / Revision and ethics

Knowledge-telling and knowledge-transforming

Experts run a dialectic between what they are trying to say and how it must be said, and change their minds about the content as a result. Which means "just write up what you found" is a category error, and delegating the write-up degrades the analysis rather than only the prose.

Lecture 2 of 8 Reading time, 32 minutes Source: Bereiter and Scardamalia (1987)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Bereiter and Scardamalia describe two models of composing. Knowledge-telling takes a topic, retrieves what you know using topic and genre cues, writes it down, checks it looks appropriate, and retrieves more. The content passes through unchanged. Knowledge-transforming runs a dialectic between two problem spaces: a content space, where the question is what is true, and a rhetorical space, where the question is how to say it to this reader.

The output of each space becomes input to the other. Solving a rhetorical problem forces you back into the content, and the content change poses a new rhetorical problem. The writer's understanding of the subject therefore changes as a result of writing, which is why experienced writers say they write to find out what they think. That is a description of a process, not a pose.

The professional consequence is sharp. If the write-up is part of the analysis, then "tell me the answer and then document it" is a request to knowledge-tell, and it forfeits whatever the transforming would have found. So is handing the analysis to one person and the document to another.

Neither model is a stage and neither is always wrong. Telling is correct for a monthly monitoring report, where the content is routine and transformation is waste. The failure is telling when the problem needed transforming, and the diagnostic for that is checkable: did anything you wrote send you back to the data?

Feynman test Two ways to write. Dump what you know, or argue with yourself about what you know while trying to say it. The second changes what you know. So if you finish a hard document believing exactly what you believed before you started, the writing did nothing except record.
What is beyond the source The two models are Bereiter and Scardamalia's and are reported as they stand, including their insistence that these are strategies rather than developmental levels. Three arguments are the lecture's own. The first is the diagnostic in step 4, five checkable signs that a document was told rather than transformed. The second is the claim that separating analysis from write-up removes the loop and therefore degrades the analysis, which reframes a staffing decision as a methodological one. The third is step 5's resolution of Klinkenborg against Minto: each has described one problem space and mistaken it for the whole process.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this is the sharpest available account of what expertise in writing consists of, and it is not what people expect. It is not fluency, vocabulary or command of principles. It is the willingness and ability to let a rhetorical difficulty send you back into the substance, which means good writers are people whose documents change their minds.

The second reason is that it names something this course has demonstrated repeatedly without labelling it. The rebuttal in module 7's lecture 9 that converted the finding, the scenario narrative in module 8's lecture 3 that exposed an incoherent variable path, the warrant that turned out to be the disputed premise: each is a rhetorical problem forcing a content revision. Those were presented as techniques and they are instances of one process.

The third reason is the professional claim in step 1, which has consequences for how work is organised. If writing is part of thinking, then the common arrangement in which a senior person forms the conclusion and a junior person drafts the document has removed the loop from both of them: the senior never meets the rhetorical problems that would test the conclusion, and the junior has no authority to revise content. The document is worse and so is the analysis.

And a reason about time estimates. Transforming takes longer than telling and the extra time is not overhead, it is analysis. A plan that budgets writing as a fixed cost after the analysis is complete has assumed telling, and it will either overrun or produce a told document, and the second is the more common outcome because the deadline is real.

Step 3

The mental model

Two models side by side, with the loop that distinguishes them and a worked instance of it.

Knowledge-telling
Composing by retrieving content from memory on topic and genre cues and writing it down, with an appropriateness check but no transformation of the content. Efficient, and correct for routine work.
Content problem space
The space in which the questions are about the subject: what is true, what the evidence supports, what follows. Where analysis happens.
Rhetorical problem space
The space in which the questions are about the text and its reader: how to say it, what they will take from it, what they will resist. Where modules 1 to 8 of this course live, almost entirely.
Step 4

The detail

Five signs a document was told rather than transformed

RUN THIS AFTER FINISHING ANY NON-ROUTINE DOCUMENT. 1 THE CONCLUSION IS WHAT YOU THOUGHT BEFORE YOU STARTED. Not always damning. Sometimes you were right. But if it is true of every document you write, the loop is not running. 2 NOTHING SENT YOU BACK TO THE DATA. The strongest single indicator. In a transformed document, at least one sentence you tried to write turned out to require a figure you did not have, and you went and got it. 3 IT TOOK EXACTLY AS LONG AS YOU ESTIMATED. Transforming is unpredictable because you cannot schedule a discovery. A document that lands precisely on its estimate was, usually, a retrieval exercise. 4 YOU LEARNED NOTHING WRITING IT. Ask the question directly. If you cannot name one thing you understand better than you did a week ago, the document recorded your understanding rather than developing it. 5 YOU COULD HAVE DICTATED IT. The telling model is essentially dictation with an appropriateness filter. If the document could have been spoken into a recorder from your existing notes, it was told. WHAT TO DO IF SEVERAL ARE TRUE Not "write it again". The specific intervention is to force one rhetorical problem hard, and the most reliable is module 7's lecture 9: write out the full Toulmin structure of your central claim, including the warrant and the rebuttal. Warrants and rebuttals are rhetorical demands that cannot be met from the rhetorical space. They send you into the content, every time, which is exactly why they are the two parts writers omit.

Sign 3 deserves a note because it inverts a professional instinct. Hitting an estimate is normally a virtue, and here it is weak evidence that no analysis happened during the writing. The implication is not that overrunning is good; it is that a writing budget for a contestable document should include contingency for content work, because that work is going to appear and pretending otherwise means it gets skipped rather than scheduled.

The pattern is that the productive techniques in this course are all rhetorical demands that cannot be satisfied without returning to the substance. That is a useful selection criterion for any writing advice you meet: a technique that can be executed entirely within the rhetorical space will improve the prose, and a technique that forces you back into the content may improve the work.

The organisational consequence

If writing a contestable document is part of analysing it, then three common arrangements are methodological choices rather than administrative ones, and all three cost something.

The first is delegating the write-up. A senior analyst who forms the conclusion and hands drafting to someone junior has removed the loop from both: the senior never meets the rhetorical problems that would test the conclusion, and the junior lacks the standing to revise content when they do. The output is a document that reads acceptably and encodes an untested conclusion.

The second is pre-agreeing the finding. A conclusion settled in a meeting and then written up has been fixed before the rhetorical space could act on it. This is often done for good governance reasons, to avoid surprises, and the cost should be recognised: it converts the document into a record and forfeits the discovery.

The third is the slide sent ahead. Circulating the answer as a summary before the analysis is documented commits you publicly at the point where transformation is still possible, which makes reversal expensive. Everyone has experienced the finding that could not be revised because the summary had already gone out, and the mechanism is this one.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Klinkenborg and Minto were each describing one problem space

This course has carried an unresolved dispute since module 4 and can now settle it. Minto's method requires the structure to be settled before drafting: know your answer, build the pyramid, then write. Klinkenborg's objection, given its own lecture in module 5, is that this is backwards, because you find out what you have to say by writing sentences, and a structure imposed beforehand is a structure for material you do not yet possess.

Both are right about their half. Minto is describing the rhetorical problem space and describing it well: given content, the ordering, emphasis and reader-management problems are real and her apparatus solves them. Klinkenborg is describing the content space, and the observation that sentences generate content is exactly the loop Bereiter and Scardamalia model. Each has taken the space they work in for the whole process.

Which makes the practical resolution the one lecture 1 arrived at from the other direction. Write to discover, because that is content work and prose is the instrument for it. Then structure, because that is rhetorical work and the pyramid is the instrument for it. Then write the deliverable. The dispute was never about method; it was two writers generalising from the space each spends most time in, and the loop model contains both.

It is worth adding that the dispute is asymmetric in one respect. Minto's method applied to unformed content produces a confident document about nothing, which is a serious failure. Klinkenborg's method applied to settled content produces a slow, exploratory document that a busy reader cannot use, which is a lesser failure and easier to repair. If you must err, err towards discovery.

The models are strategies, not levels

Bereiter and Scardamalia are careful about this and it is routinely lost in secondary accounts. Knowledge-telling is not the novice model that experts have outgrown. It is a strategy, and expert writers use it constantly and correctly, because most writing does not require transformation and running the loop on a status update is waste.

What distinguishes an expert is therefore not that they always transform, but that they can, and that they choose. The failure mode has two directions: telling when the problem required transforming, which produces an untested conclusion in fluent prose, and transforming when telling would have done, which produces a four-day monitoring report and a missed deadline. The second is less discussed and it is a real failure with real costs.

So the judgement is the skill, and step 3's two panels are the guidance. Contestable conclusion, interested reader, or genuine uncertainty about what you have found: transform. Settled content, fixed genre, reader wants a record: tell, and do it quickly.

Where the evidence is firm and where it is not

The descriptive claim is well supported. Bereiter and Scardamalia's protocol studies show real and consistent differences between how less and more skilled writers compose, and the two-model account organises those differences coherently. Nothing controversial there.

The causal claim is harder and should be stated more carefully than it usually is. That transforming produces better content, rather than being what people who already understand a subject well happen to do while writing about it, is not established by the protocol evidence. Expertise, subject knowledge and composing strategy travel together, and separating them experimentally is difficult.

What can be said is narrower and still worth acting on. The loop is a mechanism by which writing can change content, the mechanism is observable in the specific cases this lecture and its predecessors document, and the techniques that force it are cheap. A finding that changed because someone wrote out its rebuttal is a direct observation, not an inference from a model, and the model's value is that it tells you where to look for more of them.

Common failure Treating the loop as a licence to keep reopening the content indefinitely. The dialectic has to terminate, and it terminates when the rhetorical problems stop producing content revisions, not when you feel finished. The practical stopping rule is that you have written the warrant and the rebuttal, gone where they sent you, and the second pass produced nothing new. A writer who cannot stop is not transforming, they are avoiding the deliverable, and lecture 4's protocol exists partly to bound this.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Run the five-sign diagnostic on the last non-routine document you wrote. Report the score, and if three or more signs hold, write out the full Toulmin structure of its central claim and report where the warrant sent you.
  2. Then compose. On your next contestable document, keep a note of every moment a phrasing problem sent you back to the data. Report the count.
  3. Describe the knowledge-telling model in its four steps. What happens to the content?
  4. Name the two problem spaces and state what circulates between them.
  5. Why do experienced writers say they write to find out what they think? What kind of claim is that?
  6. Give the five signs a document was told. Which is the strongest single indicator, and why does sign 3 invert a professional instinct?
  7. Why do warrants and rebuttals reliably force content revision? What does that say about why writers omit them?
  8. Give the selection criterion for judging any writing technique you meet.
  9. Name the three organisational arrangements that remove the loop, and what each costs.
  10. Resolve Klinkenborg against Minto using the two spaces. In what respect is the dispute asymmetric?
  11. Why are the two models strategies rather than levels? Give both directions of failure.
  12. What is firmly established here and what is not? Why is the model still worth acting on?
  13. What is the stopping rule for the loop?
