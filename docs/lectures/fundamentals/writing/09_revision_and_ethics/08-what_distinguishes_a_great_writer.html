What distinguishes a great writer

Five things, argued rather than listed, of which four turn out to depend partly on understanding your subject and one is craft. Which means the last requirement is not a writing skill, and a course cannot supply it.

Lecture 8 of 8 Reading time, 34 minutes Source: this course, argued
Step 1

The 30-second version

Five candidates, in ascending order of how hard they are to acquire. Judgement about what to leave out. A reader model held continuously and without effort. Structure invented for the material rather than borrowed. A stance the writer actually holds. And sentences that carry thought rather than report it.

Each is necessary and none is sufficient, and the argument of this lecture is that they are not five independent virtues. Four of them depend, in part, on understanding the subject: you cannot know which detail to cut without knowing what matters, cannot invent a structure for material you do not grasp, and cannot hold a stance on a question you have not thought about. Only the fifth is craft in the narrow sense.

Which produces the conclusion the course has been building towards since lecture 2 divided the content space from the rhetorical space. This course is almost entirely the rhetorical space. The last requirement, having something worth saying, sits in the other one, and no writing course reaches it.

That is better news than it sounds for this collection's reader. A great writer in a technical field is a person who understands the field and has learned to write, not a writer who has picked up a field. The order matters, and the harder half is the one you already have.

Feynman test Knowing what to cut, knowing who you are writing to, building a shape that fits this thing rather than a template, actually believing something, and sentences that make the reader think rather than just telling them. The first four need you to understand the subject. So the last thing you need is not a writing skill.
What is beyond the source This lecture cites nothing new. It is an argument assembled from the course, and it is the only lecture here that makes no empirical claim, because the question it asks has no empirical answer available. Its two contributions are the ordering, which treats the five as dependent rather than parallel, and the reduction in step 5, which argues that four of the five partly collapse into subject understanding and therefore that the course's own subject is the smaller half of the problem.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the question is unavoidable and usually answered badly. Ask what makes a great writer and you get a list of admired qualities, which is not an answer but an inventory of things that correlate with the outcome. An argued answer has to say how the items relate, which of them can be acquired, and what happens when one is missing.

The second reason is diagnostic and immediately useful. If you know which of the five your own writing lacks, you know what to work on, and the five have completely different remedies: one is judgement, one is a capacity built by practice, one is a habit of not reaching for a template, one is intellectual honesty, and one is craft. Treating "I want to write better" as a single goal is why most attempts at it fail.

The third reason is that the honest answer changes how you should read the preceding sixty-seven lectures. If four of the five depend on subject understanding, then this course is a multiplier on something it does not supply, and its value is proportional to how much you know about what you are writing about. That is a claim about the course's own scope and it belongs in the course.

And a reason about what to do next. A reader finishing a writing course reasonably expects a summary and a set of instructions. The module index promised a question instead, and the reason is that the last increment of quality has never been reducible to technique, so a summary would be a false ending.

Step 3

The mental model

The five, with what each requires, and the reduction that connects four of them to something outside writing.

Selection
Choosing which of the available material belongs. The only irreducibly evaluative act in writing, since every other operation in this course is corrective or additive, and the one no principle can perform for you.
Invented structure
A shape derived from the material rather than selected from a repertoire. Testable: a borrowed structure could have carried a different document, and an invented one could not.
Stance
A position the writer holds and can be held to. Its absence is visible as prose that qualifies every claim and therefore reads as having no author, which module 7 diagnosed structurally through the missing qualifier and rebuttal.
Step 4

The detail

Each one, tested

RequirementHow to tell whether you have itWhat its absence looks like
Five diagnostics, because "write better" is not a goal you can act on
1 Selection Count what you cut from the last document, as a proportion. Then ask whether you can name what each cut improved. Documents whose length is determined by how much you found rather than by what the reader needs. Everything relevant is present, and nothing is prominent.
2 Reader model Stop mid-draft and ask who you are writing to. If the answer takes more than a second, you were not holding one. Register that drifts, definitions of terms your reader coined, and a finding pitched at whoever you last spoke to about it.
3 Invented structure Ask whether this document's structure could have carried last quarter's document. If yes, it was borrowed. Every paper has the same six sections. The material is fitted to the shape, so what does not fit is either omitted or padded.
4 Stance Find the sentence you would defend in a committee against someone senior. If there is not one, there is no stance. Every claim qualified, every finding "may warrant further consideration", and a document nobody can disagree with because it asserts nothing.
5 Sentences that carry thought Read your best paragraph aloud and ask whether its shape does any of the work, or whether the same content in flat sentences would land identically. Prose that is correct, clear, well ordered, and forgotten by the reader an hour later.

The fourth diagnostic is the most uncomfortable and the fastest. A document with no sentence you would defend against a senior colleague is a document with no stance, and the reason is usually not cowardice but that the writer never formed a view, which returns to the content space. Lecture 7's five levels are what that looks like on the page.

The fifth one, concretely

Sentences that carry thought is the requirement most likely to collapse into mysticism, so here is an instance rather than a definition. Two versions of the same finding, both clear, both accurate, both passing every test in modules 2 to 5.

VERSION A · correct and forgettable "The data are accurate for the period they cover, but the period is not representative of a downturn, so the estimate is unreliable even though the inputs are sound." Clear. Character in subject position, action in the verb, new information last, no nominalisations. It would pass pass 3 of the protocol without a mark. VERSION B · the same content, doing more "The data are accurate; the sample is not representative." Nine words. An antithesis, so the parallel structure isolates the single thing that changed between the two clauses, and the reader performs the inference rather than receiving it. The semicolon does the work "but" was doing, without asserting a contrast the reader has not yet seen. WHAT MAKES B DIFFERENT Not concision, though it is shorter. Version A tells the reader that accuracy and representativeness come apart. Version B's FORM comes apart, in the same way, so the reader discovers the distinction by reading a sentence built out of it. That is what "carries the thought" means, and it is why module 7's lecture 6 argued the figures belong to memory rather than to style: B is the sentence that gets repeated in the committee. WHY THIS IS THE HARDEST TO TEACH Every technical component of B was taught: antithesis, isocolon, the semicolon, the stress position, concision. What was not taught, and cannot be, is noticing that THIS finding has a two-part shape that a two-part sentence could enact. That noticing depends on having understood the finding, which is item 1 and item 3 again, in a different form.

The last paragraph is the honest limit. Every ingredient of version B is in this course and the decision to reach for it is not, because the decision depends on seeing the shape of the content, which is subject understanding rather than craft. That is the fifth item partly collapsing into the others, which is why step 3 drew it as the only pure-craft arrow and why the argument in step 5 is a reduction rather than a ranking.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The reduction

The claim to defend is that four of the five partly reduce to understanding the subject, and it is worth doing carefully because it sounds like a way of avoiding the question.

Selection requires knowing what matters, and what matters is a fact about the subject rather than about the document. A validator who does not know whether the discounting convention bears on a downturn LGD finding cannot decide whether to cut it, and no principle about concision supplies the answer. Structure invented for the material requires grasping the material's own shape, which is the same knowledge again: McPhee could invent a structure per piece because he had reported the piece exhaustively first. A stance requires having formed a view, and forming a view about a technical question is analysis. And the fifth, as step 4 showed, requires noticing that a finding has a shape a sentence could enact, which is a fact about the finding.

Only the reader model survives as something writing practice reliably builds, and even it depends on knowing enough about the subject to predict what a specialist reader will resist. So the honest accounting is that this course teaches the operations and the subject supplies almost all of the judgement about when to perform them.

The obvious objection is that this proves too much, since by the same argument nothing can be taught about writing at all. The answer is that the operations are genuinely separable and genuinely learnable, and a person with excellent subject understanding and no craft writes documents whose findings do not reach anybody. Both halves are necessary; the claim is only that the half this course covers is the smaller one, and that it is nonetheless the half where good work is routinely lost.

What this means for the reader of this collection

The conclusion is not deflating and it is worth stating in the second person, because it is the only useful thing this lecture has to offer somebody who has read all sixty-eight.

You are a credit risk modeller reading a writing course. The reduction says the difficult, slow, largely unteachable half of great writing in your field is subject understanding, and you have spent years acquiring it. What you have been missing is the operations, and the operations are learnable in months. That is a considerably better position than the reverse, and the reverse is common: capable writers in technical organisations who cannot tell which finding matters, because they do not know the subject well enough to have a view.

Which also predicts where your writing will improve fastest, and it is not at the sentence level. If the analysis is sound and the judgement about what matters is already there, then the gains are in structure, in placement and in the willingness to state a stance you already hold, which are passes one and zero of lecture 4's protocol and the level 0 formulation of lecture 7. Those are the changes that cost nothing technically and change whether a finding is acted on.

And it predicts the failure mode to watch for. A technically expert writer who acquires craft can produce documents that are clear, well structured, elegantly argued and wrong, and the craft makes the wrongness harder to see. Lecture 7's dual-use problem and lecture 4's pass zero are both about this, and it is the specific hazard of finishing this course.

The last thing, which is not a summary

The module introduction promised that this course would end on a question rather than a summary, and the reason is now statable. A summary would imply that the material adds up to a competence, and the argument of this lecture is that it adds up to half of one. The other half is not in any course, and pretending otherwise at the end would undo the honesty the preceding sixty-seven lectures have tried to maintain about what is established and what is not.

So there is no summary. What there is instead is a question, and it is a real one rather than a rhetorical flourish, because the answer determines whether reading this course was the right use of the time.

The question Take the document of yours that failed most consequentially: the finding that was not acted on, the paper that was overtaken, the recommendation that went into a register and stayed there. Was its failure in the rhetorical space or the content space? And if it was in the content space, what did these sixty-eight lectures have to do with it?
Common failure Reading this lecture as permission to stop working on the writing. The reduction says that subject understanding supplies most of the judgement, not that craft is optional, and the whole argument for craft is that the smaller half is where good work is lost. A person who concludes from this lecture that their prose is adequate because their analysis is strong has drawn the one inference the lecture rules out.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. The final exercise, and it is the only one that matters. Answer the question in step 5, in writing, about a specific document. One page. Then decide what to do differently, and write that down too.
  2. Name the five requirements in order and say which one is pure craft.
  3. Why is selection the only irreducibly evaluative act in writing?
  4. Give the test for whether a structure was invented or borrowed.
  5. What does the absence of a stance look like on the page, and what is the fastest test for it?
  6. Explain what "sentences that carry thought" means using the two versions in step 4. What makes version B different, and why is it not concision?
  7. State the reduction: which four requirements partly depend on subject understanding, and why does each?
  8. Give the objection that the reduction proves too much, and the answer to it.
  9. Why is the conclusion good news for a technical specialist? What is the common reverse case?
  10. Where will a subject expert's writing improve fastest, and which passes does that correspond to?
  11. What is the specific hazard of having finished this course?
  12. Why does the course end on a question rather than a summary?

Further reading
