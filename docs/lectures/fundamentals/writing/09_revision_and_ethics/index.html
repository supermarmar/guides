Module 9 · Writing track

Revision, practice, ethics and greatness

Where knowledge becomes skill. Eight lectures on how writing actually happens, how experts revise differently from everyone else, what deliberate practice looks like for a writer, why clarity is an ethical position, and an argued answer to the question the whole course has been circling.

What this module is

This module reinterprets the eight before it. Almost nothing in modules 1 to 8 is a composition rule. Nobody drafts by consulting the character and action principle, and anyone who tries produces four sentences an hour. They are revision heuristics, and the research on how writers actually work explains why that distinction is the most important one in the course.

Flower and Hayes established in 1981 that planning, translating and reviewing are recursive rather than sequential. Bereiter and Scardamalia sharpened it six years later into the distinction that matters most for an advanced writer: novices knowledge-tell, retrieving what they know and putting it on the page with little transformation, while experts knowledge-transform, running a continuous dialectic between what they are trying to say and how it must be said, and changing their minds about the content as a result. That is why experienced writers say they write to find out what they think. It is a description of a cognitive process, not a romantic pose.

Sommers supplies the corresponding finding about revision: students revise by rewording, because they perceive the word as the unit of discourse, while experienced writers describe revision as finding the shape of the argument. Global before local is therefore not a stylistic preference. It is what expertise looks like from the outside.

The module ends on two questions the field usually avoids. Why obscurity persists when everyone claims to want clarity, which turns out to have an uncomfortable answer. And what actually separates a great writer from a good one, which this course answers with an argument rather than a list.

Module sequence

Part 1 · The cognitive evidence (lectures 1 to 3)

What research shows about how writing works, and what separates a novice from an expert at the process level.

  • builtLecture 1: how writing actually happens. Flower and Hayes's cognitive process model: planning, translating and reviewing as recursive and interleaved, operating within a task environment and drawing on long-term memory. What follows for when to outline and when outlining is avoidance.
  • builtLecture 2: knowledge-telling and knowledge-transforming. Bereiter and Scardamalia's two models. The dialectic between a content problem space and a rhetorical problem space, and why the writer's understanding changes as a result of writing rather than before it.
  • builtLecture 3: what experts do differently when revising. Sommers's study: students revise as lexical substitution, experienced writers revise for the form and shape of the argument. Global before local, stated as an observed behaviour rather than as advice.

Part 2 · The practice (lectures 4 to 6)

Turning all of it into a procedure, a feedback loop, and a training regime.

  • builtLecture 4: a revision protocol. Sequenced passes: structure, paragraph coherence, sentence clarity, concision, rhythm, proofreading. Why running them in the wrong order wastes most of the work. King's second draft equals first draft minus ten per cent, and McPhee's fourth draft as the one where material comes out.
  • builtLecture 5: feedback and peer review. Eliciting descriptive rather than evaluative feedback: where did you slow down beats did you like it. Graham and Perin's effect sizes as the guide to what is worth doing, with strategy instruction and summarisation at 0.82, peer assistance at 0.75, product goals at 0.70, and traditional grammar instruction at minus 0.32.
  • builtLecture 6: deliberate practice for writers. Exercises with a defined target, immediate feedback and repetition. Sentence combining, the one content topic the meta-analysis endorses at 0.50, plus imitation, paramedic drills and deliberate constraint. Why writing volume alone plateaus.

Part 3 · The stakes (lectures 7 to 8)

Why clarity is a moral position, and an argued answer to what the course has been about.

  • builtLecture 7: the ethics of clarity. Williams's closing lessons: obscurity is often a strategy for evading accountability rather than an accident of drafting. The passive that hides the actor, the nominalisation that hides the act, the euphemism that hides both. Orwell's political argument, with the fair criticism that his rules are unfollowable and that his sixth rule concedes it.
  • builtLecture 8: what distinguishes a great writer. A synthesis, argued rather than listed: judgement about what to leave out, a reader model held continuously and without effort, structure invented for the material rather than borrowed from a template, a stance the writer actually holds, and sentences that carry thought rather than report it. Then the uncomfortable conclusion, that the last requirement, having something worth saying, is not a writing skill and cannot be taught by a writing course.

After this module

Nothing. The course ends here, and it ends on a question rather than a summary, which is the only honest way to close a subject where the last increment of quality has never been reducible to technique.
