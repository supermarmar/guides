What experts do differently when revising

Novices revise by substituting words, because they take the word as the unit of discourse. Experts revise to resolve a felt dissonance about the shape of the argument, which is the knowledge-transforming loop reporting a problem. Global before local is an observed behaviour, not advice.

Lecture 3 of 8 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: Sommers (1980); Faigley and Witte (1981)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Nancy Sommers interviewed student writers and experienced adult writers about what they did when they revised, and the two groups did not describe the same activity. Students talked about crossing out words, using better words and changing words around. They took the word as the unit of discourse, and Sommers named the underlying belief a thesaurus philosophy of writing: the meaning is fixed and settled, and only the expression is at issue.

Experienced writers described something else entirely. They talked about finding a framework, a pattern, a design, about the shape of the argument, and about anticipating a reader. Their revisions changed what the text meant, where the students' revisions mostly did not.

The mechanism they reported is the most useful part. Experienced writers described revising in response to a felt dissonance, a sense of something not fitting, which they used as the trigger rather than looking for errors. Read alongside lecture 2, that dissonance is the rhetorical space signalling a content problem: it is the knowledge-transforming loop, described from the inside by people who had no model for it.

And a marker that separates the groups better than anything they said. Experts delete substantial material and students almost never do, because a student sees the text as already-said and therefore fixed. Professionals do not delete either, for an extra reason that is specific to regulated writing and worth naming: the material is evidence, and cutting it feels like losing rigour.

Feynman test Beginners rewrite words. Experts rewrite shapes, and they start when something feels wrong rather than when they spot a mistake. The clearest tell is deletion: if nothing substantial came out, no expert revision happened.
What is beyond the source Sommers's findings and Faigley and Witte's complication are reported as they stand. Three arguments are the lecture's own. The first is that the dissonance experienced writers report is the knowledge-transforming loop from lecture 2, observed introspectively before the model existed. The second is that regulated writing adds a governance disincentive to deletion that student writers do not face, and that module 8's appendix is what removes it, since material can leave the body without leaving the record. The third is step 5's point that global-before-local is a behaviour rather than a rule, so instructing a novice to do it supplies the behaviour without the capacity, which is what lecture 4's protocol exists to scaffold.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this course has told you to work from the largest unit inwards on three separate grounds, and Sommers supplies a fourth of a different kind. Modules 3 and 4 argued it from repair economics, module 7 from the canons, lecture 1 from working memory. Sommers observed that competent writers already do it, which turns the recommendation from a derivation into a description of the target behaviour.

The second reason is that the thesaurus philosophy has a precise professional form, and naming it is useful. Believing that a finding is settled and only its expression remains in play is exactly the state that lecture 2 identified as forfeiting the loop, and it is institutionalised whenever a conclusion is agreed before the document is written. The student's error and the pre-agreed finding are the same error at different scales.

The third reason is the deletion problem, which is worse in this field than in most. A validation report's material is evidence, and cutting evidence feels like weakening the record rather than improving the document. That instinct is defensible and it blocks the single behaviour that most distinguishes expert revision, so the resolution matters: relocation is not deletion, and the appendix is what makes the distinction real.

And a reason about review comments. Once you can see the difference, you can classify the feedback you receive and the feedback you give. A page of wording changes is a student-mode review of somebody else's document, and it is what most professional review consists of, for the structural reason lecture 1 identified about when the gate sits.

Step 3

The mental model

Two accounts of the same word, and the trigger that distinguishes them.

Thesaurus philosophy of writing
Sommers's name for the belief underlying student revision: that the meaning is settled and the words are merely not yet the right ones, so revision is a search for better expression of a fixed content.
Dissonance
The felt sense that something does not fit, which experienced writers reported using as the trigger for revision. Precedes any ability to name the fault, and signals a content problem surfacing as a rhetorical one.
Relocation
Moving material out of the body into an appendix rather than removing it. The mechanism that makes expert-style cutting available in a setting where the record has to be preserved.
Step 4

The detail

The two revisions, on one document

A VALIDATION REPORT COMES BACK FROM TWO REVIEWERS. REVIEWER A · 34 comments, all in track changes "utilise" -> "use" (x6) "in order to" -> "to" (x4) "significant" -> "material" (x3) "The review notes that" -> deleted (x5) "suggest" -> "recommend" (x2) ... 14 further wording changes Plus: "consider adding a caveat here", "is 3.1% the right figure?", "tone in section 4 feels strong" VERDICT Every change is an improvement. Several apply principles from this course correctly. And the document's meaning is unchanged, which is what makes this student-mode revision performed by a competent professional. REVIEWER B · 4 comments, none in track changes 1 "Your finding is in section 4.3, on page 11. The reader who decides this reads two pages. Move it." 2 "Sections 2.1 to 2.6 are six pages of model description. Which of them bears on the finding? I think two sentences do. Appendix for the rest." 3 "Findings 2 and 4 look like one finding to me. Both are the stress window. If they are one, say so; if they are two, the difference needs a sentence." 4 "You have no statement of what would make you wrong. The model owner will supply one and it will be theirs rather than yours." VERDICT Nothing about wording. Two structural moves, one content question that may collapse two findings into one, and one demand that will send the author back to the data. Comment 3 might change what the report says. WHICH IS MORE USEFUL B, by a wide margin, and B took twenty minutes while A took ninety. WHY YOU MOSTLY GET A Lecture 1's answer: the gate sits after translating. A reviewer holding a finished draft knows that comment 1 means the author rebuilds the document, so a considerate reviewer supplies the cheap half instead. Reviewer A is not lazy. Reviewer A is being kind at the wrong point in the process.

Comment 3 is the one to notice, because it is not a writing comment at all. Asking whether two findings are one is a content question arriving through a structural observation, and if the answer is yes then the report's substance changes. That is the dissonance mechanism operating in a reviewer rather than an author, and it is why structural review is worth having even when the structure turns out to be fine.

Making deletion possible

What blocks the cutWhether the instinct is soundThe move that unblocks it
Deletion is the expert marker, and regulated writing resists it for real reasons
"This is evidence. Cutting it weakens the record." Sound Relocate to an appendix. The record is preserved, the body is shorter, and module 8's lecture 5 argued the appendix should be complete and repetitive anyway.
"A reviewer will ask why it is missing." Sound, and cheap to fix One sentence in the body pointing at the appendix. "The segmentation logic is at appendix B and does not bear on this finding."
"I spent three weeks on this section." Not sound Nothing to unblock; recognise it. Module 8's lectures 3 and 5 both found the same fault, that effort creates a false sense of relevance. The reader does not know what it cost.
"The template has a section for it." Sometimes sound Module 7's lecture 7 distinguishes an obligatory move from a ritual one. If nobody can say who reads it and what they do differently, it is ritual, and that case can be made.
"It is already written." Not sound, and it is the student's reason This is the already-said fallacy exactly. Text that exists has no claim on remaining, and sunk drafting is sunk.

The last row deserves its bluntness because it is the one that operates without being articulated. Nobody consciously believes that a paragraph earns its place by existing, and everybody revises as though it does, which is why the practical instruction is to make the cut and then judge the shorter version rather than to justify the cut in advance.

Working with dissonance

The trigger experienced writers reported is worth taking seriously as a working method, because it is available to anyone and it does not depend on remembering a rule. The instruction is to notice the point at which reading your own draft produces discomfort, and to stop and locate it rather than reading past it.

What makes this course useful at that moment is the vocabulary. Sommers's writers felt the dissonance and worked by feel; you can feel it and then name it, which turns an hour of circling into a diagnosis. The paragraph feels wrong because the topic string breaks in the third sentence, or because the finding is in the subordinate clause, or because the exposition arrived four paragraphs before the claim that needed it. Recognition locates the problem and diagnosis identifies it, which is module 8's lecture 7 applied to your own draft.

The failure to avoid is treating the discomfort as a signal about wording, which is where it presents. Dissonance almost always surfaces at a sentence and it originates above the sentence, so the first question is never how to rephrase this but what this sentence is doing here.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Global before local is a behaviour, not a rule

The distinction matters because it changes what instruction can achieve. Sommers did not recommend that writers work from the whole inwards; she found that competent writers already did, and that novices did not and could not describe the activity in those terms at all.

Which means telling a novice to revise globally first hands them the behaviour without the capacity that produces it. Global revision requires holding the document as a whole, and holding a document as a whole requires a reader model, a vocabulary for structural problems, and enough experience to feel dissonance at the level of the argument rather than the phrase. None of those arrives by instruction on the day.

So the honest account of what this course can do is narrower than its confidence sometimes suggests. Modules 1 to 8 supply the vocabulary, module 7's lecture 8 supplies the reader model, and lecture 4 supplies a protocol that imposes the ordering from outside. The protocol is a scaffold for a disposition, and it is worth having for exactly as long as the disposition is absent, which for most professional writers on most documents is a long time.

That framing also explains why the protocol should feel artificial. An expert does not run six passes; they revise in waves with shifting focus, triggered by dissonance, and the ordering emerges from what they notice rather than from a list. A writer who finds the protocol constraining after two years of use should probably stop using it, and that is the intended outcome rather than a failure of the tool.

The complication, which is real

Sommers's study is foundational and small: a qualitative protocol study of twenty students and twenty experienced writers, published in 1980. It has shaped the field and it is not a large-sample quantitative finding, and the difference matters for how confidently the results should be stated.

The specific complication came quickly. Faigley and Witte, in 1981, built a taxonomy distinguishing surface changes from meaning changes and applied it to actual revisions rather than to writers' accounts of revising. They found the picture considerably messier: variation among expert writers was large, some experienced writers made few meaning changes, and the neat two-group contrast did not hold uniformly across their sample.

Both results can stand. Sommers was studying how writers conceive of revision, which is where the two groups differ starkly; Faigley and Witte were counting what changed in the text, where individual style dominates. An experienced writer who plans thoroughly may have little global revision left to do, which produces surface-level revisions from an expert conception of the task. So the defensible claim is about the conception rather than the count: experts understand revision as work on the whole, and whether a given document shows it depends on how much of that work happened before drafting.

It should also be said that composition studies has criticised the deficit framing of the original study, in which student writers are characterised by what they lack. That criticism is fair as pedagogy and does not affect the observation, which is what this lecture uses.

The professional version of the student's error

The thesaurus philosophy is not a novice's mistake that professionals have outgrown. It is institutionalised, and the institutional form is more consequential than the individual one.

A conclusion agreed in a meeting and then written up is a fixed meaning awaiting expression, which is the thesaurus philosophy as a governance arrangement. So is a review process that arrives after translating, since it can only offer better words for a settled content. So is the tracked-changes document with thirty-four wording improvements and no structural comment. In each case a competent professional is performing student-mode revision, not because they lack skill but because the process has fixed the content before the writing could act on it.

Which gives the point that connects this lecture to the two before it. Lecture 1 located the failure in when the review gate sits, lecture 2 in what gets forfeited when content is settled early, and Sommers supplies the behavioural signature to detect it: revision that does not change meaning. If your documents come back with wording changes and go out saying what they said before review, the process is working exactly as designed and the design is the problem.

Common failure Deleting to demonstrate expert revision. Cutting is the marker of expert revision because experts cut what is not earning its place, not because cutting is itself the skill. A writer who removes a third of a document to prove they are revising globally will remove things the reader needed, and the reliable test is not volume but whether the shorter version answers the reader's question better. If you cannot say what the cut improved, put it back.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take the last set of review comments you received and classify each as reviewer A or reviewer B in step 4's sense. Report the ratio, and whether any comment could have changed what the document said.
  2. Then compose. Give reviewer-B feedback on a colleague's draft: at most four comments, none about wording. Report what you found that a wording pass would have missed.
  3. What unit do student writers take as the unit of discourse, and what belief underlies it?
  4. Give the vocabulary each group used. What did experienced writers say revision was for?
  5. What trigger did experienced writers report, and what is it in lecture 2's terms?
  6. Why is deletion the clearest single marker? Give the student's reason for not deleting and the professional's, and say which is sound.
  7. What move makes cutting available in regulated writing, and why does it preserve the record?
  8. Why does dissonance surface at the sentence when it originates above it? What is the first question to ask?
  9. Distinguish global-before-local as a behaviour from global-before-local as a rule. What does that imply about what a protocol is for, and when should you stop using one?
  10. State Faigley and Witte's complication and reconcile it with Sommers. What is the defensible claim?
  11. Give three institutional forms of the thesaurus philosophy.
  12. What is the reliable test for whether a cut was justified?
