Lectures / Writing / Revision and ethics

A revision protocol

Six passes in one order, because each can invalidate the work of the passes after it and none can invalidate the work of those before. Plus a pass zero that is not a writing pass at all, and an honest account of which passes to drop when the deadline is real.

Lecture 4 of 8 Reading time, 32 minutes Source: this course, sequenced; King; McPhee
Step 1

The 30-second version

Six passes: structure, paragraph coherence, sentence clarity, concision, rhythm, proofread. Each has one question, one set of tools from earlier modules, and one output. The order is not a preference. A structural change discards the sentence work done inside the sections it moves, and sentence work never changes the structure, so the dependency runs one way and the sequence follows it.

The waste from getting it wrong is easy to quantify. Polish forty paragraphs, then restructure and cut fifteen of them, and you have wasted thirty-seven per cent of the polishing. That is the argument, and lecture 1 supplied the deeper reason: the passes contend for the same working memory, so they cannot be merged either.

Before all six there is a pass zero that is not a revision pass. It asks lecture 2's question, whether anything in the writing sent you back to the data, and it exists because a protocol run faithfully produces a document that passes every check and may still be about nothing. The content space is not in the protocol and cannot be put there.

And a concession that matters more than the protocol. Most professional documents get one pass, under deadline, with all six mixed together. The minimum viable version is pass zero, pass one, a read-aloud, and pass six, which fits in ninety minutes and captures most of the available gain.

Feynman test Fix the big things before the small ones, because fixing a big thing throws away the small fixes underneath it. And before any of that, ask whether writing the thing taught you anything, because a beautifully revised document about nothing is still about nothing.
What is beyond the source The six passes assemble tools taught earlier in this course; the sequencing argument was made piecemeal in modules 3, 4 and 7 and is consolidated here. Three things are this lecture's own. The first is pass zero, placed outside the protocol on the grounds that the content space cannot be proceduralised. The second is step 4's cooling-period argument, that you cannot run pass one on a document finished an hour ago because you still hold the intended structure and will read it into the text. The third is step 5's triage, which states which passes to drop under deadline and in what order, rather than pretending the full protocol is always available.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this is where sixty-three lectures become usable. A reader who has followed the course holds a large inventory of principles and no procedure for applying them, and an inventory without a procedure gets applied opportunistically, which means whichever principle happens to be salient gets applied and the rest do not.

The second reason is the waste, which is large and invisible. Nobody notices the polishing they discarded, because the discarded version is gone and the remaining document reads well. So the cost of a bad revision order is never observed directly, and the only way to see it is to reason about the dependency, which is what step 3 does.

The third reason is that the protocol is a scaffold for the disposition lecture 3 described. Experts revise in waves triggered by dissonance and do not need a list. A writer who does not yet feel dissonance at the level of the argument needs the ordering imposed from outside, and imposing it is what makes the expert behaviour available before the expert instinct arrives.

And a reason about honesty. A protocol that assumes eight uninterrupted hours describes a situation professionals rarely have, and a course that offers only the full version will be ignored rather than adapted. The triage in step 5 is therefore not a concession appended at the end; it is the version most readers will actually use.

Step 3

The mental model

The six passes, the one-way dependency that fixes their order, and the pass that sits outside them.

Pass zero
The check that writing the document did analytical work: did anything in it send you back to the data? Sits outside the protocol because the content space cannot be proceduralised, and is the only pass whose failure cannot be repaired by revising.
One-way dependency
The property that fixes the order: a structural change invalidates local work, and local work never invalidates structure. There is therefore exactly one sequence in which no pass destroys another's output.
Cooling period
The interval between finishing a draft and revising its structure. Necessary because a writer who still holds the intended structure in memory reads it into the text and cannot see what the page actually says.
Step 4

The detail

The cooling period, and why it is not about freshness

You cannot run pass one on a document you finished an hour ago. The usual explanation is fatigue, and the real reason is more specific and more interesting: you still hold the structure you intended, and lecture 1's model says the text produced so far is part of your task environment. So you read your intention rather than your text, and every transition seems to work because you know what it was for.

WHAT THE COOLING PERIOD BUYS, BY PASS PASS 1 STRUCTURE needs a night, minimum You must be able to read the document as a stranger would, which means having forgotten the plan. An hour is not enough; overnight usually is. SUBSTITUTE IF YOU HAVE NO TIME: read only the first sentence of each paragraph, in sequence, and nothing else. That produces something close to a stranger's view of your structure in four minutes, because it strips the connective material you would otherwise supply from memory. PASS 2 PARAGRAPHS needs an hour Enough to have lost the sentence-level intentions. PASS 3 SENTENCES needs nothing Diagnosis at sentence level does not depend on having forgotten your intentions, because the tests are mechanical. Where is the subject, where is the verb, what is in the stress position. PASS 4 CONCISION needs nothing, and benefits from distance You will cut more from a document you have cooled on, because attachment fades faster than memory. PASS 5 RHYTHM needs to be aloud, not cooled Reading aloud substitutes for distance here, because the ear does not care what you intended. PASS 6 PROOFREAD needs a different pair of eyes, or a changed presentation Print it, change the font, or read it backwards by paragraph. Anything that defeats familiarity. THE PRACTICAL SCHEDULE Finish drafting the day before you need to revise. That single habit buys pass 1 and pass 2, which are the two that matter most, and it costs nothing except not working right up to the deadline.

The four-minute substitute for pass one is worth taking seriously, because it is the pass that most needs cooling and the one professionals most often have no time to cool. Reading only the first sentence of each paragraph, in order, reproduces module 4's vertical logic check and simultaneously defeats your memory of the connective tissue, since you are not reading the sentences that carried it.

What each pass actually looks like

PassThe question, asked concretelyHow you know it is done
Six passes with a completion test each, so that "done" is not a feeling
1 Structure Read the first sentence of each paragraph in sequence. Does that alone state the argument? Then: which section is longest, and is that what the document is about? The first-sentence sequence reads as a coherent argument, and the longest section is the one you want the reader to take as the subject.
2 Paragraphs For each paragraph: what one thing does it do? Does its first sentence connect to what precedes? Do the subjects across its sentences form a topic string? You can write a three-word label for every paragraph, and no label repeats.
3 Sentences Underline the first seven or eight words of each sentence. Is there a character and an action in there? Is the new information at the end? No sentence has its subject and verb separated by more than a few words, and no nominalisation carries the action of its clause.
4 Concision Delete every word you can without changing the meaning. Then run the hedging and litotes audits. Length has dropped by around a tenth, and every remaining hedge marks a real uncertainty.
5 Rhythm Read aloud, standing up, at speaking pace. Mark every stumble, every place you run out of breath, every sentence you re-read. You can read the whole document aloud without stumbling, and sentence lengths vary rather than clustering.
6 Proofread Sweep for brackets. Verify every number against source. Click every cross-reference. Check the figures match the text that describes them. Zero unresolved brackets, every figure traced, every reference resolves.

Pass 3's instruction to underline the opening words is worth doing literally rather than mentally, because the fault it detects is invisible to a reader who knows the content. Module 2's diagnosis becomes mechanical once the first eight words are isolated: if they contain no character and no action, the sentence has one of the faults that module named, and which one becomes obvious immediately.

Two writers on where the passes stop

Stephen King reports being sent a formula early in his career, on a rejection slip, that he has quoted ever since: the second draft equals the first draft minus ten per cent. That is pass four with a target attached, and the target is useful precisely because it is arbitrary. A concision pass with no target stops when the writer tires; one with a stated ten per cent continues until the arithmetic is satisfied.

John McPhee's account of his fourth draft describes something different and more granular. By that stage he is no longer adding or restructuring, and the work consists of finding words that are not quite right, drawing a box around each one, and listing alternatives beside it, consulting a dictionary for the exact sense rather than a thesaurus for a synonym. That is a distinct activity from any of the six passes here, and it belongs between three and four for a writer who has the time for it.

Both are worth noting because they mark the boundary of what a protocol can specify. King's ten per cent is a quantity and it can be stated as a rule. McPhee's boxes require knowing that a word is not right, which is the recognition system from module 8's lecture 7, and no protocol can install it. The protocol gets you to the point where that judgement is the remaining constraint.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Triage, stated honestly

The full protocol on a forty-page document is perhaps two days of work, and most professional documents get an afternoon. A course that offers only the complete version will be abandoned rather than adapted, so here is the order in which to give things up.

Pass zero and pass six are never dropped. Pass zero costs five minutes and its failure cannot be repaired by any amount of revision, since a well-made document about nothing remains about nothing. Pass six is cheap and its failures are the most damaging in professional writing, because a wrong figure discredits a document in a way that clumsy prose does not: bad prose suggests the author writes badly, and a wrong number suggests they check badly.

Pass one is never dropped either, and under pressure it becomes the four-minute first-sentence sweep rather than a full structural review. That substitution loses real value and retains most of it, and it is the highest-yield four minutes available in the whole course.

Then, in order of what to abandon: pass five goes first, because rhythm is the smallest marginal gain in professional prose and the reading-aloud check partly duplicates passes two to four anyway. Passes three and four merge next, since concision and clarity are largely the same operation performed with different emphasis, and a merged pass captures most of both. Pass two is the last to go, and dropping it hurts, because paragraph incoherence is the fault readers experience most and locate least.

THE NINETY-MINUTE VERSION 5 min PASS 0 Did this send me back to the data? If not and it is contestable, stop and write the warrant and the rebuttal. 4 min PASS 1 First sentence of each paragraph, in sequence. Does it state the argument? Fix the order before anything else. 15 min PASS 2 Three-word label per paragraph. Split, merge, cut where labels repeat. 40 min PASS 3+4 Merged. First eight words of each sentence; delete what carries nothing. 15 min PASS 5 Read aloud. Fix stumbles only. 10 min PASS 6 Brackets, numbers, references. This captures most of the available gain and it fits between a draft and a deadline. WHAT IT DOES NOT DO It does not give you a structural rebuild, because four minutes buys detection and not repair. If the first-sentence sweep shows the argument is not there, the ninety-minute version is not enough and the honest response is to say so and ask for a day.

The protocol cannot reach the thing that matters most

Pass zero is placed outside the six because it is a different kind of activity, and the reason is worth stating plainly rather than leaving as a structural quirk of the diagram.

Every one of the six passes operates in what lecture 2 called the rhetorical problem space. They improve how the document says what it says, and they are all procedural, checkable and teachable, which is why sixty-three lectures could be written about them. None of them can tell you whether the document is worth writing, whether the finding is right, or whether you have understood the subject. That work happens in the content space, and the content space has no protocol.

Which produces the uncomfortable observation that a document can pass all six checks perfectly and be worthless. It will have its finding first, coherent paragraphs, clear sentences, no redundancy, varied rhythm and no wrong numbers, and it will be an elegant statement of something nobody needed to know or, worse, something not true. The protocol has no test for that, and pass zero is the closest available substitute: not a check on the content, but a check that the content was worked on.

This is the argument lecture 8 will close the course with, so it is worth registering here in its narrow procedural form. The revisable part of writing is not the part that determines whether a document is any good. It is the part that determines whether a good document reaches its reader, which is worth a great deal and is not the same thing.

When to stop, and the over-revision failure

The stopping rule for each pass is that the pass produces no changes, not that you feel finished, and the difference is large in both directions. A writer who stops on feeling will stop early on the passes they dislike and late on the ones they enjoy, which for most people means abandoning structure and over-working sentences.

Over-revision is real and less discussed than its opposite. Sommers's experienced writers knew when to stop, and the failure mode of a writer who has learned this course is a document revised past the point of return, where further passes trade one acceptable formulation for another and consume time that the analysis needed. The signature is a pass whose changes are all lateral: you rewrote nine sentences and none of them is clearer, only different.

The practical guard is to keep the previous version and compare, once, at the end. If you cannot articulate what the last pass improved, the last pass was not necessary, and that information should change how many passes you run on the next document rather than only how you feel about this one.

Common failure Running the protocol on a document that failed pass zero, because the protocol is comfortable and the content work is not. Six passes of careful revision on a finding you have not tested is the most sophisticated form of avoidance available to a trained writer, and it produces a document that survives review precisely because everything checkable about it is correct. If pass zero fails, close the document and go back to the data.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Run the ninety-minute version on a real document. Report what pass one's first-sentence sweep found, and whether pass zero passed.
  2. Then compose. On the next document, finish drafting a day early and run passes one and two after a night. Report the difference from your usual practice.
  3. Name the six passes in order and give the question each asks.
  4. State the one-way dependency and quantify the waste from reversing the order.
  5. What is pass zero, why is it outside the protocol, and why can its failure not be repaired by revising?
  6. Why is the cooling period not about fatigue? What does lecture 1's model say is happening?
  7. Give the four-minute substitute for pass one and explain why it defeats your own memory.
  8. Give the completion test for passes 2, 3 and 4.
  9. What does King's ten per cent add to a concision pass, and why does the arbitrariness help?
  10. What is McPhee's box method, where does it sit, and why can a protocol not specify it?
  11. Give the triage order: what is never dropped, what goes first, what goes last, and what does the ninety-minute version fail to do?
  12. Why can a document pass all six checks and be worthless?
  13. What is the stopping rule, what is the signature of over-revision, and what is the guard against it?
