Lectures / Writing / Revision and ethics

How writing actually happens

Planning, translating and reviewing are recursive and share one pool of working memory. That single finding explains why revision passes must be separate, why institutional review gates arrive too late to change anything structural, and when an outline is avoidance.

Lecture 1 of 8 Reading time, 32 minutes Source: Flower and Hayes (1981); Hayes (1996)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Flower and Hayes established in 1981, using think-aloud protocols, that writing is not a sequence of stages. Planning, translating and reviewing are distinct processes that interleave continuously under the control of a monitor that decides which one runs next. A writer plans mid-sentence and reviews while generating, and the pre-write, write, re-write model is empirically false as a description of what happens.

Two parts of the model do more work than the recursion does. The task environment includes the text produced so far, which means what you have already written constrains what you write next, and that is the mechanism by which drafting changes your plan rather than executing it. And the processes compete for the same limited working memory.

That competition is the practically important finding, because it explains something this course has asserted three times without a mechanism. You cannot draft while running the diagnostic checks from modules 2 to 5, since both draw on the same resource, and attempting it produces four sentences an hour. Separate passes are not a tidiness preference. They are a response to a capacity limit.

The same finding indicts a common institutional arrangement. Review gates are placed where the stage model says the work is finished, which is after translating, and that is exactly too late for a reviewer to affect planning. Hence comments on a draft that can only be local, on a document whose structure was settled three weeks earlier.

Feynman test Writing is not three stages, it is three activities you keep switching between, and they all run on the same limited attention. So you cannot compose and check at once. And whatever you have written so far is quietly changing what you are going to write next, which is why plans do not survive drafting.
What is beyond the source Flower and Hayes, and the 1996 revision, are reported as they stand. Three arguments are the lecture's own. The first is that the shared working-memory constraint is the mechanism behind separate revision passes, which modules 3, 4 and 8 recommended on other grounds. The second is step 5's claim that institutional review gates inherit the stage model and are therefore placed after translating, which structurally prevents reviewers from affecting structure. The third is step 4's distinction between an outline as goal-setting and an outline as avoidance, with the test being whether you have the content the structure is for.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this module's whole claim rests on it. The course has taught sixty lectures of principle and is about to argue that almost none of it is a composition rule, and the evidence for that reinterpretation is here. If planning, translating and reviewing were sequential stages, then rules could be applied during translating. They are not, and the rules therefore belong to reviewing.

The second reason is that the capacity limit is the reason writing feels hard in a specific way that most people misattribute. The sensation of drafting slowly while trying to write well is not a lack of skill or of clarity about the subject; it is two processes contending for one resource. Knowing that is what makes it possible to stop doing it.

The third reason is the review-gate problem, which is a real and expensive institutional failure. A reviewer who receives a finished draft can comment on sentences and cannot repair a structure, because repairing a structure means discarding the translating work that the draft consists of. Everybody involved knows the comments are superficial and nobody locates the cause in when the review happens.

And a reason about the blank page. A professional who cannot start a document usually diagnoses it as a writing problem and it is usually a planning problem, or further back, an invention problem in module 7's sense. The model separates those, which means the remedy can be matched to the actual failure rather than to the symptom.

Step 3

The mental model

The model as Flower and Hayes drew it, with the two features that carry the practical weight marked.

Translating
Flower and Hayes's term for converting planned content into language on the page. Distinct from planning and from reviewing, and by far the most working-memory-expensive of the three.
The monitor
The control structure that decides which process runs next and when to switch. The part of the model most directly trainable, since knowing when to stop generating and start organising is a learnable judgement.
The text produced so far
Part of the task environment rather than the writer. What has been written constrains what can follow, which makes drafting a source of new constraints rather than the execution of a plan.
Step 4

The detail

When an outline is goal-setting and when it is avoidance

An outline is planning externalised, specifically the organising and goal-setting sub-processes written down. Whether it helps depends on a single question, and the question is not about your preferences as a writer.

THE TEST: DO YOU HAVE THE CONTENT THE STRUCTURE IS FOR? YES -> outline, and it will save you a great deal. You know the four findings, their evidence and their severities. The problem is order, emphasis and what the reader needs first. That is a pure organising problem and solving it on one page is much cheaper than solving it in twelve. NO -> the outline is avoidance, and a productive-feeling kind. You do not yet know whether the stress window issue is one finding or three, or whether the monitoring override is a symptom of it or separate. Producing headings for that material generates a structure for content you have not got, and it FEELS like progress because a page fills up. What you actually need is module 7's lecture 4: run the topics, find out what you have. THE PROFESSIONAL SIGNATURE OF THE AVOIDANCE CASE A document skeleton with section titles, no findings, and a sense of having started. Two days later the skeleton is unchanged and the analysis has not moved, because the skeleton was never the blocked thing. THE HYBRID THAT ACTUALLY WORKS Write badly and fast, to find out what you have. Then outline, from the bad draft. Then write the deliverable, from the outline. The first draft is a planning instrument and should be thrown away. Most people try to edit it into the deliverable, which is why it retains the shape of their own discovery process rather than the shape the reader needs.

The hybrid is the resolution of a dispute this course has carried since module 4. Klinkenborg's objection was that you find your material in the sentences; Minto's method requires the structure first. Both are describing real activities, and the model says they are different sub-processes rather than rival methods, so the sequence above uses each for what it is good at. Lecture 2 sharpens this further.

What the capacity limit implies for practice

HabitWhat is competingWhat to do instead
Failures that are capacity problems rather than skill problems
Perfecting each sentence before writing the next Translating and reviewing, at the same grain, continuously Draft the section, then review it. The rejected sentences were often rejected for structural reasons that a later paragraph would have resolved anyway.
Running module 2's character-and-action check while composing Translating and a deliberate diagnostic procedure Compose on recognition, which module 8's lecture 7 argued is the only system fast enough. Apply diagnosis in a separate pass.
Fixing typos as you draft Translating and low-level proofreading Leave them. Proofreading is the cheapest pass and the one most easily done last, and interrupting translation for it is the worst trade in the list.
Reorganising while drafting a section Translating and organising, which is the most expensive collision Note the reorganisation and continue. A structural change mid-draft invalidates the translating you are in the middle of, so the work is lost twice.
Checking a figure in the data mid-sentence Translating and retrieval from an external source, which flushes the sentence you were holding Write "[CHECK: 3.1%?]" and continue. The bracket costs two seconds; the flush costs the paragraph.

The last row is worth a note because it is the one professionals do most and defend most. Verifying a figure feels like rigour, and the rigour is real and belongs in a different pass. A bracketed placeholder discharges the obligation without discarding the sentence, and a sweep for brackets before circulation is a five-minute pass that catches all of them.

Training the monitor

Most of the model is not directly trainable, since you cannot decide to have more working memory or a richer long-term store on demand. The monitor is the exception, because it is a set of switching decisions, and switching decisions can be made deliberately rather than by feel.

Practically that means giving yourself explicit switch conditions rather than switching when discomfort suggests it. Generate until you have run all five of module 7's topics, then stop generating. Draft until the section is complete, then review. Review structure until the outline is stable, then review sentences. Each of those replaces a felt impulse with a stated condition, and the felt impulse is unreliable in a specific direction: it tends to switch away from translating, because translating is the effortful one.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The review gate is in the wrong place, and the stage model put it there

Every institutional document workflow has the same shape: an author drafts, a reviewer comments, an approver signs off. That is a governance structure and it is fine as one. It also encodes the stage model, because it assumes the writing is finished when the draft is finished, and the consequence is a review that arrives after translating and therefore cannot touch planning.

The symptom is universally recognised and rarely diagnosed. Reviewer comments on a finished draft are overwhelmingly local: wording, tone, a request for a caveat, a query about a number. Structural comments, when they appear, are resented, because acting on one means discarding the translating work the draft consists of, and both parties know it. So the reviewer learns not to make them, and the document's structure is effectively unreviewed.

The repair is cheap and almost nobody does it. Review the outline, not the draft. A one-page structure, circulated before translating begins, can be restructured at negligible cost, and it is the only point at which structural review is actually free. That single change moves the reviewer from commenting on sentences to commenting on the thing that determines whether the document works.

It is worth being precise about the claim, because the model does not say the draft review is useless. Sentence-level review has real value and it is the cheap half. What the model says is that a process with only one gate has placed it where structural feedback is most expensive, and adding an earlier and much shorter gate costs less than the rework that its absence guarantees.

What the model gets criticised for, fairly

Flower and Hayes's paper is among the most cited in composition studies and it has taken serious criticism, some of which the authors accepted.

The methodological objection concerns think-aloud protocols. Asking writers to verbalise while composing may distort the process being measured, particularly by favouring the parts that are verbalisable, and it cannot capture what happens below the level of report. That is a genuine limit on the evidence rather than a refutation of the model, and it means the model's fine structure is less secure than its broad claims.

The larger objection came from the social turn in composition studies during the 1980s. Critics argued that the model treats writing as an individual cognitive act and brackets out the social context that determines what counts as a good text at all: discourse communities, institutional constraints, genre obligations. That criticism is right, and this course has spent module 7 on precisely the material the model omits. Miller's genre and Bitzer's situation are not inside the box labelled "the rhetorical problem"; they constitute it.

Hayes revised the model himself in 1996, and honestly. The revision brings working memory in explicitly, adds motivation and affect, expands the task environment to include the social as well as the physical, and reorganises the processes so that reviewing is decomposed rather than treated as a peer of the other two. The 1981 version remains the one everyone cites and the 1996 version is the one to believe.

Why this reinterprets sixty lectures

The claim the module index makes, and that this lecture supplies the evidence for, is that almost nothing in modules 1 to 8 is a composition rule. It is worth stating carefully because it sounds like a demotion and is not.

Nobody drafts by consulting the character and action principle. Nobody composes a sentence by checking whether the stress position holds the new information. Those procedures are deliberate, slow, and they contend with translating for the same resource, so applying them while drafting produces the four-sentences-an-hour failure. They are reviewing operations, and the model says reviewing is a separate process from translating.

Which is not a demotion, because reviewing is where most of a document's quality is determined. It does change what you should expect from having read this course, though, and the expectation is precise: you should not expect to draft better sentences. You should expect to be able to find and fix worse ones, which is a different and more reliable capability, and it is what modules 3 and 8 were pointing at when they insisted on ordered passes without being able to say why.

Common failure Taking the recursion finding as licence to abandon sequence entirely. The processes interleave in cognition and the deliberate work still has to be ordered, because structural repairs invalidate local ones and not the reverse. Recursive does not mean unordered: it means you cannot expect to finish planning before you start translating, not that a revision pass on sentences before a revision pass on structure is a reasonable use of an afternoon.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take your next document and run the hybrid from step 4: fast bad draft, then outline from it, then write the deliverable. Report whether you threw the first draft away, and if not, what shape it left in the final text.
  2. Then compose. Pick the habit from step 4's table you do most and suppress it for one document. Report the effect on drafting speed.
  3. Name the three writing processes and the thing that governs them. What does the model say about their order?
  4. What two things does the task environment contain? Why does the second explain plans not surviving drafting?
  5. State the capacity finding and say what it explains about separate revision passes.
  6. Give the test for whether an outline will help. What is the professional signature of the avoidance case?
  7. How does the hybrid sequence resolve Klinkenborg against Minto? What must happen to the first draft?
  8. Why is checking a figure mid-sentence the worst item in step 4's table, and what discharges the obligation?
  9. Why is the monitor the trainable part of the model, and in which direction is the felt impulse unreliable?
  10. Why is the institutional review gate in the wrong place? What is the cheap repair, and what does the model not claim?
  11. Give the methodological objection and the social objection to the model, and say what the 1996 revision changed.
  12. What should you expect from having read this course, and what should you not expect?
