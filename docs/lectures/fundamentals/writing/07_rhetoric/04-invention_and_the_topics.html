Invention and the topics

A systematic procedure for finding something to say, and the four-question test that locates what a dispute is actually about. Stasis theory is the most immediately useful thing in classical rhetoric and almost nobody in professional life has heard of it.

Lecture 4 of 9 Reading time, 34 minutes Source: Aristotle, Rhetoric; Hermagoras via Cicero and Quintilian
Step 1

The 30-second version

The classical tradition holds that finding something to say is a procedure rather than a gift. The topics are the procedure: a small set of question types that can be asked of any subject and that reliably generate material. Ask what kind of thing it is, what it resembles and differs from, what causes it and what it causes, what the circumstances permit, and who says so. Five questions, and an empty analysis section stops being empty.

Stasis theory is the other half and the more valuable one. It says a dispute can rest at one of four levels, and the levels are hierarchical. Did it happen? What kind of thing is it? How serious is it? Whose decision is it? Two people arguing at different stases talk past each other indefinitely, and no amount of force at your level moves someone standing at another.

That is the practical payload. A validation finding can be resisted at conjecture ("your number is wrong"), at definition ("that is a data issue, not a model deficiency"), at quality ("it is immaterial") or at jurisdiction ("that is second line's call"). Each resistance needs a different answer, and the commonest professional failure is to keep arguing materiality at somebody who is disputing the number.

Feynman test Two lists. One helps you find things to say: define it, compare it, trace its causes, check what is possible, cite who agrees. The other tells you where the fight is: does it exist, what is it, how bad is it, who decides. Get the second one wrong and you will argue well and lose.
What is beyond the source The topics and the stases are ancient and the four-stasis scheme is standard. Two things here are not. The first is step 4's mapping of the stases onto model-risk resistance, which is the lecture's centre and which turns a forensic scheme into a diagnostic for institutional deadlock. The second is the claim in step 5 that stasis mismatch, rather than disagreement, accounts for most of the arguments that go nowhere in professional life, and that recognising it is a listening skill before it is a writing one.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that lecture 3 left a hole. The canons put invention first and say nothing about how to do it, and six modules of this course taught style and arrangement while assuming you already had material. The topics are the missing method, and they are the answer to the blank analysis section that every professional writer meets and most treat as a mood problem rather than a procedural one.

The second reason is stasis theory, which earns its place on frequency alone. Every disputed document in an institution is disputed at some stasis, and the parties usually have not established which. An hour of a committee's time goes on one side asserting materiality while the other side has not accepted the measurement, and both leave believing the other is being unreasonable. Naming the stasis takes ten seconds and converts a deadlock into an ordinary disagreement.

The third reason is that the stases are hierarchical, which yields a rule about order that is worth more than the taxonomy. You cannot win quality while conjecture is contested. If the reader does not accept that the peak loss rate is 3.1 per cent, arguing that a 40 basis point understatement matters is arguing about the properties of a number they do not concede exists. Settle the lower stasis first, always, even when the higher one is where your interest lies.

And a reason that connects back to lecture 1. Vatz's objection was that the rhetor constructs the situation, and stasis theory is the mechanism. Choosing to frame a finding as a definitional question rather than a factual one is choosing a different argument with different evidence and a different audience. That choice is invention, and it is made before a sentence is written.

Step 3

The mental model

Two instruments. The topics generate material; the stases locate the dispute. The second is a ladder and the rungs must be climbed in order.

Topic
A topos, literally a place: a standard region of thought you go to in order to find an argument. Not a subject but a question type, applicable to any subject.
Stasis
The point at which a dispute rests, being the level at which the parties actually disagree. Four levels: conjecture, definition, quality, jurisdiction.
Stasis mismatch
Two parties arguing at different levels, each treating the other as unreasonable. The commonest cause of professional arguments that go nowhere, and diagnosable in one question.
Step 4

The detail

The topics as a working checklist

Run all five against your subject and write down what each produces. The discipline is completeness rather than insight: the value is that you notice the missing comparison or the absent authority before a reader does, and that the fifth topic gets run at all.

SUBJECT: the downturn LGD calibration understates peak loss by about 40 basis points. 1 DEFINITION what kind of thing is this? A calibration deficiency, because the estimator is fitted to a sample that cannot contain the quantity being estimated. Not a data-quality issue, because the data are accurate for the period they cover. Not a monitoring failure, because the monitoring is working: it flagged the drift. -> This is the definitional ground you must hold, and step 4's stasis table shows why it will be attacked. 2 COMPARISON what is it like, unlike, more or less than? Like the 2019 supervisory finding on the unsecured portfolio, which had the same cause. Unlike a downturn add-on shortfall, which is a conservatism question rather than an estimation one. Smaller than the 2019 gap (40bp against 120bp) but in a portfolio 3.5 times the size. -> The comparison is where the magnitude becomes interpretable. 40bp means nothing on its own. 3 RELATIONSHIP causes, effects, contraries, contradictions? Cause: the window runs 2011 to 2023 and contains no severe downturn. Effect: understated downturn LGD, understated RWA, overstated headroom. Contradiction: the model documentation asserts the window is downturn-representative, which cannot be true of a window with no downturn in it. -> The contradiction is the strongest single item the topics produced, and it came from a question most writers never ask. 4 CIRCUMSTANCE what is possible, what has happened, what follows? Possible: 2008 recovery data exist for this portfolio but the collateral records are incomplete. Past: the same window was accepted at the 2021 review. Future: the next capital return is in five months. -> Circumstance is where the ask becomes realistic. An unachievable remediation is not a recommendation. 5 TESTIMONY who says so, what rule, what precedent? CRR article 181(1)(b) requires LGD appropriate for an economic downturn. The 2019 finding, and its accepted remediation. The model owner's own 2021 paper conceding the window is short. -> The last item is the most useful thing in the list and the topic most often skipped.

Note what the procedure did. It produced one item, the documentation contradiction, that changes the character of the finding, and one item, the model owner's own concession, that changes who can resist it. Neither came from insight. Both came from asking a question in a list.

The stases against real resistance

Now the selection instrument. The same finding, taken to a committee, meets four different objections, and the table gives what each one is, what answers it, and what fails.

The objection you hearStasisWhat answers itWhat fails, and why
One finding, four resistances, four different answers
"Your 3.1 per cent peak is an artefact of the collateral gap." Conjecture Evidence. The reconstruction method, its sensitivity to the missing records, the range under the worst assumption. Materiality. You are describing the properties of a number they do not accept. Every sentence about 40 basis points is wasted.
"That is a data limitation, not a model deficiency." Definition Argument from the agreed definition. The data are accurate; the estimator is fitted to a sample that excludes the estimand. That is calibration. More evidence about the magnitude. They have conceded the number. The dispute has moved and your measurement work no longer bears on it.
"Forty basis points is immaterial against our headroom." Quality Comparison and consequence. The 2019 precedent, the portfolio size, what the understatement does at the next stress. This is the only stasis where materiality is the argument. Re-proving the number, which they have conceded twice over. Repeating evidence at a conceded stasis reads as not listening.
"This is second line's call, not a validation finding." Jurisdiction The mandate, and lecture 1's audience test. Who is capable of mediating change, and whether the proposed forum can act. Arguing the merits again. They have conceded the merits entirely, which is what makes jurisdiction such an effective place to stand.

The fourth row deserves attention because it is the most sophisticated resistance and the easiest to mistake for agreement. A jurisdictional objection concedes fact, category and seriousness, and still produces no action. Responding by restating the merits confirms what nobody is disputing. The answer is about mandate and forum, and it is a different document.

Finding the stasis before you write

THE DIAGNOSTIC Ask, of the reader who will resist: Q1 Do they accept that the thing happened, at the magnitude I state? NO -> stasis is CONJECTURE. Write evidence. Do not mention materiality at all. YES -> go on. Q2 Do they accept my characterisation of what it is? NO -> stasis is DEFINITION. Write from the agreed definition to the disputed case. Stop producing measurements. YES -> go on. Q3 Do they accept it matters enough to act? NO -> stasis is QUALITY. Write comparison and consequence. Precedent is your best evidence. YES -> go on. Q4 Do they accept that this forum should decide? NO -> stasis is JURISDICTION. Write mandate, not merits. Possibly write to a different reader. YES -> there is no dispute. Write the ask and stop. IF YOU DO NOT KNOW THE ANSWERS Then that is the finding, and the first thing to do is ask rather than draft. A paper written to the wrong stasis is not a weak paper, it is a paper about a different disagreement. IF THE ANSWERS DIFFER BY READER Lecture 8's problem. The short version: you cannot serve two stases in one document without the reader at each thinking the other's section is padding. Write to the lowest contested stasis, because the higher ones are unreachable until it closes.

The last note is the operational consequence of the hierarchy. With a mixed committee, the lowest contested stasis governs, because a reader who disputes your measurement will not follow you into materiality however well you argue it. That sometimes means writing a duller paper than you wanted, about evidence rather than consequence, and it is the paper that moves.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Most deadlock is mismatch, not disagreement

The claim worth taking from this lecture is that a large share of the arguments that go nowhere in institutional life are not disagreements at all. They are two parties arguing correctly at different stases, each with a coherent position, each experiencing the other as evasive.

The signature is specific and once you know it you will see it weekly. Neither side's arguments land; both sides repeat rather than develop; each becomes convinced the other is acting in bad faith. That pattern is diagnostic, because genuine disagreement at a shared stasis produces movement, even if only towards a sharper statement of where the parties differ. Repetition without movement means the parties are not addressing the same question.

The remedy is a sentence and it is not a writing technique. "Before we go on, are we disagreeing about whether the number is right, or about whether it matters?" That question resolves more meetings than any amount of preparation, and its cost is nothing. It is also, and this is the part professionals find hardest, a question you should ask when you are winning, because winning an argument at a stasis your reader has left is losing slowly.

Stasis theory is a listening instrument first

The tradition presents the stases as an invention tool: work out the stasis, then compose. That is real and it is the smaller half. The larger use is receptive, because in practice you learn the stasis from what the other party says, and the skill is hearing which of four things an objection is.

That reframes the four table rows as a comprehension exercise. "That is a data limitation" sounds like a quibble and is a definitional move that concedes your measurement entirely, which is a substantial win you will miss if you hear it as resistance. "Forty basis points is immaterial" concedes both fact and category, which means two thirds of your paper has already done its work. Reading objections for their stasis tells you what you have won, and professionals who cannot do it treat every objection as total.

Which suggests a use in the other direction, and it is worth naming rather than leaving implied. If you want to concede something without conceding everything, move up a stasis deliberately. Granting the number while disputing the category is an honest position and a strong one. The technique is available to both sides, and knowing it is available is most of the defence against it.

What the topics cannot do

The topics generate material and they do not evaluate it. Running all five produces perhaps twenty items of which three matter, and the classical tradition has no procedure for the selection because selection is judgement. This is the honest limit of invention as a method, and it is why lecture 3 called the canons content-free about the very thing they order first.

There is a second limit that bears on this collection specifically. The topics are excellent at finding arguments and indifferent at finding truths, since nothing in the procedure distinguishes the item that is correct from the item that is merely available. A writer who runs the five topics on a position they hold will produce a well-supported case for it whether or not it is right, and the discipline that guards against that is not rhetorical. It is the ordinary analytical practice of trying to falsify your own finding before you write it up, which lecture 9 treats under steelmanning and which no amount of invention supplies.

Common failure Using stasis theory to win rather than to locate. The scheme makes it easy to notice that a dispute at quality can be shifted to jurisdiction, and shifting it is sometimes exactly the manoeuvre that a party with no case uses to avoid one. If you find yourself moving the stasis because your position at the current one is weak, you are not analysing the dispute, you are stalling it. Module 9's lecture 7 is about that line and about what happens when clarity's own instruments are turned to obscuring.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a finding of your own and run all five topics on it in the format of step 4's worked example. Report which topic produced the item you had not already thought of, and whether you had run topic 5 before.
  2. Then compose. Run the four-question diagnostic on the same finding against a real reader. State the stasis, and say what your existing draft argues at. If they differ, say what has to be cut.
  3. Name the five common topics and the question each asks.
  4. Name the four stases in order, in English and in Latin, and say what each disputes.
  5. Why can you not win a higher stasis while a lower one is open? Give the worked example.
  6. Someone says "that is a data limitation, not a model deficiency". What stasis is that, what have they conceded, and what answers it? What fails?
  7. What is the signature of a stasis mismatch, and why is repetition without movement diagnostic?
  8. Why is stasis theory a listening instrument before it is a writing one? What do you learn about your own position from hearing an objection's stasis?
  9. What can the topics not do, and what discipline outside rhetoric has to supply it?
  10. When is moving the stasis honest, and when is it stalling?

Source material

  • Aristotle, Rhetoric, books I and II, for the topics, both the common topics and the enthymeme they feed.
  • Hermagoras of Temnos, to whom the four-stasis scheme is attributed. His work is lost and it survives through later accounts, principally Cicero's De Inventione and Quintilian's Institutio Oratoria book III, which differ in detail from one another.
  • Edward P. J. Corbett and Robert Connors, Classical Rhetoric for the Modern Student, for the five-way grouping of the common topics used in step 3. The grouping is theirs rather than Aristotle's, who does not present the topics as a tidy list of five.
  • CRR article 181(1)(b), for the downturn LGD requirement cited in the testimony topic.
