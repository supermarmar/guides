Arrangement

The six-part classical oration against Minto's pyramid. One builds to its conclusion and one opens with it, and the choice is not a matter of taste but of whether your reader is captive and whether they will accept the answer cold.

Lecture 5 of 9 Reading time, 32 minutes Source: Rhetorica ad Herennium; Cicero, De Inventione; Quintilian, Institutio Oratoria III; Minto
Step 1

The 30-second version

The classical oration has six parts. An exordium that secures a hearing, a narratio that lays out the facts, a partitio that says what will be argued and in what order, a confirmatio that makes the positive case, a refutatio that answers objections, and a peroratio that recapitulates and asks. The sequence is climactic: it builds towards the conclusion.

Minto's pyramid is the opposite. It states the answer first and supports it downwards, on the premise that the reader is busy and may stop reading at any point. Both structures are correct and they assume different readers. The oration assumes a captive audience who will hear you out; the pyramid assumes a reader with a diary and no obligation.

The practical answer for professional documents is nested rather than exclusive: pyramid at the document level, so the answer arrives on page one, and classical within the argument, so the reader who wants the mechanism gets walked through it. That is what a good validation report already looks like, and the classical scheme explains why.

One part of the classical scheme has no equivalent in the pyramid at all. Minto has nowhere to put a refutatio, because her method handles objections by making the logic exhaustive so that objections should not arise. That works against logical gaps and not against interests, which is most of what a professional writer actually faces.

Feynman test Six parts: get their attention, tell them what happened, say what you will argue, argue it, deal with the objections, sum up and ask. That order builds to the point. Minto puts the point first because your reader might stop on page two. Use both: point first for the document, six parts inside the argument.
What is beyond the source The two schemes are well documented and setting them against each other is standard. Three things here are not. The first is the claim in step 4 that the pyramid has no slot for refutation and that this is a substantive gap rather than an omission, because Minto's exhaustiveness answers logical objections and not interested ones. The second is step 4's rule that stasis theory determines where refutation goes: an objection at a lower stasis than your case must precede it, an objection at the same stasis may follow. The third is the argument in step 5 that the exordium's real job, securing goodwill, is the part professional writers skip entirely, and that lecture 2's ethos apparatus is its modern replacement.
Step 2

Why this matters

Module 4 taught the pyramid and treated it as the professional default, which it is. What module 4 could not do, because it had no alternative to compare against, was say what the pyramid gives up. Setting it against the classical scheme makes the trade visible: the pyramid buys reach at the cost of persuasion, because a conclusion stated cold to a reader who does not want it invites rejection before the support arrives.

That trade is live in a specific professional situation which module 4 did not address. When your reader has an interest in the finding being wrong, answer-first hands them the conclusion at the moment they are least willing to accept it, and everything after reads as post-hoc justification. The classical alternative walks them through the facts they already accept and arrives at the conclusion as a consequence, which is slower, reaches fewer readers, and sometimes the only thing that works.

The second reason is the refutatio, which is the most useful part of the scheme and the part professional documents most often lack. Lecture 4 gave you a method for identifying what will be objected to; the classical arrangement tells you where the answer goes and that it needs a place. Most validation reports have no refutation section at all, which means every objection gets raised for the first time in the meeting, where you answer it under pressure and from memory.

And a reason about exam answers, which are the one genuinely captive audience most readers of this collection still write for. A marker reads to the end because they must, which removes the premise the pyramid rests on. The classical structure is available there and its partitio in particular, the explicit statement of what you will argue in what order, is worth marks in a way it is not in a board paper.

Step 3

The mental model

Two shapes, opposed in direction, with the professional resolution nested rather than chosen.

Climactic arrangement
Building towards the conclusion, so that the reader arrives at it as a consequence of what precedes. Assumes the reader will stay, and buys acceptance at the cost of reach.
Refutatio
The part of an argument that states and answers the objections. Present in every classical scheme, absent from the pyramid, and missing from most professional documents.
Partitio
An explicit statement of what will be argued and in what order. Derided in modern style guides as throat-clearing, and worth its space when the argument has more than three moves or the reader is a marker.
Step 4

The detail

Choosing the direction

Reader conditionDirectionWhy, and what it costs
The choice is about the reader, not the subject
Busy, free to stop, neutral on the conclusion Pyramid Reach. Everyone who reads one sentence gets the answer. Costs nothing here, which is why it is the professional default.
Has an interest in the finding being wrong Classical Acceptance. Stated cold, the conclusion triggers resistance and everything after reads as justification. Costs you the readers who stop early, which may be acceptable if they were never the audience.
Cannot accept the conclusion without the mechanism Classical Comprehension. A counterintuitive conclusion asserted first is not understood, only noted. Costs length.
Captive: exam marker, tabled paper, rubric Either, and partitio earns its keep The premise behind the pyramid does not hold, so the constraint is gone. A marker rewards visible structure, which is what partitio supplies.
Mixed, which is the real case Nested Pyramid for the document, classical for the argument. Costs discipline, because the summary and the argument must not restate one another.

The second row is the one that departs from module 4's advice, and it is worth stating plainly. Where the reader has an interest, answer-first can be the wrong choice, and the reason is not that the pyramid is badly designed. It is that Minto's reader is assumed to want the answer, and a model owner reading a finding about their own model does not.

Where the refutatio goes

Classical practice places refutation after the positive case: build your argument, then dismantle theirs. That is right when the objection is a competing account. It is wrong when the objection is a precondition, and lecture 4 supplies the test for telling them apart.

THE PLACEMENT RULE Compare the stasis of the objection with the stasis of your case. OBJECTION AT A LOWER STASIS THAN YOUR CASE -> refutation must come FIRST, before the confirmatio. Your case is at quality: a 40bp understatement matters. Their objection is at conjecture: the 3.1% peak is a data artefact. They will not follow your materiality argument, because it is about a number they have not conceded. Deal with the measurement, then argue materiality. In classical terms the refutation has become part of the narratio: it is establishing the facts of the case rather than answering a rival case. OBJECTION AT THE SAME STASIS -> refutation comes AFTER, as the tradition has it. Your case is at quality: 40bp matters given the 2019 precedent and the portfolio size. Their objection is at quality: headroom absorbs it. Two accounts of the same question. Put yours first, then answer theirs, because a case stated after a rebuttal reads as defensive. OBJECTION AT A HIGHER STASIS -> refutation comes LAST, or belongs in a different document. Your case is at quality. Their objection is at jurisdiction: not validation's call. They have conceded your entire case. Arguing it again is answering a question nobody asked. Address the mandate, briefly, at the end, and consider whether the real document is a note to whoever owns the boundary. WHAT NEVER WORKS Omitting the refutatio and handling objections in the meeting. You will answer from memory, under pressure, to a room that has not seen your reasoning, and the objection will have been stated first by someone else.

The gap in the pyramid

Minto's method has no refutation slot, and this is a design position rather than an oversight. The pyramid handles objections structurally: if the supporting points are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive, and each answers the question its parent raises, then there is nothing left to object to on logical grounds. Exhaustiveness is the refutation.

That reasoning is sound and it covers a specific class of objection, being the kind that says your argument has a hole. It does not cover the objection that says your argument is complete and I do not accept its premise, or I accept all of it and will not act, which are the two objections professional writers meet most. MECE structure cannot answer an interest, because an interest is not a logical gap.

So the practical amendment to module 4 is small and consequential. Build the pyramid, then add a section the pyramid does not provide for, in which the two or three objections you actually expect are stated in their strongest form and answered. It will look like an appendage to the logical structure because it is one, and it is the section that decides whether the paper does anything.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The exordium, and the job professionals have stopped doing

Read what the classical sources actually ask of an opening and it is not what a modern document does. The exordium's job is to make the audience attentive, receptive and well disposed, and the third of those is the one that has vanished. A modern opening establishes relevance and scope; it does not attempt to secure goodwill, because professional convention holds that goodwill is not the writer's business.

The convention is defensible and the need it papered over did not go away. A model owner reading a finding about their own work needs a reason to read it as help rather than as attack, and no amount of clear scope-setting supplies one. What supplies it is what lecture 2 called ethos construction: the marked concession, the calibrated hedge, the named limit of your own analysis. Those are not politeness. They are the exordium's function performed by other means, distributed through the document rather than concentrated at the front.

Which explains something module 4 could not. Module 4's lecture 1 treated the opening as a contract about content and said nothing about disposition, and the reason is that the modern opening has no room for it. The classical scheme had a whole part for the job, and its disappearance is a real loss that the ethos apparatus only partly covers.

The tradition could not agree on the number

The Rhetorica ad Herennium gives six parts. Cicero's De Inventione gives six with different names for two of them. Quintilian, in book III, gives five and reports that his sources disagreed about whether partitio is a part at all or merely a feature of the narratio. The scheme was contested within the tradition that transmitted it.

That is worth knowing because of how the six parts are usually taught, which is as a template. A structure whose own authorities differed about its parts is a checklist of functions that a persuasive text tends to perform, not a form to be filled in. The functions are stable: gain a hearing, establish facts, signal structure, argue, refute, close with an ask. Whether refutation is a part or a phase of the argument is a question about labels.

Treating it as a checklist also fixes the obvious failure, which is a document with six visible sections including a partitio nobody needed and a peroratio that repeats the summary. The test for each part is whether its function is being performed somewhere, not whether it has a heading.

What the pyramid's dominance conceals

The pyramid won, in consulting and then in banking, and it won for a good reason: the reader it assumes is the reader who exists. It is worth being clear about what that victory selected for, though, because the selection is invisible from inside it.

Answer-first structure optimises for a reader who has delegated the reasoning and wants a conclusion they can act on. It is therefore superbly suited to advice and poorly suited to disagreement, because disagreement requires that the reader follow the reasoning rather than accept the conclusion. An institution that writes exclusively in pyramids becomes fluent at recommending and clumsy at arguing, and you can hear it in a committee where every paper opens with an answer and nobody can walk through why.

The honest position is that both structures are needed and only one is taught. If you write pyramids by default and cannot construct a climactic argument, you have a gap that will show the first time you need to move somebody who does not want to be moved, which in a validation function is monthly.

Common failure Nesting badly, so that the executive summary and the argument section are the same content at two lengths. The nested structure only works if the summary states the answer and the argument does something the summary did not: establishes the facts, walks the mechanism, answers the objections. If a reader who has read your summary learns nothing from your argument, you have written a long document with a short one inside it, and module 5's lecture 1 has a name for that.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a paper of yours built as a pyramid and add the section it lacks: two objections in their strongest form, each answered. Then apply the placement rule and say where each belongs relative to your case.
  2. Name the six classical parts in order and give each one's function in a phrase.
  3. What does climactic mean, and which reader does it assume? Which does the pyramid assume?
  4. Give the two reader conditions under which classical arrangement beats answer-first, and say what each choice costs.
  5. State the placement rule for refutation in terms of stasis. What happens when the objection sits at a higher stasis than your case?
  6. Why does the pyramid have no refutation slot? What class of objection does exhaustiveness answer, and what two classes does it not?
  7. What was the exordium's third job, and what performs it in a modern document?
  8. How many parts does the classical oration have? Answer carefully, and say what the disagreement implies about how to use the scheme.
  9. What does exclusive fluency in the pyramid make an institution clumsy at, and why?
  10. What is the test for whether the nested structure has been built properly?
