Module 7 · Writing track

Rhetoric

Situation, audience, genre and persuasion. Writing does work in the world, and the oldest continuous tradition in the field is the one that treats every text as an intervention in a situation rather than as an object. Nine lectures restoring it.

What this module is

The preceding six modules treat writing as a transmission problem: you have something to say and the reader has to receive it intact. Rhetoric treats it as an action problem. Somebody has a decision to make, or a belief to revise, or an interest that your document threatens, and the text exists to move them. That reframing changes what counts as a good sentence.

Most of the apparatus is two and a half thousand years old and has survived because it works. The five canons, invention, arrangement, style, memory and delivery, still map onto a modern drafting workflow more accurately than any productivity system. Stasis theory is the fastest available method for working out what an argument is actually about before you write a word of it. The distinction between schemes and tropes gives you names for effects you have been producing by accident.

Two lectures are modern and both are directly practical for professional writers. Genre theory, in Miller's formulation, treats a document type as a typified response to a recurring situation rather than as a template, and Swales's move analysis gives you a procedure for reverse-engineering an unfamiliar genre from three examples of it. That is the skill of walking into a new institution and working out what a credit paper is supposed to look like there. Audience analysis, in the final part, is where the module becomes uncomfortable, because it forces you to write for a reader who may not want what you are offering.

Prerequisites

  • Module 4: architecture. Lecture 5 sets the classical arrangement against Minto's pyramid, so you need the pyramid.
  • Module 6, lecture 3: the styles classic style is not. The oratorical and prophetic styles are rhetorical stances, and this module explains what they are for.

Module sequence

Part 1 · The frame (lectures 1 to 3)

What a rhetorical situation is, how persuasion is conventionally analysed, and the production process the tradition hands down.

  • builtLecture 1: the rhetorical situation. Bitzer's exigence, audience and constraints, with Vatz's objection that the rhetor constructs the situation rather than discovering it. Purpose, occasion, and the question of who can actually act on what you write.
  • builtLecture 2: ethos, pathos, logos and kairos. The three appeals plus timing. Ethos as something constructed within the text rather than imported from your job title, and pathos as legitimate whenever the emotion genuinely fits the case.
  • builtLecture 3: the five canons. Invention, arrangement, style, memory and delivery, codified in Cicero and transmitted through Quintilian, mapped onto a modern drafting workflow. Memory and delivery survive in presentation and formatting rather than having died out.

Part 2 · Invention, arrangement, style (lectures 4 to 6)

The three canons that still do daily work, taken one at a time.

  • builtLecture 4: invention and the topics. Aristotle's common topics, definition, comparison, relationship, circumstance and testimony, as a systematic procedure for finding something to say. Stasis theory, conjecture, definition, quality and jurisdiction, as the fastest way to locate what a dispute is actually about.
  • builtLecture 5: arrangement. The six-part classical oration, exordium, narratio, partitio, confirmatio, refutatio and peroratio, set against Minto's pyramid. When a reader needs the answer first and when they need to be walked to it.
  • builtLecture 6: figures, schemes and tropes. Schemes as departures from ordinary word arrangement, anaphora, antithesis, asyndeton, polysyndeton and isocolon. Tropes as departures from ordinary meaning, metaphor, metonymy, synecdoche, irony and litotes. Taught as effects with known uses rather than as terminology to memorise.

Part 3 · Genre, audience, argument (lectures 7 to 9)

The modern half. How to enter an unfamiliar document type, how to model a reader who resists, and how to argue honestly.

  • builtLecture 7: genre as social action. Miller's argument that a genre is a typified rhetorical response to a recurring situation, not a container. Swales's move analysis and the CARS model as a practical procedure for reverse-engineering a document type you have never written.
  • builtLecture 8: audience analysis in practice. Building a reader model: what they know, what they want, what they will resist, and what decision they face. Multiple audiences with conflicting interests, hostile audiences, and the difference between the audience you address and the audience you invoke.
  • builtLecture 9: argument and honest persuasion. Toulmin's claim, grounds, warrant, backing, qualifier and rebuttal, with the warrant as the part writers most often leave unstated. Steelmanning, conceding well, and where argument shades into advocacy.

Where this reappears

Metaphor
A trope among others in lecture 6. Module 8, lecture 2 argues from Lakoff and Johnson that it is not a figure at all but the ordinary machinery of thought.
The reader model
Built deliberately in lecture 8. Module 9, lecture 8 argues that holding one continuously, without effort, is one of the few real markers of a great writer.

Lecture 9 draws the line between argument and advocacy. Module 9, lecture 7 asks what happens when a writer crosses it deliberately and uses clarity's own techniques to obscure.
