Argument and honest persuasion

Toulmin's six parts, the warrant nobody states, and the discovery that advocacy is structurally diagnosable: an argument with no qualifier and no rebuttal is not an argument at all.

Lecture 9 of 9 Reading time, 36 minutes Source: Toulmin, The Uses of Argument (1958)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Stephen Toulmin's analysis breaks an argument into six parts. A claim, the grounds it rests on, the warrant that licenses moving from one to the other, the backing that supports the warrant, a qualifier giving the claim's force, and a rebuttal naming the conditions under which it fails.

The warrant is the part writers leave unstated, because among people who share a field it is too obvious to say. It is also where disputes actually live. When someone tells you your model finding is really a data issue, they are not disputing your grounds or your claim; they are rejecting the general principle that connects them, which is lecture 4's definitional stasis under another name.

Two disciplines follow. Steelmanning means stating an objection in the strongest form its ablest advocate would give it, with the test being whether they would sign your version. And conceding well means conceding something that actually costs you, because a concession that costs nothing reads as a tactic and does the opposite of what it intends.

The module closes on a structural test rather than an appeal to integrity. Argument follows evidence to a conclusion; advocacy starts from a conclusion and collects evidence for it. A Toulmin diagram with no qualifier and no rebuttal is advocacy, whatever the writer believes, because those two parts are where an honest arguer records what would make them wrong.

Feynman test Six parts: what you claim, what you rest it on, the rule that connects them, why that rule holds, how strongly you claim it, and what would make it fail. Most professional writing states the first two and omits the rest. The rule is where the fight is, and the last two are how you can tell an argument from a campaign.
What is beyond the source Toulmin's scheme is standard and steelmanning is a common informal notion. Three claims are this lecture's own. The first is that the warrant is exactly what a stasis-two dispute contests, so Toulmin and Hermagoras describe the same battleground with different vocabulary. The second is step 4's worked case in which the rebuttal does not weaken the finding but converts it into a different finding, which is what makes rebuttals worth writing rather than fearing. The third is step 5's claim that advocacy is diagnosable structurally from a missing qualifier and rebuttal, which turns a question about motive into a question about the page.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that unstated warrants are the commonest cause of arguments that feel airtight to the writer and unpersuasive to the reader. You have the grounds and you have the claim, the connection between them is obvious to you because you have spent three weeks inside the problem, and the reader does not have it. They experience the argument as a leap, and they cannot tell you which step is missing because the missing step was never written down.

The second reason is diagnostic and it saves whole meetings. Once you can see that a dispute is about a warrant rather than about grounds, you know that more evidence will not help, because evidence supports grounds and warrants are supported by backing, which is a different kind of thing: a rule, a precedent, an accepted definition. That is why the "this is a data issue" objection cannot be answered with more numbers.

The third reason belongs to this collection specifically. A validator's professional obligation is to argue rather than advocate, and the distinction is usually taught as a matter of independence and mindset, which makes it unfalsifiable. Toulmin makes it checkable. If your finding carries no qualifier and names no condition under which it would be wrong, you have written advocacy, and that is visible on the page to anyone including you.

And a reason about steelmanning, which is the cheapest quality control available. Lecture 5 established that a refutatio needs the objection in it; this lecture supplies the standard the statement has to meet. An objection you have weakened before answering produces a document that reads as complete and collapses the first time the real objection is spoken aloud.

Step 3

The mental model

Six parts, with the two that professional writing omits marked, and the warrant sitting exactly where the stasis-two dispute happens.

Warrant
The general principle licensing the inference from grounds to claim. Usually unstated among specialists, because shared, and therefore the part a reader outside your field cannot supply.
Qualifier
The modal force of the claim: probably, presumably, in this portfolio, by roughly this much. Not a hedge but a statement of strength, and its absence is a claim of certainty whether or not you meant one.
Steelmanning
Stating an opposing position in the strongest form its ablest advocate would give it, often stronger than they gave it themselves. The opposite of a straw man, and testable: would they sign your version?
Step 4

The detail

The rebuttal that converts the finding

Writers omit rebuttals because a rebuttal looks like a concession that weakens the claim. Sometimes it does. More often, and this is the reason to write them, the rebuttal turns out to specify a different finding, which is information you needed and would otherwise have discovered in a meeting.

THE FULL ARGUMENT, WRITTEN OUT CLAIM The downturn LGD estimate is understated. GROUNDS The estimation window (2011 to 2023) has a worst observed arrears rate of 1.4 per cent. The 2008 peak for this portfolio was 3.1 per cent. WARRANT A downturn LGD estimate is valid only if its estimation window contains a period of severity comparable to the regulatory downturn definition. BACKING CRR article 181(1)(b). The 2019 supervisory finding on the unsecured portfolio, which turned on the same principle and was accepted. The model documentation's own assertion that the window is downturn- representative. QUALIFIER Probably, and by roughly 30 to 50 basis points. The range reflects uncertainty in the 2008 reconstruction, not in the direction of the effect. REBUTTAL Unless the 2008 collateral records are so incomplete that the 3.1 per cent figure cannot be relied on. >>> NOW READ THE REBUTTAL AGAIN. It does not weaken the claim. It CONVERTS it. If the 2008 records are unusable, then the bank cannot demonstrate downturn representativeness for this portfolio at all, and the finding is no longer "the estimate is 40bp low". It becomes "the estimate cannot be validated", which is a more serious finding, sits at a different stasis, and has a different owner and a different remedy. A writer who omitted the rebuttal to protect the claim would have concealed the stronger version of their own finding from themselves. THE GENERAL FORM Write the rebuttal, then ask: if the rebuttal condition holds, what is true? Sometimes "nothing, I have no finding". Sometimes a different and larger finding. You cannot know which until you write it.

Steelmanning, with a test that fails honestly

Version of the objectionWhat it isVerdict
One objection, three ways of stating it before answering
"The model owner considers the finding immaterial." A report of a position, with no content Straw man by omission. Nothing here can be answered, so answering it demonstrates nothing. This is the most common form in practice.
"It has been suggested that headroom absorbs the effect, but headroom is not a substitute for correct calibration." The objection stated in order to be dismissed Straw man. The rebuttal is embedded in the statement, so the objection never gets said. A reader who holds the objection does not recognise it here.
"On the strongest version of the counter-argument: the understatement is within the 120 basis points of CET1 headroom, the portfolio is scheduled for model replacement in 2027, and the cost of rebuilding on reconstructed 2008 data is real while the capital effect is absorbed. On that view remediation destroys value." Steelman Answerable, and worth answering. It also concedes two true things, which is why it is stronger than what the owner actually said.
THE SIGNATURE TEST Would the person holding this objection sign your statement of it as a fair account of their view? If no, you are refuting something they did not say, and your refutation will not survive contact with them. THE STRONGER VERSION OF THE TEST Could you show them your statement of their objection BEFORE showing them your answer, and would they say "yes, that is my point, and better put than I put it"? This is not hypothetical. You can actually do it, and in a validation context it is often the single most useful twenty minutes available: take your steelman to the model owner, ask whether it is fair, and revise. A finding that survives that process rarely fails in committee. WHY WRITERS RESIST A well-stated objection is persuasive, and there is a real fear that stating it well hands the reader a reason to disagree with you. That fear is correct about the mechanism and wrong about the outcome: the reader who holds the objection already has it, and what your document controls is only whether it is answered on the page or in the room.

Conceding well

Lecture 2 argued that a marked concession builds ethos, and module 5's lecture 7 required a counter-position. Both leave a question this lecture has to answer, which is what makes a concession real.

The answer is that a concession is real when it costs you something a reader can identify. "The analysis has limitations" costs nothing. "The 2008 reconstruction relies on collateral records that are incomplete for about a fifth of the portfolio, and a different treatment of the missing records would move the estimate by around 15 basis points" costs you a quantified piece of your own case. The first reads as ritual, and the second is the sentence that makes a sceptical reader trust the rest.

Which produces an uncomfortable corollary that is worth stating because it explains why cheap concessions backfire. A reader who has met many documents can tell the difference, and a ritual concession does not read as neutral, it reads as an attempt to appear balanced, which damages the ethos it was meant to build. If you have nothing that genuinely weakens your case, say nothing, and consider whether that is because you have not looked.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Toulmin and Hermagoras describe the same fight

Lecture 4 gave the four stases as the levels at which a dispute can rest, and this lecture gives Toulmin's six parts as the anatomy of an argument. They were developed two thousand years apart for different purposes, and they overlap so precisely that either can be used to locate the other.

Conjecture disputes are grounds disputes: the parties disagree about the facts. Definition disputes are warrant disputes: the parties agree about the facts and disagree about the principle that connects them to a conclusion. Quality disputes are qualifier disputes: the claim is accepted and its force is contested. Jurisdiction disputes fall outside Toulmin's frame, which is a limitation of his scheme rather than of the stases, because Toulmin analyses arguments and jurisdiction is a question about who gets to make one.

The practical value of noticing this is that each scheme is better at a different job. Stasis theory is faster for diagnosing a live dispute, because you can run its four questions in a meeting. Toulmin is better for constructing an argument on paper, because it names the parts you have to write. Use the stases to find the fight and Toulmin to prepare for it.

The unstated warrant is the mark of expertise, not of laziness

Toulmin's larger project in 1958 was an attack on formal logic's claim to model real reasoning, and one of his central moves was to argue that the standards of a good argument are field-dependent. What counts as adequate backing in law differs from what counts in medicine or engineering, and there is no field-neutral standard above them.

That explains why warrants go unstated, and it is not a failing. A warrant is the shared principle of a field, and specialists do not state their field's principles to one another any more than they define its terms. Among validators, the principle that an estimation window must contain the phenomenon being estimated is not worth saying. It is a warrant precisely because everyone competent holds it.

Which locates the failure exactly. Unstated warrants are harmless within a field and fatal across a boundary, and professional writing crosses boundaries constantly: validator to committee, actuary to board, modeller to auditor. This is module 6's curse of knowledge in its most consequential form, because a missing definition merely puzzles the reader while a missing warrant makes your argument look like an assertion. The revision check is small: for each finding, write the "since" sentence, then decide whether this reader holds it.

Advocacy, and the test that does not rely on knowing your own motives

The distinction between arguing and advocating is normally taught as a matter of independence of mind, which makes it useless as a check, because nobody experiences themselves as an advocate. You experience yourself as someone who has looked at the evidence and reached a conclusion, and so does the advocate.

Toulmin gives a test that does not require self-knowledge. Look at the page. Is there a qualifier calibrated to what the evidence supports, or is the claim unmodalised? Is there a specific rebuttal condition, or none? Those two absences are what advocacy looks like structurally, and they are visible to a colleague and to you, without anyone having to adjudicate your motives.

The deeper version of the test is a question about the process rather than the document: what evidence would have changed your conclusion, and did you go and look for it? An argument can be reconstructed after the fact to have a qualifier and a rebuttal, and a reader cannot tell the difference. You can. That question is the whole content of independence, it takes thirty seconds, and module 9's lecture 7 takes up what happens when the answer is uncomfortable and the document goes out anyway.

Common failure Writing in Toulmin order. The scheme is an analytical instrument for checking an argument you have drafted and a poor template for drafting one, because "claim, grounds, warrant, backing, qualifier, rebuttal" produces prose that reads like a form return. The parts must all be present somewhere and they need not be adjacent or labelled. Lecture 5 governs the order; Toulmin governs the inventory.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Write out one of your own findings in the full six-part form from step 4. Then read your rebuttal and answer the question: if the rebuttal condition holds, what is true? Report whether it produced nothing, or a different finding.
  2. Then compose. Steelman the objection to that finding, apply the signature test, and if you can, take it to the person who holds it.
  3. Name Toulmin's six parts and give the linking word for the warrant and the backing.
  4. Why can more evidence not answer a warrant objection? What does answer one?
  5. Map the four stases onto Toulmin's parts. Which stasis falls outside his scheme, and why is that a limit on Toulmin rather than on the stases?
  6. Give the two straw-man forms from step 4 and say what is wrong with each. Why is the first the commonest in practice?
  7. State the signature test and its stronger version. What is the real fear behind resisting steelmanning, and why does the outcome not follow from it?
  8. What makes a concession real? Why does a cheap concession damage the ethos it was meant to build?
  9. Why do experts leave warrants unstated, and where does that become fatal?
  10. Give the structural test for advocacy, and say why it beats a test based on the writer's independence of mind.
  11. What is the deeper version of that test, and why can only you run it?
