Figures, schemes and tropes

Names for effects you already produce by accident. The value is not in deploying them but in auditing them, and in noticing that professional prose is saturated with two figures nobody thinks of as figures: litotes and the dead metaphor.

Lecture 6 of 9 Reading time, 32 minutes Source: Quintilian, Institutio Oratoria VIII and IX; Corbett and Connors
Step 1

The 30-second version

A scheme is a departure from the ordinary arrangement of words. A trope is a departure from the ordinary meaning of words. Antithesis, which sets two clauses in opposed parallel, is a scheme; metaphor, which says one thing is another, is a trope. Everything in the classical catalogue of figures falls on one side or the other of that line.

Module 5's lecture 6 already taught four of these under other names, so the content is partly familiar. What the names add is not fluency but audit: once you can see that a sentence contains an isocolon, you can ask whether the parallel is doing work or whether you fell into it, and that question has an answer.

Two figures matter far more than the rest for professional writers and neither is usually recognised as a figure. Litotes, affirming by denying the opposite, is endemic: "not immaterial", "not unreasonable", "cannot be ruled out". It lets you assert something while retaining deniability, which is exactly why it is everywhere in supervisory and validation prose and exactly why it should make you suspicious of your own drafts.

And the technical vocabulary of finance is metaphor all the way down. A haircut, a waterfall, a floor, headroom, stress, exposure. Those metaphors are dead, which is fine, and the error is reviving one by accident: losses do not cascade through a floor.

Feynman test Two kinds of figure. Schemes rearrange words: repetition, parallel, opposition, leaving conjunctions out or piling them in. Tropes bend meaning: metaphor, irony, understatement. Learning the names will not make you eloquent. It will let you check whether an effect in your draft is earning its place.
What is beyond the source The taxonomy is ancient and the scheme-trope division is standard. Three things here are not. The first is step 4's verdict table on which figures belong in professional prose, which puts most schemes in and most tropes out with two named exceptions. The second is the treatment of litotes in step 4 as the figure that hedging hides in, which connects it to module 5's lecture 2 and makes it the most consequential figure in the catalogue for this readership. The third is step 5's argument that the figures serve the memory canon rather than decorating the style canon: patterned prose is retainable prose, and the sentence of yours that gets repeated in a committee is the patterned one.
Step 2

Why this matters

The standard objection to teaching the figures is that nobody writes better for knowing that anaphora is called anaphora, and the objection is largely right. This lecture is therefore not an attempt to make you deploy figures. It is an attempt to make three specific things visible that are otherwise invisible.

The first is accidental patterning. A parallel structure creates an expectation of equivalence, so three items in parallel assert that the three are of a kind. When you fall into a parallel without meaning it, you have made a claim you did not intend, and readers act on it. Naming the scheme is what lets you catch that in revision, and module 5's lecture 6 taught the effect without the diagnostic.

The second is litotes, which is the single most consequential figure for anyone writing validation findings or supervisory correspondence. "The model is not unfit for purpose" and "the model is fit for purpose" are not the same claim, and the difference is deniability rather than meaning. Recognising the figure in your own prose tells you when you are hedging under cover of a construction, which module 5's lecture 2 attacked without naming where it hides.

The third is the dead metaphor, which matters because financial vocabulary is built from them and because reactivating one produces prose that is unintentionally ridiculous. That failure is common in exactly the documents that can least afford it, and the remedy is a check that takes seconds once you know to run it.

Step 3

The mental model

One division, a working set of about a dozen figures, and a verdict on each for professional prose.

Scheme
A figure that departs from the ordinary arrangement of words while leaving their meanings intact. Repetition, parallel, opposition, omission, inversion.
Trope
A figure that departs from the ordinary meaning of words. Metaphor, metonymy, irony, understatement, overstatement.
Litotes
Affirmation by denial of the opposite. "Not immaterial" for material. Asserts a claim while leaving room to deny having made it, which is what makes it the professional writer's favourite figure and worth policing.
Step 4

The detail

Litotes, and what it is doing when you reach for it

Module 5's lecture 2 argued that hedging should be calibrated rather than habitual, and gave the test: one hedge per claim, only where the uncertainty is real. What that lecture could not do was say where habitual hedging hides, and the answer is mostly here.

THE LITOTES AUDIT For each construction, what it says, and what it does. "The understatement is not immaterial." SAYS the understatement is material DOES asserts materiality without owning the word, so that a challenge is met with "I did not say it was material" FIX "The understatement is material." KEEP IT IF you genuinely mean the weaker claim: that it clears the immateriality threshold and you are not asserting how far. "It cannot be ruled out that the window is unrepresentative." SAYS almost nothing, on inspection DOES places a concern on the record while assigning it no probability, which is unfalsifiable FIX "The window is probably unrepresentative, because it contains no downturn." Or delete it. KEEP IT IF you are recording a possibility you have genuinely no basis to weight, and say so. "The model owner's response was not unhelpful." SAYS the response was somewhat helpful DOES conveys reservation while denying it, which the reader hears anyway FIX say what was helpful and what was not. "This is not an unreasonable approach." SAYS the approach is defensible DOES withholds endorsement while appearing to give it. In supervisory correspondence this is sometimes exactly the intended act. KEEP IT IF the deniability is the point and you have decided that deliberately. THE TEST Write the positive form beside it. If the positive form is what you mean, use the positive form. If the positive form is stronger than you can defend, the litotes is not fixing that, it is concealing it, and the honest move is a weaker positive claim with its grounds attached.

The last note is the important one. Litotes is not always wrong and in supervisory prose it is sometimes precisely the right instrument, because "not unreasonable" is a recognised way of declining to endorse. What makes it a fault is reaching for it unconsciously, so that a document ends up making twelve claims none of which the author has committed to, which reads as evasion whether or not it was.

Schemes doing real work

SchemeWhat it makes the reader doWhere it earns its place professionally
Schemes as instruments rather than ornaments
Isocolon and antithesis Read two things as comparable, and their difference as the point Any comparison. "The data are accurate; the sample is not representative" puts the whole finding in nine words because the parallel isolates what changed.
Tricolon Accept three items as members of one class, and feel the set is complete Genuine three-part sets only. Its danger is the false closure: three findings in parallel read as all the findings.
Anaphora Feel accumulation, and register the repeated element as the theme Sparingly, where the repeated element is the finding. Three uses in a paper is one too many.
Asyndeton Move faster, and treat the list as open Enumerating symptoms where you do not want the reader to pause on each. Also useful because a list without "and" does not claim to be exhaustive.
Polysyndeton Slow down, and feel each item separately When the weight of a sequence is the argument. "The window is short and the sample is thin and the benchmark is stale" is heavier than the comma version, deliberately.

The asyndeton row contains the most useful and least obvious point in the table. Dropping the final conjunction leaves a list looking open rather than closed, so it is the honest form when you have listed the symptoms you found rather than all the symptoms that exist. Adding "and" before the last item makes an exhaustiveness claim that most professional lists cannot support.

The dead metaphor check

Financial vocabulary is metaphor that has stopped being noticed. A haircut, a waterfall, a floor, a ceiling, headroom, a buffer, exposure, stress, a shock, contagion, a stress test's severity. These are dead in the sense that no reader pictures anything, and dead is the right state for them, because a dead metaphor is just a precise technical term.

The failure is reviving one accidentally, and it comes from combining two dead metaphors whose original pictures are incompatible. The check is mechanical: read any sentence containing two or more of these terms aloud, and ask whether the pictures agree. Losses cannot cascade through a floor, because floors are stood on and cascades fall. A buffer cannot be breached from beneath. Contagion does not have headroom.

One further note, which module 8 will develop and this lecture should not pre-empt. The claim that metaphor is a figure at all is contested: Lakoff and Johnson argue it is the ordinary machinery of thought rather than a decorative departure from literal language, which would put it outside the trope category entirely. That argument matters for how you read your own technical vocabulary, and it is module 8's lecture 2.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The figures belong to memory, not to style

The figures are conventionally filed under the third canon, style, and there is a case that their real home is the fourth. Patterned language is retainable language: that is why oral traditions are patterned, why the classical schools drilled the figures, and why proverbs are almost all isocolon or antithesis.

Which has a direct professional consequence, and it is the reason this lecture exists in a course that otherwise disdains ornament. Lecture 3 argued that the sentence you can state from memory is the sentence that survives a committee. The sentence you can state from memory is the patterned one. "The data are accurate; the sample is not representative" survives because its antithesis makes it one unit, and the unpatterned equivalent, which says the same thing in twenty-two words, does not.

So the figures are not decoration on top of your argument. They are what determines which sentence of your paper gets quoted back to you in three weeks, and you can choose that sentence in advance. Write the finding as an antithesis or an isocolon, put it in the summary and the peroratio, and it becomes the paper's handle. That is a genuinely available piece of technique and it takes one sentence.

Naming does not produce, but it does audit

The standard objection stands: nobody has ever written a better sentence because they knew the word anaphora, and centuries of schoolroom drilling in the figures produced mostly stilted prose. Generative use of the catalogue does not work, and a writer who consults a list of figures while drafting will produce something worse than one who does not.

The value is in the other direction and it is real. Consider the tricolon. A three-part parallel asserts that the three members belong to one class and that the class is complete, and readers act on both claims. If you have four findings and list three in parallel because three sounded better, you have told the reader something false with the shape of the sentence rather than its content, and no amount of careful wording elsewhere undoes it.

You cannot catch that without the concept. It is invisible at the level of "does this sentence read well", because it reads beautifully, which is precisely the problem. That is the argument for the whole apparatus: the figures are how prose makes claims that its propositions do not contain, and a writer who cannot see them is making those claims by accident.

The catalogue is mostly clutter

An honest closing limit. The Renaissance handbooks name upwards of two hundred figures and the classical sources are not much leaner. Almost none of that is technique. Knowing that repeating a word at the end of successive clauses is called epistrophe rather than anaphora adds nothing you can act on, and the proliferation of names is why the figures acquired their reputation as pedantry.

The working set is the dozen in step 3, and even that is generous. If you retain four, take isocolon and antithesis, because comparison is most of professional writing; asyndeton, because the exhaustiveness point is worth having; and litotes, because you are already using it constantly and should stop doing so unconsciously. The rest is available when you need a name and not otherwise.

Common failure Producing a striking figure and then keeping it because it is striking. An antithesis that opposes two things which are not actually opposed is worse than plain prose, because the shape has made a claim the content cannot support and the reader will believe the shape. Test every parallel against its content: are these really comparable, really opposed, really three of a kind? If not, the sentence is elegant and false, which module 9's lecture 7 treats as the characteristic vice of a writer who has become good at this.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Run the litotes audit from step 4 over a document of yours. For each instance write the positive form beside it, then decide: positive form, weaker positive claim with grounds, or keep the litotes deliberately. Report the count and how many you kept.
  2. Then compose. Write your principal finding as an antithesis or isocolon in under fifteen words, and check that the parallel's implied claim is true.
  3. Define scheme and trope, and classify: antithesis, litotes, asyndeton, metonymy, tricolon.
  4. What does "not immaterial" say, and what does it do that "material" does not? When is keeping it the right choice?
  5. Why does dropping the final conjunction make a list more honest? What claim does adding "and" make?
  6. What implied claims does a tricolon make, and what goes wrong when you have four findings?
  7. Give the dead metaphor rule and explain what is wrong with "once the capital floor was breached, losses cascaded through into the buffer".
  8. Argue that the figures belong to the memory canon rather than the style canon. What professional technique follows from it?
  9. State the standard objection to teaching the figures, concede what is right in it, and say what value survives.
  10. Which four figures would you keep, and why those four?
