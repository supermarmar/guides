Lectures / Writing / Rhetoric

The rhetorical situation

Bitzer's three constituents, and one of them contains a test most professional documents fail. An audience, in his sense, is not everyone who reads: it is those who can be influenced and who can act. A great deal of institutional writing is addressed to people who cannot act on it, which is why nothing happens.

Lecture 1 of 9 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: Bitzer (1968); Vatz (1973)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Bitzer describes a rhetorical situation as having three constituents. An exigence is an imperfection marked by urgency, something waiting to be done, and it is rhetorical only if discourse can modify it. An audience consists of those capable of being influenced by discourse and of mediating the change it calls for. Constraints are the persons, facts, beliefs, traditions and interests that bear on the decision, including the writer's own standing.

The audience definition is the operative content of the lecture. It excludes readers who cannot act, which means the test for a professional document is not who will receive it but who can do the thing it asks for. A validation report circulated to a committee with no authority over the model is addressed to a non-audience in Bitzer's sense, however carefully it is written, and the usual outcome, that it is noted and nothing changes, is the correct prediction rather than a failure of the prose.

Vatz objects to the whole framework, and the objection matters. Bitzer treats the situation as something that exists and calls forth a response. Vatz argues the rhetor creates it: events have no intrinsic significance, so choosing what to write about and how to frame it is what makes something an exigence. The practical consequence is that you cannot claim your document was compelled by circumstances, because you chose the circumstances you attended to.

Feynman test Before writing, ask who could actually do the thing you want done, and whether they will read this. If the person who could act will not read it and the people who will read it cannot act, you are not writing a document, you are creating a record. Both are legitimate. They are not the same activity and they should not be confused, because they are written differently.
What is beyond the source Bitzer and Vatz are arguing about the philosophy of rhetoric, not about professional writing. What this lecture does is take Bitzer's audience criterion literally and apply it as a test to institutional documents, which yields the uncomfortable finding in step 3. And it connects Vatz's point to module 4's manufactured complication: a document that invents an exigence is Vatz's thesis used as a technique, and naming it as such makes it easier to spot.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this is the module's reframing and it changes what counts as success. The preceding six modules treated writing as transmission: you have something to say and the reader must receive it intact. On that view a document that was read and understood has succeeded. On the rhetorical view it has not, because nothing happened, and the question of whether anything could have happened is one you should have asked before writing.

The second reason is the audience test, which has an immediate practical use. Take any document you are about to write and ask who can act. If the answer is a person who will not read it, you have a distribution problem rather than a writing problem, and no amount of module 4's architecture fixes it. The remedy is to address the person who can act, or to accept that you are creating a record and write it accordingly.

The third reason is the constraints constituent, which professionals systematically underweight. Bitzer includes interests among the constraints, and in an institutional setting the most important constraint is usually that somebody's position is threatened by what you have found. A document written as though the only constraint were the reader's understanding will be defeated by a constraint the writer never modelled.

And a reason about the Vatz objection, which is more than a philosophical quibble. If exigences are constructed, then a writer who opens with "increasing regulatory focus" has manufactured one, and module 4's lecture 6 called that a fabricated complication and warned that experienced readers stop at the phrase. Vatz explains why the move is available at all, which is that no event carries its own significance.

Step 3

The mental model

The three constituents, then the audience test applied to four real document situations.

Row 3 deserves separating out because it describes most professional writing accurately. Bitzer's audience includes mediators of change, not only deciders, and a committee that cannot rebuild a model can escalate a finding to someone who can fund the rebuild. Writing for a mediator means giving them what they need to carry the exigence onward: a claim they can repeat, a magnitude they can quote, and a specific ask. That is a different document from one written for the person who will act, and most papers are written as though the reader were the actor.

Exigence
An imperfection marked by urgency: something waiting to be done. Rhetorical only if discourse can modify it, which excludes a great deal that is merely regrettable.
Audience
Those capable of being influenced by discourse and of mediating the change it calls for. Not the distribution list. The test most professional documents fail.
Constraints
Everything bearing on the decision: facts, beliefs, traditions, interests, and the writer's own standing. In institutions the binding constraint is usually whose position the finding threatens.
Mediator
A reader who cannot act but can carry the exigence to someone who can. Needs a repeatable claim, a quotable magnitude and a specific ask, which is a different document from one written for a decider.
Step 4

The detail

Running the situation analysis

FOUR QUESTIONS BEFORE WRITING 1. WHAT IS THE EXIGENCE, and can discourse modify it? Not "what am I reporting on" but what is waiting to be done. Then check: could any document change it? Example: the calibration understates capital by 40bp. Can discourse modify it? Yes: someone must decide to rebuild, and a document can produce that decision. Counter-example: the 2008 downturn was severe. True, regrettable, and no document modifies it. Not an exigence, and a paper organised around it has no rhetorical situation. 2. WHO CAN ACT, and will they read this? Name the person. Not the committee: the person. Can act: the model owner (rebuild), the CRO (mandate it), the board (fund it). Will read: the model risk committee. If those sets do not intersect, decide now whether you are writing for a decider, for a mediator, or for the record. All three are legitimate. They are different documents. 3. WHAT ARE THE CONSTRAINTS, including the interests? Facts: the arrears series, the capital calculation. Beliefs: the committee believes the benchmark test is an adequate control. Your finding contradicts that. Interests: the model owner approved the calibration. Your finding is adverse to them. Your standing: second line, formally independent, informally junior to the model owner. The belief and the interest are the constraints that will actually defeat the document if unaddressed, and lecture 9 handles how. 4. WHAT DID I CHOOSE TO TREAT AS THE EXIGENCE? Vatz's question. There were several candidates: - the calibration understates capital - the benchmark control cannot detect this class of defect - nobody reviewed the window for six years All three are true. Choosing the first makes it a model problem; the second a control problem; the third a governance problem. Different documents, different audiences, different outcomes. You will choose one. Choose it deliberately, and be able to say why.

Question 4 is the one that repays the effort, and it is Vatz's contribution converted into a procedure. The same set of facts supports three different exigences with three different audiences, and the choice determines whether the document goes to a modeller, a control function or a governance committee. Writers make that choice by default, usually selecting whichever framing sits closest to their own function, and the framing then determines the outcome more than the evidence does.

When there is no audience

SituationDiagnosisWhat to do
Documents with no audience in Bitzer's sense
The person who can act will not read it A distribution problem masquerading as a writing problem. No amount of architecture fixes it. Address them directly, or find a mediator and write for the mediator. Do not write a better document for the wrong reader.
Nobody can act, because the decision is already taken The exigence has expired. This is a timing failure, which lecture 2 treats as kairos. Write it as a record, explicitly, and say what it is for. A record written as though it were persuasion reads as complaint.
Nothing needs doing No exigence. Legitimate and common: most periodic reporting is correctly like this. Write the short note module 4's lecture 6 recommended. Do not manufacture an exigence to justify the length the process expects.
The obligation is to produce the document, whatever it says The exigence is the obligation, not the subject. Also legitimate, and worth being clear-eyed about. Satisfy the obligation efficiently and put any genuine finding somewhere with an audience. A finding buried in a mandatory return has been disclosed and not communicated.

The last row is worth stating plainly because it is common and rarely acknowledged. Some documents exist because a process requires them, and their audience is an archive. There is nothing wrong with that, and the error is to put a genuine finding into such a document and consider it reported. Module 2's lecture 7 made the same point about the stress position: disclosure without emphasis is technically compliance, and the reader's memory is built from what was emphasised.

Constraints, and the one that defeats documents

Bitzer's constraints include beliefs and interests, and in institutional settings those two do more work than the facts. A finding that contradicts a belief the audience holds will be resisted on grounds that appear technical and are not. A finding adverse to someone's position will be resisted by that person, using whatever technical objection is available.

Modelling those constraints in advance is not cynicism; it is the difference between a document that anticipates the objection and one that is surprised by it. If the committee believes the benchmark test is an adequate control, then a paper reporting that the calibration failed must also address why the control did not catch it, or the first question will be whether the finding is real. Module 4's vertical logic would have required that section anyway, and the rhetorical analysis is what tells you it is load-bearing rather than optional.

The writer's own standing is also a constraint, and it cuts both ways. Formal independence gives a second-line author authority the model owner cannot easily dismiss. Informal juniority means the document will be read by people who know the author is junior. Lecture 2 treats how ethos is built in the text rather than imported, which is the available response.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The reframing, and what it costs the earlier modules

Six modules treated writing as transmission and this one treats it as action, and the two are not simply compatible. On the transmission view, a document that was read and understood succeeded. On the rhetorical view it may have failed completely, because understanding is not action and the question is whether anything changed.

That does not invalidate the earlier material; a document that is not understood certainly will not produce action, so clarity remains necessary. What it does is demote it. Clarity is a precondition rather than the objective, and a writer who has mastered modules 2 to 6 and never asked who can act has become very good at the part of the problem that was easier.

The uncomfortable version of this is worth stating. Some of the most carefully written documents in any institution are the least consequential, because their authors optimised the thing they could control. Conversely, a badly written email to the right person at the right moment can accomplish what a beautiful report cannot. That is not an argument against writing well; it is an argument for asking the rhetorical questions first, since they determine whether writing well will matter.

Vatz's objection, taken seriously

The dispute between Bitzer and Vatz is usually taught as a philosophical disagreement and it has a sharp practical edge. If situations are constructed rather than discovered, then every professional document involves a choice of what to make salient, and that choice is where most of the influence lies.

Step 4's fourth question makes this concrete: the same facts support a model problem, a control problem or a governance problem, and the writer picks. Notice what follows. The person who frames the exigence has more influence over the outcome than the person who supplies the analysis, and in most institutions those are the same person, which is a considerable and largely unexamined power.

The corresponding responsibility is to be able to say why you chose your framing. A finding framed as a model problem when the honest reading is a governance problem has protected a governance process at the model's expense, and the writer who did it may not have noticed. Module 9's final lecture treats this as an ethical matter. Here the point is descriptive: Vatz is right that you construct the situation, and the constructing is not a neutral act.

What this module will and will not give you

Nine lectures of rhetoric are about to arrive, and it is worth setting expectations. The classical apparatus is genuinely good: the canons map onto a real workflow, stasis theory locates disputes faster than anything else available, and the figures give names to effects you produce accidentally. Two modern lectures, on genre and audience, are the most directly useful in the module.

What the module will not do is make you persuasive in a way that is independent of having something true to say. Every technique here works better on a sound argument than on an unsound one, and lecture 9 is where that gets tested properly with Toulmin. The tradition has always attracted the suspicion that it teaches manipulation, and the honest answer is that it teaches the mechanics of influence, that the mechanics are available to anyone, and that knowing them is at least as useful for resisting persuasion as for exercising it.

Common failure Treating the situation analysis as something to do once, at the start of a document. Exigences expire, audiences change, and constraints move: a finding that had an audience in March may have none in June because the decision was taken. Lecture 2 treats timing as its own subject, and the practical instruction is to re-ask question 2 before circulating rather than only before drafting.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Run the four-question analysis in step 4 on a document you must write. Name the person who can act, the person who will read it, and the three candidate exigences your facts support. Say which you chose and why.
  2. Give Bitzer's three constituents. Which contains a test, and what does the test exclude?
  3. What makes an exigence rhetorical rather than merely regrettable? Give an example of each from your own work.
  4. What is a mediator, and how does writing for one differ from writing for a decider?
  5. Give the four cases where a document has no audience in Bitzer's sense, and the right response to each. Which two are legitimate?
  6. Why do beliefs and interests do more work than facts as constraints? Give an example where an unaddressed belief would defeat a document.
  7. State Vatz's objection and its practical consequence. Why does it mean the framer has more influence than the analyst?
  8. How does the rhetorical view demote the preceding six modules without invalidating them?
