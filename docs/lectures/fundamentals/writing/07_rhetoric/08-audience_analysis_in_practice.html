Audience analysis in practice

Four questions that build a reader model, the readers you cannot serve simultaneously, and the uncomfortable fact that every document casts its reader in a role. Getting the role wrong offends more than getting the content wrong.

Lecture 8 of 9 Reading time, 34 minutes Source: Ede and Lunsford (1984); Park (1982); Perelman
Step 1

The 30-second version

A reader model is four questions. What do they know? What do they want? What will they resist? What decision do they face? Answer all four before drafting and most structural choices make themselves. Skip them and you will write to a reader assembled by accident out of your own knowledge and interests.

Ede and Lunsford's distinction sharpens the whole business. The audience addressed is a real reader with knowable attributes; the audience invoked is a fiction your text constructs and invites the reader to occupy. Both are always operating, and the second is where professional writers do damage without noticing.

Because pitch casts a role. A paper that explains what a probability of default is invokes a reader who does not know, and a model owner reading it has been cast as a novice. They will resist the finding and be unable to say why, because the offence was in the register rather than the content. That is a bigger failure than a wrong number and it is invisible on rereading.

Multiple readers make this worse in a way the standard advice does not address. "Write for the primary audience" fails when four real readers face four different decisions and stand at different stases, and the honest answer is to separate by section, signpost it, and accept that everyone will skip most of your document.

Feynman test Ask four things about your reader: what they know, what they want, what they will fight, what they must decide. Then notice that how you pitch the document tells the reader who you think they are. Explain too much and you have called them ignorant. Explain too little and you have shut them out.
What is beyond the source Ede and Lunsford's distinction and the four-question model are standard, though the four questions are assembled here rather than quoted. Three things are this lecture's own. The first is the argument in step 3 that pitch is role-casting, so a misjudged explanatory level offends in a way the reader cannot articulate, which makes it more dangerous than an error they can point at. The second is step 4's treatment of multiple audiences via stasis, giving the rule that you may split a document by decision but not by stasis. The third is step 5's line between adapting the route and adapting the claim, offered as the boundary between rhetoric and capitulation.
Step 2

Why this matters

Every module of this course has assumed a reader and none has said how to find out about them. Module 3's given-new contract assumes you know what the reader already holds. Module 6's register question assumes you know the relationship. Module 4's pyramid assumes you know what decision is wanted. All of that machinery runs on inputs this lecture supplies, and running it on guesses is how a technically excellent document misses.

The second reason is that the failure mode here is silent. A reader who spots a wrong number tells you. A reader who has been cast as a novice, or excluded by unexplained notation, or handed a decision that is not theirs, does not say so, because the offence is not nameable. They report that the paper was not helpful, or they resist the finding on other grounds, and you conclude that the argument was weak.

The third reason is that professional documents genuinely have multiple readers, and the advice available is inadequate. A validation report is read by the model owner, a committee, a supervisor and a future validator, and those four want different things, know different amounts and face different decisions. Treating this as a problem of choosing a primary audience discards three readers who all have to be served.

And there is a use for audience analysis that saves more time than any of this, which is that it sometimes tells you not to write. Lecture 1's criterion was that an audience must be capable of both being influenced and mediating change. If nobody in your reader set can act, the document is a record rather than an intervention, and knowing that before you spend a week on it is the analysis paying for itself.

Step 3

The mental model

Four questions, and the role your pitch assigns whether you intended it or not.

Audience addressed
The reader as an existing person or group with researchable attributes: what they know, their role, their interests. The reader you can ask about.
Audience invoked
The reader your text constructs and invites the actual reader to become, through its pitch, register and assumptions. Operating in every document whether or not the writer intended it.
Reader model
A working hypothesis about a reader, built from the four questions. Always partly wrong, and decisively better than the default, which is a reader who knows what you know and wants what you want.

The fourth reader is why the redundancy in lecture 7's scope-section row is not redundancy. The three present readers all find it dead weight and the future reader cannot work without it, and there is no version of the document that serves all four with the same words. That is a genuine multiple-audience problem rather than a failure of concision.

What you may and may not split

THE SPLITTING RULE YOU MAY SPLIT BY DECISION. Different readers facing different decisions can be served by different sections, provided the sections are signposted so each reader can find theirs. Findings and method -> model owner Severity and options -> committee Mandate and tracking -> supervisor Scope, limitations -> the 2029 reader Everyone skips most of the document. That is the design working, not failing. YOU MAY NOT SPLIT BY STASIS WITHIN A SECTION. From lecture 4: a section arguing that the number is right and a section arguing that it matters are two documents' worth of work, and interleaving them loses both readers. Reader disputing the number reads your materiality paragraphs as evasion. Reader who has conceded the number reads your evidence paragraphs as padding. The rule: the LOWEST contested stasis governs the document's main line. Higher-stasis material goes later, briefly, flagged as conditional. "If the measurement is accepted, the consequence is as follows." That conditional is honest and it keeps the sceptic reading. WHAT TO DO WHEN THE STASES REALLY DIVERGE Two documents. A short evidence note to the reader at conjecture, and the substantive paper once that is settled. This is slower and it is the only thing that works, and it is often what a good validator is doing when they circulate a method note first.

The reader who is hostile, and the one who is bored

Hostility gets the attention and boredom is the larger problem. Deal with hostility first, because there is a real technique and also a real limit to it.

A finding lands better when the reader can accept it without accepting an accusation about themselves, so where the failure genuinely sits in a process, a data availability constraint or a decision made by someone else, locate it there and say so. That is not softening; it is accuracy, and a finding attributed to the wrong cause is a defective finding regardless of its diplomatic effect.

The limit matters more than the technique. Sometimes the honest account is that the model owner did the work badly, and there is no formulation that lets them accept it comfortably. Write the true thing, write it without adjectives, and accept the cost. A course that taught you to always find an impersonal cause would be teaching you to misattribute, and module 9's lecture 7 is about exactly that failure dressed as tact.

Now boredom, which is the condition of most of your readers most of the time. A committee member with forty pages before a two-hour meeting is not hostile and not engaged, and the only thing that reaches them is the visual and structural layer: the assertion heading, the one-sentence finding, the table that answers the question they would have asked. Everything module 4's lecture 8 said about delivery is aimed at this reader, and they are more numerous than the model owner and the supervisor combined.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The line between adapting the route and adapting the claim

Audience analysis has an obvious moral hazard, which is that a discipline devoted to what your reader will accept slides towards telling them what they want to hear. The boundary is precise and worth stating as a rule: you may adapt the route and you may not adapt the claim.

Adapting the route means everything in this lecture. Changing the order so the sceptic reaches your evidence before your consequence. Choosing climactic arrangement for a reader who will reject the answer cold. Locating the cause accurately rather than accusatorially. Cutting a definition because the reader wrote the thing being defined. All of that changes how the reader gets to the finding and leaves the finding untouched.

Adapting the claim means softening the finding because the reader will not like it: grading a material gap as minor, hedging a measurement you are confident in, dropping a recommendation because the owner will resist. Each of those is available, each feels like reading the room, and each is a different act from the first list. The test is mechanical: write down your finding before you analyse your audience, then compare it with what you wrote. If the finding moved, the analysis was not analysis.

Which is worth keeping because it also protects you from the opposite error. Writers who fear capitulation sometimes refuse to adapt anything and take their bluntness as integrity, and they lose findings that a different order would have carried. Route and claim are separable, and knowing they are separable is what lets you be flexible about one and immovable about the other.

The reader model is a fiction, and you need one anyway

You do not know your reader. You know a role, some history, and whatever you have inferred from three meetings, and the model you build from that is a hypothesis with unknown error. Douglas Park's 1982 survey of what "audience" even means in composition theory found the term doing at least four incompatible jobs, and Perelman's universal audience, the ideal of all reasonable people, exists partly because real audiences are so hard to know.

The practical conclusion is not scepticism, though. An explicit hypothesis about your reader is better than the implicit one, and the implicit one is always the same: a reader who knows what you know, wants what you want, and objects to nothing. That default is not a neutral fallback, it is a specific and reliably wrong model, and any deliberate alternative beats it.

Which gives the honest standard for this lecture's four questions. They will not tell you the truth about your reader. They will make your assumptions visible, which means a colleague can correct them in ten seconds, and that is the mechanism by which audience analysis actually improves documents. It is not insight, it is falsifiability.

When the analysis says do not write

The highest-value output of audience analysis is occasionally a decision not to produce the document, and this is worth stating because nothing else in this course gives you permission to stop.

Lecture 1's criterion was that an audience must be capable both of being influenced by discourse and of mediating change. Run the four questions and you sometimes find that nobody in the reader set faces a decision: the model is already scheduled for replacement, the committee has no mandate over this, the owner has already conceded and is waiting for capacity. In that situation the document is not an intervention. It is a record, which may be worth writing, and it should be written as a record: short, dated, filed, with no argument in it.

The failure this prevents is the well-argued paper addressed to nobody, which is more common in second-line functions than anyone admits, and which consumes weeks. The four questions catch it in twenty minutes, and the answer is not always to abandon the work. It is sometimes to find the reader who can act, which is a different document to a different person, and which lecture 1's third row called the mediator.

Common failure Building the reader model after drafting, as a review step. By then the document has already invoked a reader, and the invoked reader is whoever your unexamined register implied, usually a version of yourself. Reviewing against a model at that point produces local patches, a definition added here, a hedge removed there, on a document whose whole pitch is aimed at the wrong person. The four questions belong to invention, which lecture 3 put first for exactly this reason.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Build the step 4 table for a real document of yours: every reader, with know, want, resist and decision filled in. Report which reader you had not been writing for.
  2. Then compose. Write your finding down before doing any audience work. Draft, then compare. State whether the finding moved, and if it did, why.
  3. Give the four questions and say what drafting decision each one settles.
  4. Distinguish audience addressed from audience invoked. Which is usually deliberate?
  5. Why is a misjudged explanatory pitch more dangerous than a wrong number?
  6. What may you split a document by, and what may you not? Why does the second fail?
  7. Which reader needs the material every other reader finds redundant, and why is cutting it wrong?
  8. Give the honest limit on locating a finding's cause impersonally.
  9. State the line between adapting the route and adapting the claim, and give the mechanical test.
  10. What is the implicit reader model you use when you build no explicit one? Why does an explicit model beat it even when it is wrong?
  11. Under what condition should audience analysis stop you writing the document, and what should you write instead?
