Six kinds of redundancy

"Omit needless words" is the best line in the literature and it is not an instruction, because it does not say which words are needless. Williams's answer is a taxonomy: six recognisable kinds, each with a signature you can search for, which turns one vague pass into six targeted ones.

Lecture 1 of 7 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: Williams, Style, on concision; Strunk & White
Step 1

The 30-second version

Module 2 gave you the lard factor, which measures how much of a draft was never doing any work. This lecture supplies the taxonomy of what the lard is made of. Williams identifies six kinds: meaningless modifiers, doubled words, words whose meaning is already implied, redundant categories, phrases replaceable by a single word, and negatives that should be affirmatives. To that this lecture adds the kind Williams treats separately and lecture 2 handles fully: metadiscourse, or writing about your writing.

The value of a taxonomy over an instruction is that each kind has a searchable signature. Doubled words come in fixed pairs you can list. Redundant categories attach a class name to a word that already implies it, so "in colour", "in size", "in nature" and "period of time" are findable by search. Phrases replaceable by a word are a closed set. So a concision pass becomes six passes, each mechanical, rather than one act of judgement performed while tired.

The lecture also has to settle a tension it inherits. Module 3 made a paragraph 40 per cent longer to honour the given-new contract, and this module tells you to cut. Both are right, and the test that separates them is whether deleting a phrase leaves the reader to infer something. Scaffolding carries no new information and does carry attachment. Padding carries neither.

Feynman test "Omit needless words" is like being told to remove the parts of the engine that are not doing anything. Correct, and useless without a parts list. Six named kinds of thing that are never doing anything is a parts list, and once you have it the job stops being judgement and becomes inspection.
What is beyond the source Williams supplies the six kinds and the examples. What this lecture adds is the framing of them as six searches rather than six principles, which changes how they get used, and the padding-against-scaffolding test in step 4, which is needed because this module and module 3 pull in opposite directions and neither source addresses the conflict. Strunk and White supply the slogan and, as module 1 established, none of the analysis.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that concision advice fails in practice for a specific and fixable reason: it is stated as a disposition rather than a procedure. A writer told to be concise reads their draft looking for fat, finds some, and stops when the sentences start feeling short. A writer with six searches finds all six kinds regardless of how they feel about the result, which is the same argument module 2 made for mechanical diagnostics over careful reading.

The second reason is that these six are the cheapest edits in the entire course. Every other improvement in modules 2 to 4 requires a decision: who the character is, where the point goes, what the topic string should be. These require none. "In the event that" becomes "if" without anyone deciding anything, and a document can be improved measurably in twenty minutes by someone who does not understand its content.

The third reason is that the redundancies carry a signal beyond their cost in words. A document dense with meaningless modifiers and doubled phrases reads as unconfident, because each pair is a small hedge: "full and complete" is a writer who was not sure "complete" would be believed. Readers register that without being able to name it, which means the words are doing something after all, and what they are doing is undermining you.

And a reason about exam and word-limited writing. Twenty per cent of a typical professional draft is these six kinds, which under a word limit is a fifth of your available space returned for free. That is a larger gain than any other single technique available under time pressure, and unlike the rest of this course it requires no thought about what you are arguing.

Step 3

The mental model

Six kinds, each with its signature and its search. The panel beneath is the test that decides whether something is padding or scaffolding, which is what stops this module contradicting module 3.

Kind 4 deserves the note the figure gives it. Redundant categories are the highest-yield search of the six in professional prose, because the pattern is so productive: any noun can be given its class, and technical registers do it constantly. A period of twelve months, the amount of exposure, the level of arrears, the process of validation, an area of concern. Each names a category the following word already belongs to, and each is deletable without judgement.

Padding
Words carrying neither information nor attachment. Deleting them leaves the reader to infer nothing, because they were contributing nothing. All six kinds in this lecture.
Scaffolding
Words carrying no new information and real attachment: a backward-referring phrase, a connective naming a relation, a repeated topic. Deleting them makes the reader infer a link. Module 3's additions were all of this kind.
Redundant category
A word naming the class its neighbour already belongs to: period of time, large in size, level of arrears. The most productive redundancy pattern in technical registers and the easiest to search for.
Step 4

The detail

The pass, run once

SIX SEARCHES ON ONE PARAGRAPH BEFORE (76 words) "It should be noted that a certain degree of understatement has been identified in respect of the downturn LGD estimate over a period of twelve months. Due to the fact that the stress window does not include any period of severe downturn, the calibration is not dissimilar to a long-run average estimate. Prior to the next submission, a full and complete recalibration is required for the purpose of ensuring the adequate and appropriate treatment of downturn conditions." SEARCH 1 meaningless modifiers "a certain degree of" -> delete SEARCH 2 doubled words "full and complete" -> "complete", or delete "adequate and appropriate" -> "adequate" SEARCH 3 implied words "severe downturn" -> keep; "severe" is doing real work here SEARCH 4 redundant categories "over a period of twelve months" -> "over twelve months" "any period of severe downturn" -> "any severe downturn" SEARCH 5 phrases replaceable by a word "in respect of" -> "in" or delete "due to the fact that" -> "because" "prior to" -> "before" "for the purpose of ensuring" -> "to ensure", then consider deleting SEARCH 6 negatives "does not include any" -> "excludes" "is not dissimilar to" -> "resembles" PLUS metadiscourse (lecture 2) "It should be noted that" -> delete "has been identified" -> who identified it? (module 2, lecture 2) AFTER (38 words) "The downturn LGD estimate has understated losses for twelve months. Because the stress window excludes any severe downturn, the calibration resembles a long-run average. The model owner must recalibrate before the next submission to reflect downturn conditions." LARD FACTOR (76 - 38) / 76 = 50 per cent. Lanham's expectation exactly. WHAT ELSE CHANGED Search 6 turned two negatives into two verbs that carry actions, which is module 2's lecture 3 arriving as a side effect. And the metadiscourse deletion exposed a missing actor, which the rewrite had to supply. Concision passes routinely surface problems from earlier modules, because padding is where those problems hide.

Where each kind is not redundant

Every one of the six has legitimate instances, and a writer applying the searches without the exceptions will delete meaning. The exceptions are narrow and worth knowing precisely.

KindKeep it whenExample
The exceptions, which are real but narrow
Meaningless modifiers The word is a genuine quantifier rather than a filler. "Certain exposures were excluded" means a specific identified subset, not vagueness. Contrast "a certain degree of", which means nothing.
Doubled words The two halves genuinely differ, which in regulated writing they sometimes do. "Adequate and appropriate" is usually one idea. "Review and challenge" is two, in a second-line context where challenge is a defined additional obligation.
Implied words The modifier restricts rather than repeats. "Severe downturn" is not redundant if downturns of varying severity are in scope. "Past history" is redundant because history has no other tense.
Redundant categories The category word is part of a defined term. "Period of default" may be defined; "period of twelve months" is not. Check the glossary before deleting.
Replaceable phrases The long form is a term of art or carries a legal distinction. "In respect of" is usually deletable; in some contractual drafting it has been construed differently from "for". Rare, and check rather than assume.
Negatives The negation is the point, or the affirmative overstates. "The test did not detect the defect" is precise. "The test failed to detect" adds blame. "The test missed it" adds more. Sometimes the flat negative is the honest form.

The last row is the one to take most seriously, because it is where concision and accuracy genuinely conflict. Turning a negative into an affirmative usually strengthens a sentence, and strengthening is not always what you want. "Arrears did not exceed 1.4 per cent" and "arrears stayed below 1.4 per cent" differ slightly in what they assert about the shape of the data, and in a document someone may rely on, the flatter form is occasionally the correct one. Concision is a default, not an obligation.

Why the negatives search is worth its place

Kind 6 looks like the weakest of the six and it earns its place for a reason that has nothing to do with word count. Negatives are harder to process than affirmatives, and stacked negatives compound: "it is not unreasonable to conclude that the window does not fail to include a severe downturn" is a sentence whose meaning most readers will get wrong, and each individual negation is grammatical.

The practical rule is to count negations per clause. One is normal. Two requires the reader to compose them, which they will do slowly and sometimes incorrectly. Three is a sentence nobody should send. And the count includes negative words that do not look like negations: fail, lack, absent, except, unless, other than, preclude. A clause containing "unless" and "fails" already has two.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

What the redundancies are for

It is worth asking why professional writers produce these words at all, since nobody sets out to write "a certain degree of". The answer is not carelessness and understanding it makes the pass easier.

Most of the six are hedges in disguise. "Full and complete" doubles because the writer was not confident that "complete" would be believed. "A certain degree of understatement" softens a finding. "It should be noted that" defers responsibility for the noting. Each is a small insurance payment against being wrong or being challenged, and once you see the pattern you can predict where the padding will be: densest around the findings the writer was least comfortable with.

That gives the concision pass a diagnostic use beyond its own purpose. Run the six searches and note where the hits cluster. The paragraph with the most padding is usually the paragraph making the claim the author was least sure of, and that is worth knowing whether you are revising your own work or reviewing someone else's. In a document you are assessing, the padding is a map of the author's discomfort.

The tension with module 3, resolved properly

Module 3 added words and this module removes them, and the resolution given in step 3 is worth restating carefully because it is easy to apply lazily.

The test is not whether a phrase carries information. Scaffolding carries none: "that fall" tells the reader nothing they did not have. The test is whether deleting it obliges the reader to construct something. Delete "that fall" and they must work out which fall you mean, which is a bridging inference and costs measurable time. Delete "a certain degree of" and they construct nothing, because it was asserting nothing.

The reason the lazy application matters is that it runs in one direction. A writer applying concision enthusiastically will delete backward-referring phrases as repetition, and connectives as unnecessary, and produce a document that is shorter and less coherent. That is the failure module 3's lecture 1 warned about from the other side: the cure for one property applied to the other. When in doubt, keep the join and delete the modifier, because the modifier is never load-bearing and the join sometimes is.

The limit of the whole approach

These six kinds are the easy half of concision and it is worth being clear that they are the easy half. They operate on words and phrases. The expensive half operates on content: a section that should not exist, a paragraph making a point already made, an appendix nobody will read. Deleting a redundant category saves three words; deleting a section saves three pages, and no search will find it.

Module 4's diagnostics are what find the second kind: the question test asks whether a section answers anything, and the single-function rule asks whether a paragraph is doing two jobs. So the honest sequence is that structural cutting comes first and lexical cutting last, which is the same ordering module 3's lecture 7 argued for and for the same reason: you should not polish words in a section you are about to remove.

Common failure Treating the lard factor as a target, which module 2's lecture 8 already warned about and which this lecture makes easier to do wrong, because now you have six mechanical searches and a number to move. The searches are safe; the number is not. A pass that hits 50 per cent by deleting the six kinds is a good pass. A pass that hits 50 per cent by also deleting qualifications, conditions and scope statements has removed the parts that made the claims accurate.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Run all six searches, in order and with written output at each, on a 70 to 100 word paragraph of your own. Report the lard factor, and note any problem from modules 2 to 4 that the pass exposed.
  2. Name the six kinds. Which is the highest-yield search in technical prose, and why is that pattern so productive?
  3. State the padding-against-scaffolding test. Why is "does it carry information" the wrong test?
  4. Give a legitimate instance of each of kinds 1, 2 and 3, and say what makes it legitimate.
  5. Why does the negatives search earn its place on grounds other than word count? Give the counting rule and three negative words that do not look like negations.
  6. Where in a document does padding cluster, and what does that let you diagnose about its author?
  7. A writer applying concision enthusiastically fails in one particular direction. What is it, and what is the rule of thumb that prevents it?
  8. Distinguish the easy half of concision from the expensive half. Which comes first, and why?
