Sentence variety and rhythm

Uniform sentence length reads as machine output, and it does so for a reason worth stating precisely: length carries information about importance, so uniformity asserts that everything matters equally. The diagnosis is a count, because you cannot hear your own rhythm.

Lecture 4 of 7 Reading time, 25 minutes Source: Provost, 100 Ways to Improve Your Writing; Lanham, Revising Prose
Step 1

The 30-second version

Lecture 3 taught you to build long sentences. This one is the counterweight. A paragraph of uniformly long sentences is exhausting, a paragraph of uniformly short ones drones, and both read as though nobody chose anything. The remedy is not random variation but purposeful variation: long sentences for claims that arrive with qualifications attached, short ones for claims that should land.

The reason uniformity fails is not aesthetic. Sentence length is a signal about weight, and a short sentence among long ones is emphatic without a single intensifier, which module 2's lecture 7 established as the only reliable kind of emphasis. So a paragraph of equal-length sentences has spent that signal on nothing and asserted that every sentence carries the same weight, which is almost never true.

The diagnostic is Lanham's seventh step, which module 2's lecture 8 restored and this lecture uses: write out the sentence lengths of a paragraph as a series of numbers and look at them. You cannot hear your own rhythm reliably, for the same reason you cannot see your own missing antecedents, and a column of numbers bypasses the faculty that is compromised.

Feynman test Read four sentences of exactly twenty words each out loud and you will hear a metronome. Nothing is wrong with any sentence. What is wrong is that the reader now has a rhythm in their head, and a rhythm is a prediction, and once they can predict the shape of your next sentence they stop listening to its content.
What is beyond the source Provost supplies the demonstration and Lanham the counting method. The argument in step 5, that uniform length is the same failure as uniform paragraph spacing and as bullet points, and that all three flatten a judgement the writer had already made, is this lecture's, and it unifies three warnings the course has issued separately. The honest limit in step 5 about overcorrection is also this lecture's, and it matters because rhythm advice is the most easily overdone in the whole course.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that professional documents are uniform by construction rather than by choice. They are written to templates, assembled by accretion, and edited by people applying a house convention about sentence length. Nobody decides the rhythm, so the rhythm is whatever the process produced, and in most cases the process produces a run of twenty-two to twenty-eight word sentences from beginning to end.

The second reason is the emphasis argument, which has a practical payoff. If you want one claim in a paragraph to be remembered, the cheapest available device is to make its sentence markedly shorter than its neighbours. That costs nothing, requires no intensifier, and works on readers who are skimming. A writer who has not varied length has no such device available, and will reach for importantly or bold text instead, which lecture 2 and module 2's lecture 7 both established do not work.

The third reason is that this is the check that finds problems the other checks miss. A paragraph can pass every diagnostic in modules 2 to 4 and still read badly, and when it does, uniformity is usually why. It is the last item on the list because it is the least consequential, and it is on the list because nothing else catches it.

And a reason about how a document is received before it is read. Module 4's lecture 8 noted that a reader forms an impression of a page from its shape. Sentence length contributes to that: a page of undifferentiated blocks signals undifferentiated content. That impression is formed pre-verbally and it is the first thing your document communicates.

Step 3

The mental model

Two length series from the same content, and Provost's demonstration of what the second one is doing.

The blue bar in the second panel is the argument. A six-word sentence following a twenty-seven-word one is emphatic for free, and the reader will remember it. That device is available only in a paragraph with varied lengths, which means the decision to vary is upstream of the decision about what to emphasise: a uniform paragraph has already given up the ability to make any of its sentences land.

Length series
The sentence lengths of a passage written out as numbers, in order. Lanham's seventh step. The diagnostic exists because you cannot hear your own rhythm, and a column of numbers bypasses the compromised faculty.
Emphasis by contrast
A sentence markedly shorter than its neighbours reads as emphatic with no lexical marking at all. The paragraph-scale version of module 2's stress position, and the only emphasis device that works on skimming readers.
Opening variety
Variation in how sentences begin, not just in how long they are. A paragraph where every sentence opens with its grammatical subject reads flat even with varied lengths, and marked themes are the tool for fixing it.
Step 4

The detail

Running the count

THE LENGTH SERIES, AND WHAT TO DO WITH IT 1. Take a paragraph. Count the words in each sentence. 2. Write the numbers in order. 3. Compute the range, not the mean. The mean tells you nothing; the range is the whole diagnostic. READING THE RESULT Range under 10 words -> uniform. No sentence can be made emphatic. Fix. Range 10 to 25 -> normal working variation. Range over 30 -> check the longest sentence is cumulative rather than centre-embedded (lecture 3), and that the shortest is carrying something worth emphasising. A SECOND SERIES WORTH TAKING Write down the first three words of each sentence. "The calibration understates..." "The stress window excludes..." "The benchmark test uses..." "The model owner must..." Four sentences, varied lengths, and every one opens "The [noun] [verb]". The paragraph will still read flat, because the opening shape is uniform even though the length is not. Repair: one marked theme (module 3, lecture 4). "Because the benchmark uses the same window, no control detected the defect." One is enough. Two reads as deliberate; four reads as a writer avoiding the subject position. WHAT NOT TO DO Do not compute a target distribution and write to it. The series is a detector, not a specification, and lecture 1's warning about the lard factor applies exactly: a number that becomes a target stops measuring.

What each length is for

LengthWhat it does wellFailure when overused
Lengths are tools with jobs, not points on a scale of virtue
Very short, under 8 words Lands a claim. Marks a turn. Closes a paragraph hard. Emphatic purely by contrast. A run of them reads as hectoring, and the emphasis decays to nothing because nothing contrasts.
Medium, 12 to 22 words Carries a claim with one qualification. The working default and the bulk of any paragraph. Uniform medium is the professional default and the specific failure this lecture is about.
Long, 30 words and up Holds a claim and its qualifications in one frame, so the relations are carried structurally. Lecture 3's cumulative sentence. Exhausting in sequence, and each one demands the writer hold the whole structure, so the failure rate is high.
One word Available once in a document, if at all. Provost's "Music." is the canonical instance. Twice and it is a mannerism. In a technical document, probably never.

The last row deserves its bluntness. A one-word sentence in a validation report reads as a writer performing, and the register of the document cannot absorb it. That is a genre constraint rather than a rule about rhythm, and it is worth naming because the demonstration in the figure uses the device and a reader might reasonably take it as licence.

Paragraph shape

Beyond individual lengths there is a common shape worth knowing, not as a template but as the arrangement that most often falls out of doing the other things in this course correctly. A paragraph tends to open medium, because module 3's lecture 5 puts the point at the end of a one or two sentence issue; then run longer through the discussion, because the evidence arrives with qualifications; then close short, because the consequence is a claim that should land.

That shape is a consequence rather than a rule, and noticing it has a diagnostic use. A paragraph whose longest sentence is its last one has usually trailed off into qualification, which is module 2's lecture 7 at paragraph scale. A paragraph whose shortest sentence is its first has usually opened on a bald assertion without establishing the topic, which module 3's lecture 5 warned against. So the length series carries information about the paragraph's structure and not only about its sound.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The same failure at three scales

This course has now issued the same warning three times in different vocabularies, and collecting them is worth doing because the underlying claim is one thing.

Module 4's lecture 8 said that uniform paragraph spacing asserts that every boundary is equally strong, and that bullet points assert that every item is equally weighted, so a findings list of one major and three minor items has told the reader they are comparable. This lecture says that uniform sentence length asserts that every sentence carries the same weight. All three are the same failure: a formal property of the document flattening a judgement the writer had already made.

That unification has a practical consequence. If you have decided that one finding matters more than the others, you have three independent devices for carrying that decision, and they compose: the finding gets a short sentence, in its own paragraph, out of the bulleted list. None of the three costs a word and all three work on readers who are skimming. Conversely, a document that flattens on all three dimensions has thrown away its author's judgement three times over, and the reader is left to reconstruct it.

Why you cannot hear it

The instruction to read your work aloud is good and it does not solve this problem, which is worth being clear about because lecture 5 is about reading aloud and might seem to.

Reading aloud detects local roughness: a stumble, an unpronounceable cluster, a sentence with nowhere for your voice to land. It does not reliably detect uniformity, because uniformity is a property of a distribution and your ear processes one sentence at a time. Worse, you know your own prose, so you read it with the emphasis you intended rather than the emphasis it carries, which is the same contamination module 3 identified for antecedents and relations.

Hence the count. It is the third mechanical instrument in this course, alongside the subject-string extraction and the covered-page audit, and all three exist for the same reason: they examine a property of a sequence, which no amount of careful reading can perceive from inside.

The overcorrection, which is worse than the disease

Rhythm advice is the most easily overdone material in this course and the warning needs to be stronger than usual. A writer who takes this lecture seriously and starts varying length deliberately produces prose with a different and more irritating problem: visible technique. Short sentences dropped in for effect. Sentences that exist to break up their neighbours. A rhythm the reader can hear the writer arranging.

The reason this is worse than uniformity is that uniformity is merely flat, and visible technique is distracting, which module 4's lecture 7 established costs the reader attention they would otherwise spend on content. McPhee's principle about structure applies here exactly: a rhythm the reader admires is a rhythm that is intruding.

The workable position is that variation should be a by-product of doing the other things correctly rather than a goal in itself. If you build long sentences where the material has qualifications attached, and short ones where a claim should land, and you have not written to a template, the variation appears on its own. The count is there to catch the case where it has not, which in professional documents is common enough to be worth checking and is not the normal case for a writer applying the rest of this course.

Common failure Fixing a uniform length series by splitting long sentences at arbitrary points. That produces variation and destroys the relations, which is exactly the failure lecture 3 and module 4's lecture 8 both described: a split sentence incurs a connective obligation that an arbitrary split does not pay. If your series is uniform, the repair is to find the claim that should land and shorten that sentence, not to redistribute words.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take three paragraphs of your own writing and produce both series for each: sentence lengths, and the first three words of each sentence. Report the ranges, and identify one paragraph where the length varies and the openings do not.
  2. Why does uniform sentence length fail? Give the reason in terms of what length signals rather than in terms of how it sounds.
  3. Why is the range the diagnostic rather than the mean?
  4. What is the emphasis device that only a varied paragraph has available? Why does it work on skimming readers when italics do not?
  5. Name the same failure at three scales, and give the three composing devices that follow for a finding you have decided matters most.
  6. Why does reading aloud not detect uniformity? Name the other two mechanical instruments in this course and what they have in common with the count.
  7. State the overcorrection and why it is worse than the disease. Which of McPhee's principles applies?
  8. Your length series is uniform. Why is splitting long sentences the wrong repair, and what is the right one?
