Metadiscourse and hedging

Writing about your writing, and writing about how sure you are. Some of both is indispensable and most of it is throat-clearing. The test that separates them is not length, and the honest alternative to a stack of hedges is to state your confidence outright rather than modulating every verb.

Lecture 2 of 7 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: Hyland, Metadiscourse (2005); Williams, Style
Step 1

The 30-second version

Metadiscourse is language about the discourse rather than about the subject. Hyland's taxonomy splits it into interactive resources, which help the reader navigate, and interactional resources, which position the writer relative to the claim. Both categories contain things you need and things that are pure throat-clearing, so the useful question is never how much metadiscourse a document has but which kind.

The navigational half earns its place when it does something the reader cannot do for themselves: attributing a view to whoever held it, defining a term on first use, or telling a reader of a long document where an answer sits. The throat-clearing half announces that you are about to say something, which the sentence itself already does. "It should be noted that the estimate understates losses" says nothing that "the estimate understates losses" does not.

Hedging is the interactional half that matters most in technical writing, and its failure is not presence but stacking. One hedge on a claim expresses real uncertainty. Five hedges express a writer who will not commit, and readers discount them, so the stack conveys less confidence than a single honest may would have done. The alternative worth adopting is to state your uncertainty explicitly as a claim in its own right, which is both more informative and harder to hide behind.

Feynman test There is a difference between telling someone where the exit is and clearing your throat before speaking. Both are noises that are not the message. The first is useful because they cannot see the exit; the second is useful to nobody, and if you do it before every sentence, they stop listening for the message at all.
What is beyond the source Hyland describes and quantifies metadiscourse across disciplines without prescribing. Williams tells you to cut it. The reframing in step 5, replacing modulated verbs with an explicit statement of what you are and are not confident about, is this lecture's, and it is the part that turns a style instruction into something a validation report can actually use. The claim that hedging transfers risk from writer to reader is also this lecture's and it is the honest description of what the stack is for.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is volume. Metadiscourse is the single largest category of deletable material in professional prose, larger than any of the six redundancies in lecture 1, because it is generated by templates and by the habit of announcing structure. A document with a signposting sentence at the head of every section and an "it should be noted" in every third paragraph is carrying several hundred words that say nothing about its subject.

The second reason is that hedging is a control matter rather than a stylistic one. A finding hedged into invisibility has not been reported, whatever the document technically contains. If your paper says the estimate "may possibly be somewhat understated" and the true position is that it is understated by forty basis points, the hedge has converted a finding into a caveat, and a reader deciding whether to act will not act. That is a reporting failure with a compliance dimension, and it is produced entirely by word choice.

The third reason runs the other way and is equally real. An unhedged finding overstates, and overstating is also a reporting failure. "The estimate understates losses by forty basis points" asserts a precision that a point estimate from a rebuilt model may not support. So the professional task is calibration rather than elimination, which is why this lecture spends more effort on how to hedge than on how to stop.

And a reason about how you are read. Readers discount hedges, which is well established in the corpus literature and observable in any committee. A single hedge is read as precision. A stack is read as evasion, and the writer who stacked them to seem careful has produced the opposite impression. The word count is the smaller cost.

Step 3

The mental model

The top panel splits metadiscourse by whether it does work the reader cannot do. The lower panel is the hedge ladder, from a claim stated flat to a claim hedged into nothing, with the honest alternative at the end.

Notice what the four-hedge version has lost. It contains no number, no direction that survives the modality, and no statement of what the uncertainty is about. The alternative contains a firm claim, an explicit confidence statement, a point estimate and a range, and it is one word longer. Hedging is not a way of being careful about a claim; it is a way of not making one.

Metadiscourse
Language about the discourse rather than the subject. Hyland divides it into interactive resources, which help the reader navigate, and interactional resources, which position the writer relative to the claim.
Hedge
A device withholding full commitment: may, might, appears, suggests, relatively, around, broadly. One per claim expresses uncertainty. More than one expresses reluctance.
Booster
The opposite device, asserting more confidence than the bare claim: clearly, obviously, of course, undoubtedly. Redundant where the reader agrees and insulting where they do not.
Explicit uncertainty statement
Stating what you are and are not confident about as a claim in its own right, instead of modulating the verbs. Separates direction from magnitude, which no arrangement of hedges can do.
Step 4

The detail

The deletion test, and the one thing it must not delete

THE METADISCOURSE PASS Delete the phrase. Then ask two questions. (a) Can the reader still navigate the document? (b) Can the reader still tell who said it, what a term means, and what is out of scope? If both yes -> it was throat-clearing. Leave it deleted. If either no -> it was doing work. Restore it. WORKED "It should be noted that the validation function has concluded that the downturn LGD estimate, which for these purposes means the estimate calibrated to a severe period rather than the long-run average, may be understated. It is important to recognise that this analysis excludes the buy-to-let portfolio. In this section we set out the evidence." Candidate 1 "It should be noted that" Delete -> (a) yes (b) yes. THROAT-CLEARING. Candidate 2 "the validation function has concluded that" Delete -> (b) NO. The reader can no longer tell whose conclusion it is, and module 2's lecture 2 established that this matters. ATTRIBUTION. Restore. Candidate 3 "which for these purposes means the estimate calibrated to a severe period rather than the long-run average" Delete -> (b) NO. A term of art loses its definition. DEFINITION. Restore, and consider moving it to its own sentence, since module 1's lecture 1 forbade embedding a definition mid-sentence. Candidate 4 "It is important to recognise that" Delete -> both yes. THROAT-CLEARING. Candidate 5 "this analysis excludes the buy-to-let portfolio" Delete -> (b) NO. Scope. This is not metadiscourse at all; it is a substantive limitation. Keep. Candidate 6 "In this section we set out the evidence." Delete -> (a) yes, if the heading is an assertion. THROAT-CLEARING. RESULT (from 66 words to 44) "The validation function has concluded that the downturn LGD estimate may be understated. Downturn LGD here means the estimate calibrated to a severe period rather than the long-run average. The analysis excludes the buy-to-let portfolio." Two deletions were throat-clearing, one was a whole sentence of it, and three candidates that looked like metadiscourse were content. That ratio is typical.

Calibrating a hedge

One hedge per claim is the rule and it does not say which one. The choice matters, because hedges hedge different things and using the wrong one misdescribes your uncertainty.

HedgeWhat it actually qualifiesUse when
Hedges are not interchangeable
may, might, could Whether the thing is true at all. You are uncertain about the direction, not just the size. Strong hedge, and often stronger than intended.
appears, seems, suggests The strength of your evidence, not the fact. You believe it and your evidence is indirect. The right hedge for a finding based on one test.
around, approximately, of the order of The magnitude only. The direction is not hedged. You are sure it is understated and unsure by how much. The most useful hedge in quantitative work and the least used.
on the evidence available The completeness of the input, not the inference. Your analysis is sound and your data is partial. Precise, and it invites the reader to ask what is missing, which is usually the right conversation.
Hedge nothing
arguably, relatively, somewhat, broadly Nothing identifiable. They lower the temperature without qualifying anything specific. Never in a technical document. If the claim needs qualifying, one of the four above says what needs qualifying.

The third row is worth acting on immediately if you write anything quantitative. Around forty basis points hedges only the magnitude and leaves the direction firm, which is usually the exact shape of a modeller's uncertainty. Writers reach for may instead, which hedges whether the understatement exists at all, and so report far less confidence than they actually have.

Boosters, and why they are worse

Boosters get less attention than hedges and are the easier problem, because the argument against them is nearly complete. Clearly and obviously are used before claims the writer expects resistance to, which is precisely the case in which asserting obviousness is a substitute for evidence. If the claim is obvious, the word adds nothing; if it is not, the word has told your reader that disagreeing with you makes them slow.

The one legitimate use is genuinely deductive: "the window excludes 2008, so it clearly cannot support a downturn estimate" uses clearly to mark an inference the reader can verify in their head, which is closer to a connective than to a booster. Everywhere else, delete it, and where you find several, treat them as lecture 1's diagnostic: boosters cluster around claims the writer could not support.

The professional version of this failure is of course, which does something slightly different and worse. It asserts shared knowledge, so it flatters readers who have it and silently excludes those who do not, and it makes asking a question about the point socially expensive. In a document with a mixed readership, it is the phrase most likely to prevent a legitimate challenge.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Hedging as risk transfer

The honest account of why professionals stack hedges is not that they are being careful. It is that a hedge moves risk from the writer to the reader, and in most institutions that trade is rewarded.

Consider what happens if a hedged finding turns out wrong. The writer said the estimate may be understated, so they were not wrong; the reader who acted on it made a judgement. Now consider an unhedged finding that turns out wrong: the writer said it was understated and it was not, and that is attributable. Hedging is therefore rational for the writer and costly for the reader, who now has to decide how much of the hedge to discount without any information about how much was meant.

Naming this changes what the advice is for. Telling a writer to hedge less is telling them to accept more personal risk, which is not a style instruction and will not be followed on those grounds. The explicit uncertainty statement is a better answer precisely because it does not require that trade: "we are confident of the direction and not of the magnitude" is fully protective, since it states exactly what the writer is standing behind, and it is far more useful to the reader than a modal verb. You give up nothing and the reader gains a range.

That reframing is worth carrying into review comments as well. "Too hedged" invites a defence about prudence. "Which part are you unsure about, the direction or the size?" gets an answer, and the answer is the sentence they should have written.

What Hyland's corpus work adds

Hyland's contribution is descriptive, and two of his findings are worth knowing because they discipline the advice in this lecture.

The first is that metadiscourse density varies enormously by discipline, and the variation is not noise: fields whose claims are more contestable use more hedging, and fields with settled methods use less. That means there is no universal target, and a writer moving between a quantitative model document and a policy paper should expect the appropriate level to change. Advice to minimise metadiscourse is therefore always advice relative to a genre.

The second is that the best-regarded writers in most fields use metadiscourse differently rather than less: heavier on the resources that engage a reader and lighter on those that announce structure. That fits this lecture's split exactly, and it suggests the target is not a lower count but a different mix, which is a more defensible claim than the usual instruction to cut.

Where this leaves the module

Lectures 1 and 2 have covered the cutting half of the module, and it is worth stating what has and has not been claimed. Every category in both lectures is deletable without loss, with the exceptions tabulated. Nothing in either has told you to shorten a sentence carrying a qualification, a condition, a scope statement or a join, and the enthusiastic misapplication of both is the same: a document that is shorter and less accurate.

The rest of the module turns to shaping, which is additive rather than subtractive, and the first thing it does is defend the long sentence that this half of the module might have seemed to condemn. Lecture 3 argues that a long sentence with internal shape is not a failure of concision but a structure that holds related things in a single frame, and that the professional prejudice against length is aimed at the wrong variable, which is where module 1's lecture 5 came in.

Common failure Deleting hedges to make prose crisper and thereby overstating findings. This is the mirror of the stacking failure and it is committed by exactly the people who have read advice like this lecture. If your evidence supports a direction and not a magnitude, and you delete the hedge, you have made a claim your work does not carry, and no amount of stylistic improvement makes that acceptable. Calibrate rather than cut: one hedge, chosen from the table, qualifying the thing you are actually unsure about.

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Run the metadiscourse pass on a paragraph of your own, listing each candidate and the two-question verdict. Report how many looked like metadiscourse and turned out to be content.
  2. Give Hyland's two categories of metadiscourse and the test that separates what earns its place from throat-clearing.
  3. Take the four-hedge sentence in the figure. List everything the reader has lost, then write the explicit uncertainty version and compare the word counts.
  4. Why are hedges not interchangeable? Which hedge should a modeller use who is sure of the direction and unsure of the size, and which do they usually reach for instead?
  5. State the argument against boosters. What is the one legitimate use of clearly, and what does of course do that is worse?
  6. Explain hedging as risk transfer. Why does that mean "hedge less" will not be followed, and what review question works instead?
  7. Hyland finds that metadiscourse density varies by discipline in a non-random way. What does that imply about advice to minimise it?
  8. Describe the mirror failure of hedge-stacking, and who commits it. What is the rule that prevents both?
