Lectures / Writing / Concision and grace

The counter-position

Klinkenborg rejects the outline, distrusts the long sentence, and is hostile to the whole idea that prose should flow. Set against Minto, McPhee and most of this module, that is a real argument with consequences for how you work, and on one point the evidence in module 9 is on his side rather than this course's.

Lecture 7 of 7 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: Klinkenborg, Several Short Sentences About Writing (2012)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Verlyn Klinkenborg holds five positions that contradict what this course has taught. Write short sentences until you can genuinely control long ones, and most writers never can. Distrust outlines, because a plan made before you understood the material encodes your ignorance. Reject flow as a goal, because prose that feels seamless is often prose that has hidden the absence of an argument. Attend to one sentence at a time, and make each one able to stand alone. And do not assume you know what you want to say before you write it, because you find the material in the sentences.

Set that against module 4, which told you to settle the structure before drafting and put the answer at the top, and against this module's lectures 3, 4 and 6, which taught you to build long sentences and shape their rhythm. The disagreement is not about taste. It is about where the material comes from, and two answers are on offer: from the structure you impose, or from the sentences you fight with.

On one point Klinkenborg is better supported than this course has been. Module 9 covers Flower and Hayes, whose evidence is that composition is recursive rather than a plan-then-write sequence, and that undercuts a strict structure-first prescription. It does not support having no plan, and it does support the middle position that neither Minto nor Klinkenborg holds.

Feynman test Two ways to build something. Draw the whole thing first, then make the parts to fit, which is how you get a machine that works and a machine nobody would have discovered by fiddling. Or make one part as well as you possibly can, then the next, and let the shape emerge, which is how you find things a plan would have ruled out. Both produce good work. They produce different work, and you cannot do both at once.
What is beyond the source Klinkenborg states his positions and does not argue with Minto or McPhee, who go unmentioned. Placing the three on one axis, as step 3 does, is this lecture's construction, and so is the observation in step 5 that his hostility to flow is module 3's cohesive-but-incoherent quadrant arrived at independently. The concession that Flower and Hayes support him against module 4 is also this lecture's, and it is the point at which the course argues against itself rather than adjudicating between others.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that a course teaching only one position on a contested question would be doing what module 1's lecture 7 criticised: presenting a convention as a constraint. Module 4's material is a defensible position rather than a discovery, and you are entitled to the strongest case against it before you adopt it.

The second reason is that Klinkenborg's diagnosis fits a large class of professional writers better than this course's does. If your long sentences are out of control, the useful advice is not to learn Christensen's levels but to write short until you can hear what you are doing. If your documents are planned to death and say nothing, the useful advice is not more structure. He is describing a real failure mode, and it is the opposite of the one modules 2 to 4 address.

The third reason is the flow argument, which has more content than it first appears. The demand that prose feel effortless and continuous is exactly what module 3's lecture 1 identified as satisfiable without coherence: its dangerous quadrant reads beautifully and arrives nowhere. Klinkenborg reached that conclusion from a completely different direction, without the terminology, and being suspicious of smoothness is a defensible response to it.

And a reason about self-knowledge. The two positions suit different kinds of writer, and knowing which you are is worth more than knowing which is correct. A writer who cannot start until the structure is settled and a writer who cannot settle a structure until they have written are both real, and prescribing the wrong method to either produces paralysis.

Step 3

The mental model

One axis: how much is decided before you write. Three positions on it, with what each optimises for and what each gives up.

The synthesis at the bottom of the figure is not a compromise for its own sake. It is the position module 4's lecture 7 reached when pressed, and it survives both critiques: draft freely to find out what you think, which is Klinkenborg's method and is genuinely how the material gets discovered; then settle the structure; then write the thing the reader receives, which is Minto's method and is what a reader who did not choose your document requires. The mistake both sides make is treating one activity as the whole job.

Flow, as Klinkenborg means it
The demand that prose feel seamless and continuous, and that a reader be carried from sentence to sentence without effort. He treats it as a taught habit that conceals the absence of thought, which is module 3's cohesive-but-incoherent quadrant described from outside.
The standalone sentence
Klinkenborg's unit of work. Each sentence should be able to bear inspection alone, without support from its neighbours. Directly opposed to module 3's position that a sentence's job is partly to attach to the one before.
Recursion
Flower and Hayes's finding that planning, drafting and reviewing interleave throughout composition rather than running in sequence. Undercuts strict structure-first prescriptions without licensing the absence of planning.
Step 4

The detail

The five positions, each argued both ways

PositionStrongest case forStrongest case against
Klinkenborg against this course
Write short until you can control long Most long sentences in professional prose are centre-embedded, which lecture 3 conceded. A writer who cannot yet build a cumulative sentence produces worse prose attempting one than writing three short ones, and the failure rate is high. The short-sentence habit incurs a connective obligation most writers do not pay, producing the relation-free prose of module 4's lecture 8. Short is not safe; it is differently unsafe.
Distrust the outline A plan made before you understood the material encodes your ignorance, and then you spend the drafting defending it. Module 4's lecture 1 admitted that openings written first are records of intentions. A document with no plan has no guarantee of arriving anywhere, and a reader who did not choose it will not follow a shape that emerged. Modules 3 and 4 exist because unplanned documents fail predictably.
Reject flow Smoothness is achievable without coherence, which module 3's lecture 1 demonstrated with a paragraph that flows perfectly and walks from a credit problem to a board meeting. Cohesion is not the same as flow, and the given-new contract is an empirical finding about processing cost rather than a taught habit. Rejecting the whole category throws out Clark and Haviland with the bathwater.
One sentence at a time, each standing alone It forces you to know what each sentence claims, and a sentence propped up by its neighbours often turns out to claim nothing. This is module 2's undecided-thought finding as a working method. A sentence's job includes attaching to the previous one. Sentences built to stand alone produce the coherent-but-not-cohesive quadrant: a page of assertions the reader must connect unaided.
You do not know what you want to say Supported by Flower and Hayes, and by every writer's experience of discovering an argument while drafting. Module 2 said a sentence you cannot fix is a thought you have not finished, which concedes the point. True of exploratory writing and false of most professional writing, where the finding exists before the document and the job is to convey it. A validation report is not where you work out whether the model failed.

The last row locates the real disagreement. Klinkenborg is describing writing where the writing is the thinking, and this course is largely aimed at writing where the thinking happened in a spreadsheet three weeks earlier. Both exist, and a great deal of confusion about writing advice comes from authors generalising from their own case without saying which they are in.

What survives regardless of who is right

Four pieces of his advice hold on any view, and they are worth extracting because they cost nothing to adopt.

Hear every sentence. Lecture 5 argued this on instrumental grounds and Klinkenborg argues it on principle, and the practice is identical either way.

Be suspicious of prose that came easily. This is a genuine addition to the course, and it survives the disagreement because it is a claim about a warning sign rather than about method: a passage that wrote itself smoothly is a passage where you may have been assembling familiar phrases rather than thinking, and the six kinds of redundancy in lecture 1 are exactly what that produces.

Do not trust a plan you made before you understood the material. Module 4's lecture 1 already conceded this for openings, and the concession generalises: a structure settled at the outset should be revisited once the analysis is done, which is the middle position the figure describes.

Write short if you cannot yet write long well. This is the honest version of his first position, and lecture 3 conceded it explicitly. A tired writer, or one unsure of the material, should not be attempting four-level cumulative sentences.

Which writer are you

The practical question is not which position is correct but which failure you are prone to, and the two are distinguishable by a simple observation about your own drafts.

If your documents are structurally sound and lifeless, full of correct sections that nobody wants to read, you are over-planned, and Klinkenborg's method is the corrective: draft without a plan, work sentence by sentence, and see what you find. If your documents contain good sentences and no argument, and readers report that they are hard to follow without being able to say why, you are under-planned, and module 4 is the corrective.

Most professional writers are the second, which is why this course is weighted as it is. That weighting is a judgement about a population rather than a claim about correctness, and if you are the first kind, the course has been giving you the wrong medicine and this lecture is where that gets said.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Where he found something this course reached by another route

The most interesting thing about Klinkenborg's hostility to flow is that he arrives at module 3's central distinction without the apparatus. He has no term for cohesion, does not cite Halliday or Clark and Haviland, and appears to be reasoning entirely from having read a great deal of prose that felt good and said nothing. His conclusion, that smoothness is a warning sign, is a reasonable inference from exactly the evidence module 3's lecture 1 put in its dangerous quadrant.

Two traditions reaching the same observation independently is worth something, and it is worth being precise about where they diverge. Module 3 concluded that cohesion and coherence are independent properties requiring separate checks. Klinkenborg concluded that cohesion should be distrusted. The first is more useful, because it lets you keep the joins and check the argument, and the second is not unreasonable if you have no method for the separate check, which he does not offer.

So the disagreement here is narrower than it looks. He is right that flow can be hollow, and this course agrees and has a diagnostic. His remedy is cruder than the diagnostic and it is a remedy, which is more than most writing advice offers for that failure.

The point at which this course was wrong

Module 4 prescribed settling the structure before drafting, and presented it as straightforwardly correct. The Flower and Hayes evidence does not support that as a description of how skilled writers work, and it is worth saying plainly that the prescription was stronger than the evidence.

The defence available is narrow and it is honest. Module 4's advice is about the deliverable rather than about the process: whatever you did to discover your material, the document a reader receives should have a settled structure, and the structure should be settled before that document is written. Module 4's lecture 7 said this when pressed, and its lecture 1 said openings are written last, so the position was there. It should have been stated at the front rather than conceded in two places.

What follows is a correction worth carrying. Nothing in this course licenses drafting your deliverable while working out what you think. The exploratory draft is a real and necessary thing, it is not the document, and treating the two as one is the failure both traditions are pointing at from opposite sides.

What is left unresolved, and why

One question the evidence does not settle: whether the material is found in the structure or in the sentences. Flower and Hayes establish that discovery happens throughout composition; they do not establish where the important discoveries happen, and the two traditions have incompatible intuitions about it.

Klinkenborg's claim is that the work of making a sentence exact is where you find out what you actually think, and that a structure imposed in advance prevents that discovery. Minto's implicit claim is that the important discoveries are about relations between ideas, which happen at the level of the pyramid, and that sentence-level struggle is downstream. Both are describing real experiences and this course has no way to adjudicate between them.

The honest position is that you should find out which is true of you, by observation rather than by preference. Keep a note, over a few substantial documents, of where your genuine changes of mind occurred: while arranging sections, or while trying to write a particular sentence. Whatever the general answer is, your own answer determines which method to use, and it is knowable at the cost of paying attention.

Common failure Reading this lecture as permission to stop planning. Klinkenborg's method is demanding rather than free: it requires you to work every sentence until it is right, to have the patience to discover a structure late, and to accept that some drafts go nowhere. A writer who abandons the outline without adopting the rest of his discipline has not chosen a position; they have stopped doing the part that was working.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a document you must write. Plan it Minto's way, in full, before drafting. Separately, draft two pages of it Klinkenborg's way, sentence by sentence with no plan. Then say which produced something you did not already know.
  2. State Klinkenborg's five positions. For each, give the strongest case for and against in one sentence.
  3. Place Minto, McPhee and Klinkenborg on the axis. What does each optimise for, and what does each surrender?
  4. What do Flower and Hayes establish, and against which two positions does it count? What position does it support that neither holds?
  5. Klinkenborg's hostility to flow reaches a conclusion this course reaches by another route. State the shared observation and the point of divergence.
  6. Where was module 4 wrong, and what is the narrow defence? State the correction that follows.
  7. How would you find out whether your own material is discovered in the structure or in the sentences? Why is preference the wrong basis for answering?
  8. Which failure are you prone to, over-planning or under-planning? Give the observable evidence from your own drafts rather than an impression.
