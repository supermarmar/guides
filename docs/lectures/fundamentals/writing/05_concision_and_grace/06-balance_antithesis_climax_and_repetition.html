Balance, antithesis, climax and artful repetition

Four figures from classical rhetoric, and an argument that they are not ornament. A balanced sentence is what a genuinely parallel set sounds like; an antithesis is what a real opposition sounds like. The figures emerge when you let the syntax match the logic, and they fail exactly when the logic is not there.

Lecture 6 of 7 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: Corbett & Connors, Classical Rhetoric for the Modern Student; Williams, Style
Step 1

The 30-second version

Four figures of arrangement. Balance puts matched grammatical structures of similar length side by side. Antithesis is balance with opposed content. Climax orders three or more items by ascending intensity. Artful repetition repeats a structure at the beginning of successive units, at the end, or in reverse.

The claim this lecture makes, and which most treatments of the figures do not, is that they are load-bearing rather than decorative. A balanced sentence is what module 4's inductive set sounds like when its members are genuinely parallel. An antithesis is what a real contrast sounds like when you stop hiding it. A climax is what an inductive set ordered by degree sounds like when you let the syntax show the ordering. In each case the figure is the audible form of a structure you should already have.

Which means the figures fail in a specific and diagnosable way. False parallelism is module 1's faulty parallelism: items presented as matched that are not. False antithesis opposes things that are not opposed. False climax lists three items in an order that does not ascend. Each failure is a claim about the content, made by the syntax, that the content does not support, and readers detect it as hollowness without being able to name why.

Feynman test If two things really are the same kind of thing, say them in the same shape and the reader hears that they match. If two things really are opposites, put them in the same shape and the opposition rings. The figures are not effects you add. They are what happens when you stop obscuring a structure that was already there, which is why they cost nothing when the logic is sound and sound false when it is not.
What is beyond the source The rhetorical handbooks catalogue the figures and demonstrate them on literary examples. The argument that each figure is the audible form of a specific logical structure already covered in module 4, and that the false versions are therefore diagnosable as content failures rather than as stylistic misfires, is this lecture's. It is what makes the material usable in a technical register, where "use antithesis for effect" would be bad advice.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the figures give you a check on your own logic that runs in the opposite direction from everything else in this course. Elsewhere you inspect the structure and fix the prose. Here the prose tells you about the structure: if you try to write three findings in balanced form and the third will not fit the shape, you have discovered that it is not the same kind of thing as the first two, which is module 4's plural noun test arrived at by ear.

The second reason is that the professional register admits more of this than writers assume. Balance and antithesis are entirely at home in a validation report, because they are what a comparison and a contrast look like when written plainly. "The retail book stabilised; the wholesale book did not" is an antithesis, and nobody would call it rhetorical. The figures that read as rhetoric are the repetitions, and lecture 3's warning about visible technique applies to them.

The third reason is emphasis, which this module keeps returning to because professional writers have so few devices. A climax gives you an ordering the reader can hear, so the third item lands hardest without any lexical marking. That is a genuine addition to the stress position from module 2 and the length contrast from lecture 4, and it is the only one of the three that works inside a list.

And a reason about persuasion, stated carefully. These figures are old, they recur across unrelated rhetorical traditions, and they persist because they work on listeners. That is worth knowing and it is also the reason for caution: a device that works on listeners regardless of the truth of what it carries is a device that can make a weak argument sound strong, and step 5 takes that seriously.

Step 3

The mental model

Four figures, each with the logical structure it is the audible form of, a correct instance, and the false version.

The false-climax example is worth dwelling on because it is the one professionals produce most often, and by accident. Findings get listed in the order they were discovered, which module 4's lecture 4 identified as not an ordering at all, and the result frequently descends: the grave conclusion first, the technical detail last. The sentence deflates, and the reader's last impression is the mildest thing in it, which module 2's lecture 7 predicts they will remember.

Balance
Matched grammatical structures of similar length placed side by side. The audible form of a parallel set, and the reason faulty parallelism sounds wrong before you can say why.
Antithesis
Balance carrying opposed content. The "not X but Y" form is the strongest, and it doubles as the clearest way to mark a concession, which module 3 established must be marked or reversed.
Climax
Three or more items in ascending order of intensity, so the last lands hardest. The audible form of degree ordering, and the only emphasis device in this course that works inside a list.
Artful repetition
Anaphora repeats an opening, epistrophe an ending, chiasmus a structure in reverse. All three work and all three are audibly rhetorical, which is a cost in a document whose credibility depends on seeming undesigned.
Step 4

The detail

Building a balanced sentence, and what the attempt reveals

THE BALANCE TEST AS A LOGIC CHECK Take a set of items and try to write them in matched form. Where the shape resists, the logic is wrong. ATTEMPT 1 "The stress window excludes the 2008 downturn, the recovery lags are drawn from a benign period, and the model has not been challenged since 2019." Shapes: [subject][verb][object] [subject][verb][prepositional phrase] [subject][negated perfect][time adjunct] The third does not match, and trying to force it reveals why: the first two are properties of the model, the third is a property of its governance. Different classes. DIAGNOSIS Module 4, lecture 5's dimension test: "these are the categories of ___, divided by ___". Items one and two divide by methodological defect. Item three divides by control failure. Two dimensions, so the set was never a set. REPAIR Two balanced sets rather than one broken one. "The stress window excludes the 2008 downturn, and the recovery lags come from a benign period." "Neither defect was challenged at any review since 2019." WHAT JUST HAPPENED An attempt at a sound effect found a grouping failure that the plural noun test would also have found. The difference is that this route takes ten seconds and requires no vocabulary, which is why it is worth having as a habit: try to write the set in matched form, and notice what will not fit.

Antithesis and the concession problem

The "not X but Y" construction deserves separate treatment because it solves a problem module 3 raised and could not fully answer. That lecture established that an unmarked concession is read as its opposite, and that the repair is a connective at the front of the sentence. Antithesis gives you a stronger repair: put both halves in one sentence, in matched form, and the opposition is carried structurally rather than by a marker the reader might skim past.

Compare the two repairs. "The window excludes the downturn. Even so, the calibration passed the benchmark." That is correct and the concession depends on two words. "The calibration passed the benchmark not because it is sound but because the benchmark shares its window." That is one sentence, the opposition is in the shape, and the reader cannot take the passing test as reassurance because the sentence has told them what it means.

The second version also does something the first does not: it supplies the cause. The "not X but Y" form obliges you to say what the real explanation is, since Y has to be something. That is a case where a rhetorical figure forces an intellectual commitment, and it is the strongest argument in this lecture for treating the figures as substantive.

What the register will take

FigureIn a technical documentWhy
Freely available
Balance Use throughout. It is what a parallel set looks like written plainly. Nobody reads it as a device, and refusing it produces faulty parallelism.
Antithesis Use throughout. It is what a contrast looks like, and the "not X but Y" form is the best available way to mark a concession.
Sparingly
Climax Once or twice per document, in the summary and the conclusion. Audible as arrangement, so it reads as deliberate. In a findings summary that is appropriate; in a methodology section it is not.
Anaphora Once, if at all, and only where the repetition is the point. "No control detected it, no review challenged it, no committee questioned it" is legitimate because the repeated absence is the finding. Elsewhere it reads as advocacy.
Effectively unavailable
Chiasmus Almost never. Unmistakably crafted. In a document whose authority rests on appearing to report rather than to persuade, a chiasmus invites the reader to discount the whole thing.
Epistrophe Rarely, and never more than once. Same objection, slightly weaker. Occasionally justified where the repeated ending is a defined term the reader should notice recurring.

The anaphora row draws the line this table is really about. The device is legitimate when the repetition corresponds to something real in the world: three controls that all failed, and the sameness of the failure is the point. It is illegitimate when the repetition is imposed on items that merely happen to be adjacent. That is the same criterion as everywhere else in the lecture, which is why the figures are not a separate subject: they are what the logic sounds like, and they lie when the logic is absent.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Why the figures are dangerous

These devices have survived two and a half thousand years across unrelated rhetorical traditions, and the honest explanation is that they work on audiences. A balanced sentence sounds more true than an unbalanced one carrying the same content. An antithesis sounds like an insight. A climax sounds like an argument reaching a conclusion, whether or not it has.

That is a problem, and it is the problem this whole course has been circling. Every technique that makes prose more effective makes a wrong claim more effective too, and the figures are the clearest case because their effect is so nearly independent of content. Module 2's lecture 8 said a beautifully paramediced paragraph asserting something false is worse than a clumsy one asserting something true. A well-turned antithesis asserting a false opposition is worse again, because the shape is doing persuasive work that the reader cannot easily separate from the claim.

The defence available is the one this lecture has built its structure on. If you only use a figure where the corresponding logical structure genuinely holds, the figure cannot mislead, because it is asserting something the content supports. The false versions in the figure are not stylistic misfires; they are the figure lying about the logic, and the reason to learn to spot them in your own writing is the same reason to spot them in other people's.

Consistency with the rest of the module

Lecture 4 argued that variation in sentence length should be a by-product of doing other things correctly rather than a goal, and that visible rhythm is worse than flat rhythm. This lecture makes the same argument about the figures, and it is worth noticing that the two positions are the same one.

The unifying claim is that the sound of prose should follow from its structure. Where the structure is a parallel set, the sentence balances. Where it is a degree ordering, it climaxes. Where a claim carries qualifications, the sentence is long and cumulative; where a claim should land, it is short. None of these is a decision about sound. Every one is a decision about content, allowed to show.

Which yields the module's actual instruction, now that six of its seven lectures are in place. Cut what is not working, which is lectures 1 and 2. Build sentences whose shape matches what they carry, which is lectures 3, 4 and 6. Check the result by ear, which is lecture 5. And at no point decide how the prose should sound and then arrange the content to fit, which is the failure all four shaping lectures warn against in different vocabularies.

The dissent, arriving next

Lecture 7 gives Klinkenborg, and it is worth flagging what he would say about this lecture specifically. He is hostile to the idea that prose should flow, suspicious of technique that makes sentences pleasant, and insistent that a writer's attention belongs on one sentence at a time rather than on the shape of a paragraph. On his account the figures in this lecture are the kind of thing that produces prose which sounds accomplished and says nothing.

He is not obviously wrong, and the disagreement is not about the figures but about what writing is for. Lecture 7 sets it out without resolving it, which is the honest treatment for a disagreement that turns on a question this course cannot settle from the outside.

Common failure Learning the names and hunting for opportunities. A document containing three antitheses, a climax and an anaphora is a document whose author has been enjoying themselves, and the reader will notice. The figures are not a checklist to work through; they are what certain structures sound like when you write them plainly, and if you find yourself looking for somewhere to put a chiasmus, you have inverted the whole argument of the lecture.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a set of three or four findings from your own work and try to write them in balanced form. Report what would not fit the shape, then run module 4's dimension test on the set and say whether the two diagnoses agree.
  2. Name the four figures and, for each, the logical structure it is the audible form of.
  3. Give the false version of each figure and say what claim the syntax is making that the content does not support.
  4. Why is "not X but Y" a stronger repair for an unmarked concession than a front-loaded connective? What does it oblige you to supply?
  5. Why is a false climax the failure professionals produce most often, and by accident? Which module 4 lecture explains the cause?
  6. Which two figures are freely available in a technical register and which is effectively unavailable? Give the reason in terms of what the document's authority rests on.
  7. State the argument that the figures are dangerous, and the defence this lecture offers against it.
  8. What is the single unifying claim behind lectures 3, 4 and 6? State it in one sentence.
