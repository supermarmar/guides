Lectures / Writing / Style and voice

Style as a set of prior decisions

Thomas and Turner argue that a style is not a surface property but a stand on six questions, taken before you write a word. That reframes the previous five modules, and it obliges this course to admit something: it has been teaching one particular style throughout without declaring it.

Lecture 1 of 7 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: Thomas & Turner, Clear and Simple as the Truth (1994)
Step 1

The 30-second version

A style, on Thomas and Turner's account, is a set of positions on six questions. Can truth be known? What is presentation for? What scene does the writing imply? Who are the cast, meaning the writer and reader as the prose constructs them? What is thought, and how does it relate to writing? And what can language do? Two writers can follow identical advice about clarity and produce prose that reads nothing alike, because they have answered those six differently.

The reframing matters because it explains institutional prose as a systematic choice rather than a series of failures. The register that modules 2 to 5 kept diagnosing, with no actors, hedged claims, and the writer's own uncertainty as part of the subject, is a coherent stand on all six questions. Truth is contested, presentation is a negotiation, the scene is a defence before a hostile audience, and language is a problem rather than an instrument. Taken deliberately that is a legitimate style. Taken by default because everyone in the department writes that way, it produces prose in which nobody can find a claim.

The uncomfortable consequence is for this course. Modules 2 to 5 have consistently recommended characters in subjects, actions in verbs, the point first, the concrete over the abstract, and confidence over hedging. That is not neutral clarity advice. It is the programme of one style, and module 1's lecture 7 demanded that anyone handing you a rule say which kind it was. This lecture is where that debt is paid.

Feynman test Before you write, you have already decided whether you are pointing at something you have seen, defending yourself against people who might disagree, thinking out loud, or performing. Every sentence you then write is shaped by that decision, and you probably did not make it consciously. Most bad institutional prose is written by people who are defending themselves while believing they are pointing at something.
What is beyond the source Thomas and Turner supply the six elements and the taxonomy of styles. Two things here are not theirs. The first is the observation in step 5 that this course has been teaching classic style without declaring it, which is self-criticism rather than exposition. The second is the warning that their book is partisan: it argues for classic style, so its characterisations of the alternatives, and particularly of the reflexive, are unsympathetic and should not be read as neutral description.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that it answers a question the previous modules could not. You have followed every diagnostic, and your prose still reads unlike a colleague's who followed the same ones. That is not a failure of application. The clarity advice in modules 2 to 5 constrains prose without determining it, and what remains underdetermined is exactly what these six questions decide.

The second reason is diagnostic and it changes how you read institutional documents. A paper full of hedges, missing actors and process narration is usually read as badly written. On this account it is competently written in a style whose stand is that truth is contested and the writer's position is precarious. That is a more accurate diagnosis, and it is more useful, because it locates the problem in a decision somebody made rather than in a skill somebody lacks.

The third reason is that it is the only framework in this course that tells you when to disregard the rest of it. If your material is genuinely uncertain, if the writing is where you are working out what you think, or if the reader cannot verify what you assert, then several of module 2's recommendations are wrong for your document, and you need a principled basis for departing from them rather than an instinct. The six questions supply it.

And a reason about honesty. A course that teaches one style while presenting it as neutral good practice is doing what module 1's lecture 7 criticised in Strunk and White: presenting a convention as a constraint. Naming the commitment lets you accept or reject it, which is the whole point of that lecture's three bins.

Step 3

The mental model

The six questions, with classic style's answers on one side and the reflexive style's on the other. Both columns are coherent, and only one of them is what this course has been recommending.

Read the right-hand column charitably before dismissing it, because charity is what makes the framework useful. A modeller writing a validation finding in a firm where being wrong in writing has consequences is in a scene closer to a defence than to a conversation, their reader does have standing to object, and their exposure is real. The reflexive answers are not stupid answers to the six questions. They are the answers that situation produces, which is why the style is so stable.

Style, on this account
A set of positions on truth, presentation, scene, cast, thought and language, taken before writing and shaping every sentence that follows. Not a surface property, and not reducible to sentence-level choices.
Classic style
Truth is knowable, the writer has seen it, presentation is disinterested pointing, the cast are competent equals, thought precedes writing, and language is adequate. The field guide is the exemplary case. Lecture 2 treats it fully.
Reflexive style
Truth is contested, the writer's uncertainty is part of the subject, the scene is a defence, and language is itself a problem. Legitimate when chosen, and the default in academic and institutional writing whether chosen or not.
Step 4

The detail

Diagnosing a document's stand

READING A STYLE OFF THE PAGE Take any professional document and answer the six questions from its prose rather than from its author's intentions. DOCUMENT: a typical validation report opening "The purpose of this report is to document the annual review of the downturn LGD calibration. A number of considerations were taken into account. It may be the case that certain aspects of the current approach could be enhanced." TRUTH Contested. "May be the case", "certain aspects": nothing is presented as established. PRESENTATION Managing exposure. Nothing is shown; a position is held. SCENE A defence. The prose anticipates objection and pre-concedes. CAST Asymmetric. An author accounting for themselves to a body with authority over them. No verifying equal. THOUGHT Not visible as complete. "Considerations were taken into account" reports deliberation rather than conclusion. LANGUAGE Treated as risky. Every claim is cushioned. VERDICT Coherently reflexive. Not badly written by its own standards, and it is not doing what its author would say it was doing if asked. THE SAME CONTENT, CLASSIC STAND "The downturn LGD calibration understates the peak loss rate by about 40 basis points. Its stress window spans 2011 to 2023, a period in which arrears never exceeded 1.4 per cent against 3.1 per cent in 2008. The arrears series is in appendix B." TRUTH knowable, and stated PRESENTATION showing the reader the thing SCENE look at this CAST equals; the last sentence tells the reader where to verify THOUGHT complete before the writing LANGUAGE a window Note the final sentence. Pointing the reader at the evidence is not a courtesy, it is what makes the classic cast real rather than asserted, and lecture 2 makes it a requirement.

What the previous modules did and did not settle

It is worth being precise about how much of modules 2 to 5 is style-dependent, because the answer is not "all of it".

MaterialStyle-dependent?Note

Branching and centre-embedding No A working-memory limit. No stand on truth changes what three open dependencies cost.
Subject-verb proximity, the stress position No Processing effects. What you choose to put in the stress position is style-dependent; that the position exists is not.

Characters in subjects, actions in verbs Yes Presumes there are actors and that naming them is appropriate. A reflexive document whose subject is the difficulty of the question has no such actors.
The point first Yes Presumes you have a point and reached it before writing. Module 5's lecture 7 already conceded that this fails for exploratory work.
One hedge per claim Yes Presumes the uncertainty is a property of the evidence rather than of the writer's position. Module 5's lecture 2 named the risk-transfer motive and did not accept it.
Delete metadiscourse Yes Presumes the writing is not itself a subject. A document about methodological difficulty legitimately discusses its own limits.

The split is worth memorising because it tells you what you may not depart from. The processing findings hold under any stand: whatever style you adopt, a bare demonstrative with no antecedent costs the reader time and a triply embedded clause defeats them. Everything in the lower half is a consequence of a commitment, and if you reject the commitment, the advice goes with it.

The other styles, in one line each

Lecture 3 treats these properly. In summary: plain style assumes truth is obvious and shared, and addresses a community rather than an individual. Practical style serves a reader who needs to do something, so utility overrides elegance and it is the style of the well-made manual. Contemplative style presents the writer's mind moving over a subject. Romantic style takes the writer's individual sensibility as the point. Prophetic style claims privileged access to truth the reader cannot verify. Oratorical style aims at an audience it intends to move.

The one worth noting now is practical style, because a great deal of technical documentation should be in it and is not. A procedure document, a model implementation guide, a runbook: these serve a reader who needs to accomplish something, and classic style's disinterested pointing is the wrong stand for them. Lecture 3 makes the case.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

This course has a stand, and here it is

Module 1's lecture 7 set out three bins and insisted that anyone handing you a rule should say which bin it was in. It is time to apply that to the course itself, and the answer is that a substantial part of modules 2 to 5 belongs in a fourth category the lecture did not name: consequences of a stylistic commitment, presented as clarity.

The commitment is to classic style. Characters in subjects presumes actors worth naming. The point first presumes a point held before writing. Confidence over hedging presumes truth is knowable and that you have seen it. Deleting metadiscourse presumes the writing is not part of the subject. Every one is defensible and none is neutral, and a reader who genuinely holds the reflexive positions would be entitled to reject the whole programme.

The defence is that classic style is right for most of the documents this collection concerns. A validation finding is a thing you have established and the reader could check. A board paper supports a decision that must be made. In both, the classic stand is not merely one option: it is the honest description of the situation, and the reflexive stand would be a misdescription adopted for the writer's protection, which module 5's lecture 2 named as risk transfer.

What the defence does not license is the pretence of neutrality, and the practical consequence is worth stating. When a colleague resists the advice in modules 2 to 5, they may not be failing to understand it. They may be holding a different stand on the six questions, and the productive conversation is about the scene and the cast rather than about sentence structure.

The source is an advocate, not a describer

One caution about Thomas and Turner. Clear and Simple as the Truth is a partisan book: its purpose is to teach classic style and to recommend it, and its accounts of the alternatives are written by people who prefer something else. The reflexive style in particular gets a treatment closer to caricature than to description, and academic prose is used mainly as a source of bad examples.

That does not make the six-element framework wrong, and the framework is the durable contribution. It does mean you should not take their characterisation of a style you write in as a fair account of it, and it means the taxonomy is shaped by the argument it was built to serve. A neutral account of styles might carve them differently.

The honest use is therefore to take the six questions as a diagnostic instrument and to answer them yourself about documents you actually read, which is what step 4 does. That is more informative than accepting a taxonomy, and it keeps the framework doing analytical work rather than advocacy.

Why this module could not come earlier

The module landing says the question of which style is meaningless until you can execute one, and that is worth unpacking because it justifies the ordering of the whole course.

A writer who cannot reliably put a character in a subject has not chosen the reflexive style; they have produced its symptoms by accident. The distinction between a stand and a habit is only available to someone who could write the other way and did not. So the five modules of technique are not preliminary to the interesting question; they are what makes the question askable, and a course that opened with the six elements would have offered a choice to people with one option.

Common failure Using the framework to excuse the register you already write in. "My document is reflexive by design" is available to anyone, and it is only true if you could have written it classically and chose not to. The test is the one step 4 applies: take a paragraph and write it in the other stand. If you can, your style is a choice and you may defend it. If you cannot, it is a habit with a theory attached.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take an opening paragraph from a document you did not write and answer all six questions from its prose. Then rewrite it in the other stand, as step 4 does, and state which is the honest description of that document's situation.
  2. Name the six elements. Why do they explain how two writers following identical clarity advice produce prose that reads nothing alike?
  3. Give the reflexive answers to all six, and the strongest case for them in a firm where being wrong in writing has consequences.
  4. Which parts of modules 2 to 5 hold under any stand, and which follow from the classic one? What distinguishes the two groups?
  5. State this course's undeclared commitment and the defence for it. What does the defence not license?
  6. Why should Thomas and Turner's account of the reflexive style be treated with suspicion? What is the durable contribution?
  7. Why can the question of which style not be asked before five modules of technique?
  8. Give the test that distinguishes a style you have chosen from a habit with a theory attached.
