Plain English and its measurement

The plain English movement is genuinely well supported, and the readability formulas are not the source of its support. Separating those two is the whole lecture, because it lets you accept the evidence and reject the metric, which is the only defensible position and not the usual one.

Lecture 7 of 7 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: Kimble (2012); Schriver (1997); Flesch (1948); Kincaid et al. (1975)
Step 1

The 30-second version

The plain English movement has real evidence behind it. Redrafting official documents in plain language improves comprehension and task completion, reduces error rates and enquiry volumes, and saves money; Joseph Kimble collects several dozen such studies. Regulators have acted on it, including the SEC's plain English requirements for prospectuses from 1998 and the US Plain Writing Act of 2010.

The readability formulas are not where that evidence comes from. Kimble's studies measure whether readers understood a document and could act on it. None of them establishes that a Flesch score tracks comprehension, and the formulas are incapable of tracking it, for the reason module 2's lecture 1 gave: their inputs are counts, and counts are invariant under reordering, so they cannot see syntax.

This lecture adds two demonstrations that module 2's permutation argument did not supply. A set of sentences saying nothing at all, built from monosyllables, scores off the top of the Flesch scale. And a passage of impenetrable domain jargon can score a hundred points easier than a clear explanation of the same content, because the jargon is short and the explanation is not. Both are computed in step 3, and together they establish that the formulas are not merely insensitive but actively inverted for technical prose.

Feynman test "The book is in run-off. The tail is fat. We hold the floor." Every word is one syllable, every sentence is short, and the formula rates it easier than almost anything you have ever read. A layperson has no idea what any of it means. That is not a strange edge case; that is what technical writing looks like, and it is why the number goes the wrong way.
What is beyond the source Kimble makes the empirical case for plain language and does not attack the formulas. The readability literature defends them. What this lecture does is separate the two claims explicitly, which almost nobody does, and supply the two computed demonstrations in step 3. The practical advice in step 5 about what to do when a score is contractually mandated is also this lecture's, and it is the situation many professionals are actually in.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the conflation costs the plain English movement its credibility with technical writers, who can see that the formulas do not work on their material and conclude that the whole programme is naive. That conclusion is wrong and the reasoning is sound: if the only evidence you have been shown is a Flesch score, and Flesch scores are demonstrably inverted for your subject matter, scepticism is the correct response. The remedy is to know what the actual evidence is.

The second reason is that scores get mandated. A regulator, a house style, or a contract may require a document to hit a threshold, and then you are obliged to optimise a number that does not measure what it claims. That is a real professional situation rather than a hypothetical, and pretending the formulas are worthless does not help you comply with them. Step 5 sets out the honest response.

The third reason is that this is the third time Goodhart's law has appeared in the course, and the pattern is now worth stating as a general lesson. Module 2's lecture 1 warned about readability formulas as targets, module 5's lecture 1 about the lard factor, and this lecture about mandated scores. In each case a measure that was informative as a screen becomes corrupting as an objective, and the mechanism is always that the cheapest way to move the number is not the way that improves the thing.

And a reason specific to consumer-facing documents in this collection's domain. Mortgage illustrations, key information documents and terms and conditions are all subject to comprehensibility requirements, and firms discharge those requirements by demonstrating scores because a score is auditable and a judgement is not. Knowing that the score is not the thing means knowing that compliance and comprehensibility are two separate pieces of work.

Step 3

The mental model

Two computed demonstrations, then the separation the lecture is built on.

The second demonstration deserves one clarification, because it might look like a trick. The jargon version is not a straw man: "in run-off", "a fat tail" and "the floor" are ordinary professional shorthand, and a document written entirely in that register would be normal in this domain and would score extremely well. The clear version is not padded; it is what you must write if your reader does not have the chunks, which is lecture 4's remedy applied. So the formula penalises exactly the repair that lecture 4 recommended.

Plain English movement
The programme of redrafting official and commercial documents so ordinary readers can use them. Supported by comprehension and task-completion studies rather than by formula scores, and successful enough to have been written into regulation.
The inversion
The property that makes readability formulas worse than useless on technical prose: domain shorthand is short and its explanation is long, so the metric rates the unreadable version easier than the readable one.
Smoke alarm, not a target
The only defensible use of a readability score. It can flag a document as an outlier worth rereading. It cannot tell you whether a revision improved anything, and optimising it degrades prose systematically.
Step 4

The detail

What the plain English evidence actually shows

FindingHow it was measuredWhat it does not show
The movement's genuine support
Redrafted official forms are completed more accurately Error rates on completed forms, before and after redrafting. That any particular metric predicts which drafts will do better.
Redrafted documents reduce enquiry volumes and cost Call volumes, handling time, money. Kimble's collection is largely of this kind. That the improvement came from shorter words rather than from structure, headings, or removing irrelevant content.
Readers prefer plain versions, including expert readers Preference studies, including judges and lawyers presented with plain and traditional legal drafting. That preference tracks comprehension, though here they happen to agree.
Redesign improves findability Time to locate a specific answer in a document. Schriver's work is the strongest of this kind. Anything about sentence-level style, since most of the gain came from the visual and structural layer.
What the formulas contribute to that case
Nothing directly The studies measure outcomes, not scores. Where scores are reported they are descriptive rather than the dependent variable. The formulas were validated for a different task: matching existing graded text to reading levels, on continuous prose, for text selection.

The fourth row is the one that should change what you do. Schriver's finding that most of the gain came from the visual and structural layer means the plain English evidence supports module 4 more strongly than it supports modules 2 and 5. Headings that carry assertions, a findable structure and a table where a table belongs deliver more measurable improvement than sentence-level work does, and both are worth doing in that order.

Why optimising a score degrades prose

THE THREE CHEAPEST WAYS TO MOVE THE NUMBER All three improve the score. All three make the document worse, and each has been diagnosed earlier in this course. 1. SPLIT SENTENCES AT ARBITRARY POINTS Halves words-per-sentence, which both formulas weight heavily. Damage: a split incurs a connective obligation that an arbitrary split does not pay, so the relations between claims are deleted and the reader supplies defaults. -> module 3, lecture 6; module 5, lecture 3 2. REPLACE LONGER WORDS WITH SHORTER SYNONYMS Lowers syllables-per-word. Damage: terms of art have no synonyms. "Impairment" becomes "loss" and the claim changes. -> module 1, lecture 2 3. REPLACE EXPLANATIONS WITH SHORTHAND The strongest single move available, and the one demonstration 2 quantifies at 101 points. Damage: it is the exact reverse of the chunk-unpacking that a non-expert reader needs. -> module 6, lecture 4 WHAT DOES NOT MOVE THE NUMBER AT ALL Restoring an actor to a subject. Moving a point to the front of a paragraph. Fixing a broken antecedent. Marking an unmarked concession. Turning a label heading into an assertion. Every one of those improves comprehension and none of them changes a single count. That asymmetry is the whole argument: the cheap ways to move the score are harmful, and the real improvements are invisible to it.

What plain English actually asks for

Stripped of the metrics, the plain English programme turns out to be recognisable. Its recommendations are: state the point first, name the actor, use the active voice where the actor is the topic, prefer familiar words where a familiar word means the same thing, keep the reader's task in view, structure for findability, and test on real readers.

Those are modules 2, 3, 4 and 6 of this course, arrived at independently by people redrafting government forms. That convergence is worth noticing for the same reason lecture 5's Prague School convergence was: two traditions with different methods reaching the same recommendations is evidence that the recommendations are about something real.

It also locates the disagreement precisely. The plain English movement and this course agree on nearly everything except one item, which is the preference for familiar words. Module 1's lecture 2 argued that the underlying variable is frequency rather than length, and that terms of art are the standing exception. Plain English drafting guides sometimes push past that exception, and where they do, this course does not follow them.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

When a score is mandated

The honest situation for many professionals is that a threshold is imposed by a regulator, a house style or a contract, and complaining that the metric is invalid does not discharge the obligation. Here is the workable response, and it has three parts.

Treat the score as a separate deliverable from the document's quality. Do the real work first, using the diagnostics in modules 2 to 4, and then measure. Most documents that have been genuinely improved will score better anyway, because the six kinds of redundancy in module 5's lecture 1 shorten sentences as a by-product, and that is the honest route to the number.

Where a gap remains, close it with the least damaging moves available, and know which those are. Splitting a genuinely long cumulative sentence into two, with a connective supplied, costs little. Replacing a Latinate word with a shorter one that means the same thing costs nothing. Replacing an explanation with shorthand costs the most and should be the last resort, and if the threshold forces it, that is worth recording as a comment on the requirement rather than accepting silently.

And keep the two records separate. If your document scored 45 and needed 50, and you got there by splitting four sentences, that is a fact about compliance rather than about comprehensibility. Documents where the two get conflated are documents where somebody will later believe the score was evidence.

Goodhart, for the third time

This is the third measure in the course to fail the same way, and the pattern is worth extracting because you will meet it again in measures this course has not covered.

Module 2's lecture 1 dismantled readability formulas. Module 5's lecture 1 warned that the lard factor corrupts as a target. This lecture adds mandated scores. In each case the measure is informative as a screen, in the direction of flagging outliers worth attention, and corrupting as an objective, because the cheapest path to a better number is not the path that improves the underlying thing.

The general test, which generalises past writing, is to ask what the cheapest available way to move the measure is. If that way improves the thing, the measure is safe to target. If it does not, the measure can only be used diagnostically. For every writing metric in this course the answer has been the second, and the reason is structural: writing quality is largely about relations between elements, and measures are largely counts of elements.

Where module 6 ends

Seven lectures have made one argument in several forms. Style is a stand you take, register is what the situation selects, voice is what persists across both, the curse of knowledge makes your judgement about your reader unreliable, and no number measures any of it.

The negative results have been unusually prominent, and that is deliberate rather than a failure to find positive ones. This module's honest content is that the questions it addresses do not have measurable answers, that its central framework comes from a partisan book, that its thinnest topic is the one most written about, and that the metric its final lecture examines is inverted for the prose its readers produce. A module presenting all that as settled technique would have been the more comfortable and less useful document.

Module 7 turns to rhetoric, where the tradition is two and a half thousand years old and the frameworks are correspondingly better developed. It also asks a question this module has deferred: what a text is trying to do to somebody, which is a different question from what style it is in.

Common failure Concluding from this lecture that plain English is a naive programme. That is the error the conflation invites and it is not supported: the movement's evidence is about comprehension and task completion, it is substantial, and it converges with four modules of this course arrived at by a different route. What is naive is the metric, and rejecting a movement because its popularisers reached for a bad number is throwing out the finding with the instrument.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Build demonstration 2 for your own domain: a passage of ordinary professional shorthand and a clear explanation of the same content. Count the inputs by hand and compute Flesch Reading Ease for both. Report the gap and its direction.
  2. What does the plain English evidence measure, and what does it not establish?
  3. State the inversion. Why is it a property of technical prose specifically rather than an edge case?
  4. Give the three cheapest ways to move a readability score and the damage each does. Which module diagnosed each?
  5. Name five improvements that change no count at all. What does that asymmetry establish?
  6. Schriver found most of the gain came from the visual and structural layer. Which module of this course does the plain English evidence support most strongly?
  7. A score is mandated and your document falls short. Give the three-part response, and say what must be kept separate from what.
  8. State the general test for whether a measure is safe to target. Why has the answer been negative for every writing metric in this course?
