The curse of knowledge

The single largest cause of bad expert prose, and it has appeared three times already in this course under other names. It is not a failure of effort or empathy, which is why trying harder does not work and every remedy that does work is procedural.

Lecture 4 of 7 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: Pinker (2014), ch. 3; Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber (1989)
Step 1

The 30-second version

The curse of knowledge is the difficulty of imagining what it is like not to know something you know. Camerer, Loewenstein and Weber named it in an economic setting, where subjects who possessed private information could not discount it when predicting the behaviour of people who lacked it. Pinker's contribution is to identify it as the principal cause of impenetrable expert writing and to give three mechanisms behind it.

Chunking means experts encode information in larger units, so a phrase that is one item for you is several unfamiliar items for your reader. Abstraction means experts think and write at the level of function and category, having discarded the concrete particulars a novice needs. Functional fixity means that once you know what something is for, you see the purpose rather than the object, and you assume your reader does too.

This has already appeared three times. Module 2's lecture 4 noted that specialists tolerate nominal density because the chunking is done in their heads. Module 3's lecture 2 showed you cannot feel the bridging inferences you impose, because the antecedent is in your head whether or not it is on the page. Module 3's lecture 6 showed the same for missing connectives. Each was an instance. This lecture is where the general phenomenon gets named and where the remedies are assessed honestly, which mostly means admitting how few work.

Feynman test Tap out a familiar tune on a table and ask someone to name it. You can hear the melody perfectly while tapping, and they hear knocking. You will be genuinely surprised that they cannot get it, and you will be surprised again next time, because the melody is in your head and there is nothing you can do to stop hearing it. That is the whole phenomenon, and no amount of concentration removes the melody.
What is beyond the source Pinker supplies the mechanisms and a set of remedies. Two things here are not his. The assessment in step 4, which ranks the remedies by how much evidence supports them and identifies one common instruction as actively harmful, is this lecture's. And the connection in step 3, that functional fixity is why lecture 2's verification path can be satisfied on paper while remaining unusable in fact, is a consequence Pinker does not draw and which matters for anyone writing model documentation.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this is the mechanism behind the failure the whole course keeps finding. Nearly every diagnostic in modules 2 to 5 is mechanical, involving covering the page, extracting a list, counting something, and the reason is always the same: the faculty you would otherwise use is contaminated by knowing what you meant. Naming the contamination explains why the methods look so indirect.

The second reason is organisational and it is worth stating bluntly. Other things equal, the more expert the author, the worse the documentation. The person who understands a model best is the worst-placed person to document it alone, because they have the most chunks, the most abstraction, and the most functional fixity. That is not a criticism of anyone; it is a structural fact with a structural remedy, which is that documentation needs a reader who does not know the model.

The third reason is that it undermines lectures 2 and 3 in a specific way. Classic style presumes a competent equal, and functional fixity is precisely what makes you believe your reader is one. Practical style presumes you know where the reader will struggle, and chunking is precisely what hides it. So your choice of style rests on a judgement about the reader that the curse makes unreliable, and no style is safe from it.

And a reason about feedback. When a reviewer says they did not follow something, the natural response is that they should have read it more carefully. Given this mechanism, that response is almost always wrong: the reviewer is the only instrument you have, and their confusion is data about your document rather than about them. Treating it as data is the single most valuable habit in this lecture.

Step 3

The mental model

Three mechanisms, each with a domain instance and the specific damage it does.

The remedy under functional fixity generalises further than the appendix case, and it is the most portable single instruction in the lecture: state the purpose before the object. "Appendix B gives the arrears series" is an object. "Appendix B gives the arrears series, from which the forty basis points can be rederived" is a purpose and then an object, and the difference is that the second tells a reader what to do. The same move works for tables, exhibits, defined terms and process steps.

Chunking
The encoding of information in larger units with expertise. A phrase that is one retrievable item for a specialist decomposes into several unfamiliar items for a non-specialist, at a cost the specialist cannot perceive.
Abstraction
The expert's habit of thinking at the level of category and function, having discarded the concrete particulars that led there. Produces prose that is true, unactionable and indistinguishable from other true statements at the same level.
Functional fixity
Seeing what a thing is for rather than what it is, and assuming the reader does the same. The mechanism by which a verification path can be present in a document and absent for its reader.
Step 4

The detail

The remedies, ranked by whether they work

RemedyVerdictWhy
Works
Give it to an actual non-expert and watch Best available, by a distance The only method that does not rely on the compromised faculty. Their confusion is direct evidence. Rarely done, because it takes an hour and is socially uncomfortable.
Name a specific real reader Good Forces you to instantiate rather than imagine "the reader", which is an abstraction and therefore subject to mechanism 2. A named person has known gaps.
Unpack each chunk once, on first use Good and mechanical Attacks mechanism 1 directly, requires no judgement about your reader, and is already required by the terms-of-art discipline.
Supply a concrete instance for every abstraction Good and mechanical Attacks mechanism 2. Checkable by search: find every category noun and ask what the instance was.
State purpose before object Good, and the most portable Attacks mechanism 3, which is otherwise the hardest to touch, because nothing about your own text signals it.
Weak
Leave it a week and reread Partial You forget your sentences and not your subject. Catches local roughness; leaves chunking and fixity intact, because those are properties of your expertise rather than of your memory of the draft.
Imagine a naive reader Weak Asks the compromised faculty to simulate its own absence. Better than nothing and it is what most writers do instead of the first row.
Does not work, or is actively harmful
Reading aloud No help for this Module 5's lecture 5 established what it detects, and none of it is chunking or fixity. Your voice pronounces your chunks perfectly well.
"Explain it more" Actively harmful Produces more words at the same level of abstraction, which is padding. The remedy is not more explanation but more concrete explanation, and the instruction does not distinguish them.
Simplify the vocabulary Misdirected Chunking is about concepts, not words. Replacing "calibration" with a plainer synonym leaves the four open questions intact, and module 1's lecture 2 showed it can lose a term of art as well.

The last row is worth dwelling on because it is the most common misdiagnosis in professional writing. A committee reports that a paper was too technical, the author simplifies the vocabulary, and the paper remains impenetrable, because the difficulty was never lexical. The reader did not need "calibration" replaced; they needed to be told, once, that a calibration is the process of setting a model's parameters from historical data and that a downturn calibration sets them from a severe period specifically. That is one sentence and it is not a simplification.

The named-reader technique

NAMING A READER, PROPERLY Not "the reader" and not "a committee member". A person. BAD "I am writing for the Model Risk Committee." -> a committee is an abstraction, so mechanism 2 applies to your model of your audience GOOD "I am writing for the finance director who sits on the committee. She has an accounting background, reads twelve papers a month, understands provisioning and capital ratios, has never built a model, and does not know what a stress window is." Now the questions become answerable. Does she know what LGD is? probably, in outline Does she know what a stress window is? no Can she use appendix B? not without being told what to do with it Will she read section 4? no WHAT THIS PRODUCES A sentence explaining the stress window on first use. A sentence telling her what appendix B is for. The finding in the first paragraph, because she will not reach section 4. Three concrete changes from one act of naming, and none of them would have followed from "write clearly". THE TRAP Do not name the most expert reader, which is the temptation, because they will not force any of this. Name the least expert reader whose understanding you actually need, which in a governance context is usually the person with the vote rather than the person with the expertise.

Why this course keeps using mechanical checks

Every diagnostic in modules 2 to 5 that examines a property of a sequence is mechanical, and it is worth collecting them now that the reason has a name. The subject-string extraction covers the page so your knowledge cannot supply the missing topic. The sentence-opening audit shows six words per sentence so your knowledge cannot supply the missing antecedent. The length series turns rhythm into numbers so your ear cannot supply the emphasis you intended. The because-but-so test forces you to name a relation rather than feel it.

All four are workarounds for the same problem. They do not ask you to read more carefully, because careful reading is exactly the activity the curse corrupts. They bypass comprehension, which is why they feel oddly indirect and why they work when critical reading does not.

Which gives a general test for any writing advice you meet. If it asks you to notice something about your own draft by reading it, be sceptical, because your reading is compromised. If it asks you to extract, count, cover, or hand it to someone else, it has taken the curse seriously.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The expertise paradox, and what to do about it institutionally

Other things equal, the more expert the author, the worse the documentation. That follows directly from the three mechanisms: expertise is chunking, abstraction and functional fixity, so the properties that make someone the right person to build a model make them the wrong person to document it unaided.

This is not a reason to have models documented by people who do not understand them, which produces a different and worse failure. It is a reason to treat documentation as requiring two people: one who knows the model and one who does not, with the second having authority to say that a passage does not work. The second person's confusion is the measuring instrument, and an institution that treats their confusion as a deficiency has broken the only instrument available.

There is a practical version of this that costs almost nothing. When a reviewer says they did not follow something, record what they did not follow rather than explaining it to them verbally. The verbal explanation repairs their understanding and leaves the document unchanged, which is the commonest way this information gets destroyed. What you want is the location of the failure, and you already have it.

Why effort is the wrong response

The curse is not a failure of empathy, care or diligence, and treating it as one produces guilt without improvement. A writer who tries harder to imagine their reader is using the faculty that has the melody stuck in it, and concentration does not remove the melody.

That is why every remedy in step 4 that works is procedural rather than attitudinal. Unpacking chunks is a rule. Supplying an instance is a rule. Naming a reader is an act. Handing the draft to somebody is an act. None of them requires you to succeed at the impossible task of not knowing what you know, which is what "consider your reader" asks for.

The corollary is about how to give feedback to others. Telling a colleague to think about their audience is asking them to do the thing they cannot do. Telling them that a specific phrase decomposes into four concepts for a reader without their background is actionable, and it hands them one of the mechanical remedies rather than an aspiration.

The honest limit

Pinker's remedies, and this lecture's, are palliative. You cannot simulate ignorance, and every technique here is either a rule that partly substitutes for the simulation or a way of borrowing somebody else's ignorance. There is no version of this where you become able to read your own prose as a novice.

That has one consequence worth accepting rather than resisting. If a document genuinely must be understood by non-experts, and the stakes are real, then testing it on one is not a nice-to-have but the only method that addresses the problem. Karen Schriver's work, cited in module 4's lecture 8, found that reader testing outperforms every checklist and every formula, and this lecture explains why: the checklists are all workarounds for a faculty that testing does not need.

Most professional documents will not be tested, for reasons of time and awkwardness rather than principle. Knowing that the untested document rests on a judgement your expertise makes unreliable is not the same as fixing it, and it is better than believing the judgement was sound.

Common failure Concluding that you should write for the least expert possible reader. That produces documents padded with explanation nobody needed and is a different failure, not a cure. The named-reader technique is specific for a reason: you want the least expert reader whose understanding you actually need, and identifying that person is a judgement about the document's purpose rather than an exercise in caution.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Name a specific real reader for a document you are writing, in the detail step 4 uses. List every question about their knowledge that the naming made answerable, and the concrete changes that follow.
  2. Name the three mechanisms and give a domain instance of each.
  3. Why does functional fixity mean a verification path can be present in a document and absent for its reader? What does that imply about lecture 2's precondition?
  4. Rank the remedies. Which is best, which is actively harmful, and why is simplifying the vocabulary misdirected?
  5. Why does "explain it more" produce padding rather than explanation?
  6. State the expertise paradox and the institutional remedy. What is the commonest way the relevant information gets destroyed?
  7. Why is effort the wrong response? What follows for how you give feedback to a colleague?
  8. Give the general test for any piece of writing advice that follows from taking the curse seriously.
