Module 6 · Writing track

Style as a stand

Classic prose, register and voice. Style is not decoration applied to content; it is a set of prior commitments about what can be known and how language relates to it. Seven lectures on choosing those commitments deliberately.

What this module is

Thomas and Turner make an argument that reframes everything in the preceding five modules. A style, they say, is a stand on six questions: whether truth can be known, what presentation is for, what scene the writing implies, who the cast are, what thought is, and what language can do. Classic style takes a particular set of positions on all six. Truth is knowable, the writer has already seen the thing, language is adequate to expressing it, and the implied scene is one competent person showing something to another who could go and verify it directly. The field guide is the exemplary case.

That framing matters because it explains why so much institutional and academic prose is bad in a systematic rather than a random way. It is written in what Thomas and Turner call the reflexive style, which takes the opposite stand: truth is contested, the writer's own uncertainty is part of the subject, and language is a problem rather than an instrument. Written deliberately, that is a legitimate style. Written by default, because everyone in the department writes that way, it produces prose in which nobody can find a claim.

The module then handles the two questions that follow. Register asks what the situation demands, and Halliday's field, tenor and mode give a way of answering it that is more useful than a list of formality levels. Voice asks what is left that is yours, and this module treats it honestly as the least theorised topic in the field. The closing lecture deals with measurement, and it is largely a warning.

Prerequisites

  • Module 2: the clear sentence. The question of which style is meaningless before you can execute one reliably.
  • Module 5: concision, rhythm and grace. Style differences show up in rhythm and diction as much as in structure.

Module sequence

Part 1 · The framework (lectures 1 to 3)

Style as commitment, the classic stand, and the alternatives you may be writing in without having chosen to.

  • builtLecture 1: style as a set of prior decisions. Thomas and Turner's six elements: truth, presentation, scene, cast, thought and language. Why two writers following identical clarity advice can produce prose that reads nothing alike.
  • builtLecture 2: classic style. Truth is knowable, the writer has already seen it, language is adequate to it, and the reader could verify it. Two competent equals in conversation; the field guide as model; the characteristic moves that follow.
  • builtLecture 3: the styles classic style is not. Practical, plain, contemplative, romantic, prophetic, oratorical and reflexive. Why institutional and academic writing defaults to the reflexive, what that default costs, and the cases where it is the right choice.

Part 2 · The expert's problem (lecture 4)

The single largest cause of bad writing by people who know what they are talking about.

  • builtLecture 4: the curse of knowledge. Pinker's diagnosis: a difficulty in imagining what it is like not to know what you know. The three mechanisms, abstraction, chunking and functional fixity, and remedies that work, including naming a specific reader and testing on a non-expert.

Part 3 · Situation, self, and measurement (lectures 5 to 7)

What the context demands, what remains yours, and what a readability score can actually tell you.

  • builtLecture 5: register, field, tenor and mode. Halliday's three contextual variables and the linguistic features each selects. Keeping the context and the register distinct, which students routinely fail to do, and Biber's corpus evidence that register variation is systematic and multidimensional rather than a matter of taste.
  • builtLecture 6: voice. Characteristic diction, habitual sentence shapes, stance towards the reader, recurring moves. Whether voice can be taught at all, and the case for imitation as the mechanism by which it is actually acquired.
  • builtLecture 7: plain English and its measurement. The movement's genuine empirical support, then Flesch Reading Ease and Flesch-Kincaid: what they compute, why a meaningless sentence of short words scores well, why medical jargon scores as easy, and why optimising against them produces choppy incoherent prose. A smoke alarm, never a target.
