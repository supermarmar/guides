Register, field, tenor and mode

Register is not style, and it is not a formality dial. Halliday's three variables say what a situation selects, and Biber's corpus evidence shows the variation is multidimensional, which means module 1's five-point formality scale was a simplification this lecture has to correct.

Lecture 5 of 7 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: Halliday & Hasan (1985); Biber (1988)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Halliday describes a situation with three variables. Field is what is going on: the subject matter and the social activity in progress. Tenor is the relationship between the participants: their relative status, their social distance, and how much each is committed to. Mode is the part language itself is playing: written or spoken, monologue or exchange, and whether the language constitutes the activity or merely accompanies it.

Register is the configuration of linguistic features that a situation selects. That wording matters, because register is often used loosely to mean formality, and it is not: field selects vocabulary and the kinds of process you name, tenor selects mood, modality and person, and mode selects lexical density, cohesion and information structure. Three variables, three families of consequence.

The distinction from style is the point of the lecture. Style, on lecture 1's account, is a stand you take on six questions, and you can take a different one. Register is what the situation gives you, and you cannot simply decide to write a board paper in the register of an email. You choose a style; you inhabit a register. They interact and they are different questions, and conflating them is the most common error in this area.

Feynman test Three questions decide how you will sound before you choose anything. What is happening. Who you are talking to and how far away they are. And whether the talking is the event or just goes alongside it. Change any one and the language changes, whether or not you intended it, which is why the same finding sounds so different in an email and in a submission.
What is beyond the source Halliday supplies the three variables and Biber the corpus evidence. What this lecture adds is the explicit correction of module 1's lecture 2, which taught Joos's five-point formality scale and which Biber's multidimensional findings show to be too coarse. That is self-correction rather than exposition, and the reason for making it here rather than quietly is the same reason lecture 1 declared the course's stylistic commitment.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that it separates two questions writers routinely muddle, with a practical consequence. If you dislike how your validation reports sound, you need to know whether the problem is a style you have drifted into, which you can change, or a register the situation imposes, which you largely cannot. Lecture 1's test distinguishes a chosen style from a habit. This lecture supplies the other half: some of what you dislike is the situation talking.

The second reason is that the three variables tell you which features to expect to change when a situation changes, which makes adaptation systematic rather than intuitive. Writing up the same finding for a committee and for a peer changes tenor and mode while leaving field alone, and the features that should differ are therefore modality, person and information structure, not the technical vocabulary. Writers who adapt by simplifying the vocabulary have changed the wrong variable, which is the misdiagnosis lecture 4 identified from another direction.

The third reason is Biber's finding, which has a direct bearing on how you read advice. Register variation is not one scale. A text can be highly informational and highly persuasive at once, or explicit in its reference and abstract in its content, and these vary independently. So a document is not simply more or less formal, and instructions pitched at a formality dial are describing one dimension of at least five.

And a reason about learning a genre. You cannot compute your register from the three variables; they tell you what will vary rather than what the right configuration is. The configuration is conventional, which means it is learned by reading the genre attentively, and lecture 6 argues that imitation is how that actually happens.

Step 3

The mental model

The three variables with what each selects, then the same finding in two registers with the variables that changed marked.

The note about vocabulary is the practically useful part. Field is the variable that selects technical terms, and field is the one that did not change between these two texts, so the terms should not change either. Writers adapting a document for a different audience routinely reach for the vocabulary first, which alters field when the actual change was in tenor and mode, and the result is a text that reads as condescending while remaining structurally unadapted.

Field
What is going on: the subject matter and the social activity. Selects technical vocabulary and the kinds of process the language names. The variable that changes least when you rewrite for a new audience.
Tenor
The relationship between participants: status, social distance, and how much each is committed to. Selects mood, modality, hedging and person. Where module 5's hedge calibration is actually determined.
Mode
The part language is playing: written or spoken, monologue or exchange, read through or looked up. Selects lexical density, grammatical intricacy, cohesion and information structure.
Register
The configuration of linguistic features a situation selects. Not the situation itself, which is the context, and not a position on a formality scale, which is one dimension of several.
Step 4

The detail

Correcting module 1

Module 1's lecture 2 taught Joos's five registers, from frozen through formal, consultative and casual to intimate, and treated formality as a single dial with five settings. Biber's corpus work shows that is too coarse, and the correction is worth making explicitly.

Biber applied factor analysis to sixty-seven linguistic features across a large corpus of spoken and written genres, and found that the features do not load onto one factor. They load onto several, each grouping features that co-occur. The dimensions he identified include an involved-against-informational contrast, a narrative-against-non-narrative one, an explicit-against-situation-dependent contrast in how reference is handled, an overt-expression-of-persuasion dimension, and an abstract-against-non-abstract one.

The consequence is that texts vary along several axes at once and can be high on one and low on another. A regulatory submission is highly informational, highly abstract, explicit in its reference, and low on overt persuasion. A sales memo about the same subject might be equally informational and high on persuasion. On a single formality dial those two would sit near each other and they are linguistically quite different.

JOOS AGAINST BIBER, ON THE SAME QUESTION QUESTION: how should this document sound? JOOS'S ANSWER (module 1, lecture 2) Pick one of five: frozen, formal, consultative, casual, intimate. A validation report is formal. Useful for: spotting gross errors of distance. A report written casually or an email written frozen are both diagnosable on this scale, and that is a real gain. Fails at: everything finer. It cannot tell you that your report should be informational and abstract while being low on persuasion, because those are separate dimensions and "formal" collapses them. BIBER'S ANSWER Locate the document on several dimensions independently. informational? high abstract? high explicit reference? high, since the reader cannot ask overt persuasion? low in findings, higher in recommendations narrative? low, except in an incident report Now the recommendations section is allowed to differ from the findings section on one dimension while matching on the rest, which is what module 6's lecture 3 said about mixing styles, arrived at from the register side. WHICH TO USE Joos for a quick check that you have not badly misjudged distance. Biber's framing when you need to know why two documents that are both "formal" do not sound alike. THE HONEST STATUS OF MODULE 1'S LECTURE 2 It taught a simplification without flagging it as one. The five clocks are a serviceable teaching device and they are not the state of the evidence, and this is the kind of thing module 1's own lecture 7 said should be declared.

Analysing a document type

The three variables are most useful applied to a document type rather than to a single text, because they explain why a genre sounds the way it does and which of its features are negotiable.

DocumentField, tenor, modeWhat that selects, and what is negotiable
Four document types from this collection's domain
Validation report Field: model risk assessment. Tenor: second line to first line and to a committee, asymmetric, high commitment. Mode: written, monologic, sampled rather than read. Selects technical vocabulary, declarative mood, low person, high lexical density, and heavy signposting because it is sampled. Negotiable: the hedging level, which writers treat as fixed and which tenor only partly determines.
Model methodology document Field: how a model works. Tenor: expert to future expert, near-symmetric. Mode: written, looked up rather than read through. Selects very high lexical density, minimal narrative, and a structure organised for retrieval. The looked-up mode is what licenses deep numbering, which module 4's lecture 2 otherwise warned against.
Board paper Field: a decision. Tenor: management to board, asymmetric in the other direction, very high commitment. Mode: written, read once, briefly. Selects low lexical density despite a technical field, because mode overrides: a reader with four minutes cannot unpack noun phrases. This is the one case where field and mode genuinely conflict.
Regulatory submission Field: compliance with a standard. Tenor: firm to supervisor, asymmetric, maximum commitment. Mode: written, read adversarially and archived. Selects the highest explicit-reference setting available: nothing may be assumed, every term defined, every claim sourced. Almost nothing is negotiable, and this is the genre closest to Joos's frozen register.

The board paper row is the interesting one because it identifies a genuine conflict rather than a configuration. Field pushes towards technical density and mode pushes against it, and the resolution is not a compromise but a decision about which variable wins. In a four-minute read, mode wins, which is why a board paper on a technical subject must unpack its chunks in a way a methodology document need not. That is lecture 4's chunking remedy arriving as a register requirement.

Keeping context and register apart

Halliday's distinction is between the situation and the language that situation selects, and collapsing them produces a specific confusion worth naming. The context of a validation report includes the model, the committee, the reporting timetable and the regulatory obligation. The register is the set of linguistic features that context selects: declarative mood, high density, defined terms, low person.

The confusion arises when someone says a document has "the wrong register" and means that the situation has been misjudged. Those are different claims with different remedies. If the features are wrong for the situation, you change the writing. If the situation has been misjudged, no amount of rewriting helps, because you are writing the wrong document: a paper addressed to a committee that is not the decision-maker will not be fixed by adjusting its modality.

The practical test is to state the three variables explicitly before revising. If you cannot say who the reader is, what activity the document is part of, and whether it will be read or consulted, the problem is upstream of the prose, and lecture 4's named-reader technique is the tool for the first of those three.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Why you cannot compute a register

The three variables tell you what will vary and not what the correct configuration is, and it is worth being clear that this is a limitation rather than an incompleteness in the account. Register configurations are conventional. There is no derivation from "asymmetric tenor" to "avoid the first person"; that connection holds in some professional cultures and not others, and it has changed within living memory in academic writing.

So the framework is diagnostic rather than generative. It tells you that a change in tenor should show up in mood, modality and person, which is genuinely useful when adapting a document. It cannot tell you what your firm's regulatory submissions should look like, and the only way to know that is to read a number of them attentively.

That is an unsatisfying answer and it is the honest one. It also explains why lecture 6 treats imitation as the mechanism by which register competence is actually acquired, and why module 7's material on genre is the better framework for asking what a document type requires. Halliday tells you the dimensions; the genre tells you the coordinates.

Register as a constraint you can partly negotiate

The lecture has treated register as given, and the truth is more interesting. Some features a situation selects are genuinely fixed, and some are conventions that a competent writer can push against successfully.

Fixed: the technical vocabulary follows the field, and abandoning it loses the terms of art. The explicit-reference requirement in a regulatory submission follows from a reader who cannot ask questions. The information structure of a sampled document follows from sampling.

Negotiable: the hedging level, which module 5's lecture 2 showed is set by institutional risk transfer rather than by evidence. The presence of the first person, which many house styles forbid and which is a bin two convention in module 1's terms. The lexical density, which can be reduced substantially without losing technical precision, as lecture 4's chunk-unpacking shows.

The distinction matters because writers treat the whole configuration as fixed and therefore change nothing. A validation report whose hedges are calibrated to the evidence rather than to the author's exposure is still recognisably a validation report; it has departed from the conventional register on one negotiable dimension. That is available, it is worth doing, and it is a different act from ignoring the register altogether.

Where this leaves the module

Five lectures have separated three things that are constantly conflated. Style is a stand you take on six questions, and you can take another. Register is what the situation selects, partly fixed and partly conventional. And the curse of knowledge is why your judgement about your reader, which both style and register depend on, is unreliable.

What remains is voice, which is the residue: what stays identifiable when a writer changes style and register. Lecture 6 treats it as the least theorised topic in the field, argues that it is acquired by imitation rather than taught, and makes the case that in professional writing it matters far less than the attention it receives. Then lecture 7 returns to measurement and closes the module on a warning.

Common failure Adapting a document for a new audience by changing the vocabulary. Field selects vocabulary, and when you write for a different reader the variable that changed was tenor or mode, not field. The features that should change are modality, person, density and structure. Changing the words instead produces a document that reads as talking down while remaining exactly as hard to follow, which is the commonest failed adaptation in professional writing.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take one finding and write it for two situations, as step 3 does, then list which of the three variables changed and which linguistic features changed as a result. Confirm that your technical vocabulary is identical in both.
  2. Define field, tenor and mode, and name the features each selects.
  3. Distinguish register from style. Which do you choose and which do you inhabit?
  4. What does Biber's factor analysis show, and why does it make module 1's five-point formality scale a simplification? What is the scale still good for?
  5. In the board paper row, field and mode conflict. State the conflict and which variable wins, and connect it to lecture 4.
  6. Distinguish "the wrong register" from "the situation has been misjudged". Why do they have different remedies?
  7. Why can a register not be computed from the three variables? What follows for how register competence is acquired?
  8. Give two features of a validation report's register that are fixed and two that are negotiable. What makes the second group negotiable?
