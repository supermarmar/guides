Classic style

One competent person showing another something they could go and see for themselves. That last clause is the whole style, and it carries a promise: if your reader cannot go and see, the symmetry is asserted rather than real, and asserting it is a way of borrowing the authority of verifiability without supplying it.

Lecture 2 of 7 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: Thomas & Turner, Clear and Simple as the Truth (1994)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Classic style's scene is a conversation in which one person says to another: look at this. The writer has already seen the thing and is not searching. The reader is a competent equal, not a student. Language is a window onto the thing rather than a subject in its own right. And the reader could, in principle, go and verify what is being shown, which is what makes the whole arrangement honest rather than merely confident.

The moves follow from the scene. You present conclusions rather than searches, so no process narration. You name actors and actions, because what you are pointing at is something that happened. You use concrete nouns, because you are pointing. You do not discuss your own writing, because the window is not the view. And you do not appeal to authority, because the reader can check.

The presumption in that last clause is where this lecture parts company with its source. Thomas and Turner treat verifiability as part of the fiction the style maintains. In a professional document it is not a fiction: either your reader can reach the evidence or they cannot, and if they cannot, classic style's confident pointing is borrowing the credibility of a check that is unavailable. The remedy is to make the check real, which is cheaper than it sounds and is what model documentation standards already require.

Feynman test A good field guide does not tell you to trust it. It says: the wing bar is white, the bill is short, look at the bird. You can go and look, and if the book is wrong you will find out. That is the whole posture, and it is why the prose can afford to be so plain: it is not doing any persuading, because the bird is doing it.
What is beyond the source Thomas and Turner describe the style and recommend it. The verifiability argument in steps 3 and 5 is this lecture's departure from them: they treat the reader's ability to verify as a stance the prose adopts, and this lecture treats it as a factual precondition that professional documents can satisfy or fail. That turns a stylistic description into a testable requirement, and it produces an obligation, in step 4, that their account does not impose.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that classic style is the right stand for most of the documents this collection concerns, and knowing why makes the advice in modules 2 to 5 defensible rather than arbitrary. A validation finding is something you established and the reader could check against the data. A capital calculation is something you did and someone else could redo. In both cases the classic scene is an accurate description of the situation, so writing in it is honest rather than merely stylish.

The second reason is the failure mode, and it is specific enough to check for. When the reader cannot verify, classic style's plainness stops being a consequence of the situation and becomes a costume: prose that sounds like pointing while actually asking for trust. A confident, actor-full, unhedged sentence about a result nobody outside the team can reproduce is doing something the style was not built for, and doing it more effectively than a hedged version would.

The third reason is that the remedy is cheap and already required. Model documentation standards, reproducibility conventions and audit requirements all exist to make results checkable, and classic style gives you a writerly reason to comply with them: the style you want to write in has a precondition, and the precondition is the same thing your governance framework asks for. That alignment is worth exploiting.

And a reason about the reader's experience. Classic style is pleasant to read because it does not ask anything of the reader beyond attention: no allowance for the writer's uncertainty, no patience with process, no deference. Prose that treats a reader as a competent equal is received as respectful, and that effect is real even when the content is unwelcome, which is why a well-written finding of failure lands better than a hedged one.

Step 3

The mental model

The scene, with the verification path that makes it honest, and the same scene with the path missing.

The first repair is the one to reach for and it is almost free. "The arrears series is in appendix B" is eight words, it converts an assertion into a demonstration, and it is what a validation report should contain regardless. Notice that this is a case where a stylistic commitment and a governance requirement point at the same thing, which is unusual enough to be worth exploiting when arguing for either.

The classic scene
One competent person showing another something they could go and see. Not a lecture, not a defence, not a performance. The symmetry between the parties is the defining feature.
The verification path
The route by which a reader could check what you assert: a data reference, a reproducible method, an accessible source. Present in an honest classic document and absent in a document merely written in the style.
Borrowed credibility
The effect of classic style's plainness when the verification path is missing: the prose signals that checking is possible and no checking is available, so trust is obtained by form rather than by evidence.

The seventh row is worth noticing because it is the move professional documents find hardest. Institutional writing appeals to authority constantly: the committee approved it, the policy requires it, the external adviser confirmed it. Classic style has no use for any of that, because a reader who can check does not need to be told who agreed. Where an appeal to authority is genuinely load-bearing, that is a signal the verification path is missing and step 3's second repair applies.

Where classic style is the wrong stand

Four cases, and they are worth knowing precisely, because in each the classic posture would be a misdescription rather than a stylistic misfire.

The first is genuinely uncertain material. If you do not know the answer, prose asserting that you have seen the thing is false. The honest form states the uncertainty as its own claim, which module 5's lecture 2 recommended, and that is a partial departure from the classic stand rather than a hedge within it.

The second is exploratory writing, where the thinking is happening in the prose. Module 5's lecture 7 established that this is real and that the classic stand presumes thought is complete before writing. A research note is legitimately contemplative, and forcing it into classic style produces false confidence.

The third is a document whose subject is methodological difficulty. If what you have to say is that the question is harder than it looks, the writing is about the limits of the available methods, and language and method are then genuinely part of the subject. That is the one case where metadiscourse is content.

The fourth is the verifiability failure this lecture has been about, and it differs from the other three: it is not that classic style is inappropriate to the material but that a precondition is unmet, and it is usually fixable. The first three call for a different style; the fourth calls for an appendix.

The field guide test

A DIAGNOSTIC FOR YOUR OWN PROSE Ask of a passage: could this be a field guide entry? A field guide entry has four properties. 1. It names the thing. 2. It gives features you could check. 3. It does not tell you to trust it. 4. It does not describe the process of identification. APPLY TO A FINDING Fails: "Following extensive analysis, it was concluded that certain aspects of the downturn calibration may warrant enhancement, having regard to the observations set out below." 1. names nothing 2. offers nothing checkable 3. asks for trust in "extensive analysis" 4. describes the process throughout -> not a field guide entry. Reflexive. Passes: "The downturn calibration understates the peak loss rate by 40 basis points. Its stress window spans 2011 to 2023, in which arrears peaked at 1.4 per cent; in 2008 they reached 3.1 per cent. Both series are in appendix B." 1. names the calibration and the defect 2. two checkable numbers and a source 3. no appeal to the writer's diligence 4. no process -> field guide entry. WHY THIS TEST IS USEFUL It bundles most of modules 2 to 5 into one question you can ask quickly, and it makes the verifiability requirement explicit, because property 2 fails if there is nothing the reader could check.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Classic style's characteristic abuse

Every style has a failure mode that is specific to it rather than a general falling-off. Module 1's lecture 1 named obscurity as the abuse available to nominalised prose: a sentence with no actor cannot be traced to anyone. Classic style's abuse runs the other way, and it is worth naming with the same directness.

Classic style makes unverified claims sound verified. Its plainness is a signal, learned by readers from prose where the signal was honest, that the writer is pointing at something available for inspection. Deploy the signal without the availability and you have obtained assent by form. That is more effective than hedged overclaiming, because the reader is not weighing a modal verb; they are receiving a demonstration that is not one.

The point is sharper in a professional setting than in the literary one Thomas and Turner have in view. A confident, actor-full, specific sentence about a result nobody outside your team can reproduce is doing exactly what module 4's lecture 1 warned about with answer-first structure, and doing it with the additional force of a style that presents itself as disinterested. The two abuses compound, and a well-written executive summary asserting an unreproducible number is the most persuasive dishonest document a professional can produce.

The defence is the requirement in step 4's last row and it is not onerous. Supply the path. If you cannot, say so. The style then does what it claims, which is the only condition under which its plainness is earned rather than borrowed.

Why this style suits this collection

Classic style fits credit risk documentation unusually well, and the fit is not a coincidence. The subject matter consists largely of things that happened to portfolios, recorded in data that exists, analysed by methods that can be rerun. The classic scene, one person showing another something they could check, is a nearly literal description of what a validation report is for.

That has a consequence worth acting on. Where a document in this domain cannot be written classically, the obstacle is usually not the subject but a gap in the work: the data is not referenced, the method is not reproducible, the number cannot be traced. So the inability to write a finding as a field guide entry is diagnostic of the analysis rather than of the prose, which is the same relationship modules 2 and 3 kept finding between a stalled revision and incomplete work.

The stronger version of the claim, which is worth stating because it is checkable: if you cannot write your finding in four sentences that a competent colleague could verify, you probably do not yet have the finding. That is an uncomfortable test and it is the one classic style implies.

What the style does not give you

Two honest limits, since a lecture recommending a style should say what it forgoes.

Classic style has no way to be tentative gracefully. Its instruments are assertion and silence, so material that is genuinely half-known either gets asserted too strongly or omitted. The reflexive style handles that better, which is a real advantage rather than a concession, and a document with substantial genuinely-uncertain content may need to borrow from it.

And classic style is poor at bringing a reader along who is not already a competent equal. Its cast presumes they are, so it has no teaching apparatus: no scaffolding, no worked build-up, no anticipation of confusion. For a mixed readership, or for a paper that must inform as well as report, the practical style that lecture 3 covers is often the better stand, and this course's own lectures are practical rather than classic for exactly that reason.

Common failure Adopting classic style's confidence without its precondition, which is this lecture's whole subject and worth restating as an instruction. Before writing a finding plainly and without hedges, check that a reader could establish it independently from what your document supplies. If they could, write it plainly. If they could not, either fix that or say what they are relying on. Confidence without a verification path is not classic style; it is the sound of it.
