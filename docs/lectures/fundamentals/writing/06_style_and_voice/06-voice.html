Lectures / Writing / Style and voice

Voice

The least theorised topic in the field, and this lecture says so rather than compensating. Voice is probably not teachable directly, it is acquired by imitation, and in governance documents a recognisable one is closer to a liability than an asset.

Lecture 6 of 7 Reading time, 25 minutes Source: Aphthonius and the progymnasmata tradition; Sword (2017)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Voice is what remains identifiable about a writer when style and register change. It consists of characteristic diction, habitual sentence shapes, a stance towards the reader, and a small set of moves the writer keeps making. You can recognise a colleague's writing across an email and a board paper, and what you are recognising is voice rather than style or register, both of which have changed between the two.

That is about as far as the theory goes, and the honest thing to say is that this is the thinnest material in the course. There is no equivalent of Halliday's three variables or Thomas and Turner's six questions. What exists is a body of practitioner reflection, considerable disagreement about whether voice can be taught at all, and one mechanism with fifteen centuries of evidence behind it, which is imitation.

The claim worth taking from the lecture is contrarian. In professional writing voice matters far less than the attention it receives, and in a governance document a recognisable voice is closer to a liability than an asset, because the authority of a validation report rests partly on seeming to report rather than to express. Voice is worth cultivating for the writing that carries your name in public and is close to irrelevant for the writing that carries your firm's.

Feynman test You can tell who wrote an email without looking at the sender. That recognisable thing is voice, and it survives them switching from a chatty note to a formal submission. Notice that you have never once wanted a regulatory return to have it.
What is beyond the source The claim that voice is a liability in governance documents is this lecture's, and it runs against the general enthusiasm for voice in the writing literature. So is the observation in step 5 that a professional's voice is better understood as a pattern of recurring judgements than as a personality, which makes it a by-product of expertise rather than a thing to work on. The imitation mechanism is ancient and not this lecture's; the warning about which models a technical reader will absorb by default is.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is negative and it is the most useful thing here. A great deal of writing advice, and nearly all writing instruction outside the professional context, treats finding your voice as the goal. If you are writing validation reports and committee papers, that advice is misdirected, and knowing so saves you from pursuing something your documents do not want. The effort is better spent on the judgement that lecture 5 called the negotiable part of a register.

The second reason is that imitation is the mechanism by which register competence is acquired, and lecture 5 left that question open. You cannot compute what a regulatory submission should look like; you learn it by reading them. That is imitation whether or not it is deliberate, and making it deliberate is the only lever available.

The third reason is the reading-diet problem, which is specific and consequential for this audience. If imitation is how competence is acquired, then what you read shapes what you write, and a professional whose reading consists of regulation, validation reports and committee papers will acquire the Official Style that module 2's lecture 8 diagnosed. That is not a failure of application; it is imitation working correctly on bad models.

And a reason about this course. Every lecture's first recall exercise begins "imitate before you compose", and that has been the progymnasmata tradition all along without saying so. The exercises were not a device for making the recall sections concrete. They were the answer to this lecture's question, applied fifty times before the question was asked.

Step 3

The mental model

Three things that vary independently, and what voice is left holding.

Notice that the three concepts are ordered by how much control you have, and voice comes last. That ordering is the argument for spending your attention on the first two. Style is a decision you can make this afternoon. Register has a negotiable part, identified in lecture 5, that you can push on. Voice emerges over years from what you read and what you keep deciding, and there is no intervention that changes it quickly.

Voice
What remains identifiable about a writer across changes of style and register. Diction, habitual sentence shapes, stance towards the reader, and recurring moves. Identified by its persistence rather than by any single feature.
Imitation
Deliberate copying of a chosen model, then writing your own content in its shape, then varying. The mechanism the classical tradition used for fifteen centuries and the only one with substantial evidence behind it.
The reading-diet problem
Imitation operates on whatever you read, so a professional's default models are the documents their job supplies. Explains why institutional registers persist unchanged across firms and generations.
Step 4

The detail

Imitation, done deliberately

THE PROCEDURE, AND WHY EACH STEP IS THERE 1. CHOOSE A MODEL A specific passage by a specific writer, not a genre. "I want to write like a good validation report" is not a model; a particular two-paragraph passage is. Criteria: it should do something you cannot yet do, in a register close enough to yours to be usable. A field guide entry, a passage of Orwell's reportage, a page of Minto, a paragraph from a well-written regulatory consultation response. 2. COPY IT OUT BY HAND Slow and apparently pointless. What it does is force attention on the choices at the scale you would otherwise skip: where the commas are, how long the units are, which word came first. 3. WRITE YOUR OWN CONTENT IN ITS SHAPE Same number of sentences, same shapes, same order of moves, entirely different subject. This is the step that teaches, and it is what "imitate before you compose" has been asking for in every lecture of this course. 4. THEN VARY Once the shape is available to you, depart from it. Imitation that stops at step 3 produces pastiche, which is why the classical sequence had graded exercises rather than a single copying task. WORKED, ON A FIELD GUIDE ENTRY AS MODEL Model: "Small, dark, and restless. Feeds low in dense cover, rarely still. The white wing bar is diagnostic; no other warbler here shows one. Song a thin descending trill." Shape: four units. Three adjectives. A behaviour. A diagnostic feature with the alternative excluded. A signature. Your content, same shape: "Short, benign, and unrepresentative. Spans 2011 to 2023, omitting every stress. The absence of 2008 is diagnostic; no other window in use excludes it. Peak arrears 1.4 per cent." What the exercise taught: that a diagnostic feature can be stated by excluding the alternatives, which is a move you probably do not have and which is exactly what a validation finding needs.

How much voice a professional document will take

DocumentVoice availableWhether you want it
Ranked by whether a recognisable voice helps
Regulatory submission Almost none No. The register is near-frozen and the document speaks for the firm. A recognisable individual voice in a submission is a defect.
Validation report A little: diction, sentence shapes, what you choose to emphasise Mostly no. The authority of the document rests on seeming to report rather than to express, and a distinctive voice works against that impression.
Board paper Some, in the framing and the emphasis Marginally. What reads as voice here is usually judgement about what matters, which is worth having and is not really voice.
Where it earns its keep
Internal note arguing for a position Considerable Yes. This is writing that persuades, and a reader who recognises a writer they trust is being influenced by voice legitimately.
Teaching material, guidance, articles Full Yes, and it is most of what makes such material readable. These lectures have a voice, deliberately.
Anything published under your name Full Yes. Here the writing is partly the point, and voice is what distinguishes your paper from the eleven others on the subject.

The split is clean and worth internalising, because it tells you where to spend effort. Documents that speak for an institution do not want your voice and are not improved by it. Documents that carry your name benefit from it substantially. Most professionals write far more of the first kind and receive advice aimed at the second, which is the mismatch this lecture exists to name.

Can it be taught?

The honest answer has three parts. Directly, probably not: there is no exercise that installs a voice, and instruction that claims to be doing so is usually teaching a register or a style and calling it voice. Indirectly, yes, by the imitation route, which is slow and works. And partly it is not acquired at all but revealed, since the diction you reach for and the shapes you fall into are already there and become visible once you can control them.

Sword's interview work with academic writers found far more variety in how they work than any single prescription accommodates, and no common route to a distinctive voice. That is a negative finding and it is informative: if there were a teachable method, a study of successful writers should have found it, and what it found instead was that they read a great deal and had all imitated somebody.

Which leaves imitation as the recommendation not because the evidence for it is strong but because it is the only candidate with fifteen centuries of use and no serious rival. That is a weaker endorsement than most writing books give it, and it is what the evidence supports.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

A professional's voice is a pattern of judgements

The literature treats voice as an expression of personality, and for a novelist that is probably right. For a professional writer it is a misdescription, and a better account is available: what colleagues recognise as your voice is mostly the pattern of your recurring judgements about what matters.

Consider what is actually recognisable in a colleague's papers. That they always quantify. That they always name who is accountable. That they concede the strongest objection before making their case. That they say what they do not know. Those look like stylistic habits and they are substantive decisions, made repeatedly, about what a document owes its reader. Someone who consistently states the magnitude rather than writing "material" has a recognisable voice, and what is recognisable is a standard rather than a personality.

That reframing has a practical consequence, and it is why this lecture spends so little time on cultivating voice as such. If your voice is your judgements, then it improves when your judgements improve, and it is not a separate project. The way to acquire a distinctive professional voice is to decide what you think a document owes a reader and then do it every time, which is what modules 2 to 5 have been about.

Why a voice can be a liability

This runs against the general enthusiasm and deserves the strongest version of the argument. A validation report's authority comes partly from an impression of disinterest: the reader believes it because it reads as a report of what was found rather than as an argument by someone with a position. A distinctive voice undermines that, because it makes the author visible, and a visible author invites the question of what they wanted.

That is not an argument for anonymous prose, which is the Official Style and which modules 2 to 5 spent their length attacking. Classic style has no distinctive voice and is not anonymous: it is plain, confident and unmarked, which is precisely why it reads as pointing rather than as expressing. The target is voice as a recognisable individual signature, not voice as the presence of a human being.

The distinction is worth an example. A report that names actors, quantifies, and states what is not known has a human being behind it and no signature. A report with a characteristic wry aside, a favourite construction and a recognisable rhythm has a signature, and in a governance context the signature is a cost. Save it for the material where being recognisably you is the point.

The thinnest lecture in the course, and why it is here

This lecture has less to offer than any other, and that is a fact about the subject rather than about the treatment. There is no framework, the evidence is thin, the one recommended mechanism is ancient and slow, and the main practical conclusion is that the topic matters less than you have been told.

It is included for two reasons. Voice is the third of three things constantly conflated, and lectures 1 and 5 would be incomplete without separating it from the other two. And the reading-diet problem is genuinely important for this audience: a professional who does not choose their models deliberately will acquire the register of the documents their job supplies, and that is the single most useful thing in the lecture.

If you take one action from it, make it that one. Choose two or three passages by writers you would like to resemble, in registers you could plausibly use, and work through the four-step procedure on them. Everything else here is description.

Common failure Pursuing voice in documents that do not want it, which usually shows up as a recognisable mannerism inserted into a report: an arch aside, a favourite metaphor, a signature construction. It reads as a writer enjoying themselves in a document whose reader wanted a finding, and it costs credibility in exactly the setting where credibility is the asset. The reverse failure, prose with no human being behind it, is worse and is not the remedy for this one.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Run the four-step procedure from step 4 on a model you choose deliberately: a field guide entry, a page of Minto, a passage of reportage. Report what move the exercise taught you that you did not previously have.
  2. Define voice by what identifies it. Why is it identified by persistence rather than by any single feature?
  3. Order style, register and voice by how much control you have. What follows about where to spend effort?
  4. State the reading-diet problem. Why does it explain the stability of institutional registers across firms and decades?
  5. Why does imitation stop at pastiche if you omit step 4? What did the classical tradition do about it?
  6. Give three document types where a recognisable voice is unwanted and three where it earns its keep. What distinguishes the two groups?
  7. State the reframing of a professional's voice as a pattern of judgements. What follows about how to acquire one?
  8. Distinguish voice as a signature from voice as the presence of a human being. Which does classic style have?
