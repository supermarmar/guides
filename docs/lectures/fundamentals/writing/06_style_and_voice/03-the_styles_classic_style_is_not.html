Lectures / Writing / Style and voice

The styles classic style is not

Six alternatives, and two of them matter more than the catalogue suggests. Most technical documentation should be practical rather than classic, and this course is written that way. And classic style with no verification path turns out to have a name in this taxonomy: it is prophetic.

Lecture 3 of 7 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: Thomas & Turner, Clear and Simple as the Truth (1994)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Thomas and Turner set classic style against six others. Plain style speaks for a community whose truths are held in common. Practical style serves a reader who needs to do something. Contemplative style shows a mind moving over a subject. Romantic style takes the writer's own sensibility as the point. Prophetic style claims access to truth the reader cannot check. Oratorical style aims to move an audience. Reflexive style treats truth as contested and language as a problem.

Two of the six do real work for anyone writing professionally. Practical style is the right stand for procedure documents, implementation guides, model user guides and teaching material, because its cast is someone who must accomplish something and its measure is whether they can. Classic style has no teaching apparatus at all, so where a document must inform as well as report, practical is the honest choice. These lectures are practical, not classic.

The other is prophetic style, and it matters for an unwelcome reason. Lecture 2 argued that classic style without a verification path obtains trust by form rather than by evidence, and called that borrowed credibility. This taxonomy supplies the proper name: a writer claiming truth the reader cannot check is writing prophetically. That is a legitimate style in its own tradition and it is not what a validation report should be, and the diagnosis is more useful than the accusation because it points at the missing appendix.

Feynman test Ask what your reader is supposed to do with the document. Verify it, if they want to: classic. Follow it: practical. Watch you think: contemplative. Believe you: prophetic. Be moved: oratorical. Understand that the question is hard: reflexive. Most bad professional writing is written by people who wanted the first and produced the fourth.
What is beyond the source Thomas and Turner supply the taxonomy. Two moves here are not theirs. Identifying unverifiable classic prose as prophetic style is this lecture's, and it converts lecture 2's ethical objection into a category error, which is easier to act on. And the claim that practical style is the correct stand for most technical documentation, including this course, is a judgement they do not make: they treat practical style briefly and are not much interested in it.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that a great deal of technical documentation is written in the wrong style, and the error is systematic. A model implementation guide is written classically, with confident disinterested pointing, when its reader needs to accomplish a task and would be better served by a document organised around what they must do. Classic style's refusal to teach is a feature in a field guide and a defect in a runbook.

The second reason is the prophetic diagnosis, which turns lecture 2's warning into something you can check. Asking whether your document is dishonest invites a defensive answer. Asking which of seven styles it is in produces an answer, and if the honest answer is prophetic, the remedy is obvious and technical rather than moral: supply the verification path or stop asserting.

The third reason is that most documents mix styles, and knowing the inventory lets you mix deliberately. A validation report can reasonably be classic in its findings, practical in its remediation section, and reflexive in a passage about a genuinely contested methodological choice. That is not inconsistency; it is three sections doing three jobs. What is inconsistent is drifting between stands without noticing, which is the usual case.

And a reason for reading rather than writing. Once you can name the stand a document takes, you can see what it is asking of you. A paper written oratorically wants you to act and is not primarily informing you. A paper written prophetically wants your trust and has not supplied grounds for it. Those are useful things to know before a committee discussion, and they are visible from the prose.

Step 3

The mental model

Seven styles, sorted by what the reader is supposed to do. The prophetic entry carries the warning from lecture 2.

The oratorical entry deserves a note because professionals are squeamish about it and the squeamishness costs them. A paper written to change a decision is doing oratorical work whether or not its author admits it, and the honest version is better than the disguise: a recommendation section that says plainly what it wants and why is more useful than the same content dressed as disinterested reporting. Module 7 treats persuasion properly, and the point here is only that pretending not to persuade is itself a rhetorical move.

Practical style
The stand of a document serving a reader who must accomplish something. Utility overrides elegance, the reader's task determines the structure, and teaching apparatus is appropriate. The right style for procedures, guides and instruction.
Prophetic style
Truth is available to the writer and not to the reader, so the reader's only option is belief. Legitimate in its own traditions. What classic style becomes when its verification path is absent.
Oratorical style
Addressed to an audience with a decision to make, and aiming to move them. Present in most recommendation sections and rarely acknowledged, which makes it harder to evaluate rather than absent.
Step 4

The detail

Practical style, and why technical documentation needs it

Practical style takes a stand that differs from classic on two of the six elements, and the differences are exactly what a task-oriented reader requires.

On cast, practical style is asymmetric in a specific and honest way: the writer knows how to do the thing and the reader does not yet, which is a different asymmetry from prophetic style's, because the reader will know afterwards. That licenses the teaching apparatus classic style forbids. You may anticipate confusion, order material by the reader's sequence rather than the subject's, repeat something important, and say what a step is for before saying what it is.

On presentation, the measure is whether the reader can do the thing, not whether the prose is clean. That reverses several priorities from earlier modules. Redundancy is acceptable where it prevents an error. A step may be stated twice if it is easy to miss. Numbered lists, which module 4's lecture 8 warned delete relations, are correct here because the relation genuinely is sequence.

CLASSIC AND PRACTICAL ON THE SAME MATERIAL CLASSIC · the finding "The downturn calibration understates the peak loss rate by 40 basis points. Its stress window spans 2011 to 2023, in which arrears peaked at 1.4 per cent against 3.1 per cent in 2008. Both series are in appendix B." Correct. The reader can check it. Nothing is explained because nothing needs to be: they are an equal. PRACTICAL · the remediation instruction "To rebuild the calibration: 1. Extend the stress window to 2007 to 2023. The purpose is to include a period in which arrears exceeded 3 per cent; 2008 is the only such period in the available history. 2. Re-derive the downturn LGD on the extended window. Expect a rise of roughly 40 basis points. A rise materially larger than that suggests the window extension has also changed the recovery lag assumptions, which it should not. 3. Reconcile the new estimate to the existing one and record the difference in the model change log." Also correct, and a different style. Note what it does that classic style would forbid: - says what step 1 is FOR before saying what it is - anticipates an error and tells the reader how to recognise it - gives an expected magnitude so the reader can self-check - numbers the steps, because the relation is sequence THE POINT Neither passage is better written. They are answering different questions about what the reader needs, and a document containing both should contain both styles.

Why this course is practical

These lectures have scaffolding, worked examples, anticipated objections, deliberate repetition of key ideas across modules, and a structure ordered by what a learner needs rather than by the subject's own logic. Classic style permits none of that. So the course recommends classic style for its readers' documents and is not written in it, which is coherent but should be declared, since lecture 1 made a point of declaring the other commitment.

The justification is the one in the figure: classic style's cast are competent equals, and a reader learning this material is not yet the writer's equal in it. Pretending otherwise would produce a set of terse field-guide entries about nominalisation and topic strings, correct and useless. The teaching apparatus is not a lapse from classic style; it is the practical stand doing its job.

One consequence worth noticing. If you write documentation that teaches, the advice in modules 2 to 5 needs adjusting rather than applying wholesale. Repetition is sometimes right. Metadiscourse that orients a learner is sometimes right. The point may legitimately come after a worked build-up. Those are practical-style choices and not failures of clarity, and lecture 1's table of what is and is not style-dependent is where to check which is which.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The taxonomy is not a natural kind

Seven styles is a suspiciously tidy number, and it is worth being clear that these categories were built to serve an argument. Thomas and Turner needed a set of contrasts that would make classic style's positions visible, and the taxonomy delivers that. It is not the result of surveying prose and finding seven clusters.

Two symptoms of the construction. The categories are unequal in size and importance: practical and reflexive describe enormous quantities of real writing, romantic and plain describe very little, and prophetic is defined largely by a property classic style lacks. And they are not exclusive: a document can be practical and reflexive at once, teaching a procedure while acknowledging that the method is contested, and the taxonomy has nowhere to put that.

What survives the objection is the six-question framework from lecture 1, which is genuinely general, and the specific contrasts that do work. Practical against classic is a real and useful distinction. Prophetic against classic is a real and useful distinction. Romantic against contemplative is a distinction between two things this collection's readers will never write. Take the framework and the two useful contrasts, and treat the rest as illustrative.

Mixing styles deliberately

Most substantial documents should contain more than one stand, and the failure is drifting rather than mixing. A validation report has a natural three-part shape: findings, which are classic and checkable; remediation, which is practical and instructs; and any genuinely contested methodological choice, which is reflexive and says so.

The discipline is to make the boundaries visible. A section that shifts from classic to practical should look different: the findings read as assertions with references, the remediation reads as numbered steps with purposes. A reader who can see the shift knows what is being asked of them in each part, and module 4's lecture 8 argued that the visual layer carries meaning, which is exactly what it is doing here.

What produces drift instead is writing the whole document in whichever stand the last paragraph established. That is the same accretion mechanism module 3's lecture 1 identified for topic strings, operating a level up, and the remedy is the same: decide before writing which sections are doing which job.

Where this leaves the module

Three lectures have established that style is a stand, that classic style is the right one for established findings, and that practical style is the right one for documents that teach or instruct. Two things remain unaddressed and they are the two hardest.

The first is that every stand presumes something about the reader's competence, and you are systematically wrong about it. Classic style's cast are equals, practical style's reader will become one, and both judgements are made by someone who cannot simulate not knowing what they know. Lecture 4 is about that, and it explains why the style you chose may be unavailable for reasons that have nothing to do with your intentions.

The second is register, which is not the same question as style and is constantly confused with it. Lecture 5 separates them.

Common failure Using the taxonomy to license a style you have drifted into. Lecture 1 gave the test and it applies here: a style is a choice only if you could have written the other way. "This section is reflexive" is a defensible claim if you could have written it classically and had a reason not to, and it is a rationalisation if the reflexive register is simply what your fingers produce. The seven names make the rationalisation easier and better dressed.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take one finding of your own and write it twice, once classically as a checkable assertion and once practically as an instruction someone could follow. Report what the practical version does that the classic one forbids.
  2. Name the seven styles and what each asks the reader to do with the document.
  3. What distinguishes classic from prophetic style? Why is that distinction more useful than an accusation of dishonesty?
  4. Give the two elements on which practical style differs from classic, and three things the difference licenses.
  5. Why is this course written in practical rather than classic style? What follows for someone writing documentation that teaches?
  6. Give two reasons the seven-style taxonomy is not a natural kind. What survives the objection?
  7. Describe the natural three-part style structure of a validation report, and the mechanism that produces drift instead.
  8. State the test that distinguishes a style you have chosen from one you have drifted into. Why do the seven names make the rationalisation easier?
