The plain-fancy axis

English gives you two or three words for most things, one Germanic and one Latinate, which is a register dial rather than a vice. And the real jargon problem is not technical vocabulary but vocabulary your reader cannot retrieve, which puts house acronyms below terms of art.

Lecture 6 of 7 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: Gowers, Plain Words; Cutts; Orwell
Step 1

The 30-second version

English carries a layered vocabulary from its history, so most concepts have a short Germanic word and a longer Latinate one, and often a third from French in between. Ask, question, interrogate. Rise, mount, ascend. Gut, entrail, intestine. The Germanic word is usually shorter, more concrete and more direct; the Latinate is longer, more abstract and more formal.

The default is the shorter word, and it is a default rather than a rule, with three named exceptions. Take the longer word when it is genuinely more precise, since "terminate" is not "end". Take it when it is the term of art, because a term of art must not be paraphrased. And take it when the register requires it, which module 6 already settled.

On jargon the useful reframing is that the problem is not technical vocabulary but unretrievable vocabulary. Among specialists, "point-in-time PD" saves a paragraph and is a service. The diagnostic question is not whether a term is technical but whether this reader can get at it, which sorts vocabulary by retrievability rather than by formality.

That sorting puts house acronyms in a worse position than technical terms, which is counter-intuitive and correct. "LGD" can be looked up by any professional in seconds. "MRC" cannot be looked up at all, by anyone outside the building, ever.

Feynman test Use the short word unless the long one is sharper, is the actual name of the thing, or the document demands formality. And stop asking whether a word is jargon. Ask whether your reader can find out what it means, because that is the only version of the question with an answer.
What is beyond the source The Germanic-Latinate layering is a standard fact about English lexis, and Gowers, Cutts and Orwell are cited as they stand. Two things are the lecture's own. The first is the four-level retrievability hierarchy in step 3, which reorders the usual advice by putting house acronyms and team shorthand below terms of art rather than above them. The second is step 5's argument that plain-language campaigns are calibrated for public-facing documents and are routinely misapplied to specialist-to-specialist writing, where compression is the correct choice and plainness costs precision without gaining a reader.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the received advice on this topic is unusually bad. "Prefer the short word" is offered as a rule and stated so that it cannot be tested, most famously in Orwell's version, where "never use a long word where a short one will do" hangs entirely on "will do" and therefore forbids nothing. A writer following it either changes nothing or over-applies it and loses precision, and neither outcome is what the advice intended.

The second reason is that the jargon question, asked the usual way, has no answer. Every professional field runs on technical vocabulary, so "avoid jargon" is either impossible or means something narrower than it says. Recasting it as retrievability makes it decidable in each case, which is what a writer actually needs at the moment of choosing a word.

The third reason is specific to this collection's readers. A credit risk document typically mixes three vocabularies: standard regulatory terms, field-specific modelling terms, and house-invented labels for local processes and committees. Those three need different treatment, and they get identical treatment in most documents, which is why a paper is simultaneously over-explained for a specialist and impenetrable for anyone new.

And a reason about durability. Records outlive teams. A document dense with house shorthand becomes unreadable the moment the people who wrote it move on, which is a governance failure rather than a stylistic one, and it is entirely preventable by expanding a label the first time it appears.

Step 3

The mental model

Two axes: the register dial English's layered lexicon gives you, and the retrievability hierarchy that replaces the jargon question.

Term of art
A word whose technical sense is its meaning, so that substituting a plainer near-synonym changes the claim. "Subordinated", "default", "impairment". Never paraphrase one for the sake of plainness.
Retrievability
Whether a reader who does not know a term can find out what it means, and how much effort that takes. The axis that replaces the undecidable question of whether something counts as jargon.
Predicative definition
A definition given as a complete sentence with the term as its subject, rather than tucked into an apposition or a parenthesis. "Point-in-time PD is the probability of default over twelve months given current conditions."
Step 4

The detail

The definition rule

A TECHNICAL TERM IS DEFINED ONCE, IN A SENTENCE OF ITS OWN, AT FIRST USE. THREE REASONS, EACH FROM AN EARLIER MODULE 1 INTERRUPTION COST (module 2, lecture 6) An embedded definition forces the reader to hold an incomplete clause while processing new material. BAD "The point-in-time PD, that is, the probability of default over the next twelve months conditional on current economic conditions rather than a long-run average, exceeds the through-the-cycle estimate by 40 basis points." GOOD "Point-in-time PD is the probability of default over the next twelve months given current conditions, rather than a long-run average. It exceeds the through-the-cycle estimate by 40 basis points." Same words, almost. The second version never asks the reader to hold a suspended subject. 2 CONSULTABILITY (lecture 5) A definition inside a parenthesis on page 6 cannot be found by anyone who did not read page 6. A definition that is its own sentence can be located, quoted and cross-referenced. 3 PREDICATIVE FORM ASSERTS; APPOSITIVE FORM ASSUMES "X is Y" is a claim you have made and can be held to. "X (i.e. Y)" smuggles the same claim in as though it were common ground. If your local definition differs from the literature's, and it often does, the appositive form hides that you have chosen. ONCE, AND NOT TWICE Redefining a term in section 4 that was defined in section 2 tells the reader you do not expect them to have read section 2, which they will believe. It also doubles the maintenance: two definitions drift apart across revisions and now the document contradicts itself. THE EXCEPTION is the appendix, for exactly the dual-mode reason lecture 5 gave. Body once, appendix once, and they are checked against each other.

Choosing between the layers

PairTake the shorter?Why
Where the default holds and where it fails
use / utilise Yes, always "Utilise" carries no meaning "use" lacks. This is the pure case the received advice was written for, and it is rarer than the advice implies.
end / terminate Usually, not always "Terminate" implies an act by an agent under a provision, which "end" does not. A facility that ends and a facility that is terminated are different events.
show / demonstrate Usually "Demonstrate" implies a completed proof to a standard. Where you have evidence rather than proof, the longer word overclaims, which makes the shorter one both plainer and more accurate.
enough / sufficient No, in regulated writing "Sufficient" is the term used in the rules, and matching the regulation's wording is worth more than plainness when a supervisor is checking compliance against it.
lower / subordinated No Term of art. Subordination is a legal ranking, not a position. Paraphrasing loses the entire content.
begin / commence / initiate Yes for the first two, and note the third "Commence" is pure register with no added precision. "Initiate" does add an agent, so it survives where an actor matters.

The fourth row is the one that surprises people who have absorbed plain-language training. Where a rule uses a word, use that word, because a reader checking your document against the rule is matching text. Improving the regulator's diction inside a compliance argument is a choice to be clearer and less useful.

Jargon audit, in practice

Level 3 is the level that fails and it cannot be audited by the person who wrote the document, because a house acronym is the most familiar vocabulary they own and therefore the most transparent. Module 6's lecture 4 predicted this exactly: you cannot feel the knowledge you have.

Which means the audit is a process rather than a technique, and it is cheap. Give the document to someone outside your immediate team, ask them to mark every term they could not look up, and expand what they mark. Twenty minutes of someone else's attention finds what an hour of your own cannot, and the same reader can be used repeatedly until you have learned your own vocabulary's boundaries.

The mechanical fallback, where no such reader is available, is to search your document for capitalised abbreviations and definite-article labels: any acronym, and any phrase of the form "the X process", "the Y standard", "the Z framework". Those two patterns catch most level-3 vocabulary, because house labels almost always take a definite article and almost always get abbreviated once they are established.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Plain-language campaigns are calibrated for a different document

The plain English movement has an excellent record and a narrow original target. Gowers wrote Plain Words in 1948 for civil servants writing to the public; Cutts's Oxford Guide to Plain English and the plain-language legislation that followed address forms, notices, contracts and correspondence addressed to people with no choice about reading them and no training in the field. In that setting the case is overwhelming and the empirical support is real.

The advice then travels, and it arrives in specialist-to-specialist writing where its premises do not hold. A validation report is read by people who share the vocabulary, and rewriting "the through-the-cycle estimate exceeds the point-in-time estimate" into plain terms makes it three times longer, less precise, and no more accessible to the only people who will read it. Compression among specialists is a service, and treating it as a vice produces documents that are simultaneously longer and worse.

So the plain-fancy question is a register question, and module 6's lecture 5 already gave the apparatus: field, tenor and mode determine which layer is appropriate, and the answer for a technical document addressed to technical readers is not the answer for a customer letter. That is not a licence for obscurity, and the distinction between compression and obscurity is exactly the retrievability hierarchy.

Which yields the one thing that holds across every register. Whatever level of formality your document has settled on, every term in it should be a term the reader can get at. That single rule does the work plain-language advice is trying to do, without the collateral damage of forbidding precision.

The Latinate layer is a resource, and English is unusual in having it

The standard framing treats Latinate vocabulary as a temptation to be resisted, and that framing throws away something valuable. English's history gave it a double and sometimes triple lexicon, so it can express nearly the same content at three levels of formality with the same syntax. Most languages cannot do this to anything like the same degree, and it is why English prose has such a wide expressive range in register.

A writer who mechanically cuts every Latinate word has disabled that dial. They lose the ability to shift register deliberately, which module 6's lecture 6 identified as most of what voice consists of, and they lose the precision that the Latinate layer often carries, because words borrowed for technical purposes tend to be narrower than their native counterparts.

The right posture is therefore not suspicion but awareness. Know which layer you are in, know that the reader registers the shift even without naming it, and use the dial. A document that runs mostly plain and turns formal for a single sentence has marked that sentence, and the marking is free.

What the received rules get wrong, stated fairly

Orwell's second rule, never use a long word where a short one will do, is unfalsifiable as written, since "will do" carries the entire judgement and the rule supplies no test for it. Gowers is better because he writes in tendencies with worked exceptions rather than prohibitions, which is why Plain Words has aged better than the essay that gets quoted more.

The general fault in the rule-based tradition is that it addresses vocabulary as though the choice were between plain and fancy, when the live choice is between precise and imprecise, and register is a third axis running across both. Those three axes are what step 4's table is actually sorting, and any single-axis rule will therefore mislead a third of the time.

None of which means the rules are worthless. Default to the short word is a good default, and it is right far more often than it is wrong, which is what a default is for. The improvement this lecture offers is small and specific: three named exceptions instead of unstated judgement, and retrievability instead of jargon.

Common failure Expanding an acronym once, in a document of forty pages, and then using it two hundred times. The expansion satisfies a convention and does not serve a reader who meets the acronym again on page thirty-one having forgotten page two. For level-3 house vocabulary in a long document, expand at first use in each major section, and put the full list in the appendix that lecture 5 argued for. Duplication across a long document is the same dual-mode concession, applied to vocabulary.

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Search a document of yours for acronyms and for phrases of the form "the X process". Sort every hit into the four retrievability levels and report how many level-3 items were never expanded.
  2. Then compose. Rewrite one embedded definition from that document into predicative form as two sentences, and say what the appositive version had been assuming.
  3. Explain the Germanic-Latinate layering and what it gives an English writer that most languages lack.
  4. State the default and its three named exceptions.
  5. Give the four retrievability levels with an example of each, and say which requires the most work.
