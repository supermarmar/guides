Exposition without tedium

Front-loaded background fails for a mechanical reason: exposition delivered before the reader has anything to attach it to is all new information and no given. Braiding fixes that and breaks something else, which is why the appendix exists.

Lecture 5 of 7 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: Clark and Haviland; McPhee on structure
Step 1

The 30-second version

Exposition is material the reader needs and does not want: background, definitions, method, context. The default arrangement puts it first, and the default arrangement fails for a reason module 3 already established. The given-new contract requires each new piece to attach to something the reader holds, and a reader eight pages into a model description before the first finding holds nothing, so all of it is new and none of it sticks.

The remedy is just-in-time delivery: each piece of background arrives at the point where it is first needed, attached to the claim that needs it. At scale that becomes braiding, alternating an argument strand with an exposition strand so the reader is never on one strand long enough to lose the other.

Braiding has a cost that craft books do not mention and professionals feel immediately. A braided document cannot be consulted. Its exposition is distributed, unlabelled and dependent on its surroundings, so a reader who wants to look up the recovery-rate definition in eighteen months cannot find it. Professional documents are consulted constantly.

That conflict is what the appendix is actually for. Not "detail goes at the back", which is what everyone says, but that a document has two reading modes, and one wants exposition braided into moving prose while the other wants it complete, labelled and in one place. The resolution is structural separation rather than compromise.

Feynman test Nobody remembers background they were given before they needed it. So give each piece where it is needed. But a report also gets looked things up in, and you cannot look up something that has been woven into a paragraph, which is why the definitions go in an appendix as well.
What is beyond the source Given-new is Clark and Haviland's and braiding is standard craft vocabulary. Three arguments here are the lecture's own. The first is the explanation of front-loading's failure through given-new, which makes it a mechanical prediction rather than a complaint about tedium. The second is step 5's dual-mode argument: braiding destroys consultability, so the appendix is a response to two reading modes rather than a dumping ground. The third is step 4's observation that the curse of knowledge produces over-explanation here rather than under-explanation, because a writer who knows the whole model cannot tell which parts bear on the finding.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is the most common structural fault in technical reporting. A validation report that opens with a model description running to several pages has put its least useful material where its most attentive readers are, and by the time the finding arrives the attention is spent. Everybody involved knows this and the structure persists, because the description is what the author understood first and therefore wrote first.

The second reason is that the fix is cheap and mechanical. You do not need to rewrite the exposition, only to relocate it, and relocating it to the point of first need usually shortens it, because a good deal of front-matter turns out to bear on nothing at all and its irrelevance is invisible until you try to place it.

The third reason is the dual-mode problem, which is worth naming because most people resolve it badly. Faced with a document that must be readable and consultable, writers tend to compromise: exposition half-braided and half-collected, so the linear reader hits interruptions and the consulting reader still cannot find anything. Separation works and compromise does not.

And a reason about a specific failure this lecture can name. Writers who know a subject completely explain it completely, and the resulting over-explanation is not generosity, it is an inability to identify which parts of their own knowledge are load-bearing for the point at hand. That is the curse of knowledge running in the opposite direction from usual, and recognising it is what lets you cut four pages without losing anything.

Step 3

The mental model

Three arrangements of the same material, and what each costs.

Braiding
Alternating two or more strands, typically argument and exposition, so that the reader is never on one strand long enough to lose contact with the other.
Just-in-time exposition
Delivering each piece of background at the point where it first becomes necessary, attached to the claim that requires it. Satisfies given-new, and reveals which background was never necessary at all.
Dual-mode document
A document that must be both read linearly and consulted non-linearly. The two modes want opposite treatments of exposition, and the resolution is structural separation rather than compromise.
Step 4

The detail

Braiding, worked

FRONT-LOADED (abridged from four pages to its shape) Section 2: Model description 2.1 Portfolio scope and segmentation 2.2 Definition of default applied 2.3 Recovery definition and discounting convention 2.4 Data sources and vintages 2.5 Estimation methodology 2.6 Downturn identification approach 2.7 Application of the downturn add-on Section 3: Findings 3.1 The estimation window contains no severe downturn. A reader who reaches 3.1 has read 2.3 four pages earlier and does not remember the discounting convention. They will have to go back. Most will not. BRAIDED The estimation window runs from 2011 to 2023 and its worst observed arrears rate is 1.4 per cent. [ARGUMENT] A downturn LGD estimate is required to reflect a period of economic downturn, which the model policy defines by reference to a peak-to-trough arrears threshold of 2.5 per cent. [EXPOSITION, one sentence, exactly what the next claim needs] No year in the window meets that threshold, so the window contains no downturn on the model's own definition. [ARGUMENT, and the finding has landed on page 1] The 2008 peak for this portfolio was 3.1 per cent. That figure is reconstructed: collateral valuations are incomplete for approximately a fifth of accounts in the 2008 vintage, and the reconstruction assumes the missing valuations follow the recorded distribution. [EXPOSITION, and note it is a LIMITATION, which is the kind of exposition that must be inline because the reader cannot evaluate the claim without it] On that basis the estimate understates downturn loss by 30 to 50 basis points. [ARGUMENT] WHAT MOVED The default definition, the discounting convention, the segmentation and the data vintages are all in the appendix. None of them bears on this finding. The downturn threshold and the reconstruction limitation are inline, because the finding is unintelligible without the first and unevaluable without the second. THE SORTING RULE Inline: exposition without which the adjacent claim cannot be understood or cannot be judged. Appendix: everything else, in full.

The sorting rule is the whole technique and it has a useful property: it is a test rather than a judgement. For each piece of background, remove it and ask whether any later sentence becomes unintelligible or unevaluable. If neither, it goes to the appendix, and the fact that you spent three weeks understanding it is not a reason to keep it in the body.

The pattern in the verdict column is uncomfortable and worth stating plainly: the correlation is with how much work the material cost the author rather than with how much the reader needs it. Segmentation logic and discounting conventions are in report bodies because establishing them was laborious, and effort creates a sense of importance that has nothing to do with relevance. Module 8's lecture 3 made the same observation about crowding the wrong moment.

The one-sentence definition

Inline exposition should be short enough not to interrupt, and the standard unit is a sentence of its own. The rule, which lecture 6 develops, is that a technical term gets defined once, in a separate sentence, at first use, and never in a parenthesis or a subordinate clause.

The reason is the one module 3 established about subject-verb proximity and mid-sentence interruption: a definition embedded in the clause it modifies forces the reader to hold an incomplete structure while processing new material, which is exactly the load that makes prose feel hard. "The downturn LGD, which is the loss given default estimated for a period of economic stress as required by the model policy's 2.5 per cent arrears threshold, understates by 40 basis points" is one sentence doing two jobs badly. Two sentences do both well.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Braiding destroys consultability, and craft books never say so

Every treatment of exposition in the literary tradition recommends distribution: give the reader background where they need it, never in a block, never before they care. That advice is right for its genre and it is derived from a genre with one reading mode. A magazine piece is read once, and nobody looks up a definition in it.

Professional documents are consulted more often than they are read. A validation report is opened to answer a question: what was the scope, what did they test, how was recovery defined, what did they say about 2008. Braided exposition is unreachable in that mode, because it has no heading, no fixed location, and no independence from its surroundings. The definition that reads beautifully on page six is invisible to anyone who did not read page five.

So the appendix is not a dumping ground and it is not "where the detail goes". It is the consult-mode copy of material the body carries in read-once form, and it should be complete, labelled, indexed and frankly repetitive. That violates module 5's first lecture, which was about cutting redundancy, and the violation is correct: redundancy between two reading modes is not redundancy, because no single reader encounters both copies.

Which yields a design rule most institutions have half-discovered and rarely state. Body exposition is minimal and contextual; appendix exposition is complete and self-contained. If your appendix is a set of tables the body refers to, you have built a data annex rather than a reference section, and the consulting reader is still unserved.

Why front-loading survives

The structure is universally recognised as poor and universally reproduced, which usually means it is serving somebody. Three explanations, in increasing order of how uncomfortable they are.

The first is order of understanding. You learned the model before you found the problem, so you wrote the model description first, and the document's structure is a fossil of your learning sequence. That is the innocent explanation and it accounts for most cases.

The second is genre. Module 7's lecture 7 established that a move set can require material for a reader other than the present one, and a model description may be an obligatory move serving the file rather than the committee. Where that is true, the answer is not to cut it but to move it, and moving it is often permitted where cutting it is not.

The third is that front-loading defers the finding, and deferring the finding is comfortable. Four pages of accurate description before a difficult statement is four pages of not making it, and it reads as thoroughness. That is worth naming because it is the version you cannot fix with a structural rule, and because a writer who has front-loaded for this reason will defend the description on genre grounds without knowing they are doing it.

The general principle underneath

All three lectures in this module's middle section, narrative, person and exposition, turn out to be about the same thing: where the reader's attention goes and what the arrangement asserts without saying it.

Crowding a moment says it mattered. Choosing a person says who is accountable. Placing exposition says what the document is about, because the material you develop at length in the body is the material the reader takes as the subject. A report with four pages of model description and one paragraph of finding has said, in the only language a skimming reader reads, that it is a document about a model rather than a document about a problem.

Which gives a check that costs one glance and catches most of this module's failures at once. Look at the page lengths, not the headings. Whatever is longest is what your document is about. If that is not what you intended it to be about, no amount of sentence-level work will fix it, and the repair is a relocation rather than a rewrite.

Common failure Braiding exposition that the reader needs to evaluate the claim, then also putting it in the appendix, and shortening the inline version until it no longer does its job. A limitation that bounds confidence in a key figure has to be inline in full, because a reader cannot judge the finding while holding a promise that the caveat appears later. Duplicate freely, and never make the body version a pointer when the body version is load-bearing.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a report of yours and apply the sorting rule to every block of front matter: remove each, and ask whether any later sentence becomes unintelligible or unevaluable. Report how many blocks moved to the appendix and how many pages that freed.
  2. Then compose. Rewrite the opening of that report braided, in the format of step 4, so that the finding appears on the first page.
  3. Explain why front-loaded exposition fails, in terms of the given-new contract.
  4. Define braiding and just-in-time exposition. What does relocating background usually reveal?
  5. What does braiding cost, and why does that cost matter more in professional writing than in literary nonfiction?
  6. Give the two reading modes, who each belongs to, and what each wants from exposition.
  7. State the sorting rule for inline against appendix. Why is it a test rather than a judgement?
  8. What does the verdict column in step 4 correlate with, and what should it correlate with?
  9. Why should a definition occupy its own sentence rather than a subordinate clause?
  10. Give the three reasons front-loading survives, and say which one no structural rule will fix.
  11. What single check catches most of this module's failures, and why does it work?
