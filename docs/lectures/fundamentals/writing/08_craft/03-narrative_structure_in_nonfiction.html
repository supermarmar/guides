Narrative structure in nonfiction

Le Guin's crowding and leaping, and the test for which professional documents are narrative at all. Most are not, and the ones that are get written with uniform texture, so the reader cannot tell which moment mattered.

Lecture 3 of 7 Reading time, 32 minutes Source: Le Guin, Steering the Craft; McPhee, Draft No. 4
Step 1

The 30-second version

Ursula Le Guin's pair of terms is the most useful thing in this lecture. Crowding means loading a passage with detail so that time slows and the reader's attention sharpens; Le Guin takes the image from Keats, who told Shelley to load every rift of his subject with ore. Leaping means leaving out everything that does not need to be there. A narrative is built by choosing what to crowd and what to leap.

The professional failure is uniform texture. An incident chronology that gives every month a paragraph has crowded nothing and leapt nothing, so the reader cannot tell whether the decisive moment was in March or in November. The remedy is not more detail or less; it is unequal detail, concentrated where the decision was made.

Most professional documents are not narrative, and forcing narrative on them is worse than leaving them alone. The pyramid is anti-narrative by design, because answer-first destroys sequence, and that is correct for most papers. So the lecture supplies a test: is the sequence itself the finding? If what happened is the deliverable, write narrative. If the deliverable is whether something is fit for purpose, do not.

Three genres pass that test and one of them is a technical requirement rather than a stylistic option. Incident and root-cause reviews pass. Model development histories pass and are usually written as date lists. And the stress scenario narrative passes, where a bank must write a coherent account of how a macroeconomic path unfolds, which is narrative writing done by quantitative people and done badly.

Feynman test Slow down where it matters, skip the rest. If every part of your account moves at the same speed, you have told the reader nothing about which part was important, and they will guess. Also: most reports are not stories, and you should check before deciding to tell one.
What is beyond the source Le Guin and McPhee are cited accurately. Three things are the lecture's own. The first is the sequence test in step 3, offered as the boundary between narrative and non-narrative professional documents, which is what stops this material being misapplied. The second is the argument in step 5 that chronology is the only order that does not smuggle in causation, so thematic reordering of an incident asserts a causal structure you may be unable to support. The third is step 4's treatment of the stress scenario narrative as the case where narrative competence is a regulatory requirement rather than a refinement.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that a subset of professional documents are narrative whether or not anybody treats them as such, and they are the high-stakes ones. An incident review after a control failure, a root-cause analysis, a lessons-learned paper after a loss event: each is an account of a sequence, each will be read by people deciding accountability, and each is usually written as an undifferentiated list of dated events that requires the reader to work out the plot themselves.

The second reason is the scenario narrative, which is a genuine technical obligation. Supervisory stress testing programmes publish scenarios as narratives alongside variable paths, and a bank's internal scenarios have to be narratively coherent to be credible: the unemployment path, the house price path and the rate path must belong to the same story. Writing that story is a modelling activity performed with prose, and the people asked to do it have generally had no instruction in prose.

The third reason is negative and equally important. This lecture's techniques are widely misapplied by people who have read a book on storytelling and decided that a validation report needs a narrative arc. It does not. Establishing the test for when narrative applies is more valuable to most readers than the techniques themselves, and the honest answer is that it applies to a minority of what you write.

And a reason about attention. Crowding and leaping is a general principle about controlling where a reader's attention lands, and its non-narrative application is real: in any document, the section you develop at length is the section the reader takes as important, whatever your headings claim. Uniform development is a signal that everything matters equally, which is almost never true.

Step 3

The mental model

The test first, because it decides whether the rest applies, then the crowd-and-leap profile against the uniform one.

Crowding
Le Guin's term, after Keats's advice to load every rift with ore: filling a passage with specific detail so that narrative time slows and the reader attends closely.
Leaping
Omitting whatever does not need to be there, so that narrative time accelerates. A leap asserts that nothing in the skipped interval bears on the outcome, which is a claim and not merely an absence.
Scene and summary
Scene renders a moment at close to real time with specifics; summary compresses a stretch into its net effect. Both are needed, and the failure is a document written entirely in one of them.
Step 4

The detail

An incident chronology, twice

VERSION 1 · UNIFORM TEXTURE (what gets written) January: The model was recalibrated as part of the annual cycle. Documentation was updated. February: Monitoring reports were produced and reviewed. No exceptions were noted. March: The PD stability metric moved to amber. The threshold was reviewed and the metric was reclassified as green following discussion. April: Monitoring continued. No exceptions. May: Monitoring continued. No exceptions. [June to October: five further paragraphs of the same shape] November: A supervisory review identified that the stability metric had been amber since March. The reclassification was queried. December: A remediation plan was agreed. WHAT IS WRONG WITH IT Nothing, factually. It is complete and accurate and it does not tell the reader that March was the moment. March gets the same weight as May. A reader skimming will take November as the event, because November is where the words become interesting, and November is where the problem was found rather than where it happened. VERSION 2 · CROWDED AND LEAPT The recalibration in January was routine and its documentation complete. On 14 March the PD stability metric moved to amber, a 14 per cent relative shift against a 20 per cent threshold. [CROWD] The metric was discussed at the monthly monitoring forum on 19 March. The minute records three participants: the model owner, the monitoring analyst who produced the report, and the portfolio manager. It records that the shift was "considered attributable to a change in the origination mix" and that the metric was reclassified as green. It does not record any analysis of the origination mix, and none was performed. The reclassification was made by the model owner, who is also the approver of monitoring exceptions for this model. Monitoring then ran monthly from April to October without further exception. [LEAP] The supervisory review in November identified the amber classification and queried the March reclassification. [CROWD, briefly] Remediation was agreed in December. WHAT CHANGED One moment is rendered at close to real time, with the room, the participants, the wording of the minute, and the two facts that constitute the finding: no analysis was done, and the person who reclassified the exception was the person whose model it was. Seven months are compressed into eleven words, and the compression is a claim: nothing in them mattered. The finding is now unmissable and it was present in version 1 all along.

Notice what crowding did in the second version. It is not more prose about March; the March passage contains specifics that version 1 omitted entirely, because version 1's uniform texture had no room for them. Deciding to crowd a moment is what causes you to go and find out who was in the room.

Scene and summary as a working pair

SceneSummary
The two textures, and what each is for
Time Close to real time. A meeting takes a paragraph. Compressed. A quarter takes a clause.
Detail Named participants, quoted wording, exact dates and magnitudes. Net effect only. "Monitoring ran without exception."
Use it for The decision point. The moment a control was bypassed, an override was made, or information existed and was not acted on. Everything that establishes the shape of the period without bearing on the outcome.
Failure if overused A forty-page transcript in which every meeting is rendered and no moment stands out. Reads as thorough and cannot be acted on. A one-page account in which nothing is examined, so no finding is demonstrated and the conclusion rests on the author's authority.
Proportion, roughly One or two scenes in an incident review. Rarely three. Everything else.

The stress scenario narrative

This is the case where the material in this lecture stops being a refinement. Supervisory stress testing programmes publish their scenarios as narratives alongside the variable paths, and a bank running internal scenarios for its capital assessment has to supply the same thing: an account of how the world gets from here to the paths in the spreadsheet.

That account is doing analytical work rather than presentational work. Its job is to make the variable paths mutually coherent, and incoherence between paths is a modelling error that prose is unusually good at exposing. A scenario in which unemployment rises sharply while house prices are flat and policy rates fall is arithmetically constructible and narratively impossible, and the impossibility becomes obvious the moment somebody tries to write the sentence that connects them.

WHAT A SCENARIO NARRATIVE HAS TO DO 1 SUPPLY A MECHANISM, NOT A LIST Bad: "GDP falls 4.5%, unemployment rises to 8.2%, house prices fall 28%, bank rate rises to 6%." That is the path, not the scenario. It says what happens and not why these things happen together. Better: name the shock and trace it. An external supply shock raises input costs; policy tightens to contain second-round effects; the tightening lands on a household sector with high refinancing volumes; unemployment follows with a lag; forced sales into a market with reduced credit availability drive the house price path. 2 MAKE THE LAGS EXPLICIT Narrative is the natural medium for sequence, and lags are where scenario incoherence hides. If unemployment peaks in the same quarter as the rate rise, say why, because the default assumption is a lag of several quarters. 3 CROWD THE TRANSMISSION, LEAP THE RECOVERY The interesting part is how the stress arrives and propagates. The recovery path is usually a modelling convention and should be described as one rather than narrated as if it were a story. 4 STATE WHAT THE SCENARIO IS NOT A scenario is a coherent severe path, not a forecast and not a worst case. One sentence saying so prevents a committee from reading a probability into it, which they will otherwise supply. 5 CHECK EVERY PAIR OF VARIABLES YOU HAVE NOT MENTIONED The variables the narrative does not connect are where the incoherence is. If the narrative never explains why commercial property behaves as the path says, the path may be doing whatever the calibration did rather than what the scenario implies. THE TEST Can a reader who has only the narrative predict the direction of every variable path? If yes, the scenario is coherent. If they would guess wrong on one, either the narrative is incomplete or the path is unmotivated, and both are findings.

Point 5 is the reason narrative competence is worth having here rather than a nicety. Prose forces you to connect things, and unconnected variables are visible as gaps in the story in a way they are never visible in a table. Writing the narrative is a check on the scenario, which is why it should be written by whoever built the paths and not delegated afterwards.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Chronology is the only order that does not assert causation

McPhee's essay on structure describes his practice of laying out a piece's parts and considering alternative orders, and his default is chronology with departures made for identifiable reasons. There is a stronger argument for that default in a professional setting than in a literary one, and it is about causal claims rather than about readability.

Thematic reordering groups events by kind: all the governance failures together, all the data issues together, all the monitoring gaps together. That grouping asserts that the grouped events belong to one cause, and often you cannot support the assertion. Three monitoring gaps in different quarters might share a cause or might be three unrelated lapses, and a thematic structure has claimed the former without offering evidence.

Chronology makes no such claim. It says only that this happened and then that happened, which is what you know. So in an incident review, chronology is not merely conventional, it is the epistemically modest choice, and departing from it should require the same justification as any other causal claim. Where you do have the evidence for a causal grouping, make the claim explicitly in prose rather than implying it through the arrangement, because a claim in the structure cannot be qualified and a claim in a sentence can.

Which cuts against module 4's advice in a specific way worth naming. MECE grouping is excellent for a recommendation paper and hazardous for an incident account, because grouping is a causal assertion when the material is a sequence of events. Two structures, two document types, and the sequence test in step 3 tells you which you are in.

The leap is a claim, not an omission

Writers treat leaping as a concession to length and it is better understood as a positive assertion. When you compress seven months into a sentence, you have told the reader that nothing in those seven months bears on the outcome, and you have taken responsibility for that.

Which means a leap can be wrong in a way an omission cannot. If the origination mix genuinely did change in June, and your account leapt from March to November, you have asserted the opposite of the truth and done it invisibly. That is a serious matter in an incident review, and it produces a discipline: before leaping an interval, satisfy yourself that you know it is empty, and say what you checked. "Monitoring ran monthly from April to October without further exception" is a leap with its evidence attached, and it is materially different from silence about those months.

The stronger version of the point is that a document with no leaps has evaded this responsibility rather than discharged it. A month-by-month list has made no claim about what mattered, which feels neutral and is actually an abdication, because somebody now has to make that judgement and it will be whoever speaks first in the meeting. Uniform texture is not objectivity; it is judgement deferred to a reader who has less information than you.

Where this lecture does not apply, restated

It is worth closing on the negative case, because narrative technique is the most over-applied material in writing instruction and a reader who takes only the techniques from this lecture will make their documents worse.

A validation report is not a story. It has a finding, and the finding is a judgement about fitness rather than an account of a sequence, so the reader wants it first and does not want to be walked through the analysis in the order you performed it. Nobody needs to know that you tried three approaches before the fourth worked. Module 4's answer-first structure is correct there precisely because it is anti-narrative, and the suspense that narrative depends on is, in that genre, simply the reader not yet knowing the thing they came for.

The one piece of this lecture that does generalise is the attention principle underneath crowding and leaping. In any document, the section you develop at length is the section the reader treats as important, regardless of what your headings say. If your methodology section runs to four pages and your finding to a paragraph, you have said that the methodology is the point. That is worth checking in every document you write, narrative or not, and it takes one glance at the page lengths.

Common failure Crowding the wrong moment, which is usually the moment where you did the most work. An analyst who spent three weeks reconstructing a data history will crowd the reconstruction, because it was difficult and interesting, and leap the twenty-minute meeting where the exception was overridden. The reader then takes the reconstruction as the subject of the document. Crowd where the decision was made, not where the effort was spent, and put the effort in an appendix.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a chronology you have written or read and rewrite it crowded and leapt, in the two-version format of step 4. Report what specifics you had to go and find in order to crowd the decisive moment.
  2. Then compose. If you have written or reviewed a scenario, apply the test at the end of step 4: could a reader with only the narrative predict the direction of every path? Name the variable they would guess wrong on.
  3. Define crowding and leaping, and give the source of Le Guin's image.
  4. State the sequence test, and give two document types on each side of it.
  5. What is wrong with a uniform-texture chronology, given that it is complete and accurate? Which month will a skimming reader take as the event, and why?
  6. Distinguish scene from summary on time, detail and use. What is the failure mode of overusing each?
  7. Why is a scenario narrative an analytical activity rather than a presentational one? What does prose expose that a table does not?
  8. Why is chronology the epistemically modest order? What does thematic grouping assert?
  9. How does that cut against module 4's advice, and what decides which applies?
  10. In what sense can a leap be wrong? What discipline follows, and what should accompany a leap?
  11. Why is uniform texture an abdication rather than objectivity?
  12. Which single principle from this lecture generalises to non-narrative documents, and what one-glance check does it give you?
