Reading as apprenticeship

Imitation was the method for fifteen centuries, not a shortcut. And the uncomfortable corollary: twenty hours a week of institutional prose is training too, which is why professionals write institutional prose and why deliberate reading is defensive rather than enriching.

Lecture 7 of 7 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: the progymnasmata; Franklin; King; Graham and Perin
Step 1

The 30-second version

The classical composition curriculum, the progymnasmata, ran on imitation. Fourteen or so graded exercises in ascending difficulty, from fable and narrative up through refutation, comparison, characterisation and thesis, each beginning with a model that the student copied, paraphrased and varied before attempting anything original. Aphthonius's version was the standard textbook for over a thousand years.

Benjamin Franklin reinvented the method alone. He took essays from the Spectator, made brief notes of the sense of each sentence, set them aside for days, reconstructed the essay from the notes, and compared his version with the original to find his faults. He also jumbled the notes to practise arrangement, and turned prose into verse and back to force himself to find alternative words. Four distinct exercises, and each one maps onto a module of this course.

The empirical support is modest and it points the same way. Graham and Perin's meta-analysis, which found traditional grammar instruction actively harmful at an effect size of about minus 0.32, found the study of models positively effective at around plus 0.25. The classical method scores; the schoolroom method does not.

And the corollary that matters most professionally. Reading is training whether or not you chose the material, so twenty hours a week of badly written institutional prose is twenty hours of instruction in writing badly. Deliberate reading is therefore not enrichment. It is a counterweight to occupational exposure.

Feynman test Take something written well, work out how it was built, and rebuild it. That was the whole method for fifteen hundred years. And notice that whatever you read all day is teaching you, so if what you read all day is committee papers, that is what you are learning to write.
What is beyond the source Franklin's method, the progymnasmata and the meta-analytic figures are reported as they stand. Two arguments are the lecture's own. The first is the mapping in step 4 of Franklin's four exercises onto specific modules of this course, which turns a historical anecdote into a practice schedule. The second is step 5's claim that occupational reading is adverse training, so deliberate reading functions defensively, and that this explains why professionals who receive writing instruction often fail to improve: the instruction is a few hours against twenty a week of counter-models.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this lecture supplies what the other fifty-seven cannot. Modules 1 to 6 give you diagnosis: names for faults, tests to apply, principles to check against. Diagnosis is slow and conscious, and it works at the revision stage. It does not give you the fast recognition that lets a sentence feel wrong before you can say why, and recognition is what wide reading builds.

The second reason is that imitation carries a stigma it does not deserve, and the stigma costs people the most efficient exercise available. Copying a model to learn from it is not plagiarism, because nothing is published; it is the equivalent of a musician playing scales from a score, and the tradition that produced most of the prose we admire treated it as elementary rather than suspect.

The third reason is the adverse-training problem, which explains a common and puzzling observation. Professionals attend writing courses, agree with everything, and write the same way afterwards. The usual explanation is that they lack discipline. A better explanation is that a two-day course is competing with several thousand hours of exposure to the house style, and exposure wins because it operates on recognition rather than on rules.

And a reason about what to read, which is where most advice becomes useless. "Read widely" is true and unactionable. The specific version, in step 4, is that the best-written documents in this field are supervisory and central bank publications, and that reading outside the field matters because the pattern inventory you need is structural and rhythmic rather than technical.

Step 3

The mental model

Two systems, two sources, and the balance of exposure that decides which wins.

Progymnasmata
The graded classical composition exercises, running from fable and narrative through refutation, confirmation, comparison, characterisation and description to thesis. Each began with a model. Aphthonius's fourth-century version remained the standard textbook for over a millennium.
Recognition
The fast, automatic sense that a sentence is wrong, available while drafting and built only by volume of reading. Distinct from diagnosis, which names the fault and is available only in revision.
Adverse training
Exposure to badly written models as an unavoidable feature of professional life, which builds recognition in the wrong direction and outweighs formal instruction by two orders of magnitude in hours.
Step 4

The detail

Franklin's four exercises, mapped

FROM THE AUTOBIOGRAPHY, AND WHAT EACH ONE TRAINS EXERCISE 1 · RECONSTRUCT FROM NOTES Read an essay you admire. Write a short note of the sense of each sentence, nothing more. Put the notes away for several days, long enough to forget the wording. Rebuild the essay from the notes. Compare. TRAINS sentence construction and word choice, because the content is fixed and only the expression is yours. Every difference from the original is a decision you made worse or better. MODULES 2 and 5. The comparison step is the whole value, and it is the step people skip. EXERCISE 2 · JUMBLE THE NOTES Same notes, deliberately disordered. Rebuild, then compare your order with the original's. TRAINS arrangement. You will find you had reasons for your order and the author had better ones, and the diagnosis of why is available to you now in a way it was not to Franklin. MODULES 3 and 4. Run this on any document whose structure you admire. EXERCISE 3 · PROSE TO VERSE AND BACK Turn a passage into verse, then back into prose from the verse. TRAINS vocabulary, by force. Metre demands words of particular lengths and stresses, so you have to search for alternatives you would never reach for. Franklin's stated purpose. MODULES 5 and this module's lecture 6. The plain-fancy dial gets exercised whether you like it or not. EXERCISE 4 · SUMMARISE AND EXPAND Compress a passage hard, then expand the compression back to length. TRAINS what is load-bearing. The compression forces a judgement about what the passage is for, and the expansion shows you what you thought was decorative. MODULES 5 and 8 lecture 1. This is the concision lecture as an exercise rather than a rule. THE PROFESSIONAL VERSION, IF FOUR IS TOO MANY Take one well-written page from a supervisory publication. Note the sense of each sentence. A week later, rebuild it. Compare, and use this course's vocabulary to name every difference. Thirty minutes. It is the single highest-yield exercise in the course.

What to read, specifically

"Read widely" made actionable for this field
Central bank and supervisory publications: financial stability reports, BIS working papers, consultation papers The best-written prose in the field by a wide margin. Technical content organised for a reader who must be persuaded, with genuine attention to structure and hedging. Uneven. Some consultation papers are excellent and some are drafted by committee. Choose by quality rather than by source.
Rating agency methodology papers Explaining a model to a reader who will act on it commercially. Strong on assumptions stated as assumptions. Written partly to be defensible, which produces hedging beyond what module 5 would license.
Long-form nonfiction outside finance entirely Rhythm, arrangement, pace, and the handling of exposition. These are field-independent and they are what your recognition system is short of. None, and this is the category professionals cut first. It is the one that matters most.
Your own institution's best papers, chosen deliberately Genre, in module 7's sense. Move analysis on three well-received papers teaches the local obligations. It teaches the local genre including its faults. Read for what the genre requires, not for how to write a sentence.
Judicial and legal writing Argument under adversarial conditions, with the warrant stated because it must be. Module 7's lecture 9 in its native habitat. Sentence length and syntax are not models for anything, and legal drafting conventions do not transfer.

The third row is the one to act on. Reading outside the field feels like leisure and is the most efficient training available, because rhythm, arrangement and the management of exposition are exactly the skills that no technical reading develops and that no course installs.

The paragraph file

The single sustainable practice, for a professional with no time for four exercises, is a file of paragraphs you wish you had written. Add to it whenever you meet one, from any source, with no criterion beyond the reaction. Then, perhaps monthly, take one out and work out why it works using this course's vocabulary.

The value is in the analysis rather than the collection, and it is specific: you are converting recognition into diagnosis. You already knew the paragraph was good, which is the recognition system reporting. Naming what it does, that it is an isocolon carrying a comparison, that its stress position holds the number, that its exposition arrived one sentence before it was needed, connects the fast system to the slow one, and that connection is what lets you produce the effect deliberately rather than occasionally.

It also has a defensive function that the previous section's argument implies. A file of good models, revisited, is a small deliberate counterweight applied to the same recognition system that your working week is degrading, and unlike a course it operates continuously.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

What you read all day is training you

Stephen King's claim that a writer who does not read has neither the time nor the tools is usually taken as an exhortation to read more. The sharper version is that reading is not optional in either direction, because you are always reading something and it is always shaping what you produce.

A credit risk professional reads perhaps twenty hours a week: committee papers, minutes, house templates, email chains, model documentation, other people's validation reports. Most of that prose is bad in the specific ways this course has catalogued, and it is not neutral exposure. It builds recognition, and it builds recognition of the house style as normal, which is precisely what makes institutional prose self-perpetuating. Nobody chooses to write that way. They write what sounds right, and what sounds right is what they have read.

That explains the puzzle in step 2. Writing instruction operates on the diagnostic system, which is available in revision and only if you remember to use it. Drafting runs on recognition, which is being rebuilt every day from the material you cannot avoid. A two-day course against several thousand hours is not a fair contest, and the participant who agrees with everything and changes nothing is not lacking discipline; they are outnumbered.

Which reframes deliberate reading as defence rather than enrichment. Thirty minutes of good prose a day is not self-improvement in the aspirational sense; it is a counterweight, and the honest arithmetic says it is a small one. That is an argument for consistency rather than for volume, and for the paragraph file rather than for a reading list you will abandon.

Imitation is the tradition's method, not its shortcut

Modern writing instruction treats imitation with suspicion, as either plagiarism-adjacent or as a crutch for people who cannot invent. Fifteen centuries of practice disagree, and the disagreement is not sentimental.

The progymnasmata were graded, cumulative and explicitly imitative: a student worked through fable, narrative, chreia, maxim, refutation, confirmation, commonplace, encomium, comparison, characterisation, description, thesis and legislation, each exercise longer and more demanding than the last, and each beginning from a model to be copied, paraphrased, condensed, expanded and varied. Originality was the end point of a decade's training rather than its starting assumption. Erasmus, Milton and most of the writers whose English we still admire came through some version of this.

The reason it works is the one this lecture has been building towards. Imitation is the only exercise that trains recognition directly, because it forces you to reproduce an effect rather than to describe it, and reproduction requires the fast system. Everything else in this course trains the slow one. That is why the recall exercises throughout have been ordered imitate-then-compose, which was the tradition's order and is not a stylistic choice about pedagogy.

It should be said plainly that this is where the course's own evidence is strongest and where its own method is weakest. Fifty-seven lectures of principle score, by the meta-analysis's own reckoning, worse than the exercise described in three paragraphs of step 4. A reader who does the reconstruction exercise weekly and skims the lectures will probably write better in a year than one who studies the lectures and does no exercises.

The honest state of the evidence

Two things should be conceded about the empirical picture, because this course has claimed testability throughout.

The correlation between wide reading and good writing is real and heavily confounded. People who read a great deal differ from people who do not in education, verbal ability, time available and interest, and no observational study separates those. So the strong claim, that reading causes writing improvement at some measurable rate, is not established by the correlation and should not be asserted.

What is better supported is narrower and more useful. Graham and Perin's meta-analysis assessed the study of models as a specific instructional element, meaning students examining exemplars and being asked to imitate their features, and found a modest positive effect of roughly 0.25. That is not large, and it is measured against the minus 0.32 for traditional grammar instruction in the same analysis, which is the comparison worth carrying. The classical method has modest positive support; the method that replaced it has negative support; and neither result licenses grand claims about reading in general.

So the defensible position is this. Imitation of models has evidence behind it, at a modest effect size. The adverse-training argument is a mechanism rather than a finding, though the mechanism is consistent with what the meta-analysis reports. And "read widely" remains sound advice for which the evidence is weaker than its confidence, which is worth admitting in the last lecture of a module that has argued for evidence throughout.

Common failure Collecting without analysing. A file of admired paragraphs that is never worked through is a bookmark folder, and it trains nothing, because the value was in converting recognition into diagnosis rather than in the accumulation. Fifteen minutes on one paragraph, naming what it does in this course's terms, is worth more than a hundred saved passages. The same applies to Franklin's exercises: the comparison step is the exercise, and reconstructing without comparing is just writing.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate, in the tradition's own form. Run Franklin's first exercise on one page of well-written supervisory prose: note the sense of each sentence, wait a week, rebuild, compare. Report three differences and diagnose each using this course's vocabulary.
  2. Then compose. Start the paragraph file. Add three paragraphs this week and analyse one.
  3. What were the progymnasmata, and what role did imitation play in them?
  4. Give Franklin's four exercises and say what each trains and which modules it corresponds to.
  5. Distinguish diagnosis from recognition. Which does instruction build, which does drafting run on, and why does that matter?
  6. State the adverse-training argument. Why does it explain a writing course that changes nothing?
  7. Give the two effect sizes from Graham and Perin and say what the contrast between them implies.
  8. Which reading category is most efficient for a technical professional, and which do they cut first?
  9. What is the value of the paragraph file, and what makes it worthless?
  10. Why are the recall exercises in this course ordered imitate-then-compose?
  11. State honestly what the evidence supports and what it does not, on reading and on models.
  12. What is the uncomfortable implication of the evidence for this course's own method?
