Metaphor as thought, not ornament

Every metaphor licenses inferences, and readers make them whether or not they are warranted. "Contagion" tells you to look for a source, which is right for some crises and analytically wrong for others, and the word does that work before anyone has decided it should.

Lecture 2 of 7 Reading time, 34 minutes Source: Lakoff and Johnson (1980); Orwell (1946)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Lakoff and Johnson's argument is that metaphor is not decoration applied to an idea but a mapping between conceptual domains that structures how the idea is thought. We understand argument through war, time through money, and quantity through verticality, and those mappings are not figures of speech we could dispense with; they are how the abstract domain is organised.

That contradicts module 7's classification of metaphor as a trope, a departure from ordinary meaning, because if metaphor is the ordinary machinery of meaning for abstract domains then there is no literal baseline to depart from. This lecture takes Lakoff and Johnson's side on the classification and is careful about how far their claim can be pushed.

The professional payload is entailment analysis. Every conceptual metaphor carries inferences, and using the metaphor imports them. Risk as a substance entails that it is conserved, so risk transfer language conceals that risk is created and destroyed rather than moved. Capital as a buffer entails passive absorption. Contagion entails transmission from an infected party, so the word sends you looking for patient zero when the mechanism may be correlated exposures.

Which gives a discipline rather than a prohibition. You cannot write about finance without metaphor, because the vocabulary is metaphor throughout. What you can do is name the source domain, list what it entails, and check whether the entailments are true of your case.

Feynman test Call something a buffer and you have said it absorbs and does nothing else. Call it a constraint and you have said it stops you. Both describe capital, they suggest different actions, and the reader will follow whichever word you used without noticing they were led.
What is beyond the source Lakoff and Johnson, Orwell and the critical literature are cited accurately, and step 5 separates the strong version of the conceptual-metaphor claim, which is contested, from the weak version, which is not. The lecture's own contribution is step 4's entailment audit of six metaphors that are load-bearing in banking, and in particular the argument that "contagion" is not a harmless label but a hypothesis about mechanism which, applied to a common-shock event, directs the analysis towards a source that does not exist.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that financial vocabulary is unusually metaphorical, and the metaphors were chosen by nobody. Haircut, waterfall, floor, buffer, headroom, exposure, stress, shock, contagion, appetite, cushion, cliff. Each arrived because it was vivid, each brought a source domain with it, and none was assessed for whether its entailments hold. You inherited an analytical apparatus in the form of a word list.

The second reason is that the entailments do work in real decisions. A committee told that capital absorbs a loss and a committee told that capital constrains a business are being invited into different arguments, and the difference is one noun. This is not a subtle effect at the margin of persuasion; it is the frame within which the numbers get interpreted.

The third reason is explanatory. Most technical explanation for non-specialists proceeds by metaphor, because a mapping from a familiar domain is the only route into an unfamiliar one. Choosing the metaphor is therefore choosing which inferences your reader will draw, and the right choice is the one whose entailments are true rather than the one that is most vivid. That is a decision you can make deliberately and almost nobody does.

And a reason connecting to module 7. That lecture warned against reviving dead metaphors by accident, which is a stylistic fault. This lecture identifies the deeper problem, which is that a dead metaphor's entailments keep operating after its picture has faded, so the inference survives even when the image does not.

Step 3

The mental model

A mapping with entailments, Orwell's three states, and the audit that makes the entailments visible.

Conceptual metaphor
In Lakoff and Johnson's sense, a systematic mapping from a concrete source domain to an abstract target domain, which structures how the target is conceived rather than merely how it is described.
Entailment
An inference the source domain licenses and the metaphor imports into the target. Operates whether or not the writer intended it and whether or not it is true of the case.
Dying metaphor
Orwell's middle category: figurative enough to be recognised as such, worn out enough to evoke nothing, used because it saves the trouble of thinking. The only one of his three states he actually attacks.
Step 4

The detail

Six load-bearing metaphors, audited

MetaphorSource domain entailsTrue of the case?
Each of these is doing analytical work in ordinary banking prose
Risk is a substance
hold, transfer, sits, amount, owner		 Conserved under transfer. Has a location. Has an owner. Quantity is well defined. Conservation is false. Risk is created by structure and destroyed by hedging, and "risk transfer" language is the main reason that is hard to see. Location is often false too, since the same exposure can sit in two places at once.
Capital is a buffer
buffer, cushion, absorb		 Passive. Sits between two things. Absorbs and is consumed. Doing nothing until impact. Passivity is misleading. Capital has an opportunity cost every day it is held, which the buffer metaphor makes invisible. Contrast "capital is a constraint", which makes the cost visible and hides the absorption.
Stress is contagion
contagion, infection, spread		 A source exists. Transmission is directional. Proximity to the infected matters. Isolation helps. There was a healthy prior state. Frequently false, and consequentially so. Where many institutions held correlated exposures, there is no patient zero, transmission is not directional, and isolation does not help. The word sends the analysis looking for a source that does not exist.
A model is a machine
breaks, runs, rebuild, engine		 Has correct and broken states. Fails discretely. Can be repaired to as-new. Behaviour is determinate. Discreteness is false. Models degrade continuously and are never in a correct state, only in a state of measured fitness. Compare "a model is a map", which entails omission and scale rather than breakage, and is usually the better metaphor.
Risk appetite
appetite, hunger, taste		 An internal preference. Varies with satiety. Belongs to a person. Not chosen deliberately. Mostly false and revealingly so. A risk appetite statement is a negotiated constraint approved by a board, not a preference, and the metaphor is part of why appetite statements drift towards describing current position rather than constraining it.
The economy is a body
healthy, recovery, shock, stress		 A natural healthy state exists. Deviation is illness. Recovery means return to the prior state. There is a normal to come back to. The prior-state entailment is contestable and it structures a great deal of stress testing. A scenario built on "recovery to trend" has assumed a trend exists, which the body metaphor supplied rather than the evidence.

The contagion row is the one worth arguing about, because the word is standard in the literature and this lecture is claiming it can mislead. The claim is narrow: contagion names a specific mechanism, transmission from an affected party to an unaffected one, and where the actual mechanism is common exposure the metaphor points the analysis in the wrong direction. Both mechanisms exist and produce similar patterns of correlated failure, and the vocabulary makes only one of them easy to say.

Choosing a metaphor for explanation

EXPLAINING A PD MODEL TO A NON-SPECIALIST Three candidate metaphors, and what each licenses. "THE MODEL IS A LENS." Entails: it focuses; distortion is at the edges; you look through it at something real; a better lens shows the same thing more clearly. Good for: explaining why calibration matters. Bad for: it implies the thing being looked at is already there and determinate, which for a forward-looking probability is false. "THE MODEL IS A MACHINE." Entails: inputs and outputs; correct operation; discrete failure; repair. Good for: explaining monitoring and controls. Bad for: implies a correct state exists, so a reader concludes the model is either working or broken, and "degraded but usable" becomes unsayable. "THE MODEL IS A MAP." Entails: it omits; it has a scale and a purpose; it can be accurate and useless for your journey; all maps are wrong and some are adequate. Good for: almost everything a validator needs to convey. Fitness for purpose IS the map entailment. Bad for: implies a static territory, so it handles model drift poorly. THE CHOICE Map, for a non-specialist audience, because the entailments you need (omission, purpose-relative adequacy, no correct version) are exactly the ones it supplies for free. AND THEN SAY SO "A model is a map: it leaves things out on purpose, and the question is never whether it is right but whether it is adequate for the journey." One sentence, and the reader now has the frame you chose rather than the one they arrived with.

The mixed metaphor test

Module 7's lecture 6 gave the failure and this is the test. For any sentence containing two or more figurative terms, name each source domain and ask whether they can coexist in one picture. If they cannot, the sentence is mixed, and the reader will notice on the second reading even if you did not on the fifth.

"Once the capital floor was breached, losses cascaded through into the buffer, eroding headroom." floor -> architecture. Stood on. Breached from above. breached -> fortification. A wall, penetrated laterally. cascaded -> hydrology. Falls downwards. buffer -> mechanics. Absorbs a lateral impact. eroding -> geology. Gradual, by flow over time. headroom -> architecture again. Vertical space above. Six source domains, three incompatible directions of motion (down, sideways, gradual flow), and two architectural terms used with opposite orientations. THE PICTURE CANNOT BE DRAWN. That is the test. REPAIR: pick one domain or none. None (best here): "The capital floor was breached. Losses of £340m reduced CET1 headroom from 120 to 40 basis points." One, consistently: "The floor gave way, and the loss fell straight through to CET1." The plain version is shorter, states a magnitude, and has no entailments to audit. In professional prose that is usually the answer: the cure for a mixed metaphor is frequently a number.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

How far the Lakoff and Johnson claim can be pushed

There are two versions of the conceptual metaphor thesis and this lecture relies on only one of them, which is worth being explicit about because the stronger version is genuinely disputed.

The weak version says that conventional metaphors are pervasive, systematic rather than isolated, and carry entailments that shape inference. That is well supported and it is all the entailment audit needs. The strong version, developed in Lakoff's later work and in Philosophy in the Flesh, says that abstract thought is constituted by metaphorical mappings grounded in bodily experience, so that reasoning about abstractions just is the manipulation of these mappings.

The strong version has serious critics. Gregory Murphy argued in 1996 that the evidence for metaphorical representation of abstract concepts is compatible with metaphors being merely a convenient way of talking about concepts represented some other way, and that the psychological experiments do not distinguish the two. Pinker's treatment in The Stuff of Thought makes a related case: metaphors are learned analogies that draw on an underlying non-metaphorical conceptual apparatus, which is why we can notice when a metaphor breaks down.

That critique matters and it does not touch anything professional. Whether risk-as-substance constitutes your concept of risk or merely furnishes a convenient way of discussing it, the conservation entailment is imported either way, and readers draw the inference either way. The audit works on the weak thesis, so this lecture takes no position on the strong one.

Orwell was right about the middle case and wrong to stop there

Orwell's trichotomy in Politics and the English Language is genuinely useful and its conclusion is too narrow. He treats the fresh metaphor as an aid to thought, the dead metaphor as an ordinary word and therefore harmless, and reserves his attack for the huge dump in between: worn-out figures with no evocative power, used because they save the writer the trouble of inventing a phrase.

The middle category is the right target and the "harmless" verdict on dead metaphor is what this lecture disputes. The entailments do not die with the image. Nobody visualises a cushion when they say capital buffer, and everybody still infers passive absorption, so the inference machinery is running on a metaphor nobody can see. Dead metaphors are the most dangerous case precisely because they present as literal technical vocabulary and therefore never get audited.

It should also be said, since this course flags Orwell as a text to argue with rather than defer to, that his own rules do not survive contact with his own essay. His prohibition on any metaphor familiar from print would ban most of his own prose, and Pullum and others have made the general case that Orwell's rules are unfollowable as stated. Take the trichotomy, which is an observation, and leave the rules, which are a performance.

You cannot get out of metaphor, so the goal is audit

The tempting conclusion from all this is to write literally, and it is not available. Try describing what capital does without buffer, absorb, cushion, hold, cover or support, and you will find the alternatives are also metaphors from other domains. Abstract technical domains are talked about by mapping, and refusing to map means refusing to write.

So the discipline is the one in step 3 and it is modest by design. Know which metaphors your document is running on, know what each entails, and check the entailments that matter against your case. Where an entailment is false and load-bearing, either state the mechanism literally, which usually means giving a number, or choose a different metaphor whose entailments hold.

Which produces the one genuinely actionable recommendation in the lecture. When a dead metaphor's entailments are actively wrong for your case, revive it deliberately in order to kill it, in a single sentence, once. "Capital is described as a buffer, which suggests it sits idle until a loss arrives; it earns a return every day and the cost of holding it is the point at issue here." That sentence does more work for a lay reader than a page of explanation, because it removes an inference they were going to make anyway.

Common failure Auditing metaphors that are not doing any work. Most figurative language in a document is dead vocabulary carrying no live inference, and running the three questions on all of it produces a paralysed writer and a document full of stilted circumlocutions. The audit is for metaphors that are load-bearing: the ones in your finding, your recommendation, and any explanation aimed at a non-specialist. Everything else can stay dead.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Run the three-question audit on the central metaphor of a document you have written: name the source domain, list four entailments, mark each true, false or unknown. Report any false entailment that was load-bearing.
  2. Then compose. Choose the metaphor you will use to explain one technical concept to a non-specialist, from at least two candidates, and justify the choice by its entailments rather than its vividness.
  3. State the conceptual metaphor thesis and say why it makes metaphor's classification as a trope doubtful.
  4. What does risk-as-substance entail that is false, and what does that concealment cost?
  5. Give the entailments of contagion and say when they hold and when they mislead. What does the word do to an analysis of a common-shock event?
  6. Compare capital-as-buffer with capital-as-constraint. What does each make visible and each hide?
  7. Why is a model better described as a map than as a machine? What does the map metaphor handle badly?
  8. Give Orwell's three states, say which he attacks, and say where this lecture disagrees with him.
  9. Distinguish the weak from the strong conceptual-metaphor thesis. Which does the audit need, and who has criticised the other?
  10. Give the mixed metaphor test and apply it to the capital-floor sentence. What is the usual cure in professional prose?
  11. When should you revive a dead metaphor deliberately, and what does doing so accomplish?
