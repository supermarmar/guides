Point of view, person and tense

Person decides whether a claim is attributable, and attributable claims are the only ones a reader can challenge. So the choice is an accountability decision wearing the clothes of a style decision, and the institutional "we" is where it hides.

Lecture 4 of 7 Reading time, 32 minutes Source: Hyland on self-mention; Biber on register
Step 1

The 30-second version

Each grammatical person licenses a different kind of claim. "I" commits a named individual. "We" commits something, and which thing depends on a referent the sentence usually does not supply. Third person attributes to a function. The agentless construction attributes to nobody, which is module 2's target.

The institutional "we" is the case that matters, because it is systematically ambiguous between at least five referents and the ambiguity does real work. "We did not identify a control failure" means either that the review looked and found nothing, which is a statement about the scope of an exercise, or that no control failure exists, which is a claim about the world. Those are different assertions with different evidential requirements, and one word covers both.

Tense carries a load that is easier to miss and simpler to fix. A finding belongs in the present tense, because the deficiency persists: the model understates downturn LGD. Written in the past tense it reads as history, and an institution whose findings are all past-tense has framed its open items as closed without deciding to.

The perfect does something else again. "The threshold was overridden" closes the event; "the threshold has been overridden" asserts that the override still stands. That distinction is available in every finding you write and it is almost never made deliberately.

Feynman test Who is making this claim, and can a reader find out? That is the person question. And is this thing still true, or was it true once? That is the tense question. Both get decided by habit, and both change what the sentence asserts.
What is beyond the source The person and tense distinctions are grammatical commonplaces and Hyland's self-mention findings are cited accurately. Three arguments are the lecture's own. The first is step 3's five-way analysis of the institutional "we" and the substitution audit that resolves it. The second is step 4's claim that present-tense findings are live and past-tense findings read as closed, so tense is a governance signal worth checking in a house style. The third is step 5's attributability test, which resolves the tension between module 2's insistence on named actors and module 7's defence of the impersonal: the impersonal is compression when a reader can find out who, and concealment when they cannot.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the ambiguity of "we" has consequences beyond style. A supervisor reading "we found no evidence of override" needs to know whether the review tested for overrides, and the sentence does not say. If the review did not test, the sentence is true and misleading; if it did, the sentence understates what was established. Both readings are available and the writer has usually not chosen between them.

The second reason is that person governs challengeability, which is the property that makes a document useful in a governance process. A claim attributed to a named function can be contested by that function; a claim attributed to nobody can only be contested in general terms, which is why the agentless construction produces meetings where everyone disagrees with a document and nobody disagrees with anyone.

The third reason is tense, and it is the part of this lecture with the highest ratio of effect to effort. Rewriting a finding from past to present takes one word and changes whether the reader treats it as an open deficiency or a historical event. In a report tracking twenty findings, the tense pattern is a stronger signal about the institution's posture than any of the severity ratings.

And a reason about what you actually control. House style usually fixes person for you, so the room here is narrower than the analysis suggests. Tense is almost never specified by a house style, which means it is available in every document, and the lecture puts most of its practical weight there for that reason.

Step 3

The mental model

What each person licenses, the five referents of "we", and the tense grid.

Self-mention
Hyland's metadiscourse category for explicit authorial reference. His disciplinary comparisons find it far more frequent in the humanities and social sciences than in the hard sciences and engineering, which is evidence that person is a disciplinary convention rather than a matter of clarity.
Attributability
Whether a reader can determine who is making a claim. The property that makes a claim challengeable, and therefore the property that governance depends on.
Present perfect
"Has been overridden". A past action with asserted continuing relevance, distinct from the simple past, which closes the event. The difference is between an override that still stands and one that happened.
Step 4

The detail

The substitution audit for "we"

FOR EVERY "WE", SUBSTITUTE THE SPECIFIC REFERENT AND SEE WHETHER THE SENTENCE SURVIVES. "We did not identify a control failure." -> the review team did not identify one TRUE, and a statement about what was looked for. -> the bank has no control failure A much larger claim, unsupported by a review of this scope. VERDICT Ambiguous in a way a supervisor will resolve against you. Rewrite: "The review tested override authorisation for the 2024 monitoring cycle and found no unauthorised override. It did not test the origination controls." Longer, and it now says what was and was not examined. "We consider the calibration inadequate." -> the validation function considers it inadequate TRUE and appropriate. The finding belongs to the function. VERDICT Fine. Referent 2 is the legitimate professional use, and the reader knows which function because the document has a cover. "We hold 120 basis points of headroom." -> the bank holds it TRUE. Referent 3, unambiguous in context. VERDICT Fine, but note that this "we" and the previous one are different entities in the same document. A validator writing as the institution and as the reviewer within three pages has blurred the independence the document exists to demonstrate. >>> THIS IS THE SUBTLE ONE. If your report says "we consider the model inadequate" and "we hold adequate capital", the second "we" is the party the first "we" is reviewing. "We can see from table 3 that recovery rates fall." -> writer plus reader Referent 4, and harmless, though module 5's lecture 2 would cut it: "Recovery rates fall (table 3)." THE RULE Never let two different referents of "we" appear in one document without marking the difference. Use "the review" for the function and name the institution for the institution.

The third case is the one worth carrying away. A validation report that uses "we" for both the reviewing function and the reviewed institution has, in a small grammatical way, dissolved the distinction the document exists to maintain. Nobody notices while drafting because both uses are idiomatic, and a supervisor reading for independence notices immediately.

Tense across a findings register

What you are sayingTenseExample, and what the wrong tense would assert
One finding, five sentences, four tenses, all deliberate
What the review did Simple past "The review tested 36 months of recovery data." Present would be wrong: the testing is finished.
The deficiency itself Present "The estimation window contains no severe downturn." Past ("contained") would say it has been remedied, which is a claim about the future stated in the past tense.
A past act still in force Present perfect "The amber classification has been overridden and not reinstated." Simple past would close it; the perfect says the override still governs today's reporting.
Consequence under stress Conditional "Under the severe scenario the estimate would understate loss by 30 to 50 basis points." Present would assert this has happened.
The required action Present or imperative, with a date "The model owner rebuilds the window on a sample including 2008, by 31 March." Future ("will rebuild") reports an expectation rather than setting a requirement.

The second row carries the governance point. A finding written as "the window contained no severe downturn" is grammatically past and semantically a claim that the situation has changed, and it has not. Institutions where every open finding is written in the past tense have adopted a posture towards their own findings without anybody deciding to, and it is visible in a five-minute scan of a findings register.

Where the second person earns its place

Professional writing avoids "you" almost entirely and there is one context where it is the right choice. A recommendation addressed to a named owner, where the point is that a specific person must act, is clearer and harder to evade in the second person than in any impersonal alternative.

Compare three forms of the same ask. "It is recommended that the estimation window be rebuilt" names nobody and can sit in a register for two years. "The model owner rebuilds the estimation window by 31 March" names a role and is a considerable improvement. "You rebuild the estimation window by 31 March, using a sample that includes 2008" is uncomfortable, unambiguous, and appropriate in a covering note to the person concerned even where the report itself uses the third person.

Which suggests a practical split that most institutions do not make. The report is a record and uses the third person; the covering note to the owner is an instruction and can use the second. Writing both in the same impersonal register means the instruction has been delivered in the voice of a record, and records do not get acted on.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Resolving module 2 against module 7

This course has said two things that sit awkwardly together. Module 2's lecture 2 insisted that characters belong in subject position and that agentless prose is the characteristic vice of institutional writing. Module 7's lecture 7 then conceded that "the review noted" is the correct professional realisation, because a finding belongs to a function rather than to whoever held the pen. Both arguments are sound and they need reconciling rather than alternating.

The reconciliation is the attributability test. An impersonal construction is legitimate compression when a reader who needs to know who can find out, and it is concealment when they cannot. "The review found" is compression: the review has a name, a scope, a date and a signatory on the cover, so the actor is recoverable in one step. "It was determined that the metric should be reclassified" is concealment, because nothing in the document or its file lets a reader recover who determined it, and that is precisely the fact a governance reader needs.

So the rule is not about grammatical form at all. Two sentences with identical syntax can fall on opposite sides of it, and the question is whether the document, taken with its own cover and record, makes the actor findable. Where it does, use the impersonal freely and stop worrying about module 2's stricture. Where it does not, the passive is doing the work Orwell accused it of, and a named actor is the only honest repair.

That test has a pleasant property: it is checkable by someone other than the author. Hand your document to a colleague, point at an impersonal claim, and ask who. If they can answer from the document, it is compression.

Person is a disciplinary convention, not a clarity property

Hyland's work on self-mention across disciplines shows the first person is not distributed according to anything like clarity. It is markedly more frequent in philosophy, marketing and applied linguistics than in physics, biology and engineering, and the pattern reflects what each field takes a claim to be: a position an author occupies, or a result the method produced.

That finding is liberating in one direction and constraining in the other. It means the ban on the first person in your house style is a convention rather than a discovery about readers, so nobody should feel that "we tested" is a lapse in rigour. It also means the convention is real and carries meaning within its field, so violating it signals something whether or not you intended a signal. A validation report written in the first person singular reads as an individual's opinion in a genre where opinions are supposed to belong to functions.

Which is the honest scope of this lecture. You are usually not choosing person, you are inheriting it, and the useful move is to know what the inherited convention asserts. What you always control is tense, and tense is where the unexamined claims are.

Tense consistency, and the one deliberate shift worth making

Within a passage, tense should be stable, because an unmotivated shift makes the reader hunt for a change in time reference that is not there. The commonest professional example is a method section that drifts between "the review tested" and "the review tests" from paragraph to paragraph, which reads as carelessness and is.

There is one shift worth making on purpose and it is the finding itself. In a chronological account written in the past, moving to the present for the statement of the deficiency marks it as the one thing that is not history. "Monitoring ran monthly through the summer. The amber classification was overridden in March. The estimation window contains no severe downturn, and the calibration is therefore not downturn-representative." The tense shift does the work a heading or a bold face would do, and it does it inside the prose.

Which is the general lesson worth taking from a lecture about grammar. Person and tense are not decoration on the content; they are part of what is asserted. A document whose person and tense were chosen by habit has made a set of claims about attribution and persistence that nobody wrote and nobody checked, and readers will act on them anyway.

Common failure Using the inclusive "we" in a document that also uses the institutional "we". "As we can see from table 3" invites the reader inside the writer's viewpoint, and three paragraphs later "we hold adequate capital" puts them outside it as a member of the reviewed entity. The reader is being moved in and out of the institution without warning. Reserve the inclusive "we" for teaching material, which is what it belongs to, and keep it out of anything that also speaks as an organisation.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Run the substitution audit over every "we" in a document of yours. Report whether two different referents appear, and whether one of them is the entity the other is reviewing.
  2. Then compose. Take a findings register and check the tense of each finding. Rewrite any past-tense open finding in the present, and report how many there were.
  3. Give the five persons and what each licenses. Which one is not a person at all?
  4. Name the five referents of "we" and give the two readings of "we did not identify a control failure". Why does the difference matter to a supervisor?
  5. Why does a finding belong in the present tense? What does the past tense assert instead?
  6. Distinguish the simple past from the present perfect, using the override example.
  7. Give the tense for each of: what the review did, the deficiency, a past act still in force, a stressed consequence, a required action.
  8. Where does the second person earn its place, and what split between documents does that suggest?
  9. State the attributability test and use it to reconcile module 2 with module 7. Why is it not a rule about grammatical form?
  10. What does Hyland's self-mention evidence show about person, and in what way is that both liberating and constraining?
  11. Which tense shift is worth making deliberately, and what does it accomplish?
