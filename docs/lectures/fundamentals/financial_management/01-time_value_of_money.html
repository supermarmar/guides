Lectures / Financial management

Time value of money

The single most important idea in finance: a pound today is worth more than a pound tomorrow, and the maths to translate between the two is the bedrock of every valuation you will ever do.

Lecture 1 of 9 Reading time, 35 minutes Source: UP / Time-Value of Money.pdf
Step 1

The 30-second version

If someone offers you 1,000 GBP today or 1,000 GBP in a year's time, you take it today. The reason is not impatience. It is that you could invest the money for a year and end up with more than 1,000 GBP. That growth rate is the time value of money. Every financial decision (taking a loan, buying a bond, valuing a business, building a pension) is at heart an exercise in translating cash flows from one point in time to another, using this rate.

The two directions are future value (what is today's money worth later?) and present value (what is later money worth today?). Both use the same compound-interest engine. The rest of this lecture is a guided tour through that engine, the four ways of expressing the interest rate, and the actuarial limit (continuous compounding) that makes everything cleaner.

Feynman test Imagine a magic pipe. Money you drop in today flows out the other end, bigger, in a year's time. The rate at which it grows is the interest rate. Time value of money is just the recognition that the pipe exists. To compare any two cash flows happening at different times, you have to push them both through the pipe (forwards or backwards) until they arrive at the same date. Then you can compare them properly.
What's beyond the source The UP source covers discrete compounding (annual, semi-annual, quarterly, etc.). This lecture extends to continuous compounding and the force of interest, which is the actuarial standard, and shows the relationships between all four ways of quoting a rate (nominal, effective, discount, force). It also adds the Fisher equation for real vs nominal returns.
Step 2

Why this matters

Time value of money is not a topic. It is the syntax of finance. Every later lecture in this course depends on it. Bond pricing is a present-value calculation. WACC is the discount rate that makes the present value of future cash flows equal to today's market value of a firm. NPV is the present value of project cash flows minus the initial outlay. Dividend discount models are present values of future dividends. Even derivatives pricing reduces, in the end, to discounting risk-neutral expected payoffs.

Get this lecture wrong and nothing later makes sense. Get it right and everything later is just specialised vocabulary for the same idea.

Step 3

The mental model

Picture a horizontal time axis. On it sit cash flows: positive ones (money in) above the line, negative ones (money out) below. Any cash flow can be "rolled forward" or "discounted backward" to any other date, using a single rate i. The trick is to land all your cash flows on the same date before you compare them.

A worked example to anchor the picture: invest 1,000 GBP today at 5% per year, compounded annually. In 10 years it is worth

$$ \text{FV} = 1{,}000 \cdot (1.05)^{10} \approx 1{,}628.89 $$

Going the other way: a promise of 1,628.89 GBP in 10 years, discounted at 5%, is worth 1,000 GBP today.

Step 4

The detail

The fundamental compound interest formula

The starting point. A single amount PV invested today at a periodic rate i, compounded once per period for n periods, grows to

$$ \text{FV}_n = \text{PV} \, (1 + i)^n $$

Invert it to discount:

$$ \text{PV} = \frac{\text{FV}_n}{(1 + i)^n} $$

The factor (1 + i)n is the accumulation factor; its reciprocal is the discount factor. Most financial-calculator buttons (FV, PV, N, I/YR, PMT) are wrappers around these two operations.

Intra-year compounding

If interest is paid m times per year (semi-annual: m = 2, quarterly: m = 4, monthly: m = 12) at a nominal annual rate i, each compounding period applies a rate of i / m. After n years, you have compounded n · m times:

$$ \text{FV}_n = \text{PV} \left(1 + \frac{i}{m}\right)^{n m} $$

Increasing m increases FV, because interest earns interest sooner. The growth tapers as m grows, approaching a limit.

Continuous compounding and the force of interest

Take the limit as m tends to infinity. Using the standard result $\lim_{m \to \infty} (1 + x/m)^m = e^x$:

$$ \lim_{m \to \infty} \left(1 + \frac{i}{m}\right)^{n m} = e^{i n} $$

So under continuous compounding, with force of interest $\delta$:

$$ \text{FV}_n = \text{PV} \cdot e^{\delta n}, \quad \text{PV} = \text{FV}_n \cdot e^{-\delta n} $$

The force of interest is the instantaneous rate of growth of the investment. It is what falls out of differential calculus if you write down "money grows at a rate proportional to its current value": $\frac{dA}{dt} = \delta A$, whose solution is $A(t) = A(0) \, e^{\delta t}$.

For the actuary The force of interest is the SOA / IFoA staple. Annuities-certain, life contingencies, mortality tables and pricing models all run on $\delta$ rather than discrete i. The discrete version is a special case where you only look at the function at integer multiples of one period.

The four ways of quoting a rate

This is where students get tripped up. A bond paying 6% "nominal annual, compounded semi-annually" earns more than 6% over a year. Always check which of these four numbers you are looking at.

NameSymbolDefinition
Nominal annual rate$i^{(m)}$Quoted annual rate, paid m times per year. Each payment is $i^{(m)} / m$.
Effective annual rate$i$ or $r_{\text{EFF}}$The single equivalent rate that compounds to the same year-end amount.
Annual discount rate$d$The rate quoted in advance, used for treasury bills and similar discount instruments.
Force of interest$\delta$Continuous compounding rate. The "true" rate, with no period attached.

The conversion identities you must know by heart:

$$ 1 + i = \left(1 + \frac{i^{(m)}}{m}\right)^m = e^{\delta} = \frac{1}{1 - d} $$

So, given any one rate, you can compute the other three. Example: $i^{(2)} = 6\%$ means $i = (1.03)^2 - 1 = 6.09\%$ and $\delta = \ln(1.06) = 5.83\%$ approximately. Three different numbers describing the same thing.

Annuities: regular streams of equal cash flows

An annuity is a sequence of equal payments A at regular intervals. Two variants:

Ordinary annuity
Payments at the end of each period. The standard convention in finance. Mortgages, bond coupons, salaries.
Annuity due
Payments at the beginning of each period. Rents, insurance premiums, lease payments.

Future value of an ordinary annuity (intra-year):

$$ \text{FV} = A \cdot \frac{(1 + i/m)^{n m} - 1}{i/m} $$

Present value of an ordinary annuity:

$$ \text{PV} = A \cdot \frac{1 - (1 + i/m)^{-n m}}{i/m} $$

An annuity due adds one extra period of compounding to every cash flow: multiply either result by $(1 + i/m)$.

Actuarial shorthand uses $a_{\overline{n}|i}$ for the PV factor and $s_{\overline{n}|i}$ for the FV factor of an ordinary annuity, with dots above for an annuity due. These are interchangeable with the formulas above and just save space when writing.

Sinking funds and bond amortisation

Two practical applications of the annuity formulas.

Sinking fund
Deposit A per period to reach a target FV. Solve $A = \text{FV} \cdot \frac{i/m}{(1+i/m)^{nm} - 1}$. Used for asset replacement, debt repayment, defined-benefit pension funding.
Bond amortisation
Pay off a loan PV with equal periodic payments A. Solve $A = \text{PV} \cdot \frac{i/m}{1 - (1+i/m)^{-nm}}$. Every payment is split between interest and principal. Early payments are mostly interest; later payments mostly principal.

The balance outstanding after k payments is the present value of the payments still to come:

$$ B_k = A \cdot \frac{1 - (1 + i/m)^{-(n-k)m}}{i/m} $$

This is what your remaining mortgage balance is at any time. Banks use this exact formula on monthly statements.

Irregular cash flows

Most real-world cash-flow patterns are not equal. For a stream $\{CF_t\}$ at times $\{t\}$, the present value is just the sum of each cash flow discounted individually:

$$ \text{PV} = \sum_{t} \frac{CF_t}{(1 + i)^t} $$

This is the formula behind NPV (when one of the cash flows is the initial outlay), behind DCF valuation (when the cash flows are future free cash flows), behind bond pricing (when the cash flows are coupons and the redemption amount). All of them are this sum, with different cash flows plugged in.

The principle of equivalence

The full statement: two cash-flow streams are equivalent if they have the same present value at some common date, computed at the same interest rate. Three implications:

  • Choice of date is irrelevant, as long as both streams are translated to it.
  • Choice of rate is not irrelevant. The same two streams can be equivalent at one rate and very different at another. This is what makes the discount rate the single most important assumption in any valuation.
  • You can compare anything: a lump sum and an annuity, two annuities of different shapes, an irregular stream and a perpetuity. Reduce both to PV at the same rate, then compare.

The UP source distinguishes two principles ("time value of money" when totals are equal, "principle of equivalence" when totals differ). They are the same principle; the first is a special case of the second.

Real vs nominal: the Fisher equation

Inflation eats at returns. If your money grows at 7% but inflation is 3%, you are not 7% richer in real terms, you are about 4% richer. The exact relationship is the Fisher equation:

$$ (1 + i_{\text{nominal}}) = (1 + i_{\text{real}})(1 + \pi) $$

where $\pi$ is the inflation rate. The approximation $i_{\text{real}} \approx i_{\text{nominal}} - \pi$ is fine for small rates but breaks down at high inflation. In SA, Argentina or Turkey, use the full formula.

Long-horizon valuations almost always need to think in real terms. A pension promise made today, payable in 30 years, must be funded with assets that grow at a real rate sufficient to preserve the recipient's purchasing power.

A worked summary example

A 5-year retail bond pays semi-annual coupons of 6% (so 3% per coupon) on a face value of 100,000 GBP, redeeming at par. The market yield to maturity is 8% nominal annual, compounded semi-annually (so 4% per period). The price is the PV of all future cash flows:

Cash flows: 10 coupons of 0.03 x 100,000 = 3,000 GBP (at t = 0.5, 1.0, ..., 5.0 years) 1 redemption of 100,000 GBP (at t = 5.0 years) Discount at 4% per half-year period: PV of coupons = 3,000 x [1 - 1.04^-10] / 0.04 = 24,332.65 PV of redemption = 100,000 / 1.04^10 = 67,556.42 Bond price = 91,889.07

The bond trades below face value (a "discount bond") because the market yield is higher than the coupon rate. If the yield were lower, the bond would trade above face value (a "premium bond"). Lecture 3 develops this in full.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Every valuation reduces to a discount rate and a forecast

Once you accept that every cash flow can be moved through time using the compound-interest engine, every valuation is two things: a forecast of future cash flows, and a discount rate. Spending energy on the discount rate is usually where the action is, because small changes in the rate compound into very large changes in present value over long horizons. A 30-year cash flow discounted at 5% has half the present value it has at 2.5%, roughly.

The discount rate is not the interest rate on your bank account

This is the most common student misconception. The correct discount rate for valuing a stream of cash flows is the rate of return you could earn on an alternative investment of equivalent risk. For risk-free flows, this is a government-bond yield. For risky flows (corporate cash flows, project flows), it must include a risk premium. Lecture 5 (cost of capital and WACC) builds this idea in full.

Why actuaries prefer the force of interest

For an actuary working with continuous-time mortality, the force of interest $\delta$ is the only rate that composes cleanly with the force of mortality $\mu_x$. The standard expression for the present value of a continuous life annuity

$$ \bar{a}_x = \int_0^\infty e^{-\delta t} \cdot {}_t p_x \, dt $$

requires $\delta$, not i. The discrete formulas are convenient for bond mathematics; the continuous formulas are unavoidable in life insurance and pensions.

Why the same loan can have three different "interest rates"

A mortgage advertised at 5% almost always means 5% nominal annual, with monthly compounding (so each month: 5%/12 = 0.4167%). The effective annual rate is $(1.004167)^{12} - 1 = 5.116\%$. The APR you see in disclosures usually equals the nominal rate, but in some jurisdictions includes fees. Always check the convention before comparing.

Common pitfalls

Watch out Three traps. (1) Mixing nominal and effective rates in the same calculation. Always convert. (2) Forgetting that annuity due payments earn one extra period of compounding. (3) Discounting cash flows of different risk profiles at the same rate. A risky flow needs a higher rate; otherwise its PV is overstated.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Explain in plain English why a pound today is worth more than a pound in a year, with no reference to interest rates.
  2. State the four rates ($i^{(m)}$, $i$, $d$, $\delta$) and the conversion identity linking them.
  3. Derive the formula for the present value of an ordinary annuity from first principles, by summing a geometric series.
  4. A bank quotes 8% nominal annual, compounded monthly. Your friend's bank quotes 8.2% effective annual. Which gives the better return on a 5-year deposit? Explain.
  5. The Fisher equation gives an exact relationship between nominal and real returns. When does the linear approximation $i_{\text{real}} \approx i_{\text{nominal}} - \pi$ fail?
  6. Why is the choice of discount rate more important than the precision of the cash-flow forecast in most valuations?
  7. Write the present value of a 30-year ordinary annuity of 1,000 GBP per year at 5% as a single closed-form expression. Compute it.
