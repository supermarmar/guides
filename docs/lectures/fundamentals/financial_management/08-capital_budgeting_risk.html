Capital budgeting: risk and uncertainty

Lecture 7 produced a number. This one asks how much to believe it. Four tools, in increasing order of what they add: sensitivity finds the fragile input, scenarios make the combinations coherent, simulation replaces the point estimate with a distribution, and decision trees value the thing none of the others can see, which is that you get to change your mind.

Lecture 8 of 9 Reading time, 45 minutes Source: UP / Capital Project Appraisal 2, extended
Step 1

The 30-second version

A net present value is one number produced from a dozen forecasts, and it is the mean of a distribution nobody has drawn. Everything in this lecture is a way of recovering the distribution, or of deciding without needing it.

Carrying forward lecture 7's project, an outlay of 275 against four annual inflows of 100 at a 12 per cent cost of capital, the net present value is 28.735 and the internal rate of return 16.88 per cent. Sensitivity analysis asks how far each input can move before that turns negative, and the answer is that the annual inflow has the thinnest margin at 9.46 per cent, which is where the investigation should go.

Simulation replaces the point estimate with its distribution. On independent inflows with a standard deviation of 22, the mean net present value is unchanged at 28.7, as it must be, and the probability of destroying value is 19.7 per cent. A positive 28.7 with a one-in-five chance of loss is a different decision from a positive 28.7 with a one-in-fifty chance, and the point estimate cannot distinguish them.

Two results reframe the standard practice. Discounting at a risk-adjusted rate is equivalent to applying certainty-equivalent factors that decay geometrically, so a constant rate embeds the assumption that risk compounds at a constant rate. When the risk actually resolves early, that assumption understates the project by 49.6 per cent on the figures below. And an option to abandon after year one turns a project worth −22.7 into one worth +7.2: not a refinement of the number but a reversal of the decision.

Feynman test The answer is 28.7. Now: which guess is it most sensitive to, how often would it come out negative if the guesses were wrong in the ordinary way, and what could we do halfway through if it started going badly?
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that a point estimate invites a false comparison. Two projects with net present values of 28 and 32 look rankable, and if the first has a five per cent chance of loss and the second a forty per cent chance, the ranking is wrong. Nothing in the lecture 7 machinery reveals that, because nothing in it carries a second moment.

The second reason is that the tools are commonly run in the wrong order and at the wrong cost. Sensitivity analysis is nearly free and tells you where to spend the forecasting budget. Simulation is expensive and tells you the shape. Running the expensive one first, on inputs nobody has checked, produces a beautifully distributed answer built on the wrong assumptions.

The third reason is that the discount rate is doing more work than most users realise. Adding a risk premium is treated as the neutral, conservative default. Step 5 shows it is a specific and quite strong assumption about how risk behaves over time, and that for projects whose uncertainty resolves early it is badly wrong in a direction that rejects good projects.

And a fourth reason that is the largest in practice. A discounted cash flow appraisal projects one expected cash flow per period, which implicitly assumes management will do the same thing whatever happens. Real projects can be abandoned, expanded, delayed or switched, and those rights have value that the standard calculation is structurally incapable of seeing.

Step 3

The mental model

Four tools, each answering a question the previous one could not.

Sensitivity analysis
Vary one input at a time, holding the rest at base. Answers: which forecast is this decision hostage to? Cheap, immediate, and blind to the fact that inputs move together.
Scenario analysis
Vary a coherent set of inputs together, typically best, base and worst. Answers: what happens in a world that hangs together? Fixes the independence flaw in sensitivity analysis and gives you only three points of a continuum.
Simulation
Draw every uncertain input from a distribution, thousands of times, and collect the resulting net present values. Answers: what is the whole distribution, and how often is it negative? Introduced to capital budgeting by Hertz in 1964 and unchanged in principle since.
Decision trees and real options
Model the decisions management will take once uncertainty resolves. Answers: what is the flexibility worth? The only one of the four that changes the cash flows rather than describing them.

Step 4

The detail

Sensitivity: find the thinnest margin, not the biggest number

The useful output of a sensitivity analysis is not the swing in net present value, it is the break-even value of each input and the distance from the base case to it. A large swing on an input you are confident about matters less than a small margin on one you are guessing at.

BREAK-EVEN AND MARGIN OF SAFETY THE PROJECT outlay 275, four annual inflows of 100, cost of capital 12% annuity factor 3.03735, NPV = 28.735, IRR = 16.875% BREAK-EVEN ON EACH INPUT, holding the others at base variable break-even change needed margin annual inflow 90.539 -9.46% 9.46% initial outlay 303.735 +10.45% 10.45% discount rate 16.875% +4.88 pp 4.88 pp project life 4 years none zero READ THE LAST ROW CAREFULLY the project has NO margin on its life. Three years of inflows gives an NPV of -34.8; the fourth year is what makes it viable at all. Anything that shortens the project by a year destroys it. AND THE FIRST ROW IS THE ONE TO ACT ON a 9.46% shortfall in the annual inflow is well inside the range of ordinary forecasting error, and it is the tightest constraint of the four. THE PRACTICAL RULE rank the inputs by MARGIN, not by swing. Then ask, for the tightest one only, whether the forecast is good to that tolerance. If the answer is no, the analysis has told you what to go and find out.

Scenarios: the coherence that sensitivity throws away

Sensitivity analysis moves one input at a time, which is exactly what makes it cheap and exactly what makes it wrong. If a recession cuts your sales volume, it very likely also cuts your selling price and may raise your cost of capital. A one-at-a-time analysis says each of those alone is survivable, and the combination is what kills you.

Scenario analysis fixes it by constructing coherent states of the world. The discipline that makes it work is to build each scenario from a narrative first, then derive the inputs, rather than picking the pessimistic end of each input's range independently. A worst case assembled input by input is usually so extreme that it is dismissed, which is why the worst case in most appraisal packs carries no weight in the decision.

Its limitation is that three points are three points. It says nothing about how likely each is unless probabilities are attached, and if they are attached, the expected value across scenarios is only as good as the three states chosen to represent a continuum.

Simulation: the distribution behind the estimate

The technique was introduced to capital budgeting by David Hertz in the Harvard Business Review in 1964 and the principle has not changed: specify a distribution for each uncertain input, draw from all of them together, compute the net present value, and repeat until the shape of the output distribution is stable.

WHAT THE SIMULATION ADDS mean NPV 28.686 point estimate was 28.735 probability NPV < 0 19.72% 5th percentile -26.649 95th percentile 84.128 THE MEAN TELLS YOU NOTHING NEW, AND THAT IS THE POINT a linear function of the inputs has an expected value equal to the function of the expected inputs, so the simulation reproduces the point estimate. Everything simulation adds is in the second moment and the tails. WHERE IT WOULD DIFFER if the payoff were NON-LINEAR in the inputs, the mean would move too. That is exactly what a decision tree introduces, and it is why the option in step 5 changes the expected value rather than merely the spread. THE THREE MODELLING CHOICES THAT DRIVE THE ANSWER the distribution of each input, its standard deviation, and the CORRELATION between inputs. Independent inflows, as assumed here, are the most favourable assumption available: correlating them would leave the mean alone and widen the distribution substantially.

Two ways to charge for risk, and their exact relationship

Risk can be handled in the numerator or the denominator. The risk-adjusted discount rate discounts expected cash flows at a rate above the risk-free rate. The certainty-equivalent method shrinks each cash flow to the certain amount the firm would accept in its place, then discounts at the risk-free rate. They are not two philosophies, they are two parameterisations of one calculation, and setting them equal gives the exact relationship.

$$\frac{\alpha_t \, \text{CF}_t}{(1+r_f)^t} = \frac{\text{CF}_t}{(1+r)^t} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha_t = \left(\frac{1+r_f}{1+r}\right)^t$$

The certainty-equivalent coefficient implied by a constant discount rate therefore decays geometrically, and step 5 works out what that commits you to.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

A constant discount rate is a strong assumption about the shape of risk

WHAT THE RISK PREMIUM COMMITS YOU TO RISK-FREE 6%, RISK-ADJUSTED 12% alpha_t = (1.06 / 1.12)^t = 0.94643^t t alpha_t implied haircut 1 0.9464 5.4% 2 0.8957 10.4% 3 0.8477 15.2% 4 0.8023 19.8% SO ADDING SIX POINTS TO THE DISCOUNT RATE IS EXACTLY THE SAME AS ASSERTING THAT EACH YEAR'S CASH FLOW IS WORTH 5.36% LESS THAN THE PREVIOUS YEAR'S IN CERTAINTY TERMS, FOREVER, AT A CONSTANT RATE. THAT IS RIGHT FOR SOME PROJECTS a commodity producer whose cash flows depend on a price that follows a random walk really does face uncertainty that accumulates with the horizon. AND WRONG FOR OTHERS a technology project where year one settles whether the plant works. After that the cash flows are close to certain and the risk has GONE, not grown. WORKING THE SECOND CASE suppose the true certainty equivalents are 0.90 in year 1 and 0.98 thereafter, discounted at the risk-free rate certainty-equivalent NPV 57.033 risk-adjusted NPV at 12% 28.735 difference 28.298 THE CONSTANT DISCOUNT RATE UNDERSTATES THE PROJECT BY 49.6%, and it does so by charging year 4 a 19.8% haircut for a risk that was resolved by year 2. WHICH REVERSES THE USUAL INTUITION a risk premium feels prudent. On a front-loaded-risk project it is not prudent, it is simply wrong, and it is wrong in the direction of rejecting projects that should be accepted. THE DIAGNOSTIC IS ONE QUESTION does the uncertainty in this project grow with time, or resolve at a known point? If it resolves, price it in the numerator and discount at something close to the risk-free rate. If it accumulates, the risk premium is doing its job.

The option to abandon does not refine the answer, it reverses it

The deepest limitation of a discounted cash flow appraisal is not in the discounting. It is that projecting one expected cash flow per period assumes management is a spectator. Real projects come with rights: to abandon, to expand, to delay, to switch inputs or outputs. Those rights are worth something precisely because the future is uncertain, which means the standard calculation is least reliable exactly where risk is greatest.

VALUING ONE ABANDONMENT RIGHT THE SETUP after year 1 the outcome is known good state, probability 60%: inflows continue at 100 bad state, probability 40%: inflows fall to 40 the plant can be sold at the end of year 1 for 180 VALUE AT THE END OF YEAR 1, IF WE CONTINUE good state 240.183 bad state 96.073 salvage 180.000 so in the bad state abandoning is worth 83.927 more than continuing. The right to abandon is only valuable in one of the two states, which is what makes it an option. THE TWO APPRAISALS committed to continue NPV = -22.733 free to abandon NPV = +7.241 value of the option 29.974 CHECK IT DIRECTLY 0.40 x 83.927 / 1.12 = 29.974. The option is worth the probability-weighted, discounted gain in the state where it is exercised, exactly. READ THE TWO NPVs AGAIN the standard appraisal says REJECT. The appraisal that recognises the abandonment right says ACCEPT. This is not a refinement of a number, it is a reversal of a decision, and the difference is a right the firm already holds and was not asked to pay for. WHY THE STANDARD CALCULATION CANNOT SEE IT it averages the states BEFORE deciding: an expected inflow of 0.6(100) + 0.4(40) = 76 in each later year. Averaging first assumes the same action in both states. Deciding first and averaging second is what an option is, and the two orders differ by exactly Jensen's inequality on a payoff that is now convex. WHICH GENERALISES any managerial right creates convexity, and convexity means the expected value of the decision exceeds the decision on the expected value. That is why real-option value is always positive and why ignoring it always understates.

The tools in order, and what each is actually for

Set the four side by side and a clean division appears. Sensitivity, scenario and simulation are all descriptions of the same cash flows at increasing resolution: which input matters, what a coherent bad world looks like, and the full distribution. None of them changes the project. They change how much you know about a fixed object.

Decision trees and real options are different in kind. They change the cash flows, because they model a project in which management responds. That is why the first three can only widen or narrow your confidence in a number while the fourth can move the number itself, and it is why a firm with genuine operating flexibility will systematically undervalue its own projects if it stops at simulation.

The practical sequence follows from cost and from what each rules out. Run sensitivity first, because it is free and it identifies the two or three inputs worth modelling properly. Build scenarios next, because narrative coherence catches correlations that no input-by-input analysis will. Simulate only those inputs that sensitivity flagged, with correlations from the scenarios. And ask the decision-tree question, which is what would we actually do if this went badly, on every project regardless, because it costs nothing and it is the only one that can change the answer.

The thread through lectures 5 to 8 Lecture 5 built a discount rate, lecture 6 argued about the mix that produces it, lecture 7 applied it to cash flows, and this lecture finds that the rate carries an assumption about the time-shape of risk and that the cash flows omit the value of being able to change your mind. The discounted cash flow framework is not wrong; it is a model of a passive firm facing risk that grows with the horizon, and it should be used knowing which of those two conditions the project in front of you actually satisfies.
Where this goes wrong in practice A simulation with independent inputs, presented as a rigorous treatment of risk. Independence is the most optimistic correlation assumption available: it produces the narrowest distribution and the lowest probability of loss consistent with the marginal assumptions. The figures in step 4 are exactly this case, which is why they are labelled as such. If sales volume and selling price fall together in a recession, and they do, the honest distribution is materially wider than the one on this page.
Exam tip If asked to compare the risk-adjusted discount rate with the certainty-equivalent approach, the marks are for the relationship rather than the definitions. State that they are equivalent when the certainty-equivalent coefficient equals the ratio of discount factors, so a constant risk premium implies coefficients declining geometrically, and hence assumes risk accumulates with time. A further mark is available for the consequence: the risk-adjusted rate penalises projects whose uncertainty resolves early.
