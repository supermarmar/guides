Derivatives

Futures, options, swaps. The contracts that let you trade risk without trading the underlying asset. Bigger than every other market combined, and the source of every spectacular blow-up in finance.

Lecture 4 of 9 Reading time, 50 minutes Source: UP / Derivatives.pdf
Step 1

The 30-second version

A derivative is a contract whose value depends on the price of something else (the "underlying"). Three families to know: futures and forwards (obligations to trade at a fixed price on a fixed date), options (rights but not obligations to do the same), and swaps (agreements to exchange streams of cash flows). All three are tools for moving risk from one balance sheet to another, without moving the underlying asset.

Pricing every derivative reduces to one principle: no arbitrage. Whatever price the contract trades at, you should not be able to combine it with the underlying and risk-free borrowing or lending to make a guaranteed profit. From that one rule come the futures pricing formula, put-call parity, and (with one extra ingredient, volatility) Black-Scholes.

Feynman test Three ways to deal with a future puppy purchase. (1) Sign a contract today to buy a brown puppy in six weeks at 2,000 ZAR. You must take the puppy, whatever the price then is. That is a forward. (2) Pay 500 ZAR today for the right to buy the puppy in six weeks at 2,000 ZAR. If the puppy turns out grey instead, you walk away and lose only the 500. That is an option. (3) You swap your monthly grain payments (variable amount) for someone else's monthly hay payments (fixed amount). You both keep your underlying farms; only the payment streams switch hands. That is a swap.
What's beyond the source The UP source covers the descriptive basics. This lecture adds the no-arbitrage pricing formula for futures, the put-call parity identity (with proof sketch), Black-Scholes intuition and the five Greeks, six classic option strategies beyond plain calls and puts, and the post-2008 reshaping of derivatives markets (central clearing, ISDA, collateral).
Step 2

Why this matters

Derivatives are the world's largest financial market by notional. The BIS measures OTC derivatives at around 700 trillion USD outstanding (2024), versus global GDP of about 110 trillion. Every airline hedges fuel with futures, every multinational hedges currency with forwards, every bank hedges interest rate risk with swaps, every fund manager uses options to express views or protect portfolios. You cannot understand a modern bank or insurance company without understanding what is in its derivatives book.

You also cannot understand the disasters. Barings (1995, rogue trader on Japanese index futures), LTCM (1998, leveraged fixed-income arbitrage), AIG (2008, written credit default swaps), Archegos (2021, total-return swap leverage). Every one was a derivatives book that the firm's management did not understand. The instruments themselves are not the problem; the leverage and counterparty exposure they enable, used carelessly, is.

Step 3

The mental model

Three families along a spectrum of commitment. On the left, you must trade. In the middle, you may trade if it suits you. On the right, you exchange one stream for another without ever holding the underlying.

Step 4

The detail

Forwards and futures

The simplest derivative. Two parties agree today to trade an asset on a fixed future date at a fixed price. The "long" side agrees to buy; the "short" side agrees to sell. No money changes hands today; both sides have a contractual obligation.

Forward
Bespoke, privately negotiated, OTC. Used for tailor-made hedges: an importer locks in the EUR / ZAR rate for a specific invoice payable in 90 days. Counterparty risk: the other side might default.
Future
Standardised, exchange-traded, cleared through a central counterparty (SAFEX in South Africa, CME in the US, Eurex in Europe). The contract specifies the underlying, the size, the delivery date, the settlement method. Counterparty risk is collateralised through the clearing house.

Futures pricing: the no-arbitrage formula

Suppose the spot price of the underlying is $S_0$, the risk-free rate is $r$ (continuously compounded), and the contract matures in $T$ years. The fair forward price $F_0$ must satisfy

$$ F_0 = S_0 \cdot e^{rT} $$

The argument: if $F_0 > S_0 e^{rT}$, an arbitrageur borrows $S_0$, buys the asset, simultaneously sells a forward at $F_0$, and delivers at maturity. The arbitrageur owes $S_0 e^{rT}$ on the loan and receives $F_0$ from the contract. Pure profit. Other market participants would do the same until $F_0$ falls into line. The mirror argument bounds $F_0$ from below.

Add carry costs and income (dividends, storage, coupons) to get

$$ F_0 = (S_0 - I) \cdot e^{rT} $$

where $I$ is the present value of any income received from holding the underlying between now and $T$ (or, with the opposite sign for storage costs). This single formula prices stock index futures, bond futures, currency futures, and commodity futures with appropriate adjustments.

The clearing house and margin mechanics

Exchange-traded futures replace counterparty risk with margin. When you open a position, you post initial margin (a fraction of contract value). The clearing house then marks the position to market every day. Gains are credited to your margin account; losses are debited. If your balance falls below the maintenance margin, you receive a margin call to top back up to the initial margin level.

The daily P&L on a position with $N$ contracts of $n$ shares each is

$$ \text{VM}_t = N \cdot n \cdot (\text{CP}_t - \text{CP}_{t-1}) $$

where CP is the daily closing price. At maturity, futures are usually closed out (sell an offsetting contract) or cash-settled rather than physically delivered.

Why this matters Daily marking to market is the reason futures rarely produce a counterparty failure even in extreme markets. The losing side either keeps posting margin or is closed out by the clearing house. The whole system is engineered so that the maximum loss the clearing house could absorb in one day is roughly one margin period.

Options: the right but not the obligation

An option gives the holder a right to do something, in exchange for an upfront premium. Two basic types:

Call option
Right to buy the underlying at the strike price $K$ on / before expiry. The buyer profits if the underlying rises above $K$.
Put option
Right to sell the underlying at the strike price $K$ on / before expiry. The buyer profits if the underlying falls below $K$.

Two exercise styles:

European option
Can be exercised only at expiry. Cleaner mathematics; Black-Scholes prices these.
American option
Can be exercised at any time up to expiry. Worth at least as much as the European equivalent. Usually priced numerically (binomial tree, finite-difference).

The four basic payoff diagrams

At expiry, the option's payoff is determined by where the underlying $S_T$ sits relative to the strike $K$. Net of the premium $P$ paid, the four basic positions look like this:

The profit-and-loss formulas, ignoring brokerage and dividends:

$$ \text{Long call P\&L} = N \cdot \max(S_T - K, 0) - \text{Premium} $$

$$ \text{Long put P\&L} = N \cdot \max(K - S_T, 0) - \text{Premium} $$

Short positions are the negatives. The maximum loss when writing an uncovered call is unlimited (in principle), which is why option writing without an offsetting position is one of the great careers-ending strategies in finance.

Worked examples from the source

Long call: Jones pays 250 ZAR for a 3-month call on FBS Ltd at strike 50, contract size 100 shares. To break even the share must rise to 50 + 250/100 = 52.50. If FBS rises to 60 at expiry, his net profit is

$$ 100 \times (60 - 50) - 250 = 750 \text{ ZAR} $$

Long put: Peter pays 325 ZAR for a 6-month put on Bell Ltd at strike 40, contract size 100. Break-even: 40 - 325/100 = 36.75. If Bell falls to 30 at expiry:

$$ 100 \times (40 - 30) - 325 = 675 \text{ ZAR} $$

Put-call parity: the deep identity

For European options on a non-dividend-paying stock, with the same strike $K$ and the same expiry $T$:

$$ C - P = S_0 - K \cdot e^{-rT} $$

This is put-call parity. It says: a long call plus a short put (left side) is economically the same as a long stock plus a short risk-free bond paying $K$ at expiry (right side). The argument: both portfolios have payoff $S_T - K$ at expiry, regardless of whether $S_T > K$ or $S_T < K$. Two portfolios with identical payoffs must have identical prices today, or arbitrage is available.

This identity has three superpowers:

  • Replication: any one of the four positions can be built from the other three. You can synthesise a long stock position from a long call, a short put, and risk-free borrowing.
  • Pricing: if you know the price of a call, you immediately know the price of the equivalent put without further modelling.
  • Arbitrage screening: when put-call parity breaks (rare in liquid markets), it is a sign of either an illiquid quote, a dividend the model has missed, or a genuine arbitrage opportunity.

Black-Scholes intuition

Pricing a European call before expiry is non-trivial because the payoff depends on what $S_T$ might be, weighted by probability. The Black-Scholes formula (Fischer Black, Myron Scholes, Robert Merton; Nobel 1997) gives, under specific assumptions:

$$ C = S_0 \cdot N(d_1) - K \cdot e^{-rT} \cdot N(d_2) $$

$$ d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2) T}{\sigma \sqrt{T}}, \quad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma \sqrt{T} $$

where $N(\cdot)$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution and $\sigma$ is the underlying's volatility. The matching European put price follows from put-call parity. You do not need to memorise the formula. You do need to know the five inputs and how each one moves the price.

The five Greeks: option sensitivities

The Greeks measure how the option price changes when each input changes. Every options desk has these on the screen, every minute of the trading day.

GreekDefinitionSign for long callIntuition
Delta ($\Delta$)$\partial C / \partial S$Positive (0 to 1)How much the option moves for a 1 GBP move in the underlying. The hedge ratio.
Gamma ($\Gamma$)$\partial^2 C / \partial S^2$PositiveHow delta itself changes with the underlying. The curvature.
Theta ($\Theta$)$\partial C / \partial t$NegativeTime decay: how much option value erodes per day.
Vega ($\nu$)$\partial C / \partial \sigma$PositiveSensitivity to volatility. Long options are long vega.
Rho ($\rho$)$\partial C / \partial r$Positive for call, negative for putSensitivity to interest rates. Usually the least important.

The most operationally important is delta-hedging: an option desk that has sold a call holds $\Delta$ shares of the underlying to immunise itself against small moves. As the underlying moves, gamma forces the desk to re-hedge. The cost of re-hedging (the "gamma cost") is what the option premium pays for.

Option strategies: combining the building blocks

Real-world option positions are rarely a single naked call or put. They are combinations chosen to express a specific view (up, down, sideways, volatile) with a defined risk-return profile.

Covered call
Long stock + short call at higher strike. Generates income from the premium; caps upside above the strike. Standard yield-enhancement for an asset manager who is neutral-to-mildly-bullish.
Protective put
Long stock + long put at lower strike. Insurance against a fall. Standard portfolio protection.
Bull call spread
Long call at low strike + short call at high strike. Bets on a moderate rise. Caps both upside and downside.
Bear put spread
Long put at high strike + short put at low strike. Mirror of the bull spread, betting on a moderate fall.
Straddle
Long call + long put at the same strike. A bet that the underlying will move a lot, in either direction. Expensive (two premiums); pays off if there is volatility.
Strangle
Long call at high strike + long put at low strike. Cheaper than a straddle, but needs a bigger move to pay off.
Butterfly
Long call at low strike + 2 short calls at middle strike + long call at high strike. A bet that the underlying stays near the middle. Limited risk, limited reward.

Swaps: exchanging cash-flow streams

A swap is an agreement between two parties to exchange streams of cash flows according to a pre-agreed formula. The notional principal is usually not exchanged; only the cash flows derived from it.

Interest rate swap (IRS)
Party A pays party B a fixed rate on a notional principal; party B pays A a floating rate (historically LIBOR, now SOFR / SONIA / ESTR / Zaronia) on the same notional. Both legs settle in the same currency. The classic use: a company with a floating-rate loan swaps to fixed to lock in funding cost.
Currency swap
Two parties exchange streams of interest payments (and the notional) in two different currencies. Used by multinationals to fund in one currency and effectively borrow in another. Different from an FX forward in that it spans many periods.
Total return swap (TRS)
One party pays the total return on an asset (price change plus income); the other pays a financing leg. Used for synthetic exposure to an asset without owning it. Made famous by the Archegos collapse in 2021, which built ~50bn USD of equity exposure through TRS without showing up in public filings.
Credit default swap (CDS)
Buyer pays a periodic premium ("spread") to the seller; the seller pays a lump sum if a specified reference entity defaults. Insurance on a bond. Central to the 2008 crisis: AIG had written ~440bn USD of CDS protection it could not honour.

The pricing principle is the same as forwards: at inception, the present value of the two legs must be equal (so neither side pays the other to enter). The market then moves; one leg's PV diverges from the other; whichever side is "in the money" has counterparty exposure to the other.

The post-2008 reshaping of derivatives markets

Before 2008, most OTC derivatives were bilateral: party A and party B faced each other, with no central counterparty. Failure of one (Lehman, AIG) cascaded into the whole web. The 2009 G20 Pittsburgh commitments reshaped this:

  • Central clearing: standardised OTC derivatives must now clear through CCPs (LCH SwapClear, CME, Eurex Clearing). The CCP becomes the buyer to every seller and vice versa, with multilateral netting and margin requirements similar to futures exchanges.
  • Trade reporting: every derivative must be reported to a trade repository, giving regulators a view of the network.
  • Margin for non-cleared derivatives: bilateral OTC trades that cannot be cleared must post both initial and variation margin under the UMR (Uncleared Margin Rules) phased in 2016 to 2022.
  • ISDA documentation: standardised contractual terms via the ISDA Master Agreement and the Credit Support Annex (CSA), which governs collateral.

The result is a market that looks much more like an exchange than the pre-2008 OTC world, even for products that are still legally OTC.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Every derivative is a portfolio of Arrow-Debreu claims

The deepest pricing insight: any payoff function $f(S_T)$ can be replicated by a linear combination of European calls and puts at different strikes. The "elementary" claim is a payoff of 1 if $S_T = K$ and 0 otherwise (an Arrow-Debreu state-contingent claim). Calls and puts span those claims; combinations let you build any payoff. This is why options theory underpins all of modern derivatives pricing: complete the market with calls and puts, and you can price anything.

Volatility is the only input you cannot observe

Of Black-Scholes' five inputs, four are directly observable today ($S_0$, $K$, $r$, $T$). Volatility $\sigma$ is the only one that requires forecasting. Traders therefore quote options in terms of implied volatility: invert the formula to find the $\sigma$ that makes the model price equal the market price. Plot implied vol against strike (the volatility smile) and against time (the term structure) and you have a two-dimensional surface that summarises the market's view of risk. The smile became more pronounced after the 1987 crash and has stayed that way.

Counterparty credit risk is the hidden cost

A derivative is only worth what your counterparty pays. Before 2008, banks treated OTC derivatives as if they were risk-free, because counterparties were "too big to fail". 2008 demolished that assumption. Post-2008, the price of every OTC derivative is decomposed into the risk-free value plus a series of XVA adjustments (CVA, DVA, FVA, KVA, MVA) that capture counterparty risk, own credit, funding cost, capital cost and margin cost. The XVA desk is now one of the most quant-heavy parts of any investment bank.

The "Greek letters" model of risk

Once you start thinking in Greeks, the world looks different. A bond is delta to rates. An equity portfolio is delta to the market. An insurance company's annuity book is long theta (it benefits from time passing if the policyholder lives shorter than expected) and short vega to rates. You can decompose any balance sheet into a vector of Greeks and ask: which sensitivities am I happy to keep, which do I want to hedge? That decomposition is the modern view of enterprise risk management.

Common pitfalls

Watch out Four traps to memorise. (1) "Long" and "short" in derivatives mean buy and sell the contract, not the underlying. Long a put is bearish on the underlying. (2) The break-even on a long call is strike plus premium-per-share, not just the strike. (3) Writing uncovered (naked) calls has unlimited loss potential; writing puts has loss up to the strike (since the share cannot go below zero). (4) Put-call parity applies only to European options on a non-dividend-paying asset, in its simple form; adjust for dividends if the source mentions them.
