Financial analysis and ratios

Every decision in this course eventually shows up in three statements, and ratios are how you read it back out. The closing lecture takes two firms with an identical return on equity and shows that the headline number conceals everything that matters, then traces each component back to the lecture that produced it.

Lecture 9 of 9 Reading time, 45 minutes Source: UP / Financial Analysis, extended
Step 1

The 30-second version

A ratio is two numbers from the statements divided by each other, which makes them easy to compute and easy to over-read. The discipline is not in the arithmetic, it is in knowing what each ratio can and cannot distinguish.

The demonstration that organises the lecture: two firms with a return on equity of 14.40 per cent each, one a specialist manufacturer earning an 18 per cent operating margin on 0.83 asset turns with 1.50 times leverage, the other a grocer earning 5 per cent on 2.50 turns with 2.46 times. Identical headline, opposite businesses, opposite risk. The five-way DuPont decomposition is what separates them, and its five factors map almost exactly onto the five earlier lectures in this track.

Leverage is where the reading goes wrong most often. Holding operations fixed at a 10.80 per cent after-tax return on invested capital and letting the cost of debt rise with gearing, return on equity climbs to a peak of 13.45 per cent at 40 per cent gearing, falls thereafter, and turns negative by 80 per cent. Leverage magnifies a return, it never creates one.

And the cash conversion cycle carries the fact that no profit measure shows: the specialist runs a +112 day cycle so every 100 of extra sales consumes 26 of cash, while the grocer runs −12 days and releases 2.8. One firm's growth has to be funded, the other's funds itself.

Feynman test Two companies made the same return for their shareholders last year. One sells a few expensive things slowly with little borrowing; the other sells many cheap things quickly with a lot. The single number cannot tell you which you are looking at, and everything you care about is in the difference.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this is where the rest of the course becomes observable. The cost of capital from lecture 5, the financing mix from lecture 6, the projects accepted in lectures 7 and 8: none of them is visible directly, and all of them leave traces in the statements. Ratio analysis is the inverse problem, reasoning from the traces back to the decisions.

The second reason is that ratios are the most misused tool in finance precisely because they are the easiest. Any two numbers divide. A ratio only means something against a benchmark, and the three available benchmarks — the same firm over time, a peer group, and an industry norm — each have a failure mode that step 5 sets out.

The third reason is that the headline measures are composites, and composites hide compensating movements. A return on equity that is flat across two years can conceal a collapsing margin offset by rising leverage, which is a materially worse business reporting an unchanged number. Decomposition is not a refinement of ratio analysis, it is the whole of it.

And a fourth reason that closes the loop with the credit risk material elsewhere in this collection. Altman's Z-score, published in 1968, is a weighted combination of five ratios that predicts corporate failure, and it is the direct ancestor of the scorecards in the credit risk track. Ratio analysis is where credit modelling started.

Step 3

The mental model

Four families of ratio, one decomposition that ties them together, and two firms to test it on.

Profitability
Return on equity, return on assets, return on invested capital, operating and net margin. Answers: is the business earning more than it costs to fund? The comparison that matters is against the cost of capital from lecture 5, not against zero.
Efficiency
Asset turnover, inventory days, receivable days, payable days. Answers: how hard is the asset base working? These are the ratios that distinguish two firms with the same margin and different business models.
Liquidity and solvency
Current and quick ratios for the short term, gearing and interest cover for the long. Answers: can it pay next month, and can it survive a bad year? A firm can be profitable and insolvent, and these are the ratios that catch it.
Market
Price to earnings, price to book, dividend yield and cover. Answers: what does the market believe about the future? The only family whose inputs are not from the statements, which is exactly what makes them informative about expectations.

The decomposition itself is an identity, obtained by inserting terms that cancel:

$$\text{ROE} = \underbrace{\frac{\text{NI}}{\text{EBT}}}_{\text{tax burden}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{EBT}}{\text{EBIT}}}_{\text{interest burden}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Sales}}}_{\text{operating margin}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Sales}}{\text{Assets}}}_{\text{asset turnover}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Assets}}{\text{Equity}}}_{\text{leverage}}$$

DuPont componentWhat it measuresWhere it came from in this course
Tax burdenWhat the tax authority leavesLecture 6: the tax shield that makes debt cheap
Interest burdenWhat the lenders takeLecture 6: the financing mix
Operating marginPricing power and cost controlLecture 7: the projects accepted

Two of the five are financing, two are operations and one is tax. That division is the reason the decomposition is worth doing: it separates the part of the return that comes from running the business well from the part that comes from borrowing to run it.

Where leverage stops helping

The equity multiplier raises return on equity mechanically, which makes gearing look like free performance. It is not, for two reasons that the arithmetic separates: the interest burden ratio falls as gearing rises, partly offsetting the multiplier, and the cost of debt itself rises as lenders price the increased risk.

The cash the profit measures cannot see

Profit is an accrual concept and a business fails when it runs out of cash. The cash conversion cycle, inventory days plus receivable days less payable days, measures how long the firm's own money is tied up between paying for inputs and collecting from customers.

Composite scores: where ratio analysis became credit modelling

Altman's 1968 study combined five ratios into a single discriminant score for predicting corporate failure, and it remains the best-known composite in the field.

THE Z-SCORE, APPLIED TO BOTH FIRMS Z = 1.2 X1 + 1.4 X2 + 3.3 X3 + 0.6 X4 + 0.999 X5 X1 working capital / total assets X2 retained earnings / total assets X3 EBIT / total assets X4 market value of equity / total liabilities X5 sales / total assets zones: above 2.99 safe, 1.81 to 2.99 grey, below 1.81 distress firm X1 X2 X3 X4 X5 Z specialist A 0.2167 0.3583 0.1500 3.7500 0.8333 4.339 retailer B 0.0375 0.1562 0.1250 0.9474 2.5000 3.742 both safe, and again for different reasons: A on the strength of its equity cushion, B on asset productivity. NOTE WHAT THE WEIGHTS SAY X3, operating return on assets, carries the largest coefficient at 3.3, and X5, asset turnover, carries very nearly 1.0. So the model rewards asset PRODUCTIVITY heavily, which is why the thin-margin retailer scores well despite more than twice the gearing. AND WHY THIS BELONGS IN THIS COURSE the Z-score is a weighted sum of characteristics fitted to a binary outcome, discretised into bands with names. That is a scorecard. Everything in the credit risk collection's application scoring track is a descendant of this, built on more data with better statistics, and the structure has not changed in sixty years.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

A ratio has no meaning without a benchmark, and all three benchmarks are flawed

A current ratio of 1.4 is neither good nor bad. It acquires meaning only against a comparison, and there are exactly three available. Each is standard, each is used daily, and each fails in a specific way worth knowing before quoting it.

BenchmarkWhat it controls forHow it fails
The same firm over timeBusiness model, accounting policy, industryBlind to a whole industry deteriorating together, and broken by any change in accounting policy or acquisition
A chosen peer groupIndustry conditions and the cycleThe peer group is chosen by the analyst, and firms genuinely comparable on operations rarely match on financing
An industry averageIdiosyncratic firm effectsAverages a distribution that is usually bimodal, and the average firm in a fragmented industry does not exist

The practical response is not to pick one but to state which is being used and what it cannot see. A ratio quoted against a peer group without naming the group is an assertion, not an analysis, and the choice of group is frequently where the conclusion was actually made.

Every ratio inherits an accounting choice

Ratios are computed from reported figures and reported figures embed policy decisions. Depreciation method changes the asset base and therefore both return on assets and asset turnover, in opposite directions and by more in early years. Inventory valuation changes cost of sales and therefore margin. Whether leases are capitalised changes both assets and gearing. Revaluation of property changes equity and therefore every ratio with equity in it.

The consequence is narrow and important. A ratio comparison across two firms is only as good as the comparability of their accounting policies, and the analyst's job includes reading the accounting policy note before the ratios, not after. Where policies differ materially, the honest move is to restate one firm onto the other's basis and say so, rather than to present incomparable ratios side by side with a caveat nobody reads.

What the five components say about the two firms, and about the course

READING THE DECOMPOSITION AS A DIAGNOSIS SPECIALIST A: 0.72 x 0.8889 x 0.18 x 0.8333 x 1.50 the return comes from the OPERATING MARGIN, which is the only component materially above its peer's. that margin has to be defended. It implies pricing power, which implies a moat, which is exactly the Capacity and the competitive-advantage assessment from the credit frameworks. If the moat goes, nothing else in the chain compensates. RETAILER B: 0.72 x 0.65 x 0.05 x 2.50 x 2.4615 the return comes from TURNOVER and LEVERAGE together, with a margin so thin that a two-point movement in operating margin would halve the ROE. 5.0% to 3.0% margin, holding everything else, takes ROE from 14.40% to 8.64%. The same two points at firm A takes it from 14.40% to 12.80%. SO THE SAME SHOCK IS THREE TIMES AS DAMAGING TO B and the interest burden of 0.65 means B is already paying 35% of operating profit to lenders, so it has less room to absorb the shock before covenants bind. THIS IS THE WHOLE POINT OF THE DECOMPOSITION the ROE says the firms are identical. The components say one is a margin business with resilience and the other a volume business with none, and only the second reading supports a lending or investment decision.

And it closes the course, because each component points back to a lecture. The tax burden and the interest burden are the financing decisions from lecture 6, whose value came from the tax shield and whose cost was financial distress. The equity multiplier is the same decision seen from the balance sheet. The operating margin and the asset turnover are the accumulated result of the investment decisions from lectures 7 and 8, since a firm's asset base is the set of projects it accepted. And the standard against which all of it is judged is the cost of capital from lecture 5: a return on invested capital above the weighted average cost of capital creates value and one below it destroys value, however healthy the ratios look.

The one comparison that matters Every profitability ratio in this lecture is uninformative on its own and informative against the cost of capital. Firm A and firm B both earn 14.40 per cent on equity; whether either is creating value depends entirely on what their shareholders require, which lecture 5 computed and no financial statement reports. That is why financial analysis cannot be done from the statements alone, and why this lecture comes last rather than first.
Where this goes wrong in practice A ratio pack with forty measures and no hierarchy. Forty ratios computed from three statements are not forty pieces of information: most are algebraically related, several are the same quantity rearranged, and the reader has no way to know which two matter. The disciplined pack is small and structured: one decomposition of the headline return, one liquidity measure, one solvency measure, one efficiency cycle, and a stated benchmark for each. Anything beyond that should earn its place by answering a question somebody asked.
Exam tip If given two firms with the same return on equity and asked to compare them, decompose before you comment. The marks are for identifying which components differ and what that implies for resilience, not for recomputing the headline. A further mark is available for noting that leverage raises return on equity without changing return on invested capital, so the comparison must be made on the latter before any judgement about operating performance.
