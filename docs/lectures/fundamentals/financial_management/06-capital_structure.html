Lectures / Financial management

Capital structure and dividend policy

Two decisions every firm makes that move billions but, under specific assumptions, should not matter at all. The cleanest illustration in finance of how theory and practice differ, and why both are worth knowing.

Lecture 6 of 9 Reading time, 55 minutes Source: UP / Cap Structure & Div Policy.pdf
Step 1

The 30-second version

A firm's financing side has two big questions. First, capital structure: how much debt versus equity should we hold? Second, dividend policy: of the profits we make, how much do we pay out to shareholders, and how much do we keep? Both questions can be answered with elaborate theories, observed industry conventions, or just by copying what comparable firms do.

The famous result, from Modigliani and Miller in 1958 (capital structure) and 1961 (dividends), is that under perfect markets, neither decision affects firm value. Real markets are not perfect, so both decisions do matter. The interesting question is precisely why, and what the resulting "right" answer looks like. The answer combines tax effects, bankruptcy costs, asymmetric information, agency conflicts, and signalling.

Feynman test Imagine you run a successful bakery. You made 100,000 GBP profit this year. Two questions. (1) Should next year's expansion be funded by borrowing from the bank or by selling a stake to a partner? (2) Should you pay the 100,000 to yourself this year, or reinvest it in the business? The textbook answer in a frictionless world is "it does not matter; the bakery is worth the same either way." The real-world answer involves your tax rate, the bank's willingness to lend, whether your partner trusts you, and what your accountant says. Most of corporate finance is this same set of questions, scaled up.
What's beyond the source The UP source covers the descriptive factors. This lecture adds the three competing theories of capital structure (trade-off, pecking-order, market-timing), the M-M 1961 dividend irrelevance proof, signalling theory (Bhattacharya), agency theory (Jensen's free cash flow), Lintner's dividend-smoothing model, the clientele effect, and the modern shift from dividends to buybacks (the dominant payout mechanism for US firms since the late 1990s).
Step 2

Why this matters

Both decisions are worth real money. Apple has returned over 800 billion USD to shareholders since 2012, almost all through buybacks rather than dividends, and the choice of mechanism saved tens of billions in tax. Tesla pays no dividend and has never bought back stock, channelling every available pound into growth. BP and Shell each cut their dividends in 2020 for the first time in decades, and saw multi-billion-dollar share-price drops on the day of the announcement.

For an actuary, capital structure decisions show up in any embedded value calculation (life insurance), Solvency II / SAM internal model (re-leverage assumptions), pension fund liability matching (leverage in LDI), and credit risk modelling (PD as a function of gearing). Dividend policy shows up in dividend-discount valuation models, in the design of shareholder agreements, and in regulator-mandated dividend halts (which the PRA imposed on UK banks in 2020 and the SARB has used during stress).

Step 3

The mental model

The whole lecture comes down to a single picture: where does cash come from and where does it go to? Capital structure is the inflow side; dividend policy is the outflow side. In a perfect world the two decisions are independent and neither affects value. In the real world, they are tied together by free cash flow, and both depend on the same set of frictions.

Step 4

The detail

Part A: capital structure

What "capital structure" means

The mix of long-term financing sources. Conventionally three components:

Equity capital
Share capital plus share premium plus retained earnings. Permanent. Residual claim on profits and assets.
Short / medium-term debt
Bank loans, revolving credit facilities, commercial paper. Maturity under five years.
Long-term debt
Bonds, debentures, term loans with maturity over five years. The "non-current liabilities" line of the balance sheet.

The ratio of debt to equity (or debt to total capital) is the gearing ratio or financial leverage. UK and South African convention favours gearing as $D / (D + E)$ (debt over total capital); US convention favours $D / E$ (debt over equity). Both convey the same information; check which the question or model uses.

The three modern theories of capital structure

Lecture 5 introduced M-M. Three competing extensions developed since. Each is a model of why the M-M assumptions break, and each makes different predictions.

Trade-off theory (Kraus and Litzenberger 1973, Myers 1984)

The textbook view since the 1970s. M-M with corporate tax says debt is good (tax shield). Bankruptcy costs say too much debt is bad. The optimal capital structure balances the two:

$$ V_L = V_U + T_c D - \text{PV(bankruptcy costs)} - \text{PV(agency costs)} $$

The first-order condition gives an interior optimum, which firms should target. Static trade-off says you target it directly; dynamic trade-off (Fischer, Heinkel, Zechner 1989) accounts for adjustment costs that make firms only partially re-leverage toward the target.

Strongest prediction: profitable firms should have more debt (they have more taxable income to shield). Strongest empirical failure: profitable firms actually have less debt (Fama and French 2002). This finding led to the second theory.

Pecking-order theory (Myers and Majluf 1984)

Founded on asymmetric information: managers know more about the firm's prospects than outside investors. Issuing equity is therefore costly because the market interprets it as a signal that managers think the stock is overvalued. So firms prefer to fund in this order:

  1. Retained earnings: no signalling, no transaction cost.
  2. Safe debt: limited signalling because the value is bounded.
  3. Risky debt: more signalling but still less than equity.
  4. Equity: only as a last resort.

Implication: there is no target capital structure. The observed ratio is the cumulative result of past investment needs minus past internal funds. Profitable firms naturally end up with less debt because they generate enough internal funds to never need external financing. This neatly explains the empirical anomaly that trade-off theory cannot.

Market-timing theory (Baker and Wurgler 2002)

The newest of the three. Argues that managers issue equity when shares are richly valued (market-to-book ratio is high) and repurchase when undervalued. They do not try to hit a target ratio; they exploit market mispricing. Current capital structure is just the cumulative result of past timing attempts.

The empirical evidence: firms with high historical market-to-book ratios have lower leverage today, even decades later. This persistence is hard to explain under trade-off or pecking-order theories. Market timing has gained traction but does not displace the other two; it complements them.

Factors that affect the gearing decision in practice

Theory aside, the source lists six practical factors that any CFO weighs. Each one corresponds to a friction that breaks M-M:

Nature of business
Asset-heavy firms (utilities, real estate, banks) can borrow more because assets serve as collateral. Asset-light firms (software, consultancies) struggle to borrow against intangibles.
Business risk
Firms with volatile operating profits should hold less debt; the buffer must absorb shocks. Cyclicals tend to gear up at peaks (when profits are high and cushion exists) and de-gear at troughs.
Tax position
Interest deductibility benefits firms with taxable profits. Loss-making firms gain nothing from debt's tax shield, so should prefer equity.
Control
Issuing equity dilutes existing shareholders' control. Family firms, founder-led firms, and firms with concentrated ownership prefer debt for this reason.
Market view
Investors react to capital-structure changes. A dividend cut or large equity issue can hurt the share price for weeks; a buyback can boost it.
Availability
Some institutions are prohibited from buying highly-geared firms' debt. Tight financing conditions can force firms into structures they otherwise would not choose.

Financial leverage maths

The fundamental result: leverage magnifies both upside and downside of equity returns. Three identities to know.

$$ \text{EPS} = \frac{\text{Earnings available to shareholders}}{\text{Number of ordinary shares}} $$

$$ \text{ROE} = \frac{\text{Earnings available to shareholders}}{\text{Market value of equity}} $$

$$ \text{ROI} = \frac{\text{Operating profit}}{\text{Share capital} + \text{Loan capital}} $$

The pivotal comparison is ROI versus the after-tax cost of debt $K_D$:

  • If ROI > $K_D$: positive leverage. Debt amplifies EPS and ROE upward. Adding debt is value-creating for shareholders.
  • If ROI < $K_D$: negative leverage. Debt amplifies losses. Adding debt destroys shareholder value.
  • If ROI = $K_D$: leverage is value-neutral. EPS unchanged.

The source gives two worked examples. In the first, operating profit is 3,000,000 (ROI 7.5%) but $K_D$ is higher, so the geared firm (B) earns lower EPS than the ungeared firm (A): 1.40 vs 1.50. In the second, operating profit is 4,000,000 (ROI 10%) which exceeds $K_D$, so the geared firm's EPS (2.40) beats the ungeared firm's (2.00). The same capital structure flips from value-destroying to value-creating depending on operating performance.

Part B: dividend policy

The Modigliani-Miller dividend irrelevance proposition (1961)

Three years after their famous capital-structure paper, Modigliani and Miller proved a parallel result for dividends. Under the same kind of perfect-market assumptions, the firm's dividend policy does not affect firm value. If a firm pays a higher dividend today, it must either issue more equity later, or invest less. In either case, the loss in future value exactly offsets the dividend received.

The argument: a shareholder who wants more income than the firm pays can sell some shares ("homemade dividend"). A shareholder who wants to reinvest dividends can use them to buy more shares. The shareholder is indifferent to firm-level dividend policy because they can replicate any payout schedule themselves at zero cost.

Formally, with no taxes, no transaction costs, no information asymmetries, and rational investors, firm value is determined solely by the investment policy:

$$ V = \sum_{t=1}^\infty \frac{\text{FCF}_t}{(1 + r)^t} $$

and the FCF stream depends only on what the firm invests in, not how it splits payouts between dividends and retained earnings.

Why dividends still matter in practice

Real-world frictions break M-M dividend irrelevance just as they break capital-structure irrelevance. Five theories of why:

Tax preference theory
In most jurisdictions, dividends are taxed differently from capital gains (often more heavily, and at the point of payment rather than at the point of sale). Tax-paying shareholders should prefer firms that pay low dividends and grow share price instead. Drives the long-run shift toward share buybacks.
Bird-in-the-hand theory (Gordon, Lintner)
Cash now is more certain than capital gains later. Risk-averse shareholders therefore prefer high dividends. Empirically weak in modern markets, but the intuition explains why some retail investors do prefer dividend stocks.
Signalling theory (Bhattacharya 1979, Miller and Rock 1985)
Managers know more than outside investors. A dividend increase signals confidence in future cash flows; a cut signals trouble. The market reacts accordingly. Empirically, dividend changes do move share prices, even after controlling for earnings news.
Agency theory (Jensen 1986, "free cash flow")
Managers with too much spare cash spend it on empire-building, prestige projects, or excessive perks. Forcing dividends out of the firm disciplines management to keep returning to capital markets, where they face external scrutiny. Drives shareholder activist demands for higher payouts.
Clientele effect
Different investors have different tax positions and income needs. Pension funds (tax-exempt) like high dividends; high-tax-rate individuals prefer buybacks. Firms attract a clientele matched to their policy. Once a clientele exists, changing the policy disrupts it.

Lintner's dividend-smoothing model (1956)

John Lintner surveyed corporate managers in the 1950s and found that almost no firm sets dividends as a simple fraction of current earnings. Instead, managers set a long-run target payout ratio, then move dividends only partially toward target each year. Algebraically:

$$ \Delta D_t = a + s \cdot (T \cdot \text{EPS}_t - D_{t-1}) + \varepsilon_t $$

where $T$ is the target payout ratio, $s$ is the speed-of-adjustment (typically 0.2 to 0.4), and $a$ is a constant capturing reluctance to cut. The model implies dividends are much smoother than earnings: managers will starve internal projects of cash before cutting the dividend. This is why dividend cuts are rare and dramatic when they happen (BP and Shell 2020, banking sector 2008 to 2009 and again briefly in 2020).

Mechanics: cash dividends, scrip, and buybacks

Cash dividend
The classic payout. Declared by the board, paid on a specific date to shareholders who hold on the record date. On the ex-dividend date, the share price drops by approximately the dividend amount (since new buyers no longer receive it).
Scrip / stock dividend
Pay shareholders in additional shares rather than cash. Mathematically equivalent to a stock split: same value, more shares. Useful when the firm wants to maintain its dividend record but lacks cash. Increases share-capital base.
Share buyback
The firm buys its own shares from the market and cancels them. Reduces share count, mechanically increasing EPS. Returns cash to shareholders who want it (those who sell) while leaving the others undisturbed. Methods: open-market purchases, fixed-price tender, Dutch auction, direct negotiation.

Buybacks versus dividends, the modern shift

Until the 1980s, US firms returned almost all payouts as dividends. By the mid-1990s, buybacks had reached parity with dividends. Since the 2010s, buybacks have exceeded dividends in most years, driven by:

  • Tax efficiency: buybacks are taxed as capital gains, often at lower rates than dividends, and only when shareholders choose to sell.
  • Flexibility: buybacks can be paused without the signalling cost of a dividend cut.
  • Targeted return: buybacks return cash only to shareholders who want it. Dividends pay everyone equally.
  • Management compensation: many executive comp schemes are tied to EPS, which buybacks mechanically boost.

UK and European firms have followed the same trend but with a lag and at lower intensity than the US. South African firms remain more dividend-heavy than US peers, partly because of dividend withholding tax versus capital gains arbitrage that varies by shareholder type.

Buybacks have also attracted political criticism (especially in the US, where the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 introduced a 1% excise tax on buybacks), with the argument that they enrich shareholders at the expense of reinvestment and worker wages. Whether buybacks crowd out productive investment is a live empirical debate.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The two M-M theorems have the same logical structure

Both 1958 (capital structure irrelevance) and 1961 (dividend irrelevance) follow the same argument. Under perfect markets, investors can replicate at the personal level whatever the firm does at the corporate level. They can borrow to "make" leverage where the firm has none ("homemade leverage"). They can sell shares to "make" a dividend the firm has not paid ("homemade dividend"). With this replication available, firm-level financial policies become irrelevant. Real-world frictions (taxes, transaction costs, information asymmetries) prevent the replication and reintroduce relevance. Mastering M-M is mastering this one trick, applied in two domains.

The "right" capital structure is industry-specific, not universal

Banks run with 8 to 10% equity to total assets. Utilities run with 30 to 40%. Tech firms often have negative net debt (more cash than borrowings). Pharmaceuticals sit somewhere in the middle. Each industry has a characteristic mix that reflects its assets, cash flow stability, and regulatory framework. "What is the right gearing for my firm?" is best answered by looking at the median of comparable listed firms in the same industry. Theory tells you the direction; practice tells you the level.

Dividend policy is sticky in a way few finance people appreciate

Lintner's smoothing model is empirically iron-clad: firms will defer investment, borrow, or even sell assets rather than cut the dividend. This stickiness has macro consequences. In 2008, UK and US banks delayed crisis recognition partly because cutting dividends would have signalled distress; regulators eventually had to force them to do so. In 2020 the PRA pre-emptively banned UK bank dividends and buybacks to avoid the 2008 problem. The political economy of dividends matters as much as the corporate finance.

The agency problem reverses sign in different industries

Jensen's free-cash-flow theory says paying dividends disciplines management. But that only works if the firm has excess cash. In growth firms with positive NPV projects, forced payouts are destructive: they prevent the firm from funding good investments. Activist investors who push for buybacks at growth firms (think Carl Icahn at Apple, or Elliott at various tech names) are sometimes correct (excess cash), sometimes destructive (forced under-investment). The cash-rich-no-projects firm wants high payouts; the cash-poor-many-projects firm wants none.

Common pitfalls

Watch out Four traps that show up regularly. (1) Treating M-M as a description of reality. It is a baseline assuming no frictions; the question is always which frictions matter. (2) Conflating capital structure (a stock, the current balance sheet) with financing (a flow, this year's decisions). They are different. (3) Forgetting that "more debt is better because it's cheap" only holds while ROI exceeds $K_D$. Below that point, debt destroys value. (4) Treating dividends and buybacks as economically equivalent. They are not: they differ in tax treatment, signalling content, and clientele effects.
