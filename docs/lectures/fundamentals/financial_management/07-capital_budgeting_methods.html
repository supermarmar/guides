Capital budgeting: NPV, IRR and friends

How firms decide which projects to invest in. NPV is the right answer; the rest are sanity checks or approximations. Master the cash-flow build, master the methods, master the pitfalls.

Lecture 7 of 9 Reading time, 50 minutes Source: UP / Capital Project Appraisal 1.pdf
Step 1

The 30-second version

A capital project is anything that needs significant cash up front, takes time, and is expected to generate future cash flows. Whether to do it boils down to one number: does the project add more value than the capital it consumes? The technique to answer that is the same every time. Build the incremental after-tax cash flows. Pick a discount rate (your WACC from Lecture 5, or a project-specific variant). Compute net present value. If NPV is positive, do the project. If NPV is negative, don't. Everything else (IRR, payback, profitability index, equivalent annual cost) is a different lens on the same calculation.

The discipline of building the cash flows is more demanding than the methods themselves. Identify what is incremental, what is sunk, what is an opportunity cost, and what is non-cash. Include working capital. Treat tax properly. Get this right and the methods all converge on the same answer.

Feynman test Imagine you are deciding whether to open a new coffee shop. The fit-out costs 500,000 GBP today. You expect 100,000 GBP profit each year for the next ten years. After ten years you can sell the equipment for 50,000 GBP. Should you do it? Add up all the future cash flows, but each pound a year from now is worth less than a pound today because you could have invested elsewhere instead. The "value created" is the sum of those discounted future cash flows minus the initial outlay. If positive, open the shop. If negative, keep the money in a tracker fund. That sum is NPV. Every capital decision in business is the same calculation, with more zeros.

Step 2

Why this matters

Every meaningful corporate decision is a capital budgeting problem in disguise. Should the bank build a new core-banking platform? Should the airline buy 30 new aircraft? Should the pension fund insurer build, buy, or rent its new offices? Should the government invest in HS2? Each is a stream of cash flows to be evaluated against an alternative use of capital.

Get the discount rate or the cash-flow assumptions wrong, and a 30-year project that "creates" 500 million GBP of value can flip to destroying 200 million. HS2 is the live UK example: original 2009 estimate ~33 billion GBP, current ~70+ billion, with a benefit-cost ratio that has slipped from 2.4 to below 1.0 as costs grew. The methods in this lecture are the framework regulators, finance committees, and consultants use to debate every such project.

For an actuary, the methods turn up wherever long-dated cash flows need valuing: insurance product profitability, pension-scheme funding decisions, infrastructure investment, regulated-utility rate cases. Capital budgeting is corporate finance's version of the actuary's day job.

Step 3

The mental model

A project is a sequence of cash flows on a timeline. Initial outlay at $t=0$, operating cash flows during the life, terminal cash flow at the end. Each one gets pulled back to today using the cost of capital. The sum is NPV.

The two governing formulas:

$$ \text{NPV} = \sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{\text{CF}_t}{(1 + r)^t}, \qquad 0 = \sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{\text{CF}_t}{(1 + \text{IRR})^t} $$

NPV asks: at the given discount rate $r$, what is the project's value? IRR asks: what discount rate would make NPV exactly zero? Both come from the same equation.

Step 4

The detail

What counts as a capital project

The definition is broader than buying a building. A capital project is any decision with three features: significant resources committed up front, time value of money matters, and a discount rate (cost of capital, usually WACC) is required to compare value across periods. Buying machines, building factories, acquiring a competitor, launching a new product line, switching IT systems, and even hiring a senior team all qualify.

Mutually exclusive projects
Projects that compete for the same role. Choosing one rules out the others. "Should we site the new factory in Cape Town or Durban?" is a mutually-exclusive decision: you pick one location.
Complementary projects
Projects whose acceptance increases another project's value. Building a warehouse may make a delivery service profitable; the warehouse decision affects the delivery decision.
Independent projects
Projects whose decisions do not affect each other. The default. Approve any with positive NPV, subject to capital availability.

Building the cash flows: three rules and three pitfalls

The hardest part of capital budgeting is not the discounting; it is the cash flow construction. Three principles:

  • Use cash, not accounting profit. Add back depreciation; that is non-cash. Subtract capital spending; that is cash that does not appear in profit.
  • Use incremental cash flows. The question is "what changes if we do the project?" Not "what is the total cash flow of the new operation?" Existing revenue that would continue without the project is not incremental.
  • After tax, including the tax shield on depreciation. Depreciation reduces taxable income, which reduces tax paid, which is a real cash benefit even though depreciation itself is not cash.

Three classic mistakes that violate these:

Sunk costs
Money already spent and unrecoverable. Always ignore. A feasibility study costing 200,000 GBP that has already been done does not enter the decision. Whether to proceed depends only on cash flows from today forward.
Opportunity costs
The value of the best alternative use of a resource. Always include. If the project uses land worth 1 million GBP that could have been sold, that 1 million is a cost of the project even though no cash changes hands.
Allocated overheads
A fixed cost that gets spread across business units. Include only the incremental portion. If the head office costs the same with or without the project, do not charge a share to the project just because accounting says so.

The three building blocks of project cash flow

1. Initial investment ($CF_0$)

The outlay at $t = 0$. Always negative (a use of cash).

+ Cost of fixed assets (equipment, facilities, land) + Transport, installation, commissioning (capitalised) + Increase in net working capital (receivables + inventory - payables) + Opportunity cost of resources used (net of tax) - Tax saving from disposal of replaced asset (if any) - Salvage value received from replaced asset (if any) = Initial investment, CF_0

For an expansion project, no asset is being replaced; the third and fourth lines are zero. For a replacement project, we work with incremental amounts: new asset cost minus old asset salvage proceeds.

2. Operating cash flows ($CF_t$ for $t = 1, \ldots, n$)

The year-by-year cash generated. Note the structure: start with operating profit, deduct tax, then add depreciation back (because it is a non-cash deduction that shielded tax).

+ Sales revenue (incremental) - Operating expenses (incremental) - Depreciation / wear and tear allowance = Profit before tax - Tax (at tax rate) = Profit after tax + Depreciation (add back: it was non-cash) = After-tax operating cash flow, CF_t

Two non-obvious points. Interest payments are excluded: they are captured by discounting at the after-tax cost of capital (WACC). Including interest would double-count. Cost savings are cash inflows: a new machine that reduces operating costs by 50,000 GBP per year produces a positive incremental cash flow of 50,000 (less the tax effect), even though no new revenue is generated.

3. Terminal cash flow ($CF_n$)

The project end. Often added to the last year's operating cash flow rather than treated separately.

+ Salvage value of fixed assets (gross sale price) - Tax on disposal: (Sale price - Book value) x tax rate If sold above book: pay tax (recoupment) If sold below book: receive tax saving (scrapping allowance) + Recovery of net working capital - Disposal / decommissioning costs (net of tax) = Terminal cash flow, CF_n

Working capital that was tied up at $t = 0$ is freed at $t = n$, so it appears as a positive cash flow at the end. If receivables drop to zero, that 200,000 GBP returns to the firm.

The six methods, ordered by quality

Six methods are commonly taught. Two are decision-grade (NPV, MIRR). Two are useful checks (IRR, profitability index). Two are quick-and-dirty (payback, discounted payback). The remainder of this section walks through each.

1. Net Present Value (NPV): the gold standard

$$ \text{NPV} = \sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{\text{CF}_t}{(1 + r)^t} $$

Where $r$ is the WACC (or a project-specific discount rate). Decision rule:

  • NPV > 0: project creates value. Accept.
  • NPV < 0: project destroys value. Reject.
  • NPV = 0: indifferent. The project just covers its cost of capital.

For mutually exclusive projects, pick the highest positive NPV. For independent projects with no capital constraint, accept all positive-NPV projects.

NPV is the theoretically correct method because (a) it uses all the cash flows, (b) it correctly accounts for time value of money, (c) it correctly handles risk through the discount rate, and (d) NPVs are additive: the value of two projects together equals the sum of their NPVs, which makes it the right metric for shareholder value maximisation.

2. Internal Rate of Return (IRR): the popular cousin

$$ 0 = \sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{\text{CF}_t}{(1 + \text{IRR})^t} $$

IRR is the discount rate that makes NPV exactly zero. Decision rule: accept if IRR > WACC, reject otherwise. Loved by managers because it produces a percentage that is easy to communicate (12% beats 8%) and is comparable across projects.

For most well-behaved projects, IRR and NPV give the same accept / reject decision. But IRR has three classical pitfalls, all of which a CFO needs to know about.

Pitfall 1: Multiple IRRs
When cash flows switch sign more than once (e.g. mine reclamation project with a large outflow at the end), the IRR equation can have multiple positive roots. There is no single rate that makes NPV zero. Use NPV.
Pitfall 2: Scale
A small project with a 50% IRR can create less value than a large project with a 15% IRR. IRR is a percentage; NPV is the absolute value created. For mutually exclusive projects of different sizes, NPV is correct.
Pitfall 3: Reinvestment-rate fallacy
IRR implicitly assumes intermediate cash flows are reinvested at the IRR itself. For a project with 25% IRR, this assumes you can keep finding 25% opportunities every year. Usually wrong. NPV correctly assumes reinvestment at the cost of capital.

3. Modified Internal Rate of Return (MIRR): the fix for IRR

MIRR fixes the reinvestment-rate problem. It assumes operating cash flows are reinvested at the cost of capital (not at IRR) until the end of the project, then computes the rate that equates the future value of those inflows with the present value of the outflows.

$$ \text{MIRR} = \left( \frac{\text{FV of inflows at WACC}}{|\text{PV of outflows at WACC}|} \right)^{1/n} - 1 $$

MIRR is always a single, well-defined number. For projects with multiple sign changes or where reinvestment matters, MIRR is the right percentage-based metric. NPV is still preferable for absolute value, but MIRR is the IRR-style number you should reach for if a percentage is what the meeting wants.

4. Profitability Index (PI): for capital rationing

$$ \text{PI} = \frac{\text{PV of future cash inflows}}{|\text{Initial outlay}|} $$

PI measures NPV per pound of investment. PI > 1 is equivalent to NPV > 0. The interesting use is when capital is rationed: you cannot fund all positive-NPV projects, so you want the most NPV per pound of capital deployed. Rank by PI, accept top-down until capital is exhausted. This greedy heuristic works well for many capital-rationing problems, though optimal solutions sometimes require integer programming.

5. Payback period: the quick-and-dirty

How long until cumulative cash inflows equal the initial outlay? Simple, intuitive, widely used in practice as a screening tool.

Strengths
Simple to compute. Easy to communicate. A rough measure of liquidity (how long is our cash tied up?) and a proxy for risk (shorter = less exposed to forecasting error).
Weaknesses
Ignores time value of money in the simple version. Ignores cash flows beyond the cut-off. Cut-off itself is arbitrary. Can reject high-NPV long-dated projects (HS2 has very long payback).

6. Discounted payback period

Same as payback, but the cash flows are discounted first. Fixes the time-value-of-money flaw. Still arbitrary at the cut-off, still ignores cash flows beyond it. A small improvement that few firms bother computing.

7. Equivalent Annual Cost (EAC): for unequal-life projects

What if you are comparing two machines, one with a 5-year life and one with a 10-year life? Direct NPV comparison is misleading because the 10-year project covers more time. Convert each into its equivalent annual cost: the level annual cost that has the same NPV as the actual project over its life.

$$ \text{EAC} = \frac{\text{NPV of total cost}}{a_{\overline{n}|r}}, \quad \text{where} \quad a_{\overline{n}|r} = \frac{1 - (1+r)^{-n}}{r} $$

Now the two projects are directly comparable as annual costs. Pick the lower EAC. Useful for replacement-cycle analysis and infrastructure decisions.

Inflation handling: real vs nominal

Cash flows can be expressed in real terms (today's purchasing power) or nominal terms (actual money paid). Two rules:

  • Nominal cash flows discounted at nominal WACC.
  • Real cash flows discounted at real WACC.

Mixing them is a common error. From Lecture 1, the Fisher equation relates the two: $(1 + r_{\text{nominal}}) = (1 + r_{\text{real}})(1 + \pi)$, where $\pi$ is inflation. Both approaches give the same NPV when applied consistently. Pick whichever is easier (usually nominal for short projects, real for very long-dated ones where inflation forecasts are unreliable).

Capital rationing

In theory, a firm should accept every positive-NPV project. In practice, firms ration capital. Reasons: senior management bandwidth, debt covenants, signalling concerns about issuing too much equity, lender restrictions. When rationing binds, the NPV-maximising strategy is not "highest NPV first" but "highest NPV per pound of constrained resource".

For a single-period budget constraint, rank projects by PI and accept top-down. For multi-period or multi-resource constraints, the problem becomes linear or integer programming. In practice, spreadsheets and approximations dominate; formal optimisation is rare.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

NPV is right; everything else is a sanity check

In any conflict between NPV and another method, follow NPV. Theory says NPV maximises shareholder value and is additive across projects. Empirical surveys show CFOs typically run NPV and IRR side by side, but when they conflict (mostly for mutually exclusive projects of different scales), the textbook answer is always NPV. The reason IRR survives is communication: "this project has a 14% IRR" is easier to defend in a board meeting than "this project has a 32 million GBP NPV."

The cash-flow build is harder than the discounting

Spend 80% of your time on the cash flows, 20% on the methods. The biggest valuation errors come from sunk costs included in error, opportunity costs ignored, working capital forgotten, or non-cash items (depreciation, amortisation) treated as cash. A perfect IRR on bad cash flows is still wrong. A rough NPV on disciplined cash flows is usually good enough to make the right decision.

A project is a sequence of options, not a fixed plan

Standard NPV assumes you commit at $t=0$ and ride the cash flows. Real projects have decision points: you can expand if early results are good, contract if bad, defer if uncertainty is high, abandon if losses mount. Each of these is an embedded option (Lecture 4). Real options analysis uses option-pricing techniques to value the flexibility, and frequently doubles or triples the NPV of pilot-phase projects whose standard NPV is barely positive.

A worked example: a pharmaceutical company spending 50 million on Phase 1 trials has a tiny chance of success. Static NPV is negative. But Phase 1 is just the cost of creating the option to invest in Phase 2 if results are promising. Black-Scholes-style valuation of that option can flip the decision. The actuarial cousin: embedded options in insurance products (surrender, conversion) are valued the same way.

Discount rates and ESG: the next frontier

What if a project is good for shareholders but bad for the climate? Standard NPV says do it if NPV is positive. ESG-adjusted approaches push back: include a shadow price on carbon emissions in the cash flows, or use a higher discount rate for projects with high climate risk. The UK regulators (Ofwat, Ofgem) and the EU Taxonomy are pushing toward explicit climate adjustments in regulated WACC. The methodology is contested; the political pressure is real. Watch this space.

Common pitfalls

Watch out Six traps that show up regularly. (1) Including sunk costs. (2) Forgetting opportunity costs. (3) Including interest payments in operating cash flow (it's in the WACC). (4) Forgetting to add depreciation back as a non-cash item. (5) Comparing IRR across projects of different scale or different life. (6) Mixing real and nominal: never discount a real cash flow at a nominal WACC.

Test yourself

  1. Build the after-tax operating cash flow line by line, starting from sales revenue and ending at $\text{CF}_t$. Why is depreciation added back at the end?
  2. State the decision rule for NPV and the decision rule for IRR. When can they disagree?
  3. Describe the three IRR pitfalls in one sentence each and explain how MIRR addresses one of them.
  4. You are choosing between Project A (NPV 50, life 5 years) and Project B (NPV 60, life 10 years). Why is direct NPV comparison wrong, and what would you compute instead?
  5. Your firm has 10 million GBP of capital and four positive-NPV projects requiring 4, 5, 6, and 7 million respectively. How do you decide which to fund?
  6. Explain in one paragraph why sunk costs are ignored in capital budgeting but opportunity costs are included.
  7. What is a real option, and why might it make a barely-negative-NPV pilot project worth doing anyway?
