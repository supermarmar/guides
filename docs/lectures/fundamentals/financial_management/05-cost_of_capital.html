Cost of capital and WACC

The single most important number in corporate finance. Wrong by one percent on the discount rate, wrong by tens of percent on the valuation. The maths underneath: Modigliani-Miller, CAPM, and the trade-off theory.

Lecture 5 of 9 Reading time, 55 minutes Source: UP / WACC.pdf
Step 1

The 30-second version

A firm's cost of capital is the return its investors collectively demand for putting their money in. Debt holders demand interest payments (cost of debt). Equity holders demand a dividend stream plus capital appreciation (cost of equity). The weighted average cost of capital (WACC) is the blend, weighted by the market value of each source of finance. It is the rate that ties the two big financial-management decisions together: which projects to invest in, and how to fund them.

The two intellectual foundations are the Modigliani-Miller theorems (which capital structure does and does not matter, and why) and the Capital Asset Pricing Model (which converts a stock's relationship with the market into a required return). Both are 1950s-1960s ideas that won Nobel prizes and still drive every DCF model built today.

Feynman test Imagine borrowing 70 GBP from your aunt at 4% interest and chipping in 30 GBP of your own savings, where your savings could earn 12% in shares elsewhere. You then start a lemonade stand with the 100 GBP. To break even, the stand must earn at least the weighted average of the two costs: 0.7 x 4% + 0.3 x 12% = 6.4%. Any project the stand considers must return more than 6.4% or you would have been better off leaving your money in shares and your aunt's money in her account. That 6.4% is your WACC. Every firm in the world runs the same calculation, scaled up.
What's beyond the source The UP source defines the components and gives the basic formula. This lecture adds the full Modigliani-Miller proofs (Proposition 1 with arbitrage, Proposition 2 algebraically, and the tax-shield extension $V_L = V_U + T_c D$), the CAPM derivation sketch (efficient frontier, capital market line, security market line), the Hamada equation for unlevered / re-levered beta, the Fama-French extensions, the trade-off theory of capital structure, and how to estimate WACC for a private company or emerging market.
Step 2

Why this matters

The cost of capital is the single most-argued-over number in corporate finance. A DCF valuation of a 30-year cash flow at a 10% discount rate is worth roughly half of the same cash flow at 7%. M&A bidders and target boards have wildly different fair-value estimates because they apply different WACCs. Regulators of utilities (water, electricity) set price caps using "allowed WACC", and the differences between 6.5% and 7.5% translate into billions of pounds of consumer bills or company profits.

For an actuary, this lecture is the bridge between the time-value-of-money mechanics in Lecture 1 and every later valuation exercise. Capital budgeting (Lecture 7), embedded value calculations in insurance, Solvency II / SAM internal models, IFRS impairment testing, and acquisition due diligence all need a discount rate. The discount rate is, almost always, WACC or a close variant. Get this lecture wrong and every downstream model is wrong by a multiple of the WACC error.

The mental model

One picture. WACC sits at the intersection of two big decisions, and links them through the firm's value.

Step 4

The detail

The two financial-management decisions

Every CFO faces two big questions. Investment: in which projects, assets, businesses should we deploy capital? Financing: where should that capital come from, in what mix of equity and debt? WACC is what makes the two decisions consistent.

The accept / reject rule for any project is:

$$ \text{Accept if } \text{IRR}_{\text{project}} > \text{WACC} \quad \text{or equivalently} \quad \text{NPV at WACC} > 0 $$

The source gives two motivating examples that look paradoxical at first. Project A has IRR 7%, financed at 6% debt. Accept? Project B has IRR 12%, financed at 14% equity. Reject? Both wrong if you analyse them in isolation, because the firm cannot fund everything with the marginal cheap source. The correct comparison is against the blended cost of capital, which is exactly what WACC delivers.

Three views of how WACC varies with leverage

If the financing decision affects WACC, then it indirectly affects firm value. Three theoretical positions on whether and how it does.

The traditional view

Pre-1958, the consensus. Debt is cheaper than equity, so adding leverage initially lowers WACC. But beyond some optimal point G, financial distress risk forces equity holders to demand a sharply higher return, and WACC rises again. The curve is U-shaped, with an interior optimum that the financial manager should target. Intuitive but unrigorous: it never explains why debt is cheaper or why the curve has the exact shape it has.

Modigliani and Miller (1958), without taxes

Franco Modigliani and Merton Miller dropped a bomb on the traditional view. Under five assumptions (no taxes, no bankruptcy costs, no agency costs, no information asymmetries, frictionless markets), they proved two propositions.

Proposition 1
The value of a firm is independent of its capital structure. $V_L = V_U$: a levered firm and an unlevered firm with identical operating cash flows must be worth the same.
Proposition 2
The cost of equity rises linearly with leverage, in exactly the amount needed to leave WACC unchanged. The benefit from cheaper debt is fully offset by costlier equity.

Both follow from the same arbitrage argument. If two firms (one levered, one not) had different total values but the same operating cash flows, investors could short the over-priced firm, buy the under-priced one, replicate the same payoff with leverage at the personal level, and pocket a riskless profit. Other investors would do the same until prices converged. Hence $V_L = V_U$.

Algebraically, Proposition 2 says

$$ K_E^L = K_E^U + (K_E^U - K_D) \cdot \frac{D}{E} $$

where $K_E^L$ is the cost of equity of the levered firm, $K_E^U$ is the cost of equity of the same firm if it had no debt, $K_D$ is the cost of debt, and $D/E$ is the debt-to-equity ratio. The WACC of the levered firm works out to

$$ \text{WACC} = \frac{E}{V} K_E^L + \frac{D}{V} K_D = K_E^U $$

That is, the levered WACC equals the unlevered cost of equity. Independent of leverage. The financing decision does not change firm value.

Modigliani and Miller with corporate tax

The M-M assumption that hurts most when relaxed is "no taxes". Once corporate tax is introduced, interest payments become tax-deductible, creating a "tax shield". The famous adjusted result:

$$ V_L = V_U + T_c \cdot D $$

where $T_c$ is the corporate tax rate. Each unit of debt adds $T_c$ units of value to the firm. Taken to its logical extreme, the firm should be 100% debt-financed. Reality clearly is not, so something else must be missing.

The trade-off theory

The "something else" is bankruptcy costs (and agency costs, and personal taxes, and information asymmetries). As debt rises, the probability of financial distress rises non-linearly. Bankruptcy costs include direct legal and accounting fees and indirect costs (lost customers, distracted management, opportunistic competitors). The trade-off:

$$ V_L = V_U + T_c \cdot D - \text{PV(bankruptcy costs)} $$

Combining the tax shield (rising linearly with $D$) and bankruptcy costs (rising more than proportionally with $D$) reproduces a U-shaped WACC curve, with an interior optimum, but for genuine economic reasons (not the hand-waving of the traditional view). This is now the textbook position.

Two further refinements you may meet in the literature:

  • Miller's "Debt and Taxes" (1977) extended the analysis to personal taxes, showing that personal taxes on equity (lower) versus interest income (higher) erode some of the corporate tax benefit. In Miller's equilibrium, the marginal tax shield is zero.
  • Pecking-order theory (Myers, 1984) abandons the optimal-leverage view entirely. Firms prefer to fund from internal sources first, then debt, then equity (in that order), because issuing equity signals overvaluation to the market. Capital structure becomes a residual, not an optimisation.

The Capital Asset Pricing Model

M-M tells you how WACC moves with leverage. CAPM tells you what the level of $K_E$ should be. Both date from the early 1960s; both won Nobel prizes (Sharpe 1990 for CAPM, Modigliani 1985 and Miller 1990).

Diversification: the free lunch

Total risk of a stock has two components:

Specific (idiosyncratic, diversifiable) risk
Risk unique to a single company: management quality, product success, supply-chain disruption. Can be eliminated by holding a large enough portfolio of uncorrelated stocks.
Systematic (market, non-diversifiable) risk
Risk that moves with the overall economy: business cycle, interest rates, inflation, exchange rates, tax policy. Cannot be diversified away; every stock is exposed.

CAPM's key insight: since investors can costlessly diversify away specific risk, the market will not pay them to bear it. Only systematic risk gets rewarded. The required return on a stock should therefore depend only on its systematic risk, measured by beta.

Beta: the sensitivity to the market

Beta is the slope of the regression of the stock's excess returns on the market's excess returns:

$$ R_i - R_f = \alpha_i + \beta_i (R_m - R_f) + \varepsilon_i $$

Equivalently, $\beta_i = \mathrm{Cov}(R_i, R_m) / \mathrm{Var}(R_m)$. Interpretation:

BetaInterpretation
$\beta = 1$Same systematic risk as the market.
$\beta > 1$Amplifies market moves. Cyclicals (housebuilders, banks, tech) often have $\beta$ around 1.2 to 2.
$0 < \beta < 1$Dampens market moves. Defensives (utilities, consumer staples, healthcare) often have $\beta$ around 0.5 to 0.8.
$\beta = 0$Uncorrelated with the market. Cash, short-dated Treasuries.
$\beta < 0$Moves opposite to the market. Gold and some gold miners. Very rare.

The CAPM equation

Putting it all together, the required return on equity is

$$ K_E = R_f + \beta_E \cdot (R_m - R_f) $$

The risk-free rate $R_f$ is normally the yield on a long-dated government bond in the same currency. The market risk premium $(R_m - R_f)$ is typically 4% to 6% for developed markets and higher for emerging markets (Damodaran publishes country risk premia annually). Beta is estimated from historical data (typically 5 years of monthly returns) or pulled from data providers (Bloomberg, Refinitiv).

Empirical problems with CAPM

CAPM is theoretically beautiful but empirically imperfect. Three observed anomalies that pure CAPM cannot explain:

  • Size effect (Banz, 1981): small-cap stocks earn higher returns than their betas predict.
  • Value effect (Fama and French, 1992): high book-to-market (value) stocks earn higher returns than their betas predict.
  • Momentum (Jegadeesh and Titman, 1993): recent winners outperform recent losers.

Fama-French three-factor model extends CAPM with two more factors (size and value):

$$ K_E = R_f + \beta_M (R_m - R_f) + \beta_{SMB} \cdot \text{SMB} + \beta_{HML} \cdot \text{HML} $$

where SMB is "small minus big" (return of small-cap portfolio minus large-cap) and HML is "high minus low" (return of high book-to-market portfolio minus low). Fama-French five-factor (2015) adds profitability (RMW) and investment (CMA) factors. Most corporate-finance practice still uses single-factor CAPM, accepting its imperfection in exchange for simplicity.

Putting WACC together

The formula is simple. The interpretation of each term requires care.

$$ \text{WACC} = \frac{D}{D + E} K_D (1 - T_c) + \frac{E}{D + E} K_E $$

With preference shares as a third leg:

$$ \text{WACC} = \frac{D}{V} K_D (1 - T_c) + \frac{P}{V} K_P + \frac{E}{V} K_E $$

The weights: book or market?

Always use market values, not book values. Reasons:

  • The costs you compute ($K_E$ from CAPM, $K_D$ from YTM) are market-based, so the weights must be too.
  • Book values are historical; market values are the current opportunity cost.
  • Book values can be distorted by accounting choices, share buybacks, retained-earnings policy.

For equity: market cap (shares outstanding times share price). For debt: the market value of outstanding bonds, or book value as a proxy if debt is not publicly traded (closer to true value for short-dated bank debt). Use end-of-period or rolling-average values; do not switch convention mid-analysis.

Cost of debt

The pre-tax cost of debt $K_I$ is the yield to maturity on the firm's existing debt:

$$ \text{Bond price} = \sum_{t=1}^{2n} \frac{C/2}{(1 + K_I/2)^t} + \frac{F}{(1 + K_I/2)^{2n}} $$

Adjust for the tax shield to get the after-tax cost:

$$ K_D = K_I (1 - T_c) $$

Worked example from the source: a 5-year 10% semi-annual coupon debenture, par 1,000, issued at 950 (discount of 50). Tax rate 30%.

Inputs to bond YTM calculation: N = 5 * 2 = 10 periods FV = 1,000 PV = -950 PMT = (10% * 1,000) / 2 = 50 per period Per-period YTM = 5.67% Annual YTM (Ki) = 5.67 * 2 = 11.33% After-tax Kd = 11.33 * (1 - 0.30) = 7.93%

For new debt (not yet issued), use the firm's credit rating to look up the yield on similarly-rated bonds with similar maturity. For private companies, build up from a credit-rating model or from spreads on comparable issuers.

Cost of equity

The CAPM-based approach is dominant. Three inputs:

  • Risk-free rate: 10-year government bond yield in the firm's home currency. Use a longer rate (20 or 30 year) for very long-dated projects.
  • Market risk premium: 4% to 6% for developed markets, higher for emerging. Two ways to estimate: historical (average of $R_m - R_f$ over many decades) or implied (back it out from a DCF on the broad market index).
  • Beta: 5-year monthly regression, or pull from a data provider. Adjust toward 1.0 if you believe in mean-reversion (the "Blume adjustment" multiplies raw beta by 2/3 and adds 1/3).

For preference shares, the source gives the basic perpetuity formula:

$$ K_P = \frac{\text{Dividend per preference share}}{\text{Market price per preference share}} $$

Retained earnings carry the same cost as ordinary equity (they are shareholders' money that could otherwise have been paid out as dividends and reinvested elsewhere).

Estimating WACC for a private company or emerging market

Private companies have no market-traded shares, so no beta to regress. Emerging markets often lack a deep risk-free rate. Two techniques:

The pure-play / Hamada method

Step 1: identify listed comparable companies in the same business ("pure plays"). Pull their betas.

Step 2: unlever each comparable's beta to strip out the effect of its capital structure (using the Hamada equation):

$$ \beta_U = \frac{\beta_L}{1 + (1 - T_c) \cdot D/E} $$

Step 3: average the unlevered betas across comparables.

Step 4: re-lever to your firm's target capital structure:

$$ \beta_L = \beta_U \cdot \left(1 + (1 - T_c) \cdot D/E\right) $$

This gives you a beta consistent with your firm's own gearing. Plug into CAPM for $K_E$.

Country risk premium

For an emerging-market firm, add a country risk premium (CRP) to the developed-market WACC:

$$ K_E = R_f^{\text{USD}} + \beta \cdot (R_m^{\text{USD}} - R_f^{\text{USD}}) + \text{CRP} $$

CRP is typically the sovereign bond spread above US Treasuries, scaled by relative equity-to-bond volatility. Damodaran publishes this annually. South Africa's CRP is typically 3% to 5% above the developed-market base.

Determinants of $K_D$ in practice

From the source, with annotations on why each matters:

  • Asset cover ratio: assets available per unit of debt. Higher cover = lower default risk = lower $K_D$.
  • Interest cover ratio: EBIT / interest. Below 3x is a yellow flag; below 1.5x is a red flag.
  • Gearing: marginal cost of debt rises with leverage as credit ratings step down (BBB to BB to B).
  • Beta: high asset beta indicates volatile cash flows, raising default probability and $K_D$.
  • Tax deductibility: lowers the after-tax cost, but only as long as the firm has taxable profits to deduct against (no benefit if it is loss-making).
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

WACC values the firm, not individual projects

The most common WACC mistake in industry: applying a single firm-wide WACC to every project, regardless of the project's own risk profile. WACC reflects the average systematic risk of the firm's existing assets. A risky new venture should be discounted at a higher rate; a safe, infrastructure-style project at a lower rate. The fix: estimate a project-specific beta (using the pure-play method) and recompute. Otherwise, the firm systematically over-invests in risky projects and under-invests in safe ones.

The Hamada equation is the bridge between M-M and CAPM

M-M Proposition 2 says cost of equity rises with leverage. CAPM says cost of equity rises with beta. The Hamada equation links them: as leverage rises, equity beta rises. Algebraically:

$$ \beta_E = \beta_U \cdot \left(1 + (1 - T_c) \cdot \frac{D}{E}\right) $$

This is the most important identity in corporate finance you have never been formally taught. It lets you take a comparable's observed equity beta, strip out the leverage, and re-lever to your own gearing. Without it, you cannot estimate cost of capital for any private firm or any new business.

WACC and APV are two routes to the same answer

The adjusted present value (APV) method values a firm in two pieces: the unlevered value (assuming all-equity financing) plus the present value of the tax shield from debt. WACC bundles both into a single discount rate. The two methods give identical results when applied correctly, but APV is cleaner when capital structure is changing over time (project finance, LBOs). WACC is simpler when capital structure is stable. Know both; the choice is a tool selection, not a correctness question.

The low-beta anomaly and why CAPM is loved despite its flaws

Empirically, low-beta stocks earn higher risk-adjusted returns than CAPM predicts. This has been documented since the 1970s and remains robust. It implies that risk and return are not as cleanly related as CAPM suggests; the security market line is flatter than the theory predicts. Yet CAPM remains the dominant practitioner tool because (a) the alternatives are no better empirically, (b) it gives a simple, defensible number, and (c) regulators expect to see it. Practitioners use CAPM the way physicists use Newtonian mechanics: known to be incomplete, useful enough.

Discount rates are the climate-policy battleground of corporate finance

A WACC of 8% rather than 5% means a 30-year project's NPV is roughly halved. For long-dated infrastructure (renewables, water, public transport, pensions), the choice of discount rate effectively determines whether the project happens. Governments increasingly intervene: UK regulated utilities have allowed WACCs set by Ofwat, Ofgem; the EU Sustainable Finance Taxonomy is pushing toward project-level cost-of-capital adjustments based on climate risk; the Stern Review on climate change controversially used a near-zero discount rate. The discount rate is no longer a neutral technical input; it is a political choice with multi-trillion-pound consequences.

Common WACC mistakes you will see in practice

Watch out Five mistakes I see weekly in real DCF models. (1) Using book-value weights instead of market-value weights. (2) Forgetting the tax shield on debt: cost of debt should always be after-tax. (3) Applying firm-wide WACC to project-specific risk. (4) Mixing real and nominal: if cash flows are in real terms, WACC must be too. (5) Using a beta from a different industry or a comparable with very different leverage without re-levering via the Hamada equation. Any of these can change valuations by 30% or more.
