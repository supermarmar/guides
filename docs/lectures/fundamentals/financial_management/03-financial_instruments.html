Lectures / Financial management

Financial instruments

Bonds, shares, and everything in between. How they are structured, priced, and ranked when things go wrong. The yield curve, duration and convexity sit at the heart of all of it.

Lecture 3 of 9 Reading time, 45 minutes Source: UP / Financial Instruments.pdf
Step 1

The 30-second version

A financial instrument is a contract that creates a claim on future cash flows. The big split is between debt (you lend, get paid coupons, then your principal back, with a fixed claim) and equity (you own, get residual profits and votes, but stand last in line if things fail). Every instrument is a position on this spectrum, with extra features (security, seniority, conversion, optionality) bolted on to fine-tune risk and return.

Pricing any of them reduces to one operation: take the future cash flows, discount each one back to today at an appropriate rate, sum them up. The interesting part is how the discount rate is built (yield curves, credit spreads, liquidity premia) and how the price reacts when rates move (duration, convexity).

Feynman test Walk into a deli. You can pay 10 GBP for a sandwich now (cash), or 10 GBP for a sandwich vouchers redeemable in a year (which actually trade at 9.40 GBP because nobody wants to wait). The voucher is a one-year zero-coupon bond. Its price reflects the time value of money. Add features (an interest coupon every quarter, a security backed by the deli's bread oven, the right to convert into shares of the deli) and you have built every instrument in this lecture. The pricing rule never changes.
What's beyond the source The UP source describes each instrument descriptively. This lecture adds the yield curve (term structure of interest rates, three classical theories, bootstrapping), duration and convexity (how bond prices move when yields shift), the LIBOR-to-SOFR / Jibar-to-Zaronia transition, and a worked example of computing yield to maturity from scratch.
Step 2

Why this matters

The instruments in this lecture are the building blocks of every financial transaction in the world. A bank balance sheet is a sum of these. A pension fund's assets are a sum of these. The market capitalisation of public companies is a sum of these. WACC (Lecture 5) is a weighted average across the firm's instrument mix. Project finance (Lecture 7) is a pricing of the bespoke instruments used to fund the project.

You also cannot read a financial newspaper without this vocabulary. "10-year gilts trading at a yield of 4.3%", "the 2s/10s curve inverted", "Apple priced a 5-year senior unsecured note at SOFR + 50", "Anglo American's convertible is trading at a 12% premium". Each headline is an instrument, a price, a rate, and a structural feature. By the end of this lecture, you should be able to decode all four.

Step 3

The mental model

The mental model is the risk-return ladder. Order every instrument by claim seniority, from "first to be paid if the issuer fails" to "last to be paid". Risk rises as you go down the ladder, and so does required return. Every instrument in the lecture sits somewhere on this ladder.

Step 4

The detail

Interest rates: a short tour

"The interest rate" is a misnomer. There is no single interest rate. There is a whole structure of rates that differ by tenor, by issuer credit quality, by currency, and by purpose. The main ones to know:

Policy rate
Set by the central bank. SARB repo rate, Bank Rate, federal funds target, ECB main refinancing rate. Sets the floor for short-term rates.
Prime rate
The lowest rate at which commercial banks lend to creditworthy clients on overdraft. In SA, prime moves in step with the repo rate (typically repo + 3.5%). In the US, prime is set by individual banks but moves with the fed funds rate.
Interbank reference rates
The rates banks lend to each other unsecured for short tenors. Historically LIBOR (London Interbank Offered Rate), Jibar (Johannesburg Interbank Average Rate), Euribor. After the 2012 manipulation scandals, LIBOR was retired in 2023 and replaced by overnight risk-free rates (SOFR in the US, SONIA in the UK, ESTR in the EU). South Africa is moving from Jibar to Zaronia.
Money market rates
Rates on short-term debt instruments (less than one year): T-bills, commercial paper, BAs, CDs. Slightly above the policy rate, reflecting credit and liquidity premia.
Capital market rates
Yields on long-term debt and the cost of equity. The whole yield curve plus credit spreads.

The yield curve and what it means

If you plot the yield on government bonds against time to maturity, you get the yield curve. Its shape encodes the market's view of future rates, inflation, and growth.

Three classical theories of why the curve has its shape:

Pure expectations theory
Long rates are the geometric average of expected future short rates. An upward-sloping curve means the market expects short rates to rise. Clean and intuitive; ignores risk premia.
Liquidity preference theory
Lenders prefer short to long (less interest-rate risk), so they demand a premium to lend long. Long rates are therefore biased above the pure-expectations level. Explains why the curve is usually upward-sloping even when no rate change is expected.
Market segmentation theory
Different investors operate at different parts of the curve and do not substitute freely (banks at the short end, insurers / pension funds at the long end). Curve shape reflects supply and demand at each tenor.

Real-world curves are explained by a mix of all three. The forward rate implied by the curve, $f(t_1, t_2)$, can be backed out from spot rates by no-arbitrage:

$$ (1 + r_{t_2})^{t_2} = (1 + r_{t_1})^{t_1} (1 + f(t_1, t_2))^{t_2 - t_1} $$

This is the formula that lets traders take a view on future short rates by trading combinations of bonds. It is also the basis for the bootstrapping procedure that turns a set of coupon-bond prices into a zero-coupon yield curve.

Bond pricing, from first principles

A bond is a contract that pays a coupon $C$ every period for $n$ periods plus the face value $F$ at maturity. Its price is the present value of those cash flows, discounted at the appropriate yield. For semi-annual coupons over $n$ years at annual yield $y$:

$$ P = \sum_{t=1}^{2n} \frac{C/2}{(1 + y/2)^t} + \frac{F}{(1 + y/2)^{2n}} $$

The first sum is the annuity formula from Lecture 1; the second term is the discounted redemption. Combining the closed forms:

$$ P = \frac{C}{y} \left[ 1 - \frac{1}{(1 + y/2)^{2n}} \right] + \frac{F}{(1 + y/2)^{2n}} $$

Three regimes:

  • Coupon = yield: bond trades at par ($P = F$).
  • Coupon < yield: bond trades at a discount ($P < F$). Yield comes from coupons and the capital gain at redemption.
  • Coupon > yield: bond trades at a premium ($P > F$). Coupons over-compensate for the yield, but the bond loses value as it pulls to par.

The yield to maturity is the unique $y$ that makes the formula hold given a market price. It is the bond's internal rate of return if held to maturity. Solving for $y$ requires numerical methods (bisection or Newton-Raphson); financial calculators do this with one button.

Worked example Five-year bond, semi-annual coupons of 6% (so 3 per half-year on 100 face), market yield 8% nominal annual (so 4% per period). PV of coupons = 3 x [1 - 1.04^-10] / 0.04 = 24.33 PV of redemption = 100 / 1.04^10 = 67.56 Price = 91.89 Trades at a discount because coupon (6%) is below yield (8%).

Duration: how much does price move when yields move?

Bond prices move inversely to yields. The sensitivity depends on the bond's coupon, time to maturity and yield level. Two quick rules and one formula:

  • The longer the time to maturity, the more sensitive.
  • The lower the coupon, the more sensitive (because more of the value sits in the distant redemption).
  • The lower the yield, the more sensitive (the effect on the discount factor is non-linear).

Macaulay duration $D_{\text{Mac}}$ is the cash-flow-weighted average time to receipt of the bond's cash flows:

$$ D_{\text{Mac}} = \frac{\sum_t t \cdot \text{PV}(CF_t)}{\sum_t \text{PV}(CF_t)} $$

Modified duration is the standard sensitivity measure used in practice. It is Macaulay duration divided by $(1 + y/m)$:

$$ D_{\text{Mod}} = \frac{D_{\text{Mac}}}{1 + y/m} $$

The first-order approximation for the price change from a small yield change $\Delta y$ is

$$ \frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{\text{Mod}} \cdot \Delta y $$

So a 10-year bond with modified duration of 7.5 will fall by approximately 0.75% if yields rise by 10 basis points (0.10%). This number is on every bond trader's desk, every day.

Convexity: the curve in the curve

Duration is a linear approximation. The actual bond price / yield relationship is convex (curved). The convexity term captures the second-order effect. Including it, the price change for a yield shift $\Delta y$ becomes

$$ \frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{\text{Mod}} \cdot \Delta y + \frac{1}{2} \cdot \text{Convexity} \cdot (\Delta y)^2 $$

where convexity is

$$ C = \frac{1}{P} \sum_t \frac{t(t+1) \cdot CF_t}{(1 + y)^{t+2}} $$

Convexity is good news for the bondholder: it means prices rise more on rate falls than they fall on rate rises. Long-duration, low-coupon bonds (think 30-year zero-coupons) have the highest convexity and are favoured when an investor expects rates to be volatile.

Categories of debt instrument

Government bonds
Gilts (UK), Treasuries (US), RSA bonds (SA). Risk-free in their own currency. Deepest market, set the benchmark yield curve. Maturities from 1 year to 50 years.
Debentures
Corporate bonds secured on specific assets. Two flavours: mortgage debenture (fixed charge on specific assets, changes need bondholder permission) and floating-charge debenture (charge over a class of assets that can change in the ordinary course of business; crystallises into a fixed charge if the company fails).
Unsecured loan stock (ULS)
No security. Rank pari passu with general creditors. Higher yield than debentures to compensate.
Subordinated / junior debt
Contractually ranked below senior unsecured. Used heavily by banks (Tier 2 regulatory capital). Higher yield again.
Eurobonds
Bonds issued internationally, typically in a currency other than the issuer's home country. Bulldogs (foreign issuer, sterling), Yankees (foreign issuer, USD). Usually unsecured, usually bearer form, annual coupons.
Floating-rate notes (FRNs)
Medium-term debt whose coupon resets periodically against a reference rate (historically LIBOR, now SOFR / SONIA / ESTR). Coupon $= \text{reference} + \text{spread}$. Less sensitive to rate changes than fixed coupon bonds: price tends to stay near par.
Commercial paper and T-bills
Short-term money market instruments. CP is unsecured corporate, T-bills are government. Both are sold at a discount and redeemed at par; no coupon. Maturities 1 to 270 days.

The LIBOR to risk-free-rate transition

LIBOR was the benchmark for hundreds of trillions of USD of contracts. After the 2008 to 2012 manipulation scandals (banks colluded to fix their submissions to benefit their trading books), it was decided to retire LIBOR. The phase-out completed by mid-2023. Replacements are overnight, transaction-based rates with no credit component:

The new rates differ from LIBOR in two ways. They are overnight (so you need to compound them in arrears to get a term rate) and they are secured in most cases (so they lack the credit / liquidity premium that LIBOR had). Floating-rate contracts and derivatives all needed to be re-papered.

Share capital: ordinary and preference

Ordinary shares
The primary form of equity. Voting rights, residual claim on profits (after debt and pref share holders) and on assets (in liquidation, after everyone else). Irredeemable. Dividend is discretionary, not contractual. Cannot be issued below par value. Owners ARE the company.
Preference shares
Equity, but with a fixed dividend (expressed as a percentage of par value) and priority over ordinary shares for dividend and liquidation. No voting rights usually. Many variations: cumulative (unpaid dividends accumulate), participating (share in surplus profits), redeemable, convertible. In banking, contractual Tier 1 instruments.

Holders of ordinary shares are the owners. Their return has two components: dividend yield (cash returned each year) and capital growth (rising share price as the company grows). High potential return for high risk: in a winding-up, ordinary shareholders get only whatever is left after every other claim has been paid. Often zero. Lecture 5 develops the cost-of-equity calculation in full using CAPM.

Convertibles, warrants and other hybrids

Convertible bond (or convertible preference share)
A bond or pref share that the holder can convert into a fixed number of ordinary shares of the issuer at a stated price. Combines bond-like downside protection with equity-like upside. Issued at lower coupons than equivalent straight bonds because the conversion right is valuable.

Key concepts:

  • Conversion ratio: par value divided by conversion price (how many shares one bond converts into).
  • Conversion value: conversion ratio multiplied by current share price.
  • Conversion premium: how much more the convertible costs today, versus just buying the equivalent ordinary shares. The premium is the price of optionality.

$$ \text{Conversion premium} = \frac{\text{Convertible price}}{\text{Conversion value}} - 1 $$

Warrant
A call option written by a company on its own stock. Long-dated (typically 3 to 10 years). Strike price and expiry set at issue. Exercising creates new shares (dilution). Often attached to bond issues to make them more attractive.
Executive stock option
A warrant given to senior management as part of remuneration, with strike price set at the share price at grant. Aligns management with shareholders, in theory. In practice, can encourage short-term price management.

That tax-deductibility row is the seed for everything that comes in Lecture 5 (WACC) and Lecture 6 (capital structure): interest is paid before tax, so a unit of debt costs the firm less than a unit of equity, all else equal. Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Every instrument is a bundle of cash flows plus optionality

The deepest way to look at this lecture is that there are really only two primitive ingredients: deterministic cash flows (which a TVM calculation handles) and options (rights but not obligations to do something). A vanilla bond is pure cash flows. An ordinary share is a perpetual residual cash flow with embedded growth optionality. A convertible bond is bond cash flows plus an embedded equity call. A callable bond is bond cash flows minus an embedded call written to the issuer. Once you start decomposing instruments this way, the whole derivatives lecture becomes a natural extension, not a new topic.

The yield curve is the single most important chart in finance

It contains, in one picture, the market's view of inflation, growth, monetary policy, and risk appetite. The slope (2s vs 10s) predicts recessions. The level (10-year nominal) prices every long-dated asset. The shape changes (steepening vs flattening) drive trading P&L. If you read the FT every morning, the curve is the first thing to look at.

Duration matching is the actuarial application of this lecture

An insurer with 30-year liabilities and 5-year assets has a duration mismatch: if rates fall, the present value of liabilities rises faster than the assets. Solvency II and SAM both require firms to manage this. The simplest technique is duration matching: hold assets whose duration equals the liability duration. The deeper technique is immunisation (Redington's theorem): match the present value, match the duration, and have higher convexity on the asset side. The UK 2022 LDI crisis was a failure of leveraged duration matching under stress.

The risk-return ladder is a useful simplification, not a law

The ladder works most of the time, but it can invert. In a banking crisis, senior unsecured bondholders can lose more than equity holders if the bank is bailed in (Credit Suisse AT1 in 2023). Distressed corporate debt can trade at 30 cents on the pound, with equity-like returns. Government bonds in their own currency are "risk-free" in nominal terms but can lose huge amounts in real terms if inflation surprises (UK 1970s, US 2022). Always read the ladder with the caveat "in normal liquidation, in normal times".

Common pitfalls

Watch out Three traps. (1) Confusing coupon rate (contractual) with yield to maturity (market). They are only equal when the bond trades at par. (2) Forgetting that duration is sensitivity to a parallel shift in the curve; a steepening or flattening is a different exposure (managed via "key rate durations"). (3) Treating preference shares as equity in capital structure but as debt in cost of capital, or vice versa. They are equity legally; their cost-of-capital treatment depends on whether they look more like debt (fixed dividend) or more like equity (perpetual, discretionary).
