Lectures / Financial management

Financial institutions and markets

Who is in the financial system, what they do, and where the money flows. From central banks to insurance companies, from stock exchanges to shadow banks.

Lecture 2 of 9 Reading time, 30 minutes Source: UP / Financial Institutions.pdf
Step 1

The 30-second version

The financial system exists to move money from people who have spare cash (savers) to people who want to invest it (borrowers, businesses, governments). It does this through two channels: direct finance in financial markets, where a saver buys a bond or share directly, and indirect finance through institutions, where a bank or fund pools savings and lends or invests them on the saver's behalf.

The cast of characters is large but learnable. Central banks set the price of money. Stock exchanges and bond markets are where securities trade. Banks take deposits and make loans. Asset managers, pension funds, insurance companies and unit trusts pool savings into investment portfolios. Hedge funds and private equity push the boundaries of what gets called "investing". Regulators try to keep all of this from blowing up.

Feynman test Imagine a village. Some villagers have spare grain (savings). Others want to build a mill (capital project) but have no grain. A trusted figure in the village offers to take grain from the savers, give them a receipt promising more grain back later, and hand the grain to the mill-builders in return for a share of the mill's future output. That trusted figure is the financial system. Banks, markets, pension funds, insurance, regulators: each is a refinement of that basic idea, designed to handle bigger flows, more risk, and more parties.
What's beyond the source The UP source focuses on SA and UK institutions. This lecture broadens to the global system: the Fed, ECB, BoJ and PBoC; shadow banking; the post-2008 regulatory map; fintech and neobanks; the rise of passive investing; and the recent reshaping of pension provision (DB collapse, DC growth, LDI in the UK gilt crisis).
Step 2

Why this matters

You cannot understand any specific instrument or transaction without knowing who is on the other side. A bond is a contract between a borrower and a lender; the identity of each side shapes the risk, the documentation, the regulation, the price. The same yield curve looks like a cost to a corporate treasurer and an opportunity to a pension fund. Every later lecture in this course assumes you know who the players are.

It also matters because the financial system is bigger and stranger than most outsiders realise. Global assets under management exceed 120 trillion USD; pension funds alone hold over 50 trillion. The Fed's balance sheet is about 7 trillion USD; the ECB's about 7 trillion EUR; the PBoC's about 6 trillion USD equivalent. Decisions taken in a handful of central bank rooms cascade through every line of every financial statement in the world.

The mental model

The flow of funds picture, with one elaboration: there is not one channel but two, with regulators sitting above all of it.

Step 4

The detail

Markets, by maturity and instrument

Money market
Short-term debt instruments, maturity under one year. Treasury bills, commercial paper, bankers' acceptances, certificates of deposit, repo. Used by governments and companies to manage short-term cash. The marginal price-setter is the central bank's policy rate.
Capital market
Long-term debt and equity. Bonds (corporate and government), ordinary shares, preference shares. This is where pension funds and insurance companies do most of their work. Long-dated capital matching long-dated liabilities.
Derivatives market
Contracts whose value derives from an underlying (a share, a bond, a commodity, an index, a rate). Futures, options, swaps, forwards. Traded on exchange (CME, Eurex, JSE Derivatives Exchange) or OTC. Used for hedging, speculation, and structured exposures. Covered in full in Lecture 4.
Primary vs secondary
Primary is where securities are issued (IPOs, new bond issues, rights issues). Secondary is where they trade after issuance (London Stock Exchange, JSE, Nasdaq). The primary market raises new capital; the secondary market provides the liquidity that makes the primary market work in the first place.

Central banks: the price-setters

Every banking system has a central bank. Its job is to manage the value of the currency (inflation, exchange rate) and to keep the financial system stable. Five tools, used in different mixes:

Policy interest rate
The rate at which the central bank lends overnight to commercial banks (repo rate in SA, Bank Rate in UK, federal funds target in US). Sets the floor for all other rates in the economy.
Open market operations
Buying and selling government securities to manage liquidity in the banking system. Increases or decreases the money supply at the margin.
Reserve requirements
The minimum fraction of deposits that commercial banks must hold as cash. Less used as an active tool in developed markets; still important in some emerging markets.
Lender of last resort
Lends to solvent but illiquid banks against good collateral at a penal rate. Bagehot's 1873 rule, still alive. The 2008 and 2023 banking crises were each managed by extended versions of this function.
Quantitative easing
Large-scale purchases of long-dated bonds, funded by creating new reserves. Used after 2008 by the Fed, BoE, ECB and BoJ to push long-rates lower when policy rates were already at zero. Started to unwind in 2022.

Regulatory architectures

Three broad models for how a country regulates its financial system. Each tries to solve the same problem (depositor / investor protection, conduct, systemic stability) with different organisational choices.

Institutional or silo model
Each type of firm has its own regulator (one for banks, one for insurers, one for fund managers). Used in China, Hong Kong, Mexico. Simple but creates gaps when firms straddle boundaries.
Integrated or unified model
A single regulator covers everyone. UK ran this from 2001 (FSA) until 2013, when it switched to Twin Peaks. Germany, Norway, Canada still use it. Efficient at the firm level; can confuse prudential and conduct priorities.
Twin Peaks model
Two regulators: one for prudential (solvency, capital, systemic stability), one for conduct (treating customers fairly, market integrity). UK (PRA + FCA), Australia (APRA + ASIC), South Africa (SARB + FSCA). Considered the post-2008 best practice.

Stock exchanges and what they do

An exchange is a regulated venue for trading securities. It has six functions, only one of which is the obvious "providing a market":

  • Primary issuance: listing new securities (IPO, rights issue, bond issue) so that issuers can raise capital.
  • Secondary trading: providing the liquidity that makes investors willing to buy in the primary market in the first place.
  • Price discovery: aggregating buy and sell orders so that a clearing price exists at any moment.
  • Regulation of members: brokers, market makers, traders must meet exchange rules.
  • Regulation of issuers: listed companies must meet disclosure rules and follow corporate governance standards.
  • Clearing and settlement: ensuring trades complete (cash and securities exchange hands properly), usually through a separate clearing house (LCH, ICE Clear, JSE Clear).

The London Stock Exchange (founded informally in 1698 at Jonathan's Coffee-house) trades UK gilts, UK equities, overseas securities and AIM (a junior market for smaller companies with lighter listing rules). The JSE Securities Exchange trades equities and bonds; the JSE Derivatives Exchange (formerly SAFEX) trades futures and options. Trading is increasingly electronic and order-driven; the older quote-driven model with telephone brokers has largely vanished.

Indirect channel: who pools and lends

The bulk of household savings does not go directly into shares or bonds. It goes through intermediaries that pool the money and invest at scale. Each type has different incentives, regulation and behaviour.

Clearing banks
High-street commercial banks (Barclays, HSBC, Standard Bank, FirstRand). Take deposits, make loans, run the payment system, hold sterling / rand current accounts. Highly regulated; subject to Basel capital and liquidity rules.
Investment banks
Advisors and intermediaries for corporate and institutional clients. M&A, capital raising, securities trading, prime brokerage, asset management. Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, JPM, UBS. Historically separated from clearing banks (Glass-Steagall in the US, repealed 1999); after 2008, regulators tightened the rules on their trading books.
Unit trusts (collective investment schemes)
Open-ended funds that pool money and invest in a diversified portfolio. NOT companies. Units are created and redeemed daily at NAV. Run by a management company, with trustees overseeing. The investor's cost is the bid-offer spread plus annual management fees. In the UK regulated by the FCA; in SA by the FSCA.
Investment trusts
Despite the name, these ARE companies, listed on the stock exchange. Closed-end: fixed number of shares, traded between investors. Can borrow ("gearing") in a way that unit trusts cannot. Can trade at a premium or discount to NAV. Older structure than unit trusts, less common today but still significant in the UK.
ETFs (exchange-traded funds)
A hybrid: open-ended fund structure (units created and redeemed daily) but listed on exchange and traded intraday like a share. The dominant retail investing structure today. Most ETFs are passive (tracking an index); some are active. Around 13 trillion USD globally as of 2024.
Asset / investment management companies
Run portfolios on behalf of clients (retail through unit trusts and ETFs, institutional through segregated mandates). BlackRock, Vanguard, State Street are the three largest globally (each over 5 trillion USD AUM). In SA: Allan Gray, Coronation, Sanlam, Old Mutual, Ninety One.
Pension funds
Pool retirement savings for employees. Two types. Defined benefit promises a specific pension (usually a fraction of final salary), with the employer bearing investment and longevity risk. Defined contribution invests a fixed contribution, with the member bearing all risk at retirement. DB is in long-term decline (closed in most UK private-sector firms by 2010); DC is now dominant. Globally about 55 trillion USD.
Life insurance companies
Pool mortality and investment risk. Sell endowment, whole-life, term-life, annuities. Liabilities are long-dated, so they invest in long-dated bonds and equities. Heavy users of asset-liability matching. In UK / EU regulated under Solvency II; in SA under SAM.
General (non-life) insurance
Short-dated insurance: motor, property, liability, marine. Claims settle quickly so assets are mostly liquid (cash, short bonds). Hugely affected by catastrophes (Hurricane Katrina, COVID business-interruption). Lloyd's of London is the global hub for specialist non-life risk.

Unit trust pricing, in one paragraph

A unit trust's NAV is the total market value of its assets divided by the number of units in issue. The management company sells units at an offer price (higher than NAV) and buys them back at a bid price (lower than NAV). The difference is the bid-offer spread, which covers management costs and profit. Two pricing conventions exist:

$$ \text{Spread \%} = 1 - \frac{\text{Bid price}}{\text{Offer price}} $$

Under bid basis, the management company values assets at the price it would receive if it sold them (use this when the fund is shrinking and units need to be cancelled). Under offer basis, it values them at the price it would pay to buy more (use this when the fund is growing and new units are being created). The convention can shift over time and affects what investors pay.

Shadow banking and other non-bank institutions

Not every institution that does bank-like things is regulated as a bank. The "shadow banking system" describes the network of money-market funds, hedge funds, structured-finance vehicles, repo dealers, and securitisation conduits that perform credit intermediation outside the banking perimeter. Estimated by the FSB at around 60 trillion USD globally (2023), comparable in size to the banking sector itself.

Shadow banking was at the centre of the 2008 crisis (Lehman, AIG, money-market fund runs). It has been re-regulated piecemeal: the FSB issues policy recommendations through its NBFI workstream; specific products (money-market funds, repo) have new rules. It remains an area where systemic risk can build up unseen, and where the post-2008 regulatory map is still unfinished.

Fintech and the disruption of distribution

The 2010s brought a wave of digital-native financial firms. Neobanks (Monzo, Revolut, Nubank, Tyme) offer current accounts and payments through phone apps with no branch network. Robo-advisers (Wealthfront, Nutmeg) automate portfolio construction. Embedded finance puts banking products inside non-financial apps. Crypto / DeFi attempts to rebuild parts of the financial system on permissionless distributed ledgers, with mixed results.

Most fintech innovation has been in distribution, not in the underlying economics. A digital current account still relies on a banking licence (or a banking-as-a-service partner). A robo-adviser still buys the same ETFs. The crypto experiment is the only attempt at structural innovation, and after the 2022 collapses (Terra, FTX) regulators have moved decisively to bring it inside the same perimeter as everything else.

Recent reshaping of the system

Three structural shifts to know:

  • Passive overtakes active. Passive (index-tracking) funds now hold more US equity assets than active funds (crossed in 2019). Drives down fees; raises questions about price discovery and stewardship.
  • DB pensions die, DC pensions grow. In the UK, almost all private-sector DB schemes are closed to new members. DC is the new norm, shifting all the risk to the saver. LDI ("liability-driven investing") was the technique that nearly broke the UK gilt market in the September 2022 mini-budget crisis.
  • Private credit grows. Non-bank lending to mid-sized companies has grown from ~300 billion USD in 2010 to over 1.7 trillion in 2024. Asset managers (Apollo, KKR, Blackstone) now play roles that banks would have played in the 1990s. Less liquid, less regulated, higher yielding.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Every institution is the answer to a maturity-mismatch problem

Look at any institutional player and ask: what mismatch are they solving? Banks turn short deposits into long loans. Pension funds turn working-life contributions into retirement income. Life insurers turn regular premiums into a one-off death benefit. Unit trusts turn small retail savings into diversified market exposure. Every institution exists because someone has cash flow X and wants cash flow Y, and bridging X to Y is hard.

Regulation always lags innovation, and the gap is where the next crisis sits

The 2008 crisis was a shadow-banking crisis. The 2022 UK gilt mini-crisis was an LDI crisis. The 2023 banking mini-crisis (SVB, Signature, First Republic, Credit Suisse) was a deposit-flight crisis amplified by social media. Each was a place where the regulatory map had not caught up with how money was actually flowing. Reading the financial press through this lens (where is the next mismatch the rules do not yet see?) is a real practitioner skill.

The size of the asset management industry creates systemic risk that is not addressed by bank capital rules

Asset managers do not take principal risk: they invest client money. So they sit outside Basel. But the largest three (BlackRock, Vanguard, State Street) collectively own ~25% of the S&P 500. They are the dominant marginal buyer / seller in many markets. If they all rebalanced the same way at the same time, prices would move dramatically. Regulators have begun to think about this through the "systemic NBFI" workstream; it is unfinished business.

Common pitfalls

Watch out Three traps. (1) Confusing unit trusts and investment trusts. Unit trusts are NOT companies; investment trusts ARE. (2) Forgetting that the FCA in the UK is NOT part of the Bank of England, while the PRA IS. (3) Assuming all "investment banks" do the same thing. Glass-Steagall, Volcker, ring-fencing have all changed what the term means in different jurisdictions.
