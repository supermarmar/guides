Reading on a screen

The screen changes less than people assume. What it does change, it changes absolutely: the reader sits further away, the light comes towards them rather than off the page, and the page has no edges until you supply some.

Lecture 12 of 28 Reading time, 30 minutes Sources: Reichenstein (iA); Rutter (2017); Butterick
Step 1

The 30-second version

Three differences between paper and screen actually matter. The reader is further away, so type must be larger: Butterick's screen range is 15 to 25 pixels against 10 to 12 points in print. The light is emitted rather than reflected, so maximum contrast is uncomfortable and both ends of the tonal range must be pulled off their extremes. And the page has no natural width, so unless you impose one, the browser will set your measure to whatever the reader's monitor happens to be.

That last one is the whole game. An unstyled page on a modern display runs to 200 characters or more per line, which is nearly three times the readable maximum. Fixing it takes one declaration. Almost every other decision on a text-led page follows from it, which is why the reading column is the first thing to set and the last thing to compromise.

The settings this module uses: 18px body, 1.65 leading, 66ch column. Eighteen rather than the house sixteen because sixteen is an accessibility floor rather than a comfortable long-form size; 1.65 rather than 1.55 because a 66-character line at this size needs the extra vertical target on the return sweep.

Feynman test Hold a book at arm's length and try to read it. You cannot, and the type has not changed. Now notice that your monitor is roughly at arm's length and a book is not. That distance is the entire reason screen type is set larger than print type, and it is why "12 point looks fine in Word" and "12 point looks small on the web" are both true statements about the same size.
The counterintuitive part A correctly set reading column on a wide monitor leaves most of the screen empty, and the instinct is to fill it with a sidebar, related links or navigation. Do not. The empty space is what the narrow column bought. Butterick's own site and gwern.net are both mostly margin at desktop width, on purpose, and both are read by people who could have chosen otherwise.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the web's defaults are wrong in a specific and predictable direction, and unlike a word processor's defaults they are wrong by a large margin. Word's default margins produce a measure around 90 to 95 characters, which is bad. An unstyled browser produces whatever the window is, which on a 27-inch monitor is catastrophic. The failure is not subtle and it is still extremely common on documentation sites, internal wikis and anything generated from Markdown without a stylesheet.

The second is that the reader can leave. A print document has usually been handed to someone with a reason to read it; a web page is one tab among thirty and the cost of closing it is zero. Every friction in the reading experience is therefore priced differently: a measure that would merely tire a report reader will lose a web reader entirely, and you will never know it happened.

The third reason is that this is the medium where you control everything, which cuts both ways. There is no template, no house style enforced by the tool, no page size, no client rendering quirk to blame. A badly set web page is a page whose author did not decide, and the fix is available to them in every case.

The fourth is that the settings compound over length. On a 400-word page, a 95-character measure is a mild irritation. On a 4,000-word essay it is the difference between a reader finishing and a reader bookmarking it and never returning. Long-form is exactly where the type layer stops being a matter of polish.

Step 3

The mental model

The column is fixed and the viewport is not. What grows is the margin, and the margin is not wasted.

Two consequences follow.

The first is that the responsive behaviour of a text page is mostly about the column rather than about the type. Below the cap, the column is the viewport and the character count falls naturally; above it, the column is fixed and the margin absorbs everything. That is why the body size does not need to scale fluidly: measure is already doing the responsive work, and scaling the body as well would change two variables at once for no gain.

The second is that ch is the right unit and it is not merely convenient. It is defined as the width of the digit zero in the current font, so 66ch tracks the typeface's actual set width. Change from Inter to a wider face and the pixel width of the column changes automatically to keep the character count where you put it. Expressing the same constraint in pixels silently breaks the moment the font changes.

The reading column
The container that holds running text, capped at {measure.reading} = 66ch. Distinct from the page container, which may be much wider and holds figures, code and tables.
Halation
The apparent bleeding of light glyphs into a dark background on an emissive display, which blurs letterforms and slows reading. The reason light-on-dark needs its contrast pulled back rather than pushed up, and the reason pure white on pure black is the worst available choice.
The ch unit
CSS unit equal to the advance width of the digit zero in the current font. Approximates a character width closely enough for measure, and adapts automatically when the typeface changes.

The leading figure is the one that looks out of line, so it is worth defending. GOV.UK's 160 per cent is the closest comparator and they serve the widest possible audience, including readers with low literacy and poor eyesight. Butterick's 145 per cent ceiling is set for print, where leading also costs paper. On screen the vertical space is free, the measure is at the top of the band, and the reader is further away, all three of which point the same direction.

The dark palette

Dark mode is a separate palette, designed, not an inversion computed from the light one. Two things go wrong if you invert.

First, pure white text on pure black produces halation, which measurably slows reading. The received figure of a 20 per cent reduction circulates widely in design writing without a traceable source, so treat the magnitude with suspicion and the direction as well supported. The remedy is to pull both ends in: #E4E3E0 on #151514 rather than #FFFFFF on #000000.

Second, an accent verified for light mode will fail on dark. The house accent #5B47E0 measures 6.1:1 against white, which is comfortable, and against #151514 it is far too dark to read. The module's dark accent is #A99BFF at 7.7:1 on the dark canvas, and it was chosen rather than derived. Every accent and semantic colour needs this treatment individually.

DARK MODE, THE MINIMUM THAT WORKS :root { --canvas: #FFFFFF; --text: #37352F; /* 12.3:1 */ --accent: #5B47E0; /* 6.1:1 */ --rule: #E8E6E1; } @media (prefers-color-scheme: dark) { :root { --canvas: #151514; --text: #E4E3E0; /* 14.2:1 */ --accent: #A99BFF; /* 7.7:1 <- re-picked, not inverted */ --rule: #33322F; } } NOTES Images need attention too. A screenshot with a white background becomes a glowing rectangle on a dark page; either give it a border, or reduce its brightness slightly under the dark query. Syntax highlighting is a second palette with the same problem. Saturated colours read brighter on dark grounds and usually need desaturating by 10 to 20 per cent. Do not offer a toggle without also honouring the system preference. A reader who has set their OS to dark has already told you.

Fluid type, and what should not be fluid

Headings scale with the viewport; body does not. The reasoning was given in lecture 4 and is worth restating in its screen form: body is already at or near its accessible floor, so a fluid body can only shrink below the floor at small viewports or grow to an arbitrary size at large ones. Fixing it and letting the measure absorb the change is both simpler and better.

THE HEADING SCALE, FLUID h1 { font-size: clamp(2.25rem, 1.4rem + 2.4vw, 3rem); } h2 { font-size: 1.875rem; } h3 { font-size: 1.375rem; } body { font-size: 1.125rem; line-height: 1.65; } @media (max-width: 767px) { body { font-size: 1.0625rem; } } .prose { max-width: 66ch; } Only h1 is fluid. Below h1 the range between the mobile and desktop sizes is small enough that a single value works, and every clamp() you add is another thing to reason about later.

The typeface question, for screen

Lecture 3's conclusion holds: the choice matters less than the setting, and the serif-versus-sans evidence does not support the folklore. Two screen-specific points sit on top.

The old argument that serifs fail on screen has expired. It rested on low-resolution rendering where the fine detail turned to mush, and displays passed that point a decade ago. Literata, Source Serif and Charter all read well on a modern screen at 18px, and a serif body is a legitimate choice for long-form web that reads as more considered than the default sans.

What has not expired is the loading problem. A web font arrives after the HTML, so the reader sees either nothing or a fallback until it does. Use font-display: swap so text is readable immediately, choose a fallback with similar metrics so the swap does not reflow the page, and subset the font to the characters you actually use. A beautiful typeface that arrives 800 milliseconds late has made the page worse.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Reichenstein's claim, taken seriously and then qualified

"Web design is 95 per cent typography" is the most quoted sentence in web design and it is a rhetorical device rather than a measurement. Reichenstein was arguing against a discipline that in 2006 was preoccupied with layout chrome, gradients and navigation furniture, and the exaggeration was the point.

The durable version of the argument is narrower and stronger. On a page whose content is text, the interface is the text: there is no separate layer where the design lives. Improving such a page therefore means improving the type layer, and effort spent elsewhere is effort spent on the frame rather than the picture.

Where it does not transfer is anywhere the content is not text, which is most of the modern web. An application, a mapping tool, a dashboard or a media site is not 95 per cent typography by any reading, and applying the slogan there produces a beautifully set page that does the wrong job. The claim is about documents, and this module is about documents.

The empty margin, defended properly

You will be asked to fill it. The request comes from the same instinct that asks to tighten whitespace in a report and it has a stronger case here, because a wide monitor showing a narrow column really does look like unused capacity.

The functional answer is the one from step 3: the margin is not empty space next to the column, it is the mechanism that produced the column. Removing it does not add content, it lengthens every line to 190 characters, and the reading cost falls on every paragraph of the page.

The second answer is about what typically goes in that space. Related links, recommended articles, a newsletter box and a navigation panel are not neutral occupants; each one competes for attention with the text, and the text is the reason the reader came. A sidebar is not free real estate, it is a permanent tax on the thing it surrounds.

The concession worth offering is the one lecture 13 covers: the margin is the correct home for sidenotes, which are content that belongs to the text rather than content competing with it. That is a genuine use of the space and it is available without giving up the column.

Common failure Setting the column on the wrong element. max-width: 66ch belongs on the text container, not on the page wrapper, or figures, code blocks and tables inherit it too and become needlessly cramped. Lecture 13 covers the three-width system that resolves this. The symptom is a page where a wide table is squeezed into the reading column and scrolls when it did not need to.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Open three text-heavy pages you read regularly: a documentation site, a news article and a personal blog. For each, count the characters in a full line at your normal window width, read off the body pixel size from the browser inspector, and compute the leading ratio. Rank the three by how comfortable they are to read and check whether the ranking follows the numbers.
  2. Name the three differences between paper and screen that matter, and give the design consequence of each.
  3. Why is ch the right unit for a measure constraint, and what breaks if you use pixels?
  4. Explain why body text does not need to scale fluidly on screen. What absorbs the responsive change instead?
  5. This module sets leading at 1.65 against Butterick's ceiling of 145 per cent. Give the three reasons and say which is strongest.
  6. Why is a dark palette not an inversion? Give two specific things that break, with values.
  7. The claim that high-contrast dark mode slows reading by around 20 per cent is widely repeated. What is the honest status of that figure and of the underlying effect?
  8. Your serif web font arrives 800ms after the HTML. State three things you would do, and say which matters most.
  9. Reichenstein's 95 per cent claim: give the durable version of the argument and one context where it does not transfer.
  10. A stakeholder asks you to put a related-articles panel in the empty margin. Give the functional argument against, and name the one thing that legitimately belongs there.
