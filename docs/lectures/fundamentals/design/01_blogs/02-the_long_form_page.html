The long-form page

Your reader is not reading. They are scanning, in one of three documented patterns, deciding whether to read. Everything on a long page is either serving that decision or interrupting it.

Lecture 13 of 28 Reading time, 30 minutes Sources: Nielsen Norman Group; gwern.net; Tufte (sidenotes)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Two decades of eye-tracking work at Nielsen Norman Group has established that web readers scan before they read, and that the scanning follows recognisable patterns. The F-pattern appears when a page offers no structure: the eye takes the first line or two, then progressively less of each subsequent line, then runs down the left edge. The layer-cake pattern appears when a page has good headings: the eye moves between them, dipping into body text only where a heading earns it. The spotted pattern appears when the reader is hunting a specific thing and fixes on the elements that look like it.

The F-pattern is the failure state. It is what the eye falls back on when there is nothing better, and it means the reader is sampling the left-hand third of your prose and inferring the rest. The layer-cake is the one to design for, and the way to get it is unglamorous: headings that state something rather than naming a topic, at intervals short enough that no reader is ever more than a screen away from one.

The second half of this lecture is structural. A long page needs exactly three widths: the reading column at 66ch, a wider band for things that cannot fit in it, and full-bleed for a hero image. Three, not five. Everything that appears on the page gets assigned to one of them, once.

Feynman test Think about how you read a menu in a restaurant. You do not start at the top left and read every dish. You bounce between the section headings, find "mains", scan down that section, and read exactly one description properly before ordering. Nobody taught you to do that and you do it every time. A long web page is a menu, and most of them are written as though the reader will start at the top.
The uncomfortable implication If most readers scan the headings and read almost nothing else, then your headings are your document for most of your audience. A page whose headings read "Background", "Approach", "Results", "Discussion" has told the scanning reader precisely nothing. One whose headings read "The 2023 vintage behaves differently" and "Two of the three drivers reverse after month 18" has told them the argument.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is arithmetic. A 4,000-word essay takes about sixteen minutes to read properly and about forty seconds to scan. Most of your readers will spend the forty seconds, decide, and either commit or leave. The forty seconds is not a lesser version of reading the page; it is a different activity with different requirements, and a page designed only for the sixteen minutes fails at the thing that happens most.

The second is that the scanning behaviour is not a defect to be corrected. It is a rational allocation of attention by someone with more candidate reading than time, and it is what you do on other people's pages. Designing against it produces a page that punishes the reader for behaving sensibly.

The third is that the structural decisions on a long page compound in a way they do not on a short one. On a 600-word post it hardly matters whether the code blocks are the same width as the text. On a 6,000-word technical piece with twenty code samples, eight figures and four tables, a consistent width system is the difference between a page that reads as one document and a page that reads as a pile of components.

The fourth reason is that the web offers a structural affordance no other medium in this track has: the reader can choose their own depth. A printed report gives everyone the same document. A web page can offer a summary, then the argument, then the detail, then the sources, with each layer available to whoever wants it and invisible to whoever does not. Step 5 argues this is the medium's real advantage and that almost nobody uses it.

Step 3

The mental model

Three scanning patterns, and what produces each.

Two consequences follow.

The first is that heading frequency and heading quality are separate problems and both must be solved. Frequent uninformative headings produce a page that looks structured and scans as noise. Infrequent informative headings leave long stretches with no purchase, and the reader falls back to the F. The working target is a heading every 200 to 300 words, each one a claim.

The second is a test you can run in thirty seconds. Extract every heading from the page, in order, into a plain list. Read the list. If it reads as an argument, the scanning reader will get the argument. If it reads as a table of contents, they will get nothing. This is the same test lecture 9 applied to hierarchy and the presentations module applies to action titles, and it is the most portable idea in this course.

Layer-cake pattern
The scanning pattern produced by a well-structured page: attention concentrated in horizontal bands at headings and subheadings, with selective descent into the body beneath the ones that interest the reader. The target behaviour.
F-pattern
The fallback pattern on unstructured text: heavy attention on the first lines, decreasing coverage of each subsequent line, and a vertical run down the left edge. Not a reader preference; a symptom of a page that offered nothing else.
Progressive disclosure
Structuring a page so that the reader can stop at any of several depths and leave with something coherent. The web's distinctive structural advantage over print, and step 5's argument for how to use it.
Step 4

The detail

The three widths

Everything on the page belongs to one of three widths, assigned once. More than three and the page loses its spine; fewer and something is being squeezed.

WidthValueWhat goes here
Column66chParagraphs, headings, lists, blockquotes. Everything read as prose
Bleed100chFigures, code blocks, tables, anything that would be cramped at 66ch
Full100vwA hero image. Nothing else
Sidenote18ch, right marginMarginal commentary, desktop only

Sidenotes against footnotes

A footnote sends the reader to the bottom of the page and asks them to come back. On paper that is a short trip. On a scrolling page of 4,000 words it is a journey, and readers either skip footnotes entirely or lose their place.

A sidenote puts the same content in the right margin, level with its reference. The reader's eye moves a few centimetres and returns without leaving the paragraph. Tufte popularised the form in his books and it transfers to the web better than almost any print convention, because the empty margin from lecture 12 is exactly the space it needs.

The rule this module uses: sidenotes on desktop where the margin exists, collapsing to inline expandable notes below 1280px. Never a numbered list at the foot of a long scrolling page, which is the worst of both.

Code, tables and figures

Code blocks
Bleed width, monospace at 15px, background {color.neutral-10}, overflow-x: auto. Never wrap code: a wrapped line changes the code's meaning to a reader trying to understand indentation. Let it scroll horizontally inside its own container while the page body does not.
Tables
Bleed width, in a scrolling container. Below 768px the container scrolls rather than the columns stacking. A stacked financial table destroys the vertical comparison the columns existed to support, so scrolling is the lesser evil.
Figures
Bleed width, caption below in {type.caption} at {color.text-secondary}, left-aligned with the reading column rather than centred under the image. A centred caption reads as decoration; a left-aligned one reads as text about the figure.
Inline code
0.9em of the surrounding text, not 1em. Monospace faces run optically larger at the same nominal size, so matching the number produces code that looks bigger than the prose around it.

Links, and the one rule

Underline them. Colour alone as a link signal fails for colour-blind readers, and it is the single most common accessibility defect in body copy on otherwise careful sites.

The usual objection is that a paragraph dense with links becomes a mess of underlines, and it is a fair objection. The answer is not to remove the underline but to soften it: text-decoration-color: {color.neutral-40} with the text still in the accent, and text-decoration-skip-ink: auto so descenders break the line cleanly. The signal survives and the texture calms down.

Heading intervals, concretely

A 4,000-WORD PIECE, STRUCTURED h1 the title 1 h2 section headings 6 to 8, one per 500 words h3 subsection headings 0 to 2 per section Interval between any two headings: 200 to 300 words. Never more than about 400. At 18px on a laptop that is roughly one and a half screens, which is the furthest a scanning reader will travel without a handhold. THE HEADING TEST Extract all h2 and h3 text into a flat list. Read it. FAILS: PASSES: Background The 2023 vintage behaves differently Methodology Why the usual split does not work here Results Two of three drivers reverse at month 18 Discussion What this changes for the LGD model Conclusion The case for rebuilding rather than recalibrating The left column describes the document. The right column IS the document, for most readers.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The layered page

The web can do one structural thing no other medium in this track can: let the reader choose their depth. gwern.net is the most thorough implementation in public and it is worth studying as a design object rather than for its content. A page there offers an abstract, then the body, with margin notes alongside, footnotes below, collapsible sections for tangents, and link previews that let you inspect a reference without leaving. A reader can take any of five depths and each one is coherent on its own.

Almost nobody does this, and the reason is that it is more work than writing linearly. You have to decide, for every paragraph, what depth it belongs to, which is a genuine editorial judgement and cannot be automated. The payoff is that one document serves the reader who has forty seconds and the reader who has forty minutes, without compromising for either.

The minimum viable version is three layers and is worth doing on anything over about 2,000 words. A summary at the top that stands alone. The body, structured so the headings carry the argument. And the detail pushed into sidenotes, collapsible sections or a linked appendix. That is not a large amount of extra work and it converts a page that half your readers bounce off into one they can use at whatever depth they brought.

The tension nobody resolves

There is a real conflict at the centre of this lecture and it would be dishonest to end without naming it.

A page optimised for scanning is a page broken into short, self-contained, heavily signposted chunks. A sustained argument is exactly the opposite: it accumulates, it requires the reader to hold the previous step while taking the next, and its force depends on being followed rather than sampled. Every technique in step 4 makes a page easier to scan and, at the margin, makes a long argument easier to leave.

The honest positions are two, and which one applies depends on what you are writing. For most professional and technical writing, scannability wins, because the reader genuinely does need to extract specific things and the argument is usually not so delicate that sampling destroys it. For writing whose whole value is a chain of reasoning, scannability is a trap: the reader who samples it has not read it, and a design that encourages sampling has helped them not read it.

What I would not do is pretend the conflict away by asserting that good structure serves both. Sometimes it does. Sometimes the right decision is a long unbroken stretch of prose with no handholds, because the point requires the reader to walk the whole path, and the design's job is then to make the path pleasant rather than to offer exits from it.

Common failure Reading the scanning research produces pages chopped into 80-word chunks under a heading each, with bulleted summaries everywhere, and it reads as a slide deck that lost its slides. Scannability is a property of the structure being visible, not of the content being fragmented. A 300-word section under a heading that states its claim is scannable. Three 100-word sections under three topic labels are not scannable, they are just short.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a long piece you have written. Extract every heading into a flat list and read it. Then hand the list, without the document, to someone who has not read it and ask what the piece argues. Whatever they say is what your scanning readers received. Rewrite the headings until the list alone conveys the argument, and note how much of the rewriting was actually about deciding what you think.
  2. Name the three scanning patterns and say what produces each.
  3. Why is the F-pattern described as a failure state rather than a reader preference?
  4. Heading frequency and heading quality are separate problems. Describe the page that results from solving only one of them, in each direction.
  5. State the three widths, their values, and one content type that belongs to each.
  6. What is the common implementation error with widths, and what is the symptom on the page?
  7. Give two reasons a sidenote beats a footnote on a long scrolling page, and state the breakpoint at which the argument stops holding.
  8. Why should a wide table scroll rather than stack on a narrow screen?
  9. Why is inline code set at 0.9em rather than 1em?
  10. State the tension in step 5 in your own words, then name a piece of writing you have produced where scannability was the wrong goal, and say what you would do differently.
