The Word document as a design system

Word has a complete design system built into it, with tokens, inheritance and a single source of truth. Almost nobody uses it, and the reason most corporate documents look the way they do is that people format instead.

Lecture 17 of 28 Reading time, 30 minutes Sources: Butterick; Microsoft typography documentation
Step 1

The 30-second version

Word's style system is a design token system. Normal is the root; every other style inherits from it unless it explicitly overrides. Change the typeface on Normal and the whole document changes, including styles you have never opened. That is exactly the property that makes a design system worth having, and it has been sitting in the software for thirty years.

The alternative, which is what nearly everyone does, is direct formatting: selecting text and pressing the bold button, or changing the size in the ribbon. It produces the same visual result and it is technical debt. It does not propagate, it does not survive editing, it breaks the navigation pane and the table of contents, it is invisible to a screen reader, and it has to be reapplied by hand every time anything changes.

The third thing is the concession. This module's font stack is Aptos, Segoe UI, Calibri, Carlito, Arial, all of which are ordinary, none of which Butterick would endorse. A circulated .docx is rendered by the recipient's machine using the recipient's fonts, and a missing font is substituted, which changes the metrics, which changes the line breaks, which moves your exhibits. A document that arrives repaginated has failed in a way that no amount of good type choice compensates for.

Feynman test Imagine painting every wall in a house individually, and then being told the colour has changed. Now imagine having mixed one tin and used it everywhere. Both houses look identical when you finish. Only one of them can be repainted in an afternoon, and documents always get repainted.
The Word default worth keeping Word's A4 margins are 25.4mm all round, which is almost exactly where lecture 14's derivation lands for an 11pt body. It is the one default that is right, and it is worth knowing which one, because the temptation once you start editing a template is to change everything you touch.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that these documents are edited by other people, repeatedly, and usually in a hurry. A report you produce as a PDF is finished. A .docx circulated for comment will come back with tracked changes from four people, get merged, get a section added, and be re-sent. Direct formatting does not survive that process; styles do. This is the single practical difference and it is worth more than every aesthetic consideration in the lecture.

The second is volume. Most professionals produce a handful of formal reports a year and several hundred working documents. If design effort follows importance rather than volume, the medium that consumes most of your writing time gets none of it, which is roughly what happens.

The third is that the style ladder is not only visual. Word's built-in heading styles drive the navigation pane, the table of contents, the outline view, the PDF bookmark tree and the reading order a screen reader follows. A document where headings are simply bold body text looks similar and is structurally empty: it cannot be navigated, its contents page cannot be generated, and it is inaccessible. That is a functional failure rather than a stylistic one.

The fourth is that the fix is nearly free and is made once. Configuring a template properly takes about an hour. Every document produced from it afterwards inherits the work, including documents produced by colleagues who never think about any of this, which is the only way a house style ever actually spreads.

Step 3

The mental model

One root, everything inheriting from it.

Two consequences follow.

The first is that styles should be named by level rather than by content. "Heading 2" generalises; "Model Caveat" does not, and the next document will have a different kind of caveat. This is the same discipline as naming a colour token by role rather than by appearance, and it fails the same way when ignored.

The second is that every child style should store as few overrides as possible. A Heading 2 that specifies its own typeface has broken the inheritance for no gain, and will not follow when Normal changes. Set only what genuinely differs: size, weight, spacing, and occasionally colour.

Normal
The root style. Every other style inherits from it directly or transitively. Its typeface, size, leading and language are the document's defaults, and changing it is how you change the document.
Direct formatting
Visual properties applied to a selection rather than through a style. Overrides the style, does not propagate, does not survive well through editing, and is invisible to every structural feature of the document. Word's "Clear All Formatting" removes it, which is the first thing to run on an inherited document.
Metric compatibility
Two typefaces having identical character widths, so substituting one for the other does not change line breaks or pagination. Carlito is metrically compatible with Calibri and Caladea with Cambria. This property is worth more in a circulated document than any aesthetic improvement.

The space-before to space-after ratios run from 3:1 to 4:1, which is lecture 2's heading rule. Applying that one change to a default Word template does more for a document's appearance than anything else on this page.

Three heading levels, not six. Word offers nine and the temptation is to use them. A working document that needs a fourth level needs restructuring, and the discipline of having only three is what forces that conversation to happen.

Word's line-spacing quirk

Word's "Multiple" line spacing is a multiplier of the font's own internal line height, not of the point size. So "1.15" in Word is not 115 per cent of 11pt, and the actual result varies by typeface, because different faces declare different default line heights. Two documents both set to "1.15" in different fonts can have visibly different leading.

Where the leading actually matters, use Exactly instead. Setting 11pt body at exactly 14pt line spacing gives 127 per cent, which sits in Butterick's 120 to 145 band and is unambiguous across every typeface. The one caution is that "Exactly" clips anything taller than the specified value, so a line containing a large inline graphic or a tall equation gets cut off. Use it for body text and leave headings on Multiple.

Microsoft replaced Calibri with Aptos as the Microsoft 365 default in 2024. Aptos was drawn by Steve Matteson, who also drew Segoe, and it ships with Office rather than with Windows, which is the detail that matters: a machine with Windows and an older Office install may not have it, which is why the stack continues past it.

Carlito is in the stack deliberately and is the most useful entry in it. It is metrically compatible with Calibri, so a document falling back to it does not reflow. An aesthetically better substitute that changed the pagination would be worse.

The concession is specific and should not be generalised. It applies to editable files circulated to other people. For a PDF the fonts embed, nothing substitutes, nothing reflows, and the reports module's recommendation of Source Serif and Inter applies instead. Choosing Aptos for a PDF is not caution, it is just a worse typeface.

Fixing an inherited template

TEMPLATE REPAIR, IN ORDER 1 MODIFY NORMAL FIRST Font, size, language, line spacing, space-after. Everything else inherits, so this is the largest single change available. 2 SET THE THREE HEADING LEVELS Sizes and weights from the ladder above. Space before roughly 3x space after. Tick "Keep with next" on all three. This alone prevents every orphaned heading in the document. 3 TURN ON WIDOW AND ORPHAN CONTROL Paragraph dialog, Line and Page Breaks tab. It is on by default and is frequently switched off by inherited templates. 4 BUILD ONE TABLE STYLE Header row with a light fill and a rule beneath. No vertical borders. Repeat header row on. "Allow row to break across pages" OFF. 5 DELETE THE STYLES YOU WILL NOT USE A style list with 60 entries guarantees that colleagues will pick at random. Reduce it to the ten in the ladder. 6 SET THE LANGUAGE ON NORMAL English (United Kingdom). Inherited templates often carry US English, which quietly changes every spellcheck decision in the document. 7 SAVE AS .DOTX, NOT .DOCX A template rather than a document to copy. The difference is whether colleagues start from it or from last quarter's file.

Things never to do

Empty paragraphs for spacing
Pressing Return twice is not spacing, it is an empty paragraph, and it will land at the top of a page the moment anything above it reflows. Use space-after on the style.
Tabs or spaces for indentation
Use the paragraph indent. A tab-indented paragraph reflows differently at a different font size and cannot be adjusted globally.
Text boxes
They float free of the text flow, break in every conversion to PDF or HTML, are invisible to screen readers, and move unpredictably when text is edited. Use a single-cell table if you need a boxed callout; it is the only construct that survives track changes.
Manual numbering
Typing "3.2" in front of a heading rather than using a linked list style. It looks identical until someone inserts a section, at which point every subsequent number is wrong and nobody notices.
Coloured headings in a circulated document
Reads as a template someone forgot to fill in, and costs a photocopy generation of legibility for nothing. Black headings, with size and weight carrying the hierarchy.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Accessibility is the same work

Everything argued above on maintenance grounds turns out to be the accessibility requirement as well, and this is worth knowing because it changes how the conversation goes.

A screen reader navigates a document by its heading structure. A user can jump between headings, get an outline, and skip to the section they want, exactly as a sighted reader does by scanning. That navigation exists only if the headings are real Heading styles. Bold body text is, to a screen reader, body text, and a document built that way is a single undifferentiated block that must be read linearly from the start.

The same applies to tables, where a header row marked as a header lets a screen reader announce the column name with each cell; to images, where alt text is the only thing a non-sighted reader gets; and to reading order, which follows the document flow and is why floating text boxes are read at unpredictable points or skipped.

So the argument for styles does not need to rest on maintenance, which some colleagues will find abstract. It rests on the document being usable by a colleague with a visual impairment, which is concrete, and in many organisations is a policy requirement rather than a preference. The two arguments recommend identical actions, which is convenient and not coincidental: both are about the document having real structure rather than the appearance of structure.

Where Butterick is right and this module still overrules him

Butterick's position is that system fonts represent the absence of a decision, and that a document set in the default is a document nobody thought about. As a diagnosis of most corporate output he is exactly right, and the rest of this track follows him.

The overruling is narrow and rests on one fact he does not have to deal with: a .docx is not a finished artefact, it is a set of instructions rendered by the recipient's machine. He writes for lawyers producing court filings, which are printed or exported to PDF, where the font travels with the document. In that world the argument has no counterweight.

In a world where a paper is emailed to eleven committee members with different Office versions, the counterweight is real and it is heavy. A substituted font changes the metrics, which moves the line breaks, which moves the page breaks, which separates an exhibit from its caption and pushes the summary onto two pages. The recipient does not know this happened and reasonably concludes the document was poorly made.

The honest framing is that this is a trade rather than a refutation. You are giving up typographic quality to buy fidelity of transmission, and it is worth making the trade knowingly rather than by default. When the constraint lifts, when you are producing a PDF, take the quality.

Common failure Building the template around the first document rather than around the levels. The symptom is styles named after content, "Risk Finding" and "Model Caveat" and "Regulatory Note", which do not generalise to the next document and which colleagues will apply inconsistently because the semantics are ambiguous. Name by level, let the content decide which level it is, and keep the ten built-in names.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Open a document you produced in Word, put the cursor in a heading and check the style box. If it says "Normal" the heading is direct formatting. Do this for every heading in the document and count how many are real. Then open the navigation pane and see what the document thinks its structure is.
  2. Explain the inheritance relationship between Normal and the other styles, and say what a child style should and should not store.
  3. Give four things direct formatting breaks that are not visual.
  4. Why should styles be named by level rather than by content? Give the failure that content-naming produces.
  5. What is wrong with Word's "Multiple" line spacing, and when would you use "Exactly" instead? What does "Exactly" break?
  6. Why is Carlito in the font stack when it is not the best-looking option available?
  7. State precisely when the font concession in this module applies and when it does not.
  8. Why is a single-cell table the right way to build a callout in Word, rather than a text box?
  9. Give the accessibility consequence of using bold body text instead of real Heading styles, and explain why it is the same work as the maintenance argument.
  10. Summarise Butterick's position on system fonts, say where it is right, and give the specific fact about .docx files that justifies overruling it.
