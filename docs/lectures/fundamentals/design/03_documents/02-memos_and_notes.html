Memos, briefing notes and the page limit

These formats are old, boring and extremely well designed. Each one is defined less by what it contains than by what it forbids, and the forbidding is where the value is.

Lecture 18 of 28 Reading time, 30 minutes Sources: Amazon narrative memo; UK and military briefing conventions; Mod (2011)
Step 1

The 30-second version

A document format is a constraint, and the constraint does the design work. The memo header is four labelled lines that let a reader triage the document in two seconds. The decision note is one to two pages that must end in a recommendation. The six-page narrative memo is a hard page limit and a ban on bullet points. In each case the format's value comes from what it will not let you do.

This is unlike everything else in this track. Elsewhere design is about presenting content well. Here the format precedes the content and shapes it, because a fixed budget forces an editorial decision that would otherwise be deferred indefinitely. You cannot write six pages without deciding what the argument is; you can write sixteen without ever deciding.

The second half of the lecture is the mirror case: notes you write for yourself, in Obsidian or plain Markdown. The design problem inverts. There is no reader to persuade, no format to conform to, and the entire question becomes whether you will be able to find and understand the note in two years. Absolute dates, a stable frontmatter block and a readable line length do most of that work.

Feynman test Twitter's character limit made people write better than they would have. Not because 280 characters is a magic number, but because a hard limit forces you to decide what you actually mean before you start. Take the limit away and the same person writes four paragraphs saying the same thing. A page limit is that, for work.
The part usually misreported Amazon's six-page memo is discussed as though six is the important number. It is not. The important thing is that a number was fixed, that it applies to everyone, and that it cannot be negotiated in the moment. Five would work. Four would work. Any limit works, and no limit works at all.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is volume. You will write a handful of formal reports a year and hundreds of these. If a working document takes twenty minutes longer than it needs to because it has no format, that is weeks over a career, and the cost falls on every reader as well as on you.

The second is that these documents exist to produce a decision, and a document that does not produce one has failed regardless of its quality. A briefing note that describes a situation beautifully and does not recommend anything sends the reader back to you, which is the outcome the note was written to avoid. The format's insistence on a recommendation is the mechanism that prevents this.

The third is triage. A senior reader receives twenty documents a week and must decide, quickly, which need action, which need reading and which need filing. The four-line header exists entirely to serve that decision, and a document without one forces the reader to read the first paragraph to find out whether they needed to. Multiply by twenty.

The fourth reason concerns the notes half and is the one people underrate. A note written for yourself is written by someone with full context for someone who has none, and the gap between them is usually about six months. Almost every convention in step 4's notes profile exists to close that gap, and the reason to adopt them is not tidiness but that a note you cannot reconstruct is a note you did not write.

Step 3

The mental model

Three formats, each defined by its limit.

Two consequences follow.

The first is that the limit must be enforced by something other than the author's judgement, because in the moment the author always believes their case is the exception. Amazon's six pages works because it is institutional. A limit you set for yourself and can relax is not a limit, it is an intention.

The second is that a good format makes the hard editorial question unavoidable and early. Deciding what to cut from sixteen pages down to six is genuinely difficult and it is exactly the thinking the document needed. Without the limit, that thinking is deferred to the reader, who will do it badly because they do not know which parts matter.

Triage header
The four labelled lines at the top of a memo: To, From, Date, Subject. Its purpose is not courtesy but speed: a reader decides in two seconds whether this document is theirs, when it is from, and what it is about.
Classification line
"For decision" or "For information", in the subject or immediately below it. The single most useful convention in institutional writing, because it tells the reader what is expected of them before they read anything.
Narrative form
Full sentences and paragraphs rather than bullet points. Forces the writer to state the relationships between ideas, which bullets allow them to leave implicit. The core of Amazon's memo convention and the part with an actual argument behind it.
Step 4

The detail

The memo header

THE FOUR LINES To: Credit Risk Committee From: M Ervedosa, Model Development Date: 4 August 2026 Subject: Recommendation to recalibrate the SME PD model before Q4 [FOR DECISION] BUILD IT AS A TWO-COLUMN BORDERLESS TABLE so the values align. Labels 600 weight, values 400. Never as tab-separated text, which misaligns the moment a name is longer than expected. THE SUBJECT LINE IS THE WHOLE DOCUMENT IN ONE LINE Not "SME PD model" (a topic). Not "Update on SME PD" (a topic with a verb). But "Recommendation to recalibrate the SME PD model before Q4" (a claim with an action). THE DATE IS ABSOLUTE, ALWAYS "4 August 2026", not "Monday" and not "04/08/2026", which means two different days depending on the reader.

The subject line follows the same rule as the exhibit title in lecture 16 and the action title in the presentations module: state the finding, not the topic. It is the most-read line in the document by a wide margin and in most memos it is wasted on a category name.

The decision note

SectionLengthWhat it must contain
Purpose1 to 2 sentencesWhat decision is being asked for, stated as a question or a proposition
Background1 paragraphOnly what the reader needs to evaluate the recommendation. Not the history
Considerations3 to 5 paragraphsThe options, the trade-offs, the risks, the costs. The honest case against
Recommendation2 to 3 sentencesWhat you think should happen, and what you need from the reader

Two disciplines make the format work. Background is the section that expands without limit if you let it, and the test is whether a sentence changes how a reader would evaluate the recommendation; if not, it belongs in an appendix or nowhere. And Considerations must contain the argument against your own recommendation, stated fairly, because a note that presents only the supporting case invites the reader to go looking for what you left out.

The recommendation goes last in the note and first in the subject line. That is not a contradiction: the subject line serves the reader deciding whether to read, and the final section serves the reader who has read the considerations and is now ready to accept or reject. Both readers are served, which is what the format is for.

Prose against bullets

Amazon's ban on bullet points is the most interesting design decision in this lecture and the argument for it is stronger than it first appears.

A bullet list presents items as parallel and equal, and suppresses the relationships between them. Consider four bullets: rising defaults, a model recalibration, a change of underwriting policy, an economic downturn. Which caused which? The list does not say and cannot say. Written as prose, you are forced to write "because", "despite", "which followed", and each of those words is a claim you have to be willing to make. The list lets you assemble facts without committing to how they fit together, and the reader supplies connections you never checked.

The honest limits of the argument. Genuinely enumerable material, where the items really are parallel and unordered, belongs in a list: four required fields, three regulatory deadlines, a set of options. Forcing those into prose produces worse writing, not better thinking. And a bulleted list is easier to scan, which matters for reference material a reader will return to.

The workable rule: prose for anything containing an argument, lists only for things that are genuinely lists. If you find yourself writing bullets that are full sentences with subordinate clauses, you are writing prose with bullets in front of it, and the bullets are hiding the structure rather than showing it.

The notes profile

A different problem. There is no reader to persuade and no format to conform to; the only question is whether you will find and understand this in two years.

Name the project, the people, the system, the version. It costs one sentence now and is unrecoverable later. 3 LINK RATHER THAN DUPLICATE. A fact in two notes will diverge. One of them will be wrong and you will not know which. 4 TITLE THE NOTE AS A CLAIM WHERE YOU CAN. "SME PD recalibration deferred to Q1" beats "SME PD notes", for the same reason as everywhere else in this course.

Screen against print

A working document read on screen wants looser leading, around 1.25 in Word's terms rather than 1.15, and a slightly larger body, because the reader is further away. A document to be printed wants the tighter values so it fits.

If a document will genuinely be both, choose the screen values. An over-spaced printed page costs a sheet of paper; a cramped screen page costs the reader. And in practice, the great majority of working documents are now read on screen and printed only occasionally, so optimising for print is optimising for the exception.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Why the constraint works, and when it does not

The mechanism behind a page limit is that it converts an open-ended task into a bounded one, and bounded tasks get finished while open-ended ones get extended. It also relocates the editorial decision. Without a limit, the writer includes everything and the reader decides what matters. With one, the writer decides, and the writer is far better placed to, because they know which parts are load-bearing.

There is a failure mode and it is worth naming, because it is what people who dislike page limits are usually reacting to. A limit applied to material that genuinely requires more space produces compression rather than editing: 9pt type, quarter-inch margins, and an appendix carrying the actual content. That is worse than an honest sixteen pages, and it happens whenever the limit is enforced as a rule about page count rather than as a discipline about scope.

Amazon's format handles this with the appendix, which is unlimited. The six pages hold the argument and the appendix holds the evidence. That is the detail that makes the constraint workable rather than merely restrictive, and it is the part most organisations that copy the format leave out.

Notes are a bet on your future self

The last thing in this module is the one with no external reader, and it inverts the course's usual argument in a way worth sitting with.

Everywhere else, the case for design has been that the reader cannot ask you what you meant. In your own notes the reader is you, and the intuition is that you will remember. You will not. The gap between the person writing a note with full context and the person reading it eight months later is very close to the gap between a writer and a stranger, and the writing version of the curse of knowledge applies with the same force.

The asymmetry that matters is between the cost of writing and the cost of finding. Adding a date, a source and a one-line context to a note costs about twenty seconds. Reconstructing that context later costs anywhere from ten minutes to the whole note. Given that most notes are never read again, and a few are read urgently, the expected value strongly favours the twenty seconds, which is a genuinely counterintuitive result about work that mostly does not pay off.

This is also why the notes profile is short and consists mostly of habits rather than styling. Whether Obsidian is set at 16px or 17px does not matter much. Whether the note says "last quarter" or "Q2 2026" decides whether it is usable, and no amount of typography compensates.

Common failure Adopting a format and then treating its sections as boxes to fill. A decision note whose Considerations section contains three paragraphs that do not consider anything has satisfied the format and defeated it. The sections are prompts for thinking, not slots, and the test is whether removing a section would change what the reader concludes. If not, the section is padding wearing a heading.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a working document you sent in the last month and rewrite it as a one-page memo with the four-line header and a subject line that states a claim. Then time how long it takes someone who has not seen either version to say what you want from them, reading each. The difference is what the format buys.
  2. Why is the specific number six not the important thing about Amazon's memo format?
  3. Give the two functions of the four-line header, and state why it should be built as a table rather than typed with tabs.
  4. The recommendation goes last in a decision note and first in the subject line. Explain why that is not a contradiction.
  5. State the argument against bullet points properly, using an example where a list conceals a causal relationship.
  6. Give the honest limits of that argument. When are bullets correct?
  7. Why must Considerations contain the case against your own recommendation?
  8. What is the failure mode of a page limit, and what feature of Amazon's format prevents it?
  9. State the asymmetry argument for adding context to a personal note, and say why it holds even though most notes are never read again.
  10. A colleague's decision note has all four sections and you still cannot tell what they want. Diagnose it using the test in the final callout.
