Hierarchy and contrast

Your page already has a hierarchy. The reader's eye is already going somewhere first. The only question is whether that somewhere is the thing you would have chosen.

Lecture 9 of 11 Reading time, 30 minutes Sources: Williams (1994); Duarte (2008); Ware (2000)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Hierarchy is the order in which things reach the reader. It is not a property of your outline, it is a property of the page, and it exists whether or not you designed it. Every page has one, produced by whatever visual differences happen to be present, and the design question is only whether it matches the importance ordering you intended.

You build hierarchy from a small set of tools, and they are not equally good. In descending order of preference: position, space, size, weight, then colour, then everything else. Most people reach for colour first because it is the most visible lever. It sits fifth, for the reasons lecture 5 gave: hue is the channel that fails for the printer, the projector and one reader in twelve.

The second rule is about depth. Most documents need exactly three levels, and most documents have six. Each additional level requires a visual distinction that must be discriminable from all the others, and past three or four the distinctions become too fine to read at a glance, which is the only speed at which hierarchy is consumed.

Feynman test Walk into a supermarket you have never been in and you find the milk in about twenty seconds without reading a single word. Big signs hanging from the ceiling, smaller signs on the aisle ends, labels on the shelf. Three levels, each one obviously bigger than the next, and you used them without noticing. Nobody needed a fourth level of sign, and if every sign were the same size you would be there all afternoon.
The test that settles arguments Squint at the page until the text is unreadable, or shrink it to a thumbnail. Note the order in which things emerge from the blur. That order is your hierarchy, as the reader receives it, and it is not open to negotiation. If the caveat emerges before the finding, the page says the caveat matters more, regardless of what you intended and regardless of what the text says.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that most readers never read your document. They scan it, extract what the hierarchy offers them, and act on that. Nielsen Norman Group's eye-tracking work has documented the patterns for two decades: readers move in F-shapes, layer-cake patterns and spotted patterns, all of which are ways of consuming the hierarchy and skipping the body. For a professional document, the hierarchy is not a navigational aid on top of the content. For a large fraction of your audience it is the content.

The second reason follows. If the hierarchy is what most readers receive, then a document whose hierarchy misrepresents its argument is misleading most of its audience, whatever the body text says. A report where the headline is the reassuring summary and the material qualification is a sub-sub-heading has communicated the reassurance and not the qualification, to almost everyone, and it will be defended on the grounds that the qualification was disclosed.

The third reason is that hierarchy is what makes a document navigable, and navigability is what makes it usable a second time. A reader coming back to find one number six weeks later depends entirely on the visual structure. Documents with weak hierarchy get read once and then re-derived rather than consulted, which is expensive in a way nobody measures.

The fourth is that hierarchy failures are cheap to fix and are almost never fixed, because the author cannot see them. You know which heading is the important one, so you see the importance whether or not it is on the page. This is the same blindness lecture 1 identified for collisions and it has the same remedy: a mechanical test rather than a judgement, which is what the squint test is.

Step 3

The mental model

The squint test, run on the same content twice.

Two consequences follow, and the second is the one that produces most of the practical improvement.

The first is that hierarchy should be designed as a set of exit points. A well-structured document is one a reader can abandon at three different depths and take away something coherent from each. That reframing is more useful than thinking about heading sizes, because it makes the test concrete: read only level one, and ask whether what you have is true and useful on its own.

The second is that contrast between levels must be decisive. Robin Williams states the rule as: if two things are not the same, make them very different. A 16px heading above 15px body text is not a hierarchy, it is a mistake that looks like a hierarchy. Adjacent levels need a difference the reader registers without comparison, which in practice means a size ratio of at least 1.25 combined with a weight change, or a larger size ratio alone.

Entry point
The element the eye reaches first. Every surface has exactly one, determined by the strongest combination of position, size, weight and isolation. Two candidates competing for it produces no entry point at all rather than two.
Level
A tier in the hierarchy, defined by a distinct combination of visual properties. Levels must be discriminable pairwise and not merely ordered, which is why the practical ceiling is three or four.
Salience
How strongly an element attracts attention relative to its surroundings. A fixed budget on any surface: raising one element's salience necessarily lowers everything else's, which is why emphasising everything emphasises nothing.

Position sits first and is the most underused. The top-left of a page or slide is the strongest location available in a left-to-right reading culture, and it costs nothing. An element placed there is prominent before any other treatment is applied, which is why the presentation specification puts the action title in exactly that spot and why the dashboard specification puts the most important measure top-left.

Isolation is second and is the tool of choice when you cannot change size. A single element surrounded by generous space is prominent regardless of how it is set, which is the mechanism behind a pull quote, a KPI tile and Morrison's poster layout. It is also the most expensive in page, which is why step 5 of the previous lecture matters.

Colour sits fifth, and the ranking is the practical conclusion of lecture 5. Colour is a genuine hierarchy tool and it fails in four ordinary circumstances that the four tools above it survive. Use it, and do not let it carry a distinction alone.

Three levels, and how to spend them

Most professional documents need three: the document or section title, the section headings, and the body. A long report can justify a fourth for subsections. Beyond that the distinctions become too fine, and the symptom is a document where the reader cannot tell whether a heading is a peer of the one above or subordinate to it.

The usual objection is that the content genuinely has six levels of nesting. Sometimes it does, and the answer is not a sixth heading style. It is to break the document into parts, so that what was level five becomes level two inside a smaller unit. Depth of hierarchy in the content is a structural problem, and solving it with visual distinctions pushes the difficulty onto the reader.

Emphasis inside running text

A separate problem from document hierarchy, and one with a clear ranking.

MethodVerdict
BoldStrong, use sparingly. Best for a term being defined or a genuinely critical phrase. More than about one per paragraph and the page becomes a field of dark patches with no signal
ItalicQuieter, correct for titles, foreign terms, and mild stress. Slows reading at length, so never for more than a sentence
CAPITALSAvoid in running text. Removes the word-shape cue readers use, measurably slowing reading. Acceptable for short labels with added tracking
ColourReserve for links. A coloured word in body text reads as a link to most readers, and disappointing that expectation is worse than not emphasising
UnderlineLinks only. Nothing else, ever. It is the one convention the web has made unambiguous
Bold plus italicNo. Pick one

The house rule this track uses, following Bringhurst, is stricter than most: bold is reserved for defining a term on first appearance, and general stress is carried by sentence structure rather than by typography. That is a stylistic choice rather than a finding, but it has the merit of making bold mean something, which the alternative does not.

Hierarchy in tables and charts

Tables have a hierarchy too and it is usually absent. A table where the header, the body rows and the total row all look alike has one level, and the reader must read every cell to find the one that matters. The report specification's rules follow directly: header in a different weight with a rule beneath, total separated by a rule above, and the cells that carry the finding given a tint. Three levels, in a table.

Charts likewise. The default state of most charting software is a chart with no hierarchy at all: every series equally prominent, gridlines as dark as the data, axis labels the same size as the title. The dashboard specification's grey-by-default rule is a hierarchy instruction: put the subject series at level one and everything else at level three, so that the chart makes its point before the reader has parsed the axis.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Hierarchy is an argument

Deciding what the reader sees first is deciding what your document claims is most important. That is a substantive editorial judgement wearing visual clothing, and it is worth recognising as such, because it means hierarchy decisions are not delegable to whoever is doing the formatting.

The clearest case is the consulting deck's action title, covered properly in the presentations module. Writing "Default rates rose 40 basis points in the second half" as a slide's largest element rather than "Default rate trends" is not a formatting choice. It commits you to a claim, and it forces the analysis to have produced one. Decks whose titles are all topic labels are usually decks whose authors have not decided what they think, and the topic labels are hiding that.

The same test applies to a report. Read only the level-one and level-two elements of a document, in order, and ask whether they form an argument. If they form a table of contents instead, the hierarchy is describing the document's organisation rather than its content, and a reader who scans will come away with a shape and no conclusion.

The ethics of the fourth level

Lecture 1 raised this and hierarchy is where it is most easily done and hardest to prove. Every document contains material its author would rather the reader did not dwell on: the caveat, the sensitivity, the assumption that is doing most of the work. Hierarchy determines whether that material is encountered or merely present.

The mechanisms are entirely ordinary and individually defensible. Put the qualification one level lower than the finding. Put it in the appendix rather than the summary. Set the footnote at 8pt because footnotes are 8pt. Nobody has lied, nothing has been withheld, and a reader who scans, which is most of them, will not encounter it.

Tufte's Columbia analysis is the canonical case and it is exactly a hierarchy failure: the crucial fact was on the slide, four levels down a bullet hierarchy, in the smallest type, under a reassuring headline. The information was disclosed and the hierarchy said it was unimportant, and the hierarchy is what was received.

The workable professional standard is a test rather than a principle: material that would change a reader's decision belongs at the level of the finding it qualifies, not one below it. That is checkable, it is arguable in a review, and it does not require anyone to have good intentions.

Common failure Learning about hierarchy produces documents with more levels, not fewer. The instinct is to give each kind of content its own treatment: a style for warnings, one for examples, one for definitions, one for cross-references. Each is defensible and collectively they produce a page with nine visual registers and no hierarchy, because none of them is discriminable from the others at a glance. Three levels plus at most two special-purpose treatments is the working budget.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a document you produced, shrink it to thumbnails so no text is readable, and write down the order in which elements emerge. Then write down the order of importance you intended. Compare the two lists. Then extract only the level-one and level-two elements as plain text and read them in sequence: do they form an argument, or a table of contents?
  2. Why is it wrong to say a page "has no hierarchy"? State what is actually true instead.
  3. Rank the hierarchy tools and justify the position of colour specifically.
  4. Why does position cost nothing while isolation costs page? Give a situation where you would use each.
  5. Two elements are competing to be the entry point. Explain why the result is not two entry points.
  6. A heading is 16px bold above 15px regular body. State why this fails and give the minimum change that fixes it.
  7. Your content genuinely has six levels of nesting. Explain why adding a fifth and sixth heading style is the wrong answer, and give the right one.
  8. Why is a coloured word in body text a poor emphasis method? The reason is not about accessibility.
  9. Give the three levels a well-designed table should have and the visual treatment of each.
  10. State the professional standard offered in step 5 for where a caveat belongs, and explain why it is framed as a test rather than as a principle.
