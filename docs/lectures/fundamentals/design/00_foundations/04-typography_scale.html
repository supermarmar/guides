Typography II: measure, leading and the scale

Three numbers decide whether a page is comfortable to read, and they are locked together: change one and at least one other must move. This is the most consequential lecture in the module and the one with the least to argue about.

Lecture 4 of 11 Reading time, 35 minutes Sources: Bringhurst (1992); Butterick; Rutter (2017); GOV.UK Design System
Step 1

The 30-second version

The three numbers are size, measure and leading. Measure is the length of a line of text, counted in characters rather than in millimetres, because characters are what the eye processes. Leading is the vertical distance from one baseline to the next. Set these three well and a mediocre typeface reads pleasantly; set them badly and no typeface will save you.

There is a target: 66 characters per line, with anything from 45 to 75 acceptable. This is the most agreed-upon number in the whole subject. Bringhurst gives 66 as ideal and 45 to 75 as the range. Rutter gives 45 to 75. The GOV.UK Design System caps at 75. Butterick is the outlier at 45 to 90, and where authorities disagree the honest move is to take the overlap, which is 50 to 75.

The lock between the three is the part people miss. A long line needs more leading, because the eye has further to travel on the return sweep and needs a bigger vertical target to land on. A short line needs less. So "1.5 line height" is not a setting you can carry from one design to another; it is correct at one measure and wrong at another. Any time you widen a column you have taken on a debt in leading, and the cheaper repayment is usually to narrow the column again.

Feynman test Reading a line is easy. Finding the start of the next one is the hard part, and your eye does it by a fast blind jump leftward and down. If the lines are long, that jump is long, and the eye is more likely to land on the line you just read or the one after. You have experienced this: you reread a line without meaning to. That was not inattention. That was a measure problem.
The claim behind the number The 66-character target is not arbitrary and not aesthetic. It falls out of two facts about the eye: a fixation takes in roughly seven to nine characters, and the return sweep is a ballistic movement whose accuracy degrades with distance. Sixty-six characters is about eight fixations, which is about as far as the sweep stays reliable. That is why the figure is stable across authorities who agree on nothing else.
Step 2

Why this matters

These three settings determine whether a reader finishes. Not whether they admire the page, and not whether they think it looks professional: whether they get to the end. A document at a 110-character measure is not slightly worse than one at 66; it is one that a tired reader abandons in the third paragraph, and the abandonment is attributed to the content.

The second reason is that this is the cheapest large improvement available. Fixing a measure costs one change to a margin or a container width. It adds no ink, no colour, no complexity and no words. Nothing else in this course delivers as much for as little, which is exactly why step 5 of the previous lecture argued that the field's attention is misallocated toward typeface selection.

The third reason is that the defaults are wrong in a specific and predictable direction. A browser with no styling sets text at the full window width, which on a modern monitor is 200 characters or more. A Word document at Microsoft's default margins on A4 at 11pt sits around 90 to 95 characters, over the band. A slide template will happily run text edge to edge. In every case the default errs long, because the default was set by something that does not know what the content is and defaults to filling the space available. Knowing the direction of the error means you know which way to reach before you measure.

The fourth is accessibility, which here is not a separate concern bolted on but the same concern stated legally. WCAG success criterion 1.4.12 requires that a user be able to set line height to at least 1.5 times the font size without loss of content, and 16px is the effective floor for body text on screen because iOS enlarges anything smaller. A design that already sits at 1.5 leading and 16px satisfies these without being asked.

Step 3

The mental model

Watch the return sweep. It is the thing the three numbers are jointly protecting, and once you can see it as a mechanical problem the settings stop being taste.

Two consequences, and the second is the useful working rule.

The first is that leading and measure are a pair, not two independent settings. Any statement of one without the other is incomplete. "Line height 1.5" is meaningless until you know how long the line is, which is why every specification in this track states measure and leading together and why the report file anchors both to a single baseline figure.

The second is a diagnostic shortcut. When text feels heavy or tiring and you cannot say why, count the characters in a full line before you do anything else. If the answer is over 80, stop looking; you have found it. This single check resolves more readability complaints than every other technique in this module combined, and it takes ten seconds.

Measure
The length of a line of text, expressed in characters including spaces. In CSS, the ch unit is the width of the digit zero in the current font, so max-width: 66ch is a direct expression of it. Bringhurst's rule of thumb without a computer: a line should hold two to three lowercase alphabets.
Leading
Baseline-to-baseline distance, named for the strips of lead once placed between lines of metal type. Expressed as a ratio of the font size (1.5) or as an absolute (11pt on 16.5pt, written 11/16.5). Use the unitless ratio in CSS so it scales; use the absolute in print where you are anchoring a baseline grid.
Return sweep
The ballistic eye movement from the end of one line to the start of the next. Unlike the small saccades along a line it is not visually guided during flight, so its accuracy falls with distance. The reason the measure band exists.

He writes for American lawyers producing court filings on US Letter, where a single-column page at conventional margins runs long and where the alternative to a 90-character measure is a two-column layout that court rules may not permit. Read in that context the figure is a concession rather than a recommendation, and his own worked examples sit far below it. Taking the overlap of the four sources gives 50 to 75, which is what this track uses.

Two adjustments sit on top. A face with a large x-height needs slightly more leading than the table suggests, because the lines are visually heavier and crowd each other; one with long extenders needs more still, because ascenders and descenders from adjacent lines can collide. And headings run tighter than body as they grow, which is why the house scale drops from 1.55 at body size to 1.05 at display: the same ratio that looks generous at 16px looks like a gap at 64px.

Building a scale

A type scale is the set of sizes a document is allowed to use. Having one matters more than which one it is, for the reason lecture 1 gave: consistency is what makes contrast legible, and a document with eleven arbitrary sizes has spent its ability to signal.

The standard method is geometric: pick a base and a ratio, multiply repeatedly. The common ratios come from musical intervals.

RatioNameCharacterSuits
1.200Minor thirdTight, many usable stepsInterfaces, dashboards, dense documents
1.250Major thirdBalancedGeneral-purpose, web and print
1.333Perfect fourthClear jumpsEditorial, marketing pages
1.500Perfect fifthDramatic, few stepsPosters, title slides
1.618Golden ratioVery dramaticDisplay work only

Now the part the generators do not tell you: a pure geometric scale is wrong at both ends, and every mature design system hand-tunes. Material, Carbon and Apple's Dynamic Type all do it. The reason is that perceptible difference is not proportional to size. At the small end, 16px and 20px are clearly different but a 1.25 ratio also demands 12.8px, which is unusable and not a round number. At the large end, adjacent steps at a constant ratio look too similar to read as different levels, so display sizes want a wider ratio than body sizes.

Every value is a whole number. This is not a scale a generator produces, and it is the shape every large design system converges on.

Anchor the scale at the body size and build outward, rather than starting at the top. The body is the size that appears ten thousand times in the document and the one every other size is judged against; a display size chosen first will drag the body somewhere inconvenient.

Fluid sizing on screen

On the web the scale should respond to the viewport, and CSS clamp() does it in one declaration without media queries.

clamp(MINIMUM, PREFERRED, MAXIMUM) The preferred value is a linear function of viewport width: preferred = min + (max - min) x ((100vw - minVW) / (maxVW - minVW)) Worked, for a hero running 36px at a 480px viewport up to 64px at 1280px: slope = (64 - 36) / (1280 - 480) = 0.035 px per px in vw = 0.035 x 100 = 3.5vw intercept = 36 - (0.035 x 480) = 19.2px = 1.2rem font-size: clamp(2.25rem, 1.2rem + 3.5vw, 4rem); SCALE THE HEADINGS. DO NOT SCALE THE BODY. Body is already at its accessible floor of 16px, so a fluid body can only shrink below it or grow to a size nobody chose. Fix the body and let measure do the responsive work instead: the column narrows, the character count stays near 66, and the reader never notices anything happened.

Numerals, briefly but not optionally

Lining against old-style
Lining figures are all cap height. Old-style figures have ascenders and descenders and sit like lowercase. Use lining in tables, headings and anything set in capitals; old-style in serif running prose, where lining figures read as shouting.
Tabular against proportional
Tabular figures all occupy the same width so digits align vertically. Proportional figures are individually spaced and read better in prose. Any column of numbers requires tabular; running text prefers proportional. In CSS, font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums.

The failure this prevents is worth naming because it is so common in financial documents. A table of figures set in proportional numerals will not align, no matter how the cells are aligned, because the digit 1 is narrower than the digit 8. The columns wobble, and the reader who cannot compare down a column has lost the reason the table existed. It is a one-line fix and almost nobody applies it.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Vertical rhythm: a position, argued

Vertical rhythm is the practice of making every vertical measurement in a document a multiple of the baseline leading, so that text on facing pages aligns and the whole document sits on an invisible horizontal grid. Bringhurst treats it as close to obligatory. Much of the web development world adopted it enthusiastically around 2010 and has quietly abandoned it. Both are right, for different media, and the reason is worth understanding.

In print the payoff is real and the cost is low. Facing pages align, text shows through the sheet in register rather than as a blur, and a long document acquires a steadiness the reader feels without identifying. You control the page, nothing reflows, and enforcing the grid is arithmetic you do once. This is why the report specification anchors every vertical value to a 15.4pt baseline and means it.

On the web the payoff mostly evaporates and the cost climbs. There are no facing pages and no show-through. Content reflows, images arrive at arbitrary heights, embedded components bring their own spacing, and one element off the grid cascades through everything below it. Maintaining strict rhythm on a responsive page means fighting the medium continuously for a benefit almost nobody perceives.

So: strict vertical rhythm in print, a consistent spacing scale on screen. The spacing scale captures most of the value, which is that vertical space comes in a small number of deliberate sizes rather than whatever each element happened to bring. That is the position the house file takes and this is the argument for it.

Why the authorities disagree, and what that tells you

It is tempting to treat the disagreement over the measure band as noise around a true value. It is more useful to read it as evidence about context. Every one of these authorities is right about the medium they wrote for.

Bringhurst is writing about books: a fixed page, a reader who has committed an hour, a face chosen for the text, and a printing process the designer specifies. He can afford 66 characters and can insist on it. Butterick is writing about legal documents on US Letter under court formatting rules, where the designer controls less and the page is wider than a book. The GOV.UK figures come from a system serving the entire British public including readers with low literacy, poor eyesight, cheap devices and no patience, which is why their guidance is the most conservative of the four and why it caps rather than targets.

The general lesson outlasts this lecture. When sources give different numbers for the same quantity, the productive question is not which one is right but what each was solving for, and which of those problems is yours. This track states its own numbers and states the medium each is for, which is why there are eight token files rather than one.

Common failure Learning about the measure produces an impulse to set a narrow column and stop. But a container constrained to 66ch inside a very wide viewport leaves a large empty region, and the usual response is to fill it with a sidebar, related links or a navigation panel, which reintroduces the distraction the narrow measure was protecting against. The empty space is not a bug. Gwern's site and Butterick's are both mostly margin at desktop width, deliberately.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a document you produced recently. Count the characters in three full lines of body text and average them. Then compute the leading as a ratio by measuring baseline to baseline and dividing by the point size. Write both numbers down, look them up in the table in step 4, and state whether they are consistent with each other. Then fix whichever is further out and read the result.
  2. Why is measure counted in characters rather than in millimetres or pixels?
  3. State the relationship between measure and leading, and explain it in terms of the return sweep rather than by restating it.
  4. A page has a 95-character measure and cannot be narrowed because the client's template fixes the margins. Give the repair and state what it costs.
  5. Why does a pure geometric type scale fail at both ends? Give a concrete example of each failure.
  6. Derive the clamp() declaration for a heading running 24px at a 480px viewport up to 40px at 1280px, showing the slope and intercept.
  7. Explain why body text should not be scaled fluidly, and say what does the responsive work instead.
  8. Your table of figures will not align even though the cells are right-aligned. Diagnose it and give the CSS.
  9. Step 5 argues for vertical rhythm in print and against it on screen. Construct the strongest case for maintaining it on screen anyway, then say what would have to be true for that case to win.
  10. Butterick permits 90 characters and Bringhurst caps at 75. Rather than picking one, explain what each was solving for and identify which of the two contexts your own most common document resembles.
