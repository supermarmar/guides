A diagnostic protocol

Ten checks, in order, that turn the previous ten lectures into something you can run on a page in twenty minutes. The order is not arbitrary: cheap mechanical checks first, judgement last.

Lecture 11 of 11 Reading time, 30 minutes Synthesis of lectures 1 to 10
Step 1

The 30-second version

Knowing the principles does not make you able to apply them, because when you look at a page you see its content and not its geometry. The remedy is a protocol: a fixed sequence of mechanical checks, each producing an observation rather than an opinion, run in an order that finds the most defects for the least effort.

Ten checks. The first five require no knowledge of the document's subject and can be run by anyone, including on a page in a language you do not read. The last five need you to know what the content is claiming. That division is the useful one, because the first five find most of the problems and the last five find the serious ones.

The single most important instruction is this: observe before you interpret. Every step asks you to write down what is on the page before asking what it means. This sounds pedantic and it is the entire technique, because the moment you start interpreting you start seeing the document you intended rather than the one that exists.

Feynman test A pilot who has flown a thousand times still reads the checklist aloud before take-off. Not because they have forgotten how, but because knowing how is exactly what makes you skip things. The checklist is not for people who do not know the aircraft; it is for people who know it so well they no longer look.
Why this cannot be done by eye You are the worst possible reviewer of your own page. You know which row is the total, so you see it as the total. You know the heading belongs to the section below, so you read it that way. This is the visual form of the curse of knowledge, and no amount of care defeats it, because the defect is in what you can perceive rather than in how hard you are trying. Mechanical checks work because they do not consult your knowledge.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that design review is otherwise unfalsifiable. Feedback on a document's appearance arrives as "it looks a bit cluttered" or "something is off", which is accurate and actionable by nobody. A protocol converts that into "the measure is 94 characters, there are seven distinct gap sizes, and the total row is undifferentiated", which three people can agree on and one person can fix.

The second is that it makes the skill transferable and delegable. A junior colleague who runs these ten checks will find real defects on their first attempt, without having read the ten lectures, because the checks do not require the theory. The theory explains why each check matters and what to do about what it finds, and it is genuinely useful, but the checks work without it. That asymmetry is the reason to write the protocol down.

The third is triage. Finding twenty defects is not helpful if you have an hour before the deadline. The protocol produces findings in a rough order of severity, and step 5 gives an explicit repair ordering, so that the hour goes on the three changes that matter rather than on the twenty that are true.

The fourth is that it works on other people's documents, which is where most of the learning happens. Running this on a regulator's consultation paper, a competitor's annual report or a vendor's pitch deck is fast, unembarrassing, and teaches you more about what works than reviewing your own material ever will.

Step 3

The mental model

The protocol, in order, with what each step catches and where it comes from.

Two consequences follow.

The first is that the content-blind checks come first because they are cheap and because they cannot be contaminated by your knowledge of the subject. Running them on your own document is nearly as reliable as running them on someone else's, which is not true of anything after step 6.

The second is that the protocol produces evidence rather than opinions, and evidence is what survives a disagreement with a stakeholder. "The measure is 94 characters and the readable band tops out at 75" is a different kind of statement from "the margins feel tight", and it is a much harder one to overrule.

Step 4

The detail

Running each step

1. Squint or thumbnail
Shrink the page until text is unreadable, or defocus your eyes. Write down, in order, the first three things that emerge. Compare with the three things you would have chosen. A page where nothing emerges first has no entry point; a page where the wrong thing emerges has a hierarchy that contradicts its argument.
2. Count the measure
Count the characters, including spaces, in three full lines of body text and take the average. Under 45 or over 75 is a finding; over 90 is the most likely single cause of a document being tiring. On screen, resize the window and check the count holds.
3. Measure every gap
List every distinct vertical gap on the page as a number. Count how many distinct values there are and compute the ratio between adjacent ones. More than about six values, or ratios under 1.25, means the spacing is encoding nothing. All values identical means the same thing more emphatically.
4. Heading space ratio
For each heading, measure the space above and the space below and divide. Anything under 2 is a finding, and 1 is the classic default failure. This takes two minutes and is the highest-yield check in the whole protocol on documents produced in a word processor.
5. Straight edge, left margin
Lay a straight edge, physical or on screen, along the left edge of the body text. Mark everything that should be flush and is not: headings, bullets, figure edges, table borders, captions, page numbers. Repeat on the right for any right-aligned material, especially number columns.
6. Difference audit
Two columns. On the left, every visual difference on the page: each change of size, weight, colour, spacing, alignment, case or shape. On the right, the meaning each one encodes. Blank right-hand cells are noise. Then invert: list the distinctions your content contains and check each appears on the left. Missing ones are collisions. The slowest step and the one that finds the most.
7. Count the levels
How many distinct tiers of hierarchy exist, and can you tell each from its neighbours at a glance without comparing? More than four is a finding. Two levels you have to compare side by side to distinguish are one level with a defect.
8. Desaturate
Convert to greyscale, or print in mono. Every distinction that disappears was encoded by hue alone. Charts, status indicators, variance figures and any use of red and green are the usual casualties.
9. Measure text contrast
Sample the actual pixel values of each text colour and the surface it sits on, and compute the ratio. Body text needs 4.5:1 against the surface it is actually on, which is not necessarily white. Captions, placeholders and text on tinted panels are where this fails.
10. Chart encoding audit
For each chart, write the reader's question in one sentence. Then list every variable and the channel carrying it. Check that the variable in the question is on the strongest channel available. Check the baseline against the rule from lecture 10. Check whether a legend could be replaced by direct labels.

DOCUMENT: a 14-page model validation report, A4, produced in Word. PAGE SAMPLED: page 6, which carries body text, two headings and a table. 1 SQUINT Emerges first: the table. Triage: what to fix first

Eight findings and, realistically, an afternoon. Repair in this order, which sorts by reader impact divided by effort rather than by the order the checks ran.

OrderFixWhy hereEffort
1Add a text label beside the RAG colourOnly finding that loses information outright, and it is a compliance failureMinutes
2Widen the margins to bring the measure to about 66Affects every line of every page. One change to one settingMinutes
3Fix the heading space ratios to 3:1Makes the structure visible throughout. One change per styleMinutes
4Separate H3 from body by size and weightRestores a level that currently does not existMinutes
5Mark the total row with a rule above and boldReal collision, but confined to one tableMinutes
6Darken the caption and table header coloursAccessibility, but low-traffic textMinutes
7Normalise decimals and remove the stray heading colourNoise. Cheap and cosmeticMinutes
8Left-align the captionGenuinely trivialSeconds

Notice that every fix on that list is a change to a style definition rather than to the page. That is not a coincidence: a defect found on a sampled page is nearly always systematic, and repairing it at the style level fixes all fourteen pages at once. If a fix cannot be made at the style level, that is itself a finding about how the document was built.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Why a protocol beats expertise, and when it does not

An experienced designer looking at that page would have spotted most of the same defects in ninety seconds, without a ruler. So why write down a thirty-minute procedure?

Because the expert's ninety seconds is not reliably available. It degrades under time pressure, on a document they have already seen twenty times, on their own work, and at five o'clock on a Friday. The checklist literature in medicine and aviation makes the same argument and has the outcome data to back it: checklists do not help because practitioners lack knowledge, they help because knowledge is not what fails.

The honest limit is that a protocol finds the defects it was written to find and is blind to everything else. It will not tell you the document is organised wrongly, that the exhibit is answering the wrong question, or that the whole thing should have been two pages. Those are the judgements that matter most and no checklist reaches them. What the protocol does is clear away the thirty mechanical defects that would otherwise consume the review, so that the conversation can be about the two that matter.

Building it into how you work

Three placements, in increasing order of value.

The weakest is at the end, as a final check before sending. Better than nothing, and by that point the document's structure is fixed and most findings will be declined for lack of time.

Better is at the template stage. Run the protocol once on a specimen page of a new template, fix everything at the style level, and every document built from it inherits the repairs. This is where the eight fixes in the table above genuinely belong, and it is why the token specifications in this track exist: they are the output of running this protocol in advance rather than in arrears.

Best is on other people's work, regularly, with no stakes. Take a consultation paper, a competitor's annual report, a vendor deck, and run the ten checks. It takes half an hour, you learn something every time, and it builds the ninety-second version of the skill faster than anything else, because you are looking at geometry rather than at content you care about.

The eleventh check

There is one more question, deliberately outside the protocol because it is not mechanical and cannot be delegated. Having found and fixed everything above, ask: what would this document look like if it were half its length?

The reason it sits here rather than in the list is that every technique in this module improves the presentation of what is on the page, and the largest available improvement to most professional documents is that less should be. A page whose measure, spacing, hierarchy and contrast are all correct is a well-designed page, and it may still be three paragraphs that should have been one. Design cannot fix that, and it is very good at concealing it.

Common failure The protocol produces a list of findings, and a list of findings sent to an author reads as an attack. Report the two or three that matter, with the reason and the fix, and hold the rest. A review that returns eight defects gets none of them fixed; a review that returns three gets three. This is a fact about people rather than about documents, and ignoring it wastes the whole exercise.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Run the full ten-step protocol on one page of a document you did not write, writing down every observation before interpreting any of it. Then run it on one page of your own. Compare how many findings each produced and, more importantly, how confident you felt at each step. The gap between the two is the curse of knowledge, measured.
  2. Why are the first five checks grouped as content-blind, and what practical advantage does that give them?
  3. State the rule that makes the protocol work, and explain what goes wrong if you break it.
  4. Which single check gives the highest yield on documents produced in a word processor, and why?
  5. On a forty-page report, would you run all ten checks on three pages or the first five on all forty? Justify your answer.
  6. In the worked pass, the desaturation finding was ranked first for repair despite being found at step 8. Explain the ranking criterion.
  7. Every fix in the triage table was a change to a style rather than to a page. Why, and what does it mean if a fix cannot be made that way?
  8. Give the strongest argument that an experienced designer does not need this protocol, then say why the argument fails.
  9. Name something important that the protocol cannot find. Why is it excluded rather than added as an eleventh step?
  10. You have run the protocol on a colleague's report and found eight defects. Describe what you actually send them and why.
