Whitespace

Space is the only encoding channel that costs no ink, no colour and no reader attention. It is also the one most people set by accident, and the one a stakeholder will ask you to remove.

Lecture 8 of 11 Reading time, 30 minutes Sources: Müller-Brockmann (1961); Bringhurst (1992); Mod (2011)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Space encodes relationship. Things close together are read as belonging together, which lecture 2 established as the proximity principle and as the grouping cue that beats colour, size and shape. That makes space an encoding channel rather than a leftover, and it makes the distribution of space a set of claims about what belongs with what.

The operative rule is that space is relative, never absolute. A 16px gap communicates nothing on its own. A 16px gap sitting next to a 48px gap communicates "these two belong together and that one does not", clearly and instantly, to every reader. When you set a space you are not choosing a distance; you are choosing a ratio against the neighbouring distances, and only the ratio is read.

This is why the most common spacing error is not "too little space" or "too much space" but uniform space. A page where every gap is 12px has used the channel and encoded nothing with it, which is exactly the noise failure from lecture 1: a great many visual differences, none of which mean anything, because they are all the same.

Feynman test Say the sentence "let us eat grandma" with no pauses, then with a pause in the right place. Same words, different meaning, and the difference is silence. Space on a page works the same way. It is not the absence of content; it is punctuation you cannot hear.
Where this goes wrong institutionally Whitespace is the only design decision a non-designer will reliably object to, because it looks like unused space and unused space looks like waste. Nobody asks you to use fewer colours. Everybody, eventually, asks whether the gap could be smaller so more fits on the page. Step 5 covers how to answer that, because you will need to.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that space is doing the work whether or not you set it. Every document already has spacing, inherited from a template or a default, and that spacing is already making claims about what belongs with what. Word's default heading styles put nearly equal space above and below a heading, which asserts that the heading belongs equally to the section above and the section below. That assertion is false in every document ever written, and it is being made on your behalf several hundred times.

The second reason is that it is free in the only currency that matters. Colour costs salience, as lecture 5 argued: every coloured element reduces the prominence of every other. A rule costs ink and adds a line the reader must process. A box costs an enclosure that then competes with proximity. Space costs page, and page is the cheapest thing you have, particularly in a PDF where there is no marginal cost to a longer document at all.

The third reason is that spacing errors are the ones that make work look amateur without anyone being able to say why. A document can have a good typeface, a sensible palette and a sound structure and still read as unprofessional, and when it does, the cause is usually spacing: headings that float between sections, lists crowded against their introductions, exhibits closer to the following paragraph than the preceding one. Readers reliably detect this and reliably misattribute it.

The fourth is that space is the mechanism through which structure becomes visible. You can have a perfect logical hierarchy in an outline and communicate none of it, because hierarchy on a page is expressed almost entirely through relative spacing. Lecture 9 covers the other tools, and space is the one that does most of the work at the level of the section.

Step 3

The mental model

Six identical items, the same total height in each panel, the same amount of space in each panel. Only the distribution changes.

Two consequences follow.

The first is a diagnostic. When a page is confusing, do not ask whether it needs more space. Measure the gaps and ask whether they differ enough. A page with gaps of 12, 14 and 16px has three sizes and no contrast between them; a page with 8, 24 and 48px has three sizes the reader can tell apart. The ratio between adjacent steps needs to be roughly 1.5 or more to be read as deliberate, which is the same threshold argument the type scale ran into in lecture 4.

The second is that space must be allocated hierarchically, from the outside in. Decide the space between sections first, then between blocks within a section, then between elements within a block, each one clearly smaller than its parent. Building outward from the smallest gap produces a document where the top-level structure is under-spaced, because by the time you get there you have used your range.

Macro whitespace
Space between blocks: margins, gutters, section breaks, the gap around an exhibit. Carries the document's structure and is the space a stakeholder will ask you to reduce.
Micro whitespace
Space within and between characters, words and lines: leading, word spacing, tracking. Carries readability rather than structure, and is almost never noticed until it is wrong.
Active whitespace
Space introduced deliberately to separate, emphasise or direct. Distinguished from passive whitespace, which is what remains around content that has been placed. Both are real; only the first is a decision.
Step 4

The detail

The spacing scale

Like the type scale, spacing should come from a fixed set rather than being chosen per instance. The house scale uses a 4px base with 8px as the working increment, and it is deliberately not a constant ratio.

THE HOUSE SPACING SCALE token value ratio to previous typical use ----------- ----- ----------------- -------------------------- space.xxs 4px - chip padding, icon gap space.xs 8px 2.00 tight component padding space.sm 12px 1.50 input padding, list gaps space.md 16px 1.33 default component padding space.lg 24px 1.50 card padding, paragraph gap space.xl 32px 1.33 component separation space.xxl 48px 1.50 block separation space.section 64px 1.33 section rhythm space.section-lg 96px 1.50 major section break space.hero 120px 1.25 hero bands Every ratio is at least 1.25 and most are 1.33 or 1.5. That is the point. Adjacent steps must be distinguishable, or the scale has ten entries and five usable values. WHY NOT LINEAR (4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28 ...)? Because the ratio collapses as the numbers grow. 4 to 8 is a doubling and obvious; 24 to 28 is 1.17 and invisible. A linear scale gives fine control exactly where you do not need it and no contrast where you do.

The nesting rule

One rule prevents a whole class of error and follows directly from lecture 2's strength ordering. External gap must exceed internal padding. If a card has 16px of padding inside it, the space between that card and the next element must be more than 16px, or the enclosure cue and the proximity cue are making contradictory claims and the reader gets an ambiguous grouping.

The failure is easy to produce and hard to spot: a set of tiles with generous internal padding and a tight gutter reads as one continuous surface with lines drawn on it rather than as separate tiles. The fix is usually to reduce the internal padding rather than increase the gap, because the gap is constrained by the layout and the padding is not.

The heading ladder, derived

Lecture 2 gave the rule that space above a heading should be roughly three times the space below it. Here is the full ladder that follows, using the scale above.

Two things about that table are worth noticing. The ratio grows as the heading level rises, from 3:1 at the minor level to 4:1 at the section level, because a more important break needs a more emphatic separation. And lists and figures are symmetric, because they belong to the surrounding text on both sides rather than introducing something. A figure with more space above than below would read as belonging to the paragraph after it, which is almost always wrong.

Margins are not waste

Bringhurst's claim is that about half of a page's character lives in its margins. Craig Mod, writing about digital reading, describes good margins as felt but not noticed, and argues that they signal care in a way readers register without articulating. Both are making an aesthetic argument, and there are two functional ones underneath it that are easier to defend in a meeting.

The first is that the margin is what fixes the measure. A page with 15mm margins at 11pt has a measure around 95 characters, which lecture 4 established as outside the usable band. The margin is not decoration around the text block; it is the mechanism that sets the text block's width, and arguing to reduce it is arguing to make the text harder to read.

The second is physical. A reader holds a printed page, and their thumbs land in the outer margins. A margin narrow enough to be efficient is narrow enough to be covered by a hand. On screen the equivalent is that a text block running to the edge of the viewport has nothing separating it from the browser furniture and the rest of the desktop.

Micro-space

Word spacing
Leave it alone. The typeface designer set it and they were right. The one exception is justified text, where the software varies it to fill the line, which is what produces rivers.
Tracking, at body size
Zero. Adding letter-spacing to body text is a reliable marker of amateur work: it degrades word-shape recognition and slows reading for no gain.
Tracking, for capitals and small caps
Add 5 to 12 per cent. Capitals were designed to sit next to lowercase, not next to each other, so a run of them is too tight by default. This is the one case where adding letter-spacing is not merely permitted but required.
Tracking, at display sizes
Negative, roughly minus 0.5 to minus 2 per cent above about 32px. Type is spaced for reading size; at display size the same relative spacing looks loose. The house scale carries negative tracking from h3 upward for this reason.

Justification, and why this track avoids it

Justified text has equal margins on both sides and is achieved by varying word spacing line by line. It looks tidy and it has a cost: where the software must stretch a line, the enlarged word spaces on successive lines can align vertically into rivers of white running down the paragraph, which the eye follows and which genuinely disrupts reading.

Good justification needs hyphenation and a sophisticated line-breaking algorithm, ideally one that optimises the whole paragraph rather than each line in turn. Professional typesetting systems have this. Word does not do it well, and browsers do it poorly enough that the CSS specification's own hyphenation support remains patchy. So the practical rule for this track is ragged-right everywhere, with the single exception of a print document typeset in a system you trust, at a measure of 60 characters or more, with hyphenation on.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Defending whitespace to someone who wants it removed

You will have this conversation. Someone senior will look at a well-spaced page and ask whether it could be tightened so the section fits on one page, and the request will feel reasonable because empty space genuinely does look like unused capacity.

The argument that does not work is the aesthetic one. Saying the page breathes, or that the whitespace is doing something, invites the reply that it is a report rather than an art object, and that reply lands.

The arguments that do work are functional and specific. The space above headings is what tells the reader which section a heading belongs to; remove it and they will read the heading as attached to the previous section. The margin is what holds the line length at 66 characters; narrow it and the line goes to 95, which is past where reading speed drops. The gap around the exhibit is what stops the reader attaching it to the wrong paragraph. Each of these is a claim about reader behaviour rather than about beauty, each is checkable, and each identifies what specifically breaks.

The concession worth offering is that space between blocks can usually be tightened by one step on the scale without losing the ratios, because what the reader reads is the ratio. Going from 48/12 to 32/8 preserves the 4:1 relationship and saves real page. Going from 48/12 to 16/16 destroys it. That distinction is the thing to defend, and it is a much narrower thing to defend than "the whitespace".

Subtraction is the main move

Every lecture so far has ended up at some version of the same instruction, and it is worth stating once explicitly because it is the closest this course has to a general method.

When a page is not working, the productive move is almost always to remove something rather than to add something. Remove a visual difference that encodes nothing. Remove a colour that has no job. Remove a rule that a gap could do instead. Remove a heading level that duplicates another. The reason is structural rather than a matter of taste: every element on a page competes with every other for a fixed budget of reader attention, so removing an element does not merely subtract it, it increases the salience of everything that remains.

Whitespace is where this becomes most obvious, because whitespace is what you get when you remove something. That is why the instinct to add space is usually mistaken and the instinct to remove content is usually right. The page did not need more space; it needed less on it.

Common failure Learning about whitespace produces documents where everything is spaced generously and uniformly, which is the panel A failure with bigger numbers. Uniform generous spacing communicates exactly as little as uniform tight spacing and wastes more page doing it. The goal is not more space; it is a clear difference between the spaces, which usually means some gaps get smaller.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a page you produced and measure every vertical gap on it: between paragraphs, above and below each heading, around each figure and list. Write the numbers in a column. Count how many distinct values there are and what the ratio is between adjacent ones. Then redraw the page using only values from the house scale in step 4, keeping the total length roughly the same, and compare.
  2. Why is uniform spacing worse than either tight or generous spacing?
  3. State the rule about relative and absolute space, and explain what it implies about fixing a confusing page.
  4. What is the minimum ratio between adjacent steps on a spacing scale for the difference to read as deliberate, and where else in this module has the same threshold appeared?
  5. A card has 16px of internal padding and sits in a grid with a 12px gutter. State the problem, name the two competing Gestalt cues, and give the fix you would prefer and why.
  6. Why are figures and lists given symmetric space when headings are not?
  7. Give the two functional arguments for generous margins that do not depend on aesthetics.
  8. When is adding letter-spacing correct, and what is the specific reason?
  9. Explain what a river is, why justification produces them, and under what three conditions justified text is acceptable.
  10. A senior reviewer asks you to tighten a section so it fits on one page. Write the two-sentence answer you would give, and name the concession you would offer.
