Colour II: building a palette

A palette is not a set of colours you like. It is a set of roles with values assigned to them, built in a fixed order, and split into three systems that must not be allowed to mix.

Lecture 6 of 11 Reading time, 40 minutes Sources: Brewer (ColorBrewer); WCAG 2.1; Radix Colors; IBM Carbon
Step 1

The 30-second version

Build in this order: the neutral ramp first, then one accent, then the semantic colours, and separately from all of them a data palette. The order is not arbitrary. Each stage constrains the next, and reversing it produces a palette that looks good as swatches and fails as a document.

Most of a page is neutral. Text, rules, surfaces, borders, gridlines and disabled states are all neutral, which means the ramp does the overwhelming majority of the work and deserves the overwhelming majority of the attention. A palette exercise that starts with the brand colour has started at the least-used element.

The second rule is separation. Interface colour and data colour are different systems and must not share values. If the accent that marks a link also appears as a chart series, the reader can no longer tell whether a colour is decoration or information, and the whole encoding collapses. Few, Stone and Brewer arrive at this independently and it is the single most violated rule in business reporting.

Feynman test Think of a palette like the staff at a restaurant rather than like a paint box. You do not hire people because you like them; you hire a chef, a waiter and a cashier because those are the jobs. If you hire five chefs the place fails, however good they are individually. A palette is a staffing plan: every colour has a job, and a colour with no job is a colour that gets in the way.
The advice nobody gives Do not generate a palette. Take one that has been tested and adjust it. ColorBrewer's schemes were built from perceptual research and validated on maps; Okabe-Ito was engineered against colour vision deficiency; Radix's ramps have contrast guarantees baked into their step semantics. A palette produced by rotating a colour wheel in a generator has none of this behind it and will be worse, which is not a comment on your taste.
Step 2

Why this matters

A role-based palette survives change and a colour-list palette does not. If your specification says "the accent is #5B47E0", then changing the brand means finding every occurrence and hoping. If it says "accent, used for links and the single primary action, currently #5B47E0, requiring at least 4.5:1 on white", then changing the brand is one substitution and the constraint travels with the role. This is the entire argument for design tokens, and it applies to a Word template as much as to a stylesheet.

The second reason is that contrast is where accessibility becomes concrete and where most work fails. Contrast failures are the most commonly cited defect in accessibility audits, and they are the easiest to prevent because they are arithmetic. Unlike almost everything else in design there is no judgement involved: a ratio either clears 4.5:1 or it does not. A palette whose contrast ratios are recorded at the point of definition cannot produce this failure later.

The third reason is that the separation rule prevents a category of misreading that is genuinely dangerous in financial documents. Consider a report where the house accent is blue and a chart in that report has a blue series. A reader who has learned that blue means "the thing we want you to click or notice" now sees a blue line and reads emphasis into it. The line was series three. Nobody lied, and the reader took away something the author did not put there.

The fourth is efficiency, and it is why this lecture is worth forty minutes rather than four. Getting the ramp right once removes hundreds of subsequent decisions. Every heading colour, every border, every disabled state, every caption is then a lookup rather than a choice, and the attention saved goes into the parts of the document that actually require thought.

Step 3

The mental model

The neutral ramp is the object to understand. Below is the one used throughout this track, with every step's measured contrast against white and what that contrast permits.

Two consequences follow.

The first is that a ramp needs more steps at the light end than feels necessary. Six of the twelve above are lighter than mid-grey, because surfaces, hairlines, stripes and borders all need to be distinguishable from each other and from white while remaining quiet. The dark end needs fewer, because there are only three things text can be: primary, secondary and emphatic.

The second is that warm and cool is a real decision and should be made once, deliberately, at the start. The ramp above is warm: every step has slightly more red than blue, which reads as paper rather than as screen and sits better with serif type in print. A cool ramp reads as clinical and technical and pairs naturally with a sans. Neither is better. Mixing them within one document is what looks wrong, and it happens easily because most default greys are cool while most brand tints are not.

Contrast ratio
(L1 + 0.05) / (L2 + 0.05), where L is relative luminance and L1 is the lighter of the two. Runs from 1:1 (identical) to 21:1 (black on white). Always computed against the surface the text actually sits on, not against white by default.
Large text
WCAG's threshold for the relaxed 3:1 requirement: 18pt (24px) regular, or 14pt (18.66px) bold. Below either figure, normal-text rules apply. Worth knowing precisely, because "it's a heading" is not the test and a 20px heading is normal text.
Token role
The job a colour does, named independently of its value. text-secondary is a role; #5C5B57 is a value. Specifications are written in roles so that values can change without the meaning changing.

Two criteria beyond contrast are worth knowing by number because they come up in review. Success criterion 1.4.1 forbids using colour as the only visual means of conveying information, which is the formal version of lecture 5's argument. Success criterion 1.4.11 is the 3:1 requirement for non-text contrast, and it is the one that catches pale form borders and low-contrast chart elements that everyone forgets.

Radix's twelve steps, and why they are the best-articulated system

Most ramps number their steps and leave the meaning implicit. The Radix Colors system assigns each of twelve steps a specific job, which converts a palette from a gradient into an interface vocabulary. It is worth knowing even if you never use the library, because it names distinctions most systems leave you to rediscover.

StepsJob
1 to 2App background, then subtle component background
3 to 5Component background: normal, hovered, then active or selected
6 to 8Borders: subtle and non-interactive, then interactive, then hovered or focused
9 to 10Solid backgrounds at highest chroma, then their hovered state
11 to 12Low-contrast text, then high-contrast text

The clever part is that steps 11 and 12 carry a contrast guarantee against step 2 of the same scale, so a designer can pair any text step with any background step from the same family and know it passes. That is the property to steal even if you build your own ramp: define the pairings that are guaranteed, rather than leaving every combination to be checked individually.

The three data palettes and how to choose

Data colour is a separate system, and it has exactly three families. Choosing the wrong family is a more serious error than choosing ugly colours within the right one, because the family itself makes a claim about the data.

A construction procedure

BUILDING A PALETTE, IN ORDER 1. NEUTRAL RAMP (this is 80 per cent of the work) Decide warm or cool. Once, for the whole system. Build 10 to 12 steps. Six light, three dark, the rest edges. Record the contrast of every step against white AND against your main surface tint. Verify: is there a step at roughly 4.5, 7, and 12? 2. ONE ACCENT Must clear 4.5:1 on the canvas, because it will be a link. Add a pressed or visited variant, darker. Add a soft tint for chip backgrounds. Verify: does body text still pass on the soft tint? 3. SEMANTICS (success, warning, error) Each needs TWO values, not one: - a fill value, needs 3:1, used for icons and bars - a text value, needs 4.5:1, darker than the fill Most saturated greens and oranges land near 3.5:1, which passes for a fill and fails for a word. This is the most common palette bug in professional work. Verify: never encode state by colour alone. 1.4.1. 4. DATA PALETTE (separate. No shared values.) Categorical: take Okabe-Ito. Do not invent one. Sequential and diverging: take ColorBrewer. Verify: desaturate and check the important comparison. 5. DARK VARIANT (if needed, and it is not a inversion) Re-pick every accent and semantic value from scratch. A colour at 6:1 on white will typically fail on near-black. Desaturate: saturated colours read brighter on dark grounds.

Step 3 is where real palettes fail, so it is worth dwelling on. Take the house green, #1E9D54. Against white it measures 3.5:1, which clears the 3:1 threshold for a bar fill or an icon and fails the 4.5:1 threshold for the words "within tolerance" set at body size. Almost every palette that has one green has this bug, because the value was chosen by looking at a swatch and the swatch was a fill.

Tints are surfaces, and surfaces change the arithmetic

One trap catches people who have otherwise done everything right. Contrast is a property of a pair, so a text colour verified against white is not verified against a tinted callout background. A body colour at 4.6:1 on white might sit at 4.1:1 on a pale peach panel and fail.

The fix is a habit rather than a rule: whenever you introduce a background tint, immediately check the two or three text colours that will appear on it. In practice this is why the house ramp has a gap between 4.5 and 6.8 rather than a step at 5:1. A value that only just clears on white has no margin for the tinted case, and a ramp that offers it will have it used.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Complying with a standard you know is imperfect

The WCAG 2 contrast formula is known to be wrong in places, and you should comply with it anyway. Both halves of that sentence matter.

The formula's defects are well documented. It handles dark backgrounds poorly, over-rating light-on-dark combinations relative to how they actually read. It is insensitive to font weight and size beyond a single crude threshold, so a hairline 24px heading and a bold 24px heading are treated identically when they are not. And it produces some frankly odd verdicts in the mid-tones, where pairs that read comfortably fail and pairs that read poorly pass.

APCA, the Advanced Perceptual Contrast Algorithm, was developed to fix this and is a candidate for WCAG 3. It uses perceptual lightness rather than relative luminance, accounts for polarity, and factors in size and weight. It is better. It is also, as of 2026, not a finished standard: WCAG 3 remains a working draft and its contrast method is not settled.

So the working position is this. WCAG 2 is the legal and contractual standard in essentially every jurisdiction that has one, so it is what you comply with and what an audit will test. Where APCA and WCAG 2 disagree, and you have room, satisfy both. In practice this means treating the WCAG minima as floors to exceed rather than targets to hit, which is what the ramp in step 3 does by leaving a gap above 4.5. Exceeding the floor is the one move that is robust to the standard changing underneath you.

There is a broader lesson here that recurs whenever a field is partly regulated. The standard is not a description of reality; it is a testable proxy for it, adopted because it can be checked mechanically. Treating it as truth produces documents that pass and read badly. Treating it as irrelevant because it is imperfect produces documents that fail an audit. The professional position is to satisfy the proxy and then keep going.

Why one accent

The house file allows one accent and the constraint deserves a defence, because every brand guideline you will encounter has five.

An accent works by being the only thing of its kind on the surface. Its meaning is "this, rather than the other things", and that meaning is definitionally exclusive: two accents on a page do not give you two emphases, they give you none, plus a question the reader has to resolve about why these two things are different from each other. The preattentive argument from lecture 2 is the mechanism, and Itten's extension contrast from lecture 5 is the same point from the artist's side.

Brand palettes have five colours for a different purpose. They are describing an identity across many surfaces over years, not specifying a single document. A brand that owns purple, teal and coral is telling you the range it may operate in, not instructing you to use all three on one page. Reading a brand palette as a per-document instruction is a common and expensive misinterpretation, and the resulting documents are the ones that look like they were made by a committee.

Common failure A palette is defined carefully, recorded with contrast ratios, and then a chart library is dropped in with its own defaults and nobody notices. The document now has two colour systems: yours and the library's, and the library's has more colours in it. Whenever a tool brings a palette, replacing it is the first configuration step, not a refinement. Every one of Power BI, Tableau, matplotlib, Plotly and Observable ships a default that will silently override the work in this lecture.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a document or dashboard you produced and list every distinct colour in it, including the ones a chart library supplied. For each, write the role it plays. Colours with no role, or with the same role as another colour, are the surplus. Then check each text colour's contrast against the surface it actually sits on rather than against white, and record which fail. Most people find both a surplus and a failure on the first attempt.
  2. State the build order and explain why starting with the brand colour is starting at the wrong end.
  3. Give the four WCAG contrast thresholds and the exact definition of "large text".
  4. Why does a semantic colour need two values rather than one? Give the arithmetic for a case where one value would fail.
  5. Explain the separation rule between interface and data colour, and describe a specific misreading that violating it produces in a financial report.
  6. Your data has values from 0 to 100 per cent with no meaningful midpoint. Which palette family applies, and what goes wrong if you use a diverging ramp?
  7. Why does the house ramp leave a gap between 4.5:1 and 6.8:1 rather than offering a step at 5:1?
  8. A colour passes 4.6:1 on white. What must you check before using it on a pale tinted callout, and why?
  9. Step 5 argues for complying with a standard known to be imperfect. Apply the same reasoning to a regulatory context you know well, and say whether it holds.
  10. Your brand guideline specifies five colours. Write the two sentences you would use to explain to a stakeholder why the report uses one of them, without implying the guideline is wrong.
