Perception and Gestalt

Your reader groups the marks on the page before they decide to read it, in about a fifth of a second, using rules they cannot switch off. Design is mostly the business of making those rules produce the grouping you meant.

Lecture 2 of 11 Reading time, 35 minutes Sources: Wertheimer (1923); Treisman & Gelade (1980); Ware (2000)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Vision runs in two stages. The first is preattentive: fast, parallel, covering the whole visual field at once, complete in roughly 200 milliseconds, and entirely outside your control. It segments the scene into regions and groups. The second is attentive: slow, serial, one object at a time, and this is the one you experience as looking at something. By the time you consciously look, the grouping has already been decided for you.

The Gestalt psychologists worked out the rules the first stage uses. Things that are close together group. Things that look alike group. Things inside a shared boundary group. Things joined by a line group most strongly of all. These are not aesthetic principles and they are not conventions that vary by culture or training. They are a description of what the visual system does, and they apply to a page whether the page was designed with them in mind or not.

One result matters more than the rest and is the reason this lecture exists in a design course rather than a psychology one. Proximity overrides similarity. When you space things one way and colour them another, spacing wins. Designers who know this stop trying to fix grouping problems with colour, because colour cannot beat a gap, and the gap is usually what is wrong.

Feynman test Look at a car park from a distance and you see rows, not fifty separate cars. Nobody decided to see rows; the gaps between the rows are bigger than the gaps between cars, so your eyes did the sorting before you got a say. Now imagine painting every third car red. You would notice the red, but you would still see rows. That is the whole lecture: gaps beat paint.
Why this is the second lecture and not the tenth Lecture 1 said design is the assignment of meaning to visual difference, and that the two failure modes are noise and collision. This lecture supplies the missing half: which visual differences the reader will actually register, and in what order of strength. Without it, "assign meaning to difference" is advice you cannot act on, because you do not yet know which differences the reader's visual system treats as significant.
Step 2

Why this matters

The practical consequence is that grouping is not something you communicate. It is something that happens to your reader, and your only influence is over the inputs. If the spatial arrangement of a page says one thing and your intended structure says another, the spatial arrangement wins, every time, for every reader, and no amount of labelling will fix it. A section heading that sits equidistant between two paragraphs belongs to neither, and writing the word "Section" in front of it changes nothing about what the reader perceives.

This explains a category of document problem that otherwise looks mysterious. You know a report is hard to follow. The sentences are fine, the structure in the outline was fine, and the headings are all there. What has usually happened is that the visual grouping contradicts the logical grouping: sub-headings look like headings, related items are separated by more space than unrelated ones, a figure sits closer to the paragraph after it than the one it illustrates. The logical structure exists and the reader cannot see it, because a competing structure is being asserted more loudly by the geometry.

There is a second and more optimistic consequence. Because the first stage of vision is parallel, it does not care how much is on the page. Finding one blue dot among ten grey ones and finding one blue dot among a thousand grey ones take about the same time. This is the property that makes dashboards possible at all, and it tells you what a dashboard should be built out of: differences the preattentive system can detect, deployed sparingly enough that they still pop out. Spend that channel on decoration and you have destroyed the only mechanism that lets a reader scan a dense screen.

The third reason is diagnostic. Once you know the grouping rules and their strength ordering, a page that feels wrong becomes a page you can interrogate. Which cue is asserting the grouping I do not want? Is it stronger or weaker than the one I am relying on? Almost every layout complaint resolves into a conflict between two cues, and knowing which one wins tells you which one to change.

Step 3

The mental model

The demonstration below is the one to remember. Identical dots, three arrangements. In the third panel, spacing and colour are made to disagree deliberately, and you can watch which one your own visual system obeys.

Two consequences, and the second is the one that changes how you work.

The first is that the cues have a strength ordering. They frequently conflict, because a real page has spacing and colour and rules and alignment all making claims at once, and when they conflict the stronger cue determines what the reader perceives. The ordering, roughly and with the caveat in step 5, runs: connection, then enclosure, then proximity, then similarity of colour, size or shape. A line joining two elements will group them across a gap; a shared background will group them despite different colours; a gap will separate them despite matching colours.

The second is that this gives you a repair procedure. When the grouping on a page is wrong, do not ask what to add. Ask which cue is currently asserting the wrong grouping, look up whether it is stronger or weaker than the cue you were relying on, and change the stronger one. In practice the answer is nearly always spacing, because spacing is the cue people set by accident and colour is the cue they set on purpose.

Preattentive processing
The first stage of vision. Parallel across the whole field, complete in roughly 200 to 250 milliseconds, not under conscious control. Detects a limited set of primitive features and performs the initial segmentation of the scene.
Pop-out
The effect where a target differing on a single preattentive dimension is found in constant time regardless of how many distractors surround it. Searching for the one red dot among grey ones takes the same time whether there are twenty distractors or two hundred.
Conjunction search
Searching for a target defined by a combination of two features, such as the one red square among red circles and grey squares. Pop-out fails; search becomes serial and the time taken scales with the number of items. Treisman and Gelade's central result, and the reason a chart should not encode two variables in a way that requires the reader to combine them.
Step 4

The detail

The grouping principles, in strength order

Wertheimer's 1923 paper set out the original list and later work added to it and put it on an experimental footing. What follows is ordered by how reliably each cue wins when cues disagree, which is the ordering a designer needs and not the order they are usually taught in.

1. Connection
Elements joined by a visible line are perceived as a unit, and this survives almost any competing cue. It is why a leader line from a label to a data point works across a crowded chart, and why an organisation chart reads as a hierarchy rather than as rows of boxes. Strongest cue available; use it when you need to group things you cannot move.
2. Enclosure
Elements sharing a boundary or a background fill are perceived as a unit. A tinted panel groups everything on it regardless of the spacing inside. This is the cue a card or callout uses, and its strength is why a stray element accidentally falling inside a tinted region reads as part of that group no matter what you intended.
3. Proximity
Elements close together are perceived as a unit. The workhorse cue of document design, because it costs nothing, needs no ink, and is already operating on every page whether or not you set it deliberately. Beats similarity reliably.
4. Similarity
Elements sharing colour, size, shape or orientation are perceived as related. Weaker than the three above, and the one most often reached for first. Useful for grouping things that cannot be placed together, such as all the figures of one category scattered through a table, and unreliable for anything else.
5. Continuity
The eye prefers to follow a smooth path, and elements arranged along one are perceived as a sequence. This is why alignment matters: a ragged left edge on a list of items breaks the implied line and weakens the grouping, and why a chart's axis reads as a single object rather than a row of tick marks.
6. Closure and figure-ground
The visual system completes incomplete shapes and assigns one region as figure against another as ground. A table with no outer border still reads as a table if the internal alignment is strong enough, which is the perceptual licence for removing rules that the report specification takes.

What the first stage can actually see

Preattentive processing works on a limited vocabulary. Roughly: hue, brightness, size, orientation, length, width, curvature, shape, added marks, enclosure, spatial position, motion and depth cues. That is the whole set, and anything outside it requires the slow serial stage.

Two properties of this set are worth memorising. First, the dimensions are not equally strong: hue and motion are extremely salient, orientation and size moderately so, shape rather weak. Second, and more useful, they interfere with one another. A page using six preattentive dimensions at once has no pop-out anywhere, because every element differs from every other element on something. Salience is a fixed budget, and each additional distinction spends some of what remains.

This is the perceptual justification for the restraint that runs through the whole track. When the dashboard specification says grey is the default and colour is spent only on meaning, it is not making an aesthetic claim. It is preserving the preattentive channel so that the one thing that matters can still pop out.

The most common error in professional documents

Proximity's dominance produces one specific failure so often that it deserves its own diagram. It arises whenever a heading is given equal space above and below.

The rule that falls out is simple enough to apply without thinking: space above a heading should be about three times the space below it. The exact ratio is not sacred and anything from two to four works; what matters is that the two spaces are visibly different and that the larger one is above. Every heading specification in all eight token files in this track implements it, and it is the single cheapest improvement available to most documents.

Where the principles conflict, and what to do

Real pages set several cues at once and they do not always agree. Three conflicts come up constantly.

Enclosure against proximity. A tinted callout box placed tight against the following paragraph will group with that paragraph in the reader's eye less strongly than its own contents group with each other, but the boundary is doing a lot of work and a reader may still read the paragraph as commentary on the box. Resolution: give an enclosed element more external space than internal padding. If a card has 16px of padding inside, it needs more than 16px of gap outside, or the enclosure and the proximity are fighting.

Similarity against proximity in tables. A table is a proximity grid crossed with similarity by column. Reading down a column relies on alignment and continuity; reading across a row relies on proximity. When rows are tall, the row grouping weakens and the reader loses their place, which is the real problem that zebra striping attempts to solve. It solves it by adding an enclosure cue, which works, at the cost of adding a fill to every other row. Reducing row height, or adding a hairline, achieves the same thing more cheaply, which is the argument the report specification makes.

Continuity against everything. Alignment is quiet and strong. A single element out of alignment breaks a continuity the reader was using, and the effect is much larger than its size suggests: it is why a slide with one box two pixels off the grid reads as careless when nobody can say what is wrong. This is also the cue with the best effort-to-reward ratio in the whole set, because fixing it costs nothing.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Descriptive, not prescriptive

The Gestalt principles are frequently taught as design rules, and they are not. They are a description of what a visual system does. "Use proximity to group related items" is a design rule derived from the description; the description itself says only that close things will be grouped, which is true of your page whether the grouping is the one you wanted or not.

The distinction is worth holding because it changes the failure mode you look for. Treated as rules, the principles suggest a checklist: did I use proximity, did I use enclosure. Treated as description, they suggest an audit: what is the page currently grouping, and is that what I meant? The second question is much harder to answer about your own work and much more likely to find something.

It also means the principles cannot be broken, only disagreed with. When someone says a layout "breaks the proximity principle" they mean the layout's proximity groupings contradict its semantic ones. The principle is operating perfectly; the page is wrong.

What the original Gestalt work does and does not support

Some honesty about the evidence, because this material is often over-claimed. Wertheimer's 1923 paper is a set of demonstrations, not experiments: he shows you configurations and reports what is perceived. The demonstrations are extremely robust and have survived a century, but the original work quantifies nothing and the broader Gestalt theoretical apparatus, particularly its explanations in terms of brain field dynamics, did not survive.

The parts that are on solid modern footing are the ones this lecture leans on. Treisman and Gelade's feature integration theory (1980) gives the preattentive and attentive two-stage account and the pop-out versus conjunction distinction, with reaction-time data behind it. Palmer and Rock's work in the 1990s argues that uniform connectedness is more fundamental than proximity and similarity, which is where the strength ordering in step 4 comes from. Colin Ware's Information Visualization is the standard synthesis for designers and is the book to own if only one.

What remains genuinely unsettled is the precise ordering when cues conflict. The ranking in step 4 reflects the consensus in the design literature and matches most people's experience, but it is not a measured constant, and the relative strength of, say, enclosure against proximity plainly depends on how strong the enclosure is and how large the gap. Treat the ordering as a reliable heuristic and not as a physical law, and when a specific conflict matters, test it by looking rather than by consulting the list.

Common failure Knowing about preattentive pop-out tempts people to use it, and using it more than about twice on a surface destroys it. The failure looks like a dashboard where the alert is red, the target line is orange, the selected filter is blue, three series are coloured, and the whole thing is on a tinted background: every element is now distinguished from every other, and consequently nothing pops out at all. Salience is zero-sum. Before adding a highlight, find the existing one it will compete with.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a page you did not write, ideally a dense one such as a regulatory document or a fund factsheet. Squint at it, or shrink it until the text is unreadable, so only the geometry survives. Sketch the groups you perceive. Then read it properly and mark the groups the content actually has. Every disagreement between the two sketches is a defect, and you have found it without reading a word of the design literature.
  2. State the two stages of vision and the property of the first one that makes dashboards possible.
  3. Rank connection, similarity, proximity and enclosure by strength, and give one document situation where you would deliberately choose the weakest.
  4. Why can a grouping problem not be fixed by colouring things? Answer in terms of the strength ordering rather than by restating the conclusion.
  5. A card has 16px of internal padding. What constraint does that place on the gap between it and the next element, and which two cues are in tension?
  6. Explain why zebra striping works, in perceptual terms, and then give two cheaper cues that achieve the same effect.
  7. Find a slide or a page where something is very slightly out of alignment. Note how large the effect feels relative to the size of the error, and say which Gestalt principle accounts for the disproportion.
  8. Step 5 says the principles are descriptive rather than prescriptive. What does that change about how you would audit your own document, compared with treating them as a checklist?
  9. The heading-spacing rule in step 4 gives a ratio of roughly three to one. Derive it from the proximity principle rather than accepting it, and say what would happen at a ratio of one to three.
