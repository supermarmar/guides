Typography I: anatomy and choosing a face

Choosing the typeface is the most conspicuous decision in typography and close to the least important. This lecture covers it anyway, because you have to choose something, and because the vocabulary you need to choose well is the vocabulary the next eight lectures are written in.

Lecture 3 of 11 Reading time, 35 minutes Sources: Bringhurst (1992); Butterick (2010 onwards); Hochuli (1987)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Separate two words that are used interchangeably and should not be. Legibility is whether a reader can tell one character from another: a property of the typeface, decided by its shapes. Readability is whether a reader can get through nine hundred words without effort: a property of the setting, decided by size, measure, leading and space. Almost every complaint about type is a readability complaint, and almost every attempted fix is a change of typeface.

That is the wrong lever. A well-drawn face set badly is unreadable; a mediocre face set well is fine. If you have twenty minutes to improve a document's typography, spend nineteen on the setting and one on the face, and lecture 4 is about the nineteen.

Where the choice does matter is at the extremes: very small sizes, very large ones, and adverse conditions such as a projector, a photocopy, or a phone in sunlight. There the measurable properties of the face take over, and there are only about five that count. The largest is x-height, the height of a lowercase letter without ascenders, because it determines how big a typeface looks at a given point size and therefore how legible it is at the size you actually set it.

Feynman test Two people can be the same height and one of them looks much bigger, because of build. Typefaces are the same. Two faces set at "11 point" can differ by a fifth in how large the letters actually appear, because the point size measures a box the letters sit in rather than the letters themselves. So "11 point" tells you almost nothing on its own, which is why comparing two faces means setting them side by side rather than trusting the number.
A claim you will see contradicted elsewhere "Serifs guide the eye along the line and make print more readable" is repeated everywhere and is not supported by the evidence. The studies that appear to show a serif advantage confound the variable with size, x-height and familiarity, and reviews of the literature find no reliable effect at text sizes. The serif-for-print, sans-for-screen convention is a convention, and a defensible one for reasons given in step 4. It is not a finding.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is the one lecture 1 established: there is no neutral option. Every document arrives in some typeface, and if you did not pick it, the software did. That is a defensible outcome and an indefensible process, because the software's choice was made without knowing your content, your reader or your output medium. Calibri was selected to look good in ClearType rendering on Windows displays in 2007. Times New Roman was drawn in 1932 for a British newspaper printed on cheap paper in narrow columns, which is why it is narrow and rather cramped, and why it looks wrong at a 160mm measure.

The second reason is that type carries register. A reader forms a view of what kind of document they are holding before they read it, and the typeface is the loudest signal available. This is not about looking attractive. A validation report set in a geometric sans with wide tracking reads as a pitch deck, and a reader who has been signalled "marketing" reads the numbers differently from one who has been signalled "technical document". You cannot opt out of sending the signal; you can only choose it or send one by accident.

The third reason is practical and gets underweighted until it bites. A typeface that is not on the recipient's machine will be substituted, and substitution changes the metrics, which changes the line breaks, which changes the pagination. A twelve-page paper becomes fourteen pages on the reviewer's screen with the exhibits in the wrong places. This is why the documents module makes a concession the rest of the track does not, and why "which face is best" is the wrong question for a circulated Word file. The right question is which face survives the journey.

The fourth is that the choice is nearly free. Unlike almost everything else in this course, picking a better typeface costs no space, no ink and no reader attention. It is the one improvement with no trade-off, which is precisely why it attracts more discussion than it deserves.

Step 3

The mental model

Type has an anatomy and about five of its measurements predict how it will behave. Learn these and you can evaluate a face by looking at it instead of by reading the marketing copy.

Two consequences follow, and the second is the one that makes the model useful rather than merely interesting.

The first is that point size is not comparable across faces. When the report specification says 11pt and names Source Serif 4, both halves are load-bearing; substituting another face at 11pt does not preserve the setting. Any time you change typeface you must re-tune the size, and usually the leading and the measure too.

The second is that a large x-height is not simply better. It buys apparent size, which helps at small settings, on screens and in adverse conditions. It costs ascender and descender length, and those are what give a word its distinctive outline. Faces with very large x-heights and short extenders look uniform at length, which is fine for a caption and tiring for nine hundred words. The trade is real, and it explains why interface faces and book faces look different: they are solving different problems.

x-height
The height of a lowercase letter with no ascender or descender, measured from the baseline. Usually given as a ratio of the em: roughly 0.45 for Times New Roman, 0.48 for Garamond, 0.53 for Inter and Georgia. The single strongest predictor of how large a face looks and how it holds up small.
Aperture
How open the gap is in partially enclosed letters such as c, s, e and a. Open apertures keep those letters distinguishable at small sizes and under poor rendering; closed apertures make them collapse toward o. The second-strongest legibility property and the one most often ignored.
Stroke contrast
The difference between the thickest and thinnest parts of a letter. High contrast (Bodoni, Didot) is elegant at display sizes and disintegrates at small ones, especially in print on absorbent paper or on a projector. Low contrast survives adverse conditions, which is why road signage uses it.
Set width
How wide the characters are relative to their height. Narrow faces fit more per line and tire the reader faster; wide faces are comfortable and expensive in space. This is the property that determines how many characters land on your measure, which makes it lecture 4's problem.
Extender length
How far ascenders rise and descenders drop. Long extenders give words distinctive silhouettes and demand more leading; short ones allow tight setting and produce a uniform grey. Inversely related to x-height for a given em.
Step 4

The detail

Classification, kept short

The historical classifications are worth about ten minutes, because they name the families you will be choosing between and because knowing the era tells you what problem a face was drawn to solve.

Humanist and old-style serif
Garamond, Bembo, Sabon, Minion. Low stroke contrast, angled stress inherited from the broad-nib pen, small to moderate x-height. Drawn for books and still best at them. Set them a point larger than you think.
Transitional and modern serif
Baskerville, Times, Georgia (transitional); Bodoni, Didot (modern). Vertical stress, higher contrast rising to extreme in the moderns. Georgia is the transitional face worth knowing: drawn by Matthew Carter for screens, large x-height, sturdy, and present on essentially every machine.
Slab serif
Charter, Clarendon, Rockwell, the serif of IBM Plex. Heavy rectangular serifs, low contrast, robust under poor reproduction. Charter is the one to know: Matthew Carter drew it to survive low-resolution printers and gave a version to the public, so it is free and excellent.
Grotesque and neo-grotesque sans
Akzidenz, Helvetica, Arial, Inter. Closed apertures in the older ones, uniform width, neutral tone. Helvetica's closed apertures are a genuine legibility weakness at small sizes, which is what Inter's designer was reacting against; Inter keeps the neutrality and opens the apertures.
Humanist sans
Frutiger, Segoe UI, Source Sans, Fira Sans. Letterforms derived from handwriting rather than geometry, varied widths, open apertures. The most legible sans category at small sizes and in signage, and the safest default for interface text if Inter is unavailable.
Geometric sans
Futura, Avenir, Poppins, Century Gothic. Circles and straight lines, near-identical o, c and e, single-storey a. Beautiful in a wordmark, poor in a paragraph, because the geometric discipline strips out exactly the distinguishing detail a reader uses. Avenir is the compromise: geometric in feel, adjusted for reading.

Serif or sans, answered honestly

The evidence for serif superiority in print does not exist. Reviews of the reading research find no reliable difference at text sizes once size, x-height and reader familiarity are controlled. The evidence for sans superiority on screen used to exist and has expired: it rested on low-resolution rendering where serifs turned to mush, and displays have been past that point for a decade.

So the convention has to be justified some other way, and it can be, on two grounds. Custom is one: readers have decades of exposure to serif books and sans interfaces, and familiarity is itself a real reading advantage even when the shapes are not. Differentiation is the other and is stronger: pairing a serif body with sans headings gives you a hierarchy cue that costs nothing and works at a glance, which is worth more than any marginal legibility difference between the two.

The practical upshot is that the choice is yours and should be made on register and contrast rather than on legibility. This track uses serif bodies for print-first mediums and sans for screen, and says in the house file that this is a convention. If you invert it and set a report in a humanist sans, nothing bad happens.

A selection procedure

Choosing well is mostly a matter of asking the right questions in the right order. Aesthetics comes last, not because it is unimportant but because it is the only one of these that cannot fail catastrophically.

CHOOSING A TYPEFACE 1. WILL IT ARRIVE? Circulated .docx -> must be on the recipient's machine, or metrically compatible with what is PDF, print -> anything, it embeds Web -> anything, with a fallback stack Email -> Arial or Helvetica. This is not a choice 2. DOES IT HAVE THE PARTS? [ ] Regular, italic, bold, bold italic at minimum [ ] Small caps, if you set acronyms [ ] TABULAR LINING FIGURES <- the one people forget, and the one that decides whether your tables can align [ ] Old-style figures, if serif long-form [ ] Language coverage for every name you will print 3. WILL IT SURVIVE THE CONDITIONS? Small sizes, poor print -> low contrast, open apertures, generous x-height Projection -> low contrast, open apertures, avoid hairline weights entirely Long-form reading -> moderate x-height, long extenders 4. IS IT LICENSED FOR THIS? Desktop, web, app and ebook are separate licences in most commercial families. Open licences (SIL OFL) cover all of it. 5. ONLY NOW: DO YOU LIKE IT? Set nine hundred words of your own text in it, print it, and read the whole thing. Specimens are designed to flatter. A face that fails 1 to 4 cannot be rescued by passing 5.

Faces worth using, all free

Every face below is openly licensed, which matters more than it sounds: a house style built on a font nobody can install is a house style nobody uses, and this is the most common reason design systems fail to spread inside an organisation.

Inter (sans, screen)
Drawn specifically for user interfaces at small sizes. Large x-height, open apertures, every weight, a variable version, tabular figures included. If you pick one sans and never think about it again, pick this. Used throughout this track.
Source Serif 4 (serif, print and long-form)
High x-height, open apertures, holds up at 10pt. Pairs with Source Sans if you want a matched pair. The house serif in this track's report and document specifications.
Charter (slab serif, print)
Matthew Carter, drawn to survive bad printers, released freely. Sturdier and more distinctive than Source Serif and a genuine alternative to it. Butterick recommends it, which is notable given how few free faces he recommends at all.
IBM Plex (superfamily)
Sans, serif, condensed and mono in one coordinated family with matched metrics. The right answer when you want everything from one source and do not want to think about pairing. Slightly cooler in tone than Inter.
Literata (serif, screen long-form)
Commissioned by Google for e-reading. Large x-height, sturdy, designed for extended screen reading specifically. The best free choice for a long-form web body if you want a serif.
JetBrains Mono (monospace)
Increased x-height, taller letterforms, careful disambiguation of the characters that matter in code. The strongest free coding face. IBM Plex Mono is the alternative if you want the whole superfamily.

Pairing, in one rule

Pair by clear contrast of category, or do not pair at all. Two faces that are similar but not identical produce the noise failure from lecture 1: the reader registers a difference and finds no meaning in it. A serif with a sans is a clear contrast and works. Two humanist sans faces is a mess.

The safest options, in order: one superfamily used throughout (IBM Plex, Source); one face in multiple weights, which is what most good interfaces do; or a serif and a sans of obviously different category. Two is the maximum in any document, and the presentation specification makes that a hard rule because projection punishes complexity.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The status and impact inversion

There is a reliable inverse relationship in this field between how much a decision is discussed and how much it changes the reader's experience. Typeface selection is the most discussed decision in typography and roughly the fifth most important. Measure is barely discussed and is probably first. Leading is second. The ratio of space above a heading to space below it, which occupies about two sentences of most style guides, matters more than whether you chose Garamond or Minion.

The inversion has a cause worth understanding, because the same pattern appears in colour and in layout. Discussable decisions are the ones with names and options: you can hold a debate about Helvetica versus Univers because both are nouns. Measure has no brand, no history to be partisan about and nothing to signal by choosing, so it does not generate conversation, and the absence of conversation gets mistaken for the absence of consequence.

The practical instruction is to distrust your own sense of what matters, because that sense was calibrated by what people talk about. When a document looks wrong and your instinct says the font is wrong, the font is probably not wrong.

Where to disagree with Butterick

Butterick's Practical Typography is the most useful book in this bibliography, free online, and the only one that gives hard numbers for everything. It is also written from a specific position that does not always transfer, and a reader should know where.

He is against system fonts more or less absolutely, and against free fonts as a category. The argument is that a font somebody bought represents a decision and a font that came with the machine represents its absence, which is rhetorically effective and mostly right about 2010. It has aged: Inter, Source Serif and IBM Plex are free and are better than most of what was commercially available when he wrote. The category argument no longer holds even if the individual judgements often do.

He is also writing for American lawyers producing documents on US Letter for court filing, and several of his numbers carry that context. His 1.5 to 2 inch margins produce a beautiful page at 12pt on Letter and a measure around fifty characters, which is at the short end of the band. On A4 at 11pt they would be wrong. Take his ranges and re-derive rather than transplanting the figures, which is what the report specification does and says it does.

Where he is simply right, and where the professional world mostly ignores him: curly quotation marks, one space after a full stop, real ellipses and dashes, and never using underline for emphasis. These cost nothing, take ten minutes to learn, and separate a document that was set from one that was typed.

Common failure Discovering typefaces produces a phase of using several. The symptom is a document with a display face for the title, a second sans for headings, a serif for body and something else for captions, each individually defensible. The reader receives four unexplained distinctions and reads a ransom note. If you are in this phase, impose a limit of one family for a month and spend the attention on weight and size instead; almost every hierarchy you need is available inside a single well-made family.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Set the same paragraph of your own writing three times at the same point size: once in Times New Roman, once in Georgia, once in a humanist sans such as Segoe UI or Source Sans. Print all three. Note which looks largest, which fits in fewest lines, and which you would rather read for twenty minutes. Then measure the x-heights with a ruler and check whether your ranking follows them.
  2. Define legibility and readability so that the difference is unambiguous, and give one problem that belongs to each.
  3. Why is "set at 11 point" not a specification? What must accompany it?
  4. Name the trade-off that comes with a large x-height. Give one document where you would want it and one where you would not.
  5. A colleague fixes a cramped, tiring report by switching from Calibri to Garamond and reports that it is much better. Give the most likely actual cause of the improvement.
  6. Your table of PD, LGD and EAD figures will not align vertically. Which property of the typeface is missing, and what is it called?
  7. State the honest position on serif versus sans for print, and then give the two grounds on which the convention can still be defended.
  8. Step 5 claims measure matters more than typeface but generates less discussion. Propose a test that would tell you whether the claim is true rather than merely plausible.
  9. You must circulate an editable Word document to a bank's credit committee. Work through the five-step selection procedure and state what you would set it in and why, showing where the constraint bites.
