Lectures / Design / Foundations

Data graphics

In 1984 two statisticians measured how accurately people read quantities from different visual encodings and produced a ranking. It settles most chart questions before taste is consulted, and it is the most transferable thing in this course.

Lecture 10 of 11 Reading time, 45 minutes Sources: Cleveland & McGill (1984); Tufte (1983); Few (2004); Cairo (2016)
Step 1

The 30-second version

A chart encodes numbers as visual properties, and the properties are not equally readable. William Cleveland and Robert McGill ran controlled experiments in which subjects judged ratios from different encodings, measured the errors, and produced an ordering. Position on a common scale is the most accurate. Colour saturation is the least. Everything else falls in between, in an order that has held up for forty years.

The design instruction is one sentence: spend the accurate channels on the quantity that matters. If the reader's real question is "which region is largest and by how much", that comparison belongs on position or length, and the merely contextual variables can go on the weaker channels. Most bad charts are not ugly; they have simply spent position on the category label and area on the number.

Alongside this sits Tufte's argument about what else is on the page. His data-ink ratio asks what proportion of the ink encodes data rather than decorating it, and his instruction is to erase non-data ink and redundant data-ink. The two ideas combine into a working method: choose the encoding by accuracy, then remove everything that is not the encoding.

Feynman test Ask someone which of two lines is longer and they will tell you almost exactly. Ask which of two pie slices is bigger and they will be roughly right. Ask which of two blobs has more area and they will be badly wrong, and will underestimate the difference. Nobody has to be taught this; it is how eyes work. A chart is a decision about which of those three questions you are asking your reader.
Why this is unusual Almost nothing else in design has a measured ranking behind it. The measure band in lecture 4 is a convergence of expert opinion; the Gestalt principles are robust demonstrations without a strength metric; the contrast formula is a standard rather than a finding. Cleveland and McGill is an actual experiment with error bars. When it disagrees with your preference, it is probably right.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that charts are where design errors turn into wrong decisions. A badly set paragraph costs the reader effort. A badly encoded chart costs them the wrong magnitude, and they will act on it without knowing they were misled. A stacked area chart makes the individual series nearly impossible to compare, and a reader who concludes that series three is flat when it is not has been given a false fact by a chart that contained the true one.

The second reason is that the defaults are bad and are getting worse rather than better. Every charting tool ships with settings that would fail the ranking: gridlines as dark as the data, legends placed where the eye is not, a categorical palette applied to ordered data, and in several cases three-dimensional effects that are still on by default in 2026. A chart produced by accepting the defaults is a chart designed by whoever wrote the library, for no particular question.

The third reason is that this material transfers everywhere. A report, a slide, a poster and a dashboard all contain charts, and all four medium modules in this track defer to this lecture rather than restating it. It is also the part of the course most likely to change what a quantitative professional actually produces, because it is the part where their existing instincts are strongest and most often wrong.

The fourth is that a chart is an argument and the encoding is part of the claim. Choosing to plot the level rather than the change, or the absolute rather than the ratio, is an editorial decision that determines what the reader can see. The most consequential chart decisions are taken before any visual property is chosen at all, and this lecture is about the ones that come after.

Step 3

The mental model

The same four values, encoded five ways, in descending order of how accurately a reader can extract them.

Two consequences follow.

The first is that a chart type is a bundle of encoding decisions, and it is more useful to think in channels than in chart types. "Should this be a bar chart or a pie chart" is really "should this comparison run on position or on angle", and asked that way it answers itself. It also lets you handle cases no chart-type list covers, because you are reasoning about the mechanism rather than matching to a catalogue.

The second is that the ranking gives you a budget. A chart usually shows several variables and they are not equally important. Put the one the reader came for on position, the second on length or colour hue as a category, and let anything genuinely contextual take a weak channel. Spending position on something nobody will compare is the most common waste in professional charting.

Encoding channel
A visual property used to represent a value: position, length, angle, area, colour hue, colour saturation, shape, texture. Each has a measured accuracy and a capacity, and each can be spent once.
Data-ink ratio
Tufte's measure: the proportion of a graphic's ink that encodes data. His instruction is to maximise it within reason, by erasing non-data ink and then redundant data-ink. Not a number to compute, a direction to move in.
Chartjunk
Tufte's term for graphical elements that carry no information: three-dimensional effects, decorative fills, moiré patterns, heavy grids, frames. Step 5 gives the case against taking this as far as he does.
Step 4

The detail

The experiment, and the full ranking

Cleveland and McGill's 1984 paper in the Journal of the American Statistical Association did something unusual for the graphics literature: it tested. Subjects were shown pairs of encoded values and asked what percentage the smaller was of the larger. The error was measured. Different encodings produced systematically different error rates, and the ordering was stable.

Two subtleties are worth extracting. Rank 2 is why small multiples work so well: even though each panel has its own axis, position within a panel is still nearly as good as position on a shared one, so you can show twenty series without the reader losing the ability to compare. And rank 3 explains a specific common failure: in a stacked bar chart only the bottom segment sits on a common scale. Every segment above it is a length starting from a varying baseline, which is why stacked bars are fine for the total and poor for everything else.

The pie chart, stated precisely

The case against pie charts is usually made too strongly and therefore ignored. Here is the precise version.

A pie encodes on angle (rank 4) and, unavoidably, on area (rank 5), both of which are read less accurately than position or length. It has no common baseline, so comparisons between non-adjacent slices are particularly poor. And it degrades quickly with the number of categories, because slices get thin and their angles become harder to distinguish.

So a pie chart is acceptable when the reader's question is coarse ("is this roughly a half or roughly a quarter"), when part-to-whole is genuinely the point rather than comparison between parts, and when there are no more than about five slices. Outside those conditions a bar chart answers the same question better. That is a narrower prohibition than "never use pie charts" and it is one you can actually defend in a meeting.

The zero baseline, resolved

This argument recurs endlessly and has a clean answer that depends on which channel the chart uses.

SHOULD THE AXIS START AT ZERO? BAR CHART: YES, ALWAYS. A bar encodes LENGTH. The reader reads the ratio of the lengths as the ratio of the values. Truncate the axis and a bar twice as long no longer means twice as much, so the encoding is simply false. This is not a stylistic preference, it is a broken chart. LINE CHART: NOT NECESSARILY. A line encodes CHANGE through slope and position. The reader reads the shape, not the ratio of heights to the baseline. Forcing zero on a series that moves between 3.10% and 3.40% flattens the signal into a straight line and hides the thing the chart exists to show. THE RULE THAT COVERS BOTH: Zero the axis when the mark's SIZE encodes the value. Do not when the mark's POSITION encodes it. EITHER WAY: label the range clearly and do not hide it. A truncated line chart is honest. A truncated line chart with the axis labels removed is not.

Data-ink, demonstrated

Four more techniques worth having

Direct labelling
Put the series name at the end of its line, or the value on its bar, instead of in a legend. A legend forces the reader to move between the key and the marks and hold an arbitrary colour-to-name mapping in memory. Direct labels remove both costs and are the single highest-value change to most charts.
Small multiples
Repeat a small chart once per category, on identical axes, arranged in a grid. Exploits rank 2 of the ranking, scales to dozens of series where colour scales to about six, and makes the comparison a matter of scanning rather than of decoding. Tufte's strongest positive recommendation and probably the most underused technique in business reporting.
Banking to 45 degrees
Cleveland's finding that slopes are judged most accurately when the average line segment sits near 45 degrees. Practically: the aspect ratio of a line chart changes what the reader concludes, and a very wide chart flattens trends while a very tall one exaggerates them. Choose the ratio deliberately rather than letting the container decide.
Sparklines and bullet graphs
Tufte's sparkline is a word-sized graphic showing shape without axes, placed inline with the number it describes. Few's bullet graph replaces a gauge with a horizontal bar over qualitative bands and a target marker, converting an angle encoding into a position one and using a fraction of the space. Both are covered in the dashboard module.

Choosing by relationship

The Financial Times' Visual Vocabulary organises chart types by the relationship being shown rather than by name, which is the right level of abstraction because it starts from what you are trying to say. Its nine families are deviation, correlation, ranking, distribution, change over time, magnitude, part-to-whole, spatial and flow.

Used as a procedure: name the relationship first, in words, before opening any tool. "I want to show that sub-prime deteriorated faster than prime after the second quarter" is a change-over-time comparison, which points to a line or slope chart. "I want to show the book is concentrated in two segments" is part-to-whole with a small number of categories. Naming the relationship usually eliminates most of the candidate chart types in one step, and it forces you to have a claim.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Where Tufte is wrong, or at least unsupported

The data-ink argument is the most influential idea in the field and parts of it have not survived testing. It would be dishonest to teach it without saying so.

Bateman and colleagues, at CHI in 2010, ran an experiment titled "Useful Junk?" comparing plain charts with heavily embellished ones in the style of Nigel Holmes. Accuracy of value retrieval was no worse for the embellished charts. Recall of the chart's subject matter and message, tested after a delay of two to three weeks, was significantly better. Participants also found them more attractive and more memorable, which Tufte's framework would predict to be irrelevant or harmful.

Other work has found that readers sometimes prefer moderate amounts of what Tufte classifies as chartjunk, and that gridlines, which he wants minimised, help when a reader genuinely needs to read values rather than compare shapes.

How to hold both. The accuracy ranking from Cleveland and McGill is on much firmer ground than the data-ink prescription, because it measures a narrow thing precisely. Tufte's argument is a strong aesthetic and rhetorical position supported by devastating examples rather than by controlled experiment, and where it has been tested it has come out partly wrong. The reasonable synthesis is that removing ink that competes with the data is well justified, while removing everything that is not data is not, particularly where memorability matters more than precision.

That said, the failure mode in professional practice is overwhelmingly in one direction. Nobody in a bank produces charts that are too austere. The Holmes-style embellishment that tested well is skilled illustration serving the message, not a default three-dimensional effect and a gradient fill, and the distance between the two is the whole point. Apply Tufte's instruction as a strong default and know that it is a default rather than a law.

What the ranking does not measure

Cleveland and McGill measured accuracy of magnitude judgement between two encoded values. That is one thing a chart does and not the only one.

A chart also has to be found, remembered, understood without instruction, and acted upon. A treemap encodes on area, rank 5, and is nonetheless the right choice for showing a hierarchy of several hundred items, because the alternatives cannot show the hierarchy at all. A choropleth map encodes on colour saturation, rank 7, and is right when the reader's question is geographic, because no better encoding preserves the geography. In both cases the ranking is correctly telling you that precise magnitude comparison will be poor, and the correct response is to accept that and label the values, not to abandon the chart.

The general principle: the ranking tells you the cost of an encoding choice, not whether to make it. Cheap and precise is best, but sometimes the expensive channel is the only one that can carry the structure you need to show. Know the price and pay it deliberately.

Common failure Learning the ranking produces a period of converting every chart to a bar chart. Bar charts are excellent and they cannot show a distribution, a correlation, a flow or a geography. The ranking is about how to encode a quantity once you know what relationship you are showing; it is not a chart-type recommendation. Name the relationship first, then apply the ranking within the candidates that can express it.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a chart from a report you did not write. Write down the reader's likely question in one sentence, then list every variable the chart encodes and the channel each one uses. Check whether the most accurate channel is carrying the variable the question is about. Then redraw it so that it is, and note what you had to give up.
  2. State the top three and bottom two of the Cleveland and McGill ranking, and describe what the experiment actually measured.
  3. Why does rank 2 make small multiples work? Explain in terms of what the reader is comparing.
  4. In a stacked bar chart, which segment is on rank 1 and which are on rank 3? What follows for how you should use them?
  5. Give the precise conditions under which a pie chart is acceptable, and explain why "never use pie charts" is a worse rule.
  6. State the zero-baseline rule in a single sentence that covers both bar and line charts.
  7. Explain why a bubble chart understates rather than merely blurs the differences it shows.
  8. What is redundant data-ink, and what has to be true before you can erase a chart's gridlines and y-axis?
  9. Summarise the Bateman et al. finding and say what it does and does not undermine in Tufte's position.
  10. A treemap encodes on rank 5 and is sometimes the right chart. Explain why, and state the general principle about what the ranking is and is not telling you.
