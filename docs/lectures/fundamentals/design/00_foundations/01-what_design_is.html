What design is

You cannot hand someone an undesigned document. You can only hand them one whose design you did not choose. Everything in this course follows from taking that seriously.

Lecture 1 of 11 Reading time, 30 minutes Sources: Norman (1988); Tufte (1983); Bringhurst (1992)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Design is the assignment of meaning to visual difference. Two things that look different are read as different. Two things that look the same are read as the same. The reader does this automatically, before deciding to, and cannot switch it off. Your only choice is whether the differences on the page correspond to the differences in your argument.

That single sentence generates the whole subject. Every design failure you will ever diagnose reduces to one of exactly two mismatches. Either the page carries a visual difference that means nothing, and the reader burns attention hunting for a distinction that is not there. Or the page shows two genuinely different things looking identical, and the reader never learns that they differ.

Call the first one noise and the second one collision. A document set in three typefaces for no reason is noise. A table where the total row looks exactly like every other row is a collision. Most bad documents contain a great deal of both, and the two feel different to a reader: noise feels cluttered and cheap, collision feels flat and untrustworthy. Neither reader can usually say what is wrong.

Feynman test Imagine handing someone a stack of paper and saying nothing. Before they read one word they have already worked out roughly how long it is, where it starts, whether it is formal, which bits are headings, and whether anyone cared. They did that from the shapes alone. Design is just deciding what those shapes tell them, instead of letting them guess.
The part that surprises people There is no neutral option. A Word document in Calibri 11 with default margins is not undesigned; it is designed by Microsoft, for no particular reader, in 2007. The defaults are somebody's decisions, made without knowing your content or your audience, and they are inherited silently. Choosing to keep them is a legitimate decision. Not knowing you made one is not.
Step 2

Why this matters

Consider what happens to a badly designed technical report. It does not come back with a note about the typography. It gets opened, scanned for about eleven seconds, and set aside for later, and later does not arrive. The reader forms a view of the document's quality during those eleven seconds, and they form it almost entirely from properties that have nothing to do with the analysis: whether the structure is visible, whether the exhibits are legible, whether the whole thing looks like someone was paying attention.

This is the part quantitative professionals underrate most, and the objection to it is reasonable, so let me state the objection first. Surely a serious reader judges the analysis, not the formatting. Surely the substance is what matters. Yes, and that is precisely the problem: the reader has to get to the substance, and how the page looks determines how much of their attention survives the trip. Every ambiguity in the layout is a small tax on the attention available for your argument. A reader who spends thirty seconds working out which column is the forecast has thirty seconds less for wondering whether the forecast is right.

There is a second cost, and it falls on you rather than the reader. A document you cannot lay out cleanly is often a document whose structure you have not settled. When a section refuses to fit the hierarchy, the usual reason is not that the hierarchy is too rigid but that the section is doing two jobs. Design pressure surfaces structural problems the way revision surfaces muddled thinking, and for the same reason: you cannot arrange something clearly until you have decided what it is.

The third reason is the one design courses skip, so this one raises it in the first lecture. Bad design is useful. A caveat set in 7pt grey at the foot of page 34 has technically been disclosed. An exhibit with a truncated axis makes a two per cent move look like a crisis, and nobody has lied. Institutions produce enormous quantities of visual material whose unreadability is load-bearing, and the same knowledge that lets you make a page clear lets you recognise when someone has made one unclear on purpose. Step 5 returns to this and the course closes on it.

Step 3

The mental model

Hold two questions about any pair of elements on a page. Do they look the same? Do they mean the same? There are four combinations and only two of them are correct.

Two consequences follow, and the second is less obvious than the first.

The first is that consistency is not a stylistic preference. It is the mechanism by which sameness communicates. If body paragraphs vary at random, then a paragraph that varies for a reason cannot be distinguished from the noise, and you have spent your ability to signal before you needed it. Consistency is what makes contrast possible, which is why every design system is mostly a list of things that must not vary.

The second is that you cannot evaluate your own page by looking at it, because you know what everything means. When you look at your table you see the total row, because you know it is the total. The reader sees seven identical rows. This is the visual equivalent of the curse of knowledge, and it is the reason step 6 of the diagnostic protocol in lecture 11 asks you to name the visual differences on the page before you are allowed to say what they mean.

Noise
A visual difference that encodes nothing. The reader's grouping machinery runs on it anyway, produces a distinction, and finds no meaning to attach. Feels like clutter.
Collision
A real distinction rendered invisibly, because the two things it separates are given identical visual treatment. Feels like flatness, and costs the reader information they never learn they missed.
Channel
A visual property that can carry meaning: position, size, weight, colour, spacing, shape, enclosure, alignment. A page has a fixed number and each can be spent once. Assigning two meanings to one channel produces ambiguity; assigning none leaves capacity unused.
Step 4

The detail

The four jobs

If design is the assignment of meaning to visual difference, it is worth asking what meanings a reader actually needs. For documents there are four, and they are worth knowing as a checklist because a page that does all four is usually adequate and a page that misses one is usually the thing you cannot put your finger on.

1. Locate
Where does this start, where am I now, where is the thing I came for? Served by running heads, page numbers, section markers and consistent placement. A reader who cannot navigate reads linearly, which for most professional documents means they do not read at all.
2. Group
Which of these things belong together? Served almost entirely by proximity and enclosure. This is the job most often done wrong, and lecture 2 explains the mechanism.
3. Rank
Which of these matters most? Served by size, weight and position. A page with no visible ranking asks the reader to decide what is important, and they will decide wrong or not bother.
4. Decode
What does this mark mean? Served by legends, labels, notation and convention. The only one of the four that can be learned once and reused, which is the entire argument for a standardised notation such as IBCS.

Affordance, signifier, and why a heading works

Donald Norman's The Design of Everyday Things is about doors and cookers, and it supplies the sharpest available vocabulary for pages. An affordance is a possible action an object offers. A signifier is the visible cue that tells you the action is there. A door affords pushing whether or not it is marked; a flat plate on it is a signifier that tells you so.

A heading is a signifier for skipping. That is its primary function, and stating it that way changes how you set one. A heading exists so a reader can decide, in under a second and without reading the section, whether this part is for them. It follows that a heading must be findable by scanning (so it needs contrast in size or weight, not merely in colour), that it must be informative (so "Background" fails and "The portfolio has doubled since 2023" works), and that it must be closer to its own section than to the one above (so the reader knows what they would be skipping). Three concrete requirements, all derived from asking what action the element affords.

Norman's other useful idea is the gulf of evaluation: the gap between the state a system is in and the state the user can perceive. A dashboard filtered to one region while displaying no indication that it is filtered has a total gulf of evaluation, and the resulting decisions are wrong through no fault of the reader. The dashboard specification's insistence that active filters always be shown is this principle, applied.

A worked diagnosis

The fragment below is invented but the dialect is not. Read the diagnosis as a demonstration of the noise-and-collision test rather than as a list of preferences.

BEFORE (a results section, as it usually arrives) Results <- 14pt bold, 6pt above, 6pt below The model was rerun on the <- 11pt Calibri 2024 vintage. Key finding <- 14pt bold, 6pt above, 6pt below Discrimination held at 0.71. Data quality <- 14pt bold, 6pt above, 6pt below Three fields were missing. Segment PD LGD EAD <- header, 11pt bold Prime 0.4% 22% 91% Near-prime 1.9% 31% 88% Sub-prime 6.2% 44% 85% Total 1.8% 28% 89% <- looks exactly like a data row DIAGNOSIS COLLISION "Key finding" and "Data quality" are subordinate to "Results" but are set identically to it. Three levels of the argument, one level of visual treatment. The reader cannot see the structure. COLLISION The total row is a different kind of number from the three above it. Nothing on the page says so. COLLISION Equal space above and below every heading. Proximity therefore groups each heading with the paragraph above as strongly as with the one below. Lecture 2 covers why this is the most common error in the set. NOISE Percentages carry one decimal in the PD column and none in LGD and EAD. The variation encodes nothing; a reader who looks for a reason finds none. AFTER (the same content, differences assigned) RESULTS <- 14pt bold, 24pt above, 8pt below The model was rerun on the 2024 vintage. Key finding <- 11.5pt bold, 14pt above, 4pt below Discrimination held at 0.71. Data quality Three fields were missing. Segment PD LGD EAD <- bold, rule below Prime 0.4% 22.0% 91.0% Near-prime 1.9% 31.0% 88.0% Sub-prime 6.2% 44.0% 85.0% ______________________________________ Total 1.8% 28.0% 89.0% <- rule above, bold Nothing was added. Two heading levels now differ, the total is marked once, spacing groups each heading with its own text, and the decimals are consistent. Four differences removed or assigned; no new ink except one rule.

Three points about that revision. The document did not become more decorated; it became less varied in the places where variation meant nothing and more varied in the two places where it meant something. The total change in ink is roughly one horizontal rule. And every single change was derived mechanically from the two-question test, without appeal to taste at any point.

What this does not claim

The framework is a floor, not a ceiling. A page can pass the noise-and-collision test completely and still be ugly, boring, or unpleasant to spend twenty minutes with. Everything the two-question test can tell you is about correctness of encoding, and correctness of encoding is roughly where the interesting part of design begins. Lectures 7 to 9 are about the part beyond it.

Nor does it claim that all visual difference must be justified before you are allowed to use it. There is a legitimate category of difference that encodes nothing propositional and is nonetheless doing work: a wordmark, a colour that signals which institution produced the document, the general impression of care. Call it register rather than information. The test still applies, because register is a meaning, but you should not find yourself defending the existence of a logo on information-theoretic grounds.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

A system beats an eye

The standard model of getting better at design is developing taste: looking at enough good work that your judgement improves, then trusting it. This works, it is how designers actually train, and for the readers of this course it is the wrong target.

Taste is a per-decision instrument. It tells you whether this particular heading looks right on this particular page, and it has to be consulted every time, which means it does not scale past what one person can personally attend to. A system is a per-decision-class instrument. It tells you, once, what every heading at this level does, and then the question never arises again. For a body of work that accumulates over years, across documents you will revise and colleagues will extend, the system is worth more than the eye, and it is worth more precisely because it is not sensitive to how you feel about a particular page on a particular afternoon.

This is also the honest reason the token specifications in this track exist and are finished before the lectures are. They are the transferable part. Someone who reads none of these lectures and simply applies the report specification will produce better reports than someone with good instincts and no system, because the specification will still be right at four in the morning on the day of the deadline.

The obvious objection is that systems produce uniform, lifeless work, and it has real force. It is also, mostly, an objection to bad systems: ones that specify outcomes rather than constraints, or that were built for a different problem and applied anyway. A system that fixes the neutral ramp and the type scale leaves every interesting decision untouched, and it removes the thirty boring ones that were consuming the attention you needed for them.

Clarity is a choice with consequences

Everything above has assumed you want the reader to understand. That assumption deserves examination, because in institutional life it is not always true, and the people who most need to know that are the ones who would never do it deliberately.

The mechanisms are ordinary. Set the caveat smaller than the finding. Put the sensitivity analysis in an appendix and the base case in the summary. Choose an axis range that makes the movement look like whatever you already concluded. Use a stacked area chart when you do not want the individual series compared. None of these is a lie, every one is defensible in isolation, and collectively they let a document say something its author could not have written in a sentence.

Tufte's argument in the Columbia analysis is precisely this: the fatal slide was not false. It contained the information. It was formatted so that the crucial magnitude, a test performed on a projectile four hundred times smaller than the one that struck the shuttle, sat in the smallest type on the slide, several levels down a bullet hierarchy, while a reassuring headline sat at the top. Nobody lied. The design did the work.

Common failure Readers of a first design lecture usually go away and add things: colour, rules, boxes, a second typeface. Almost every improvement available to a professional document is subtractive. Before you add a visual difference, find one that encodes nothing and remove it, and notice that the page got clearer without acquiring anything. Lecture 8 makes this a formal step; step 4 above did it four times and added one rule.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a one-page document you produced in the last month. List every visual difference on it: every change of size, weight, colour, spacing, alignment or shape. Do not interpret yet, just enumerate. Then, for each one, write the meaning it encodes. Any row where you cannot write a meaning is noise. Then do the reverse: list the distinctions in your content and check each one appears somewhere in the first list. Any that does not is a collision.
  2. State the definition of design given in step 1 without using the word "difference".
  3. Why does the framework imply that consistency has to come before contrast, rather than being an independent virtue?
  4. A colleague says their report is "not designed, just plain". What is wrong with the claim, and what would you have to look at to say who designed it?
  5. Give an example of noise and an example of collision from a document you did not write. Which was easier to find, and why might that be?
  6. A heading is described in step 4 as a signifier for skipping. Derive two concrete formatting requirements from that description alone, without consulting any style guide.
  7. The worked example removed three visual differences and added one. Explain why removing the decimal inconsistency counts as a design change rather than a proofreading one.
  8. Step 5 argues a system beats an eye. Make the strongest case against it that you can, then say what would have to be true for that case to win.
