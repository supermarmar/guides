Colour I: why colour deceives

The number you specify is not the colour they see. There are four independent reasons for that, they compound, and none of them is under your control. This lecture is about what survives the journey.

Lecture 5 of 11 Reading time, 35 minutes Sources: Albers (1963); Itten (1961); Stone (2003)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Colour is not a property of a thing. It is a computation your visual system performs on a patch of light, using the surrounding patches as inputs. Change the surroundings and the computation returns a different answer for the same physical stimulus. This is Josef Albers's whole argument, and it means a colour has no fixed appearance, only an appearance in context.

On top of that sit three more layers of unreliability. The same hex value emits different light on different screens, because most displays are uncalibrated and gamuts differ. Ambient light shifts perception again, which is why a palette approved in a meeting room looks wrong in a lit auditorium. And roughly one man in twelve has a colour vision deficiency, so the distinction you built may not exist for them at all.

Out of that comes one actionable rule, and it is the reason this lecture exists before the palette-building one. Lightness is the dimension that survives. Hue is fragile: it shifts with context, varies by device, and disappears for some readers. Lightness travels. If two colours differ only in hue and not in lightness, the difference between them is the first thing to be lost, and you should assume it will be.

Feynman test Take a photo of a white sheet of paper indoors under a lamp and outdoors in daylight. The pixels are completely different colours, and you saw white both times, because your brain is discounting the light source rather than reading it. That correction is running constantly and you have no access to it. It is the reason two identical greys can look different on a page, and the reason you cannot trust your own eye to tell you what value something is.
Where this bites in practice A chart with six series in six hues at similar lightness is fine on the author's monitor and fails in four ordinary situations: printed in mono, viewed by a colour-blind reader, projected in a bright room, or photographed off a screen by someone in the meeting. All four failures have one cause and one fix, and the fix is not a better set of hues.
Step 2

Why this matters

Colour is the channel professionals reach for first and understand least. It is easy to apply, it looks like effort, and unlike measure or leading it produces a visible change immediately. So it gets used to solve problems it cannot solve, and lecture 2 already gave the clearest case: a grouping problem cannot be fixed by colouring things, because proximity beats similarity. The impulse to reach for colour when the spacing is wrong is close to universal.

The second reason is that colour failures are silent. If your measure is too long, every reader suffers equally and someone will eventually mention it. If your chart encodes by hue alone, most readers are fine and a minority receive a chart with information missing, and they will not tell you, because they cannot see what they are missing. The same is true of the reader who printed it in mono. Colour is the only channel in this course whose failures are invisible to the person who introduced them and to most of the audience.

The third is regulatory and increasingly contractual. WCAG treats colour-only encoding as a failure under success criterion 1.4.1, and contrast minima under 1.4.3 are the most commonly cited accessibility defect in audits. For work that goes to a public body, a listed company's disclosures or a regulated customer communication, this stops being craft and becomes a requirement. Lecture 6 covers the numbers; this one covers why they exist.

The fourth reason is the one that changes behaviour. Because hue is fragile and lightness is durable, a palette built on lightness differences is robust to every failure mode listed above, at no cost. You do not have to choose between an accessible palette and an attractive one. You have to choose between building on the dimension that survives and building on the one that does not.

The mental model

Two consequences follow.

The first is that "use a colour-blind safe palette" is necessary and not sufficient. It solves one failure mode out of the four in step 1. The greyscale test is a separate check and takes ten seconds: desaturate the figure and see whether it still works.

The second is a positive rule. When you need a distinction to be robust, make it a lightness distinction, and let hue carry the pleasant, informative, but expendable part. A chart where the important series is darker as well as differently coloured survives everything. This is why the report specification encodes IBCS scenarios by fill pattern rather than by hue, and why the dashboard specification's default state is grey with one series darkened rather than a rainbow.

Simultaneous contrast
The shift in a colour's appearance caused by the colours adjacent to it. A patch appears lighter against a dark surround and darker against a light one, and shifts in hue away from the surround's hue. Albers's Interaction of Color is 150 pages of demonstrations of it.
Relative luminance
A weighted sum of the linearised red, green and blue components, weighted 0.2126, 0.7152 and 0.0722 to match the eye's sensitivity. It is the physical quantity that underlies perceived lightness and the basis of the WCAG contrast formula. The green weighting is why a saturated green looks so much brighter than a saturated blue.
Gamut
The range of colours a device can produce. sRGB is the conservative default that the web assumes; a modern laptop display, a projector and a CMYK press each have a different and partly non-overlapping gamut. A colour outside the target gamut is silently replaced with the nearest one inside it, which is why a saturated screen colour prints dull.
Step 4

The detail

The four deceptions, and what to do about each

1. Context (Albers)
The same value looks different against different neighbours. Mitigation: evaluate colours in situ, never on a swatch card. When a tint is used as a background, re-check the text contrast against that tint rather than against white.
2. Device
Uncalibrated displays, differing gamuts, projector contrast collapse, CMYK conversion. Mitigation: design in sRGB, keep saturation moderate so nothing sits at a gamut edge, and increase contrast beyond the minimum for anything that will be projected or printed.
3. Environment
Ambient light, screen brightness, glare, and the reader's dark mode. Mitigation: exceed the contrast floor rather than meeting it, and never rely on a distinction between two light tints, which is the first thing a bright room destroys.
4. The observer
Around 8 per cent of men and 0.5 per cent of women of northern European descent have a colour vision deficiency, overwhelmingly red-green. Ageing reduces short-wavelength sensitivity, so older readers lose blues first. Mitigation: never encode by hue alone, and use a tested palette.

The rate is worth stating precisely because it is routinely underestimated. In a credit committee of twelve people, eight of them men, the expected number who cannot reliably distinguish your red bar from your green bar is not far off one. That is not an edge case to be handled if time permits.

Colour spaces, and why HSL lies

Hex and RGB describe how to drive a screen. They do not describe how a colour looks, and no arithmetic on them corresponds to a perceptual operation. HSL was invented to fix this and only half succeeded, which matters because HSL is what most tools give you.

The problem is that HSL's L is not lightness. It is a geometric position in a cylinder, and it is not corrected for the eye's very unequal sensitivity to different wavelengths. Pure yellow and pure blue both sit at L of 50 per cent in HSL. Their relative luminances are about 0.93 and 0.07, a factor of thirteen. Building a palette by holding HSL lightness constant and rotating the hue therefore produces a set of colours with wildly different perceived brightness, which is exactly the failure the previous figure illustrates.

THE SAME "LIGHTNESS" IN HSL hsl(60, 100%, 50%) pure yellow relative luminance 0.93 hsl(240, 100%, 50%) pure blue relative luminance 0.07 Both are "50% lightness". One is nearly white to the eye and the other is nearly black. Any palette generated by rotating hue at fixed HSL lightness inherits this. Itten's seven contrasts, as a checklist

Johannes Itten catalogued seven ways two colours can differ. The list is a hundred years old and reads as an artist's taxonomy, but it is useful in one specific way: it names the channels available, so you can check which ones you are actually using.

Read the last two columns and the design instruction writes itself. The three contrasts that survive everything are the first three, and only the first three. Everything below the line in that table is a real perceptual difference that some readers, in some conditions, will not receive. Use them, and do not make them load-bearing.

Itten's extension contrast deserves a note because it is the least known and the most useful in charts. Area is a contrast: a small patch of saturated colour reads as strongly as a large patch of muted colour. This is the formal statement of why a single highlighted bar in a grey chart works, and why colouring every bar destroys the effect.

The projector problem

Projection deserves separate treatment because it fails in a specific direction. A projector cannot produce black; it produces the absence of light, which in a lit room is mid-grey. So the dark end of your palette compresses upward and contrast collapses from the bottom. A palette that measures 4.5:1 on a monitor may deliver something closer to 2:1 on a screen in a room with the lights on.

The practical consequences are three. Never use light text on a mid-tone background for projection. Never use hairline weights or thin strokes, which vanish. And treat the contrast minima as floors to exceed rather than targets to hit, which is what the presentation specification does when it sets text at near-black rather than at a mid grey.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Do no harm

Maureen Stone's summary of the position, borrowed from Tufte, is the right one to hold: colour is powerful and easy to use badly, so the first obligation is not to make things worse. A document with no colour at all is never unreadable. A document with too much colour frequently is.

This inverts the usual instinct, which treats colour as an addition that can only improve a page. Colour subtracts as well: every additional coloured element reduces the salience of every other one, for the reason lecture 2 gave about the preattentive budget. A dashboard where six things are coloured has no highlight. A report where every heading is brand-blue has spent the ability to mark anything as urgent.

The strongest version of the argument, and one worth sitting with: most professional documents need exactly one colour beyond the neutral ramp, and many need none. The report specification in this track uses a single darkened accent for section numbers and exhibit labels, and nothing else, and it does not look impoverished. It looks like a document produced by someone who decided.

Why colour comes last

Working designers choose colour late and amateurs choose it first, and the reason is not that professionals are more disciplined. It is that colour is the only channel that cannot fix a structural problem, so choosing it early means choosing it before you know what it has to do.

The order that works is: get the structure right, then the type, then the space, and only then ask what remains ambiguous. Frequently the answer is nothing, and the colour budget goes unspent. When something does remain ambiguous, you now know exactly what job the colour has, which makes the choice narrow and easy rather than open and agonising.

There is a diagnostic that follows from this. If you are choosing colours and it feels like a large open decision with many good options, you are probably choosing too early. A colour decision made at the right time feels like a small closed decision with two or three candidates, because the constraints have already accumulated.

An honest limit on this lecture

Everything above treats colour as an encoding channel to be used efficiently and safely, and that framing is deliberate for a course aimed at professional documents. It is not the whole truth about colour, and it would be wrong to leave the impression that it is.

Albers and Itten were not writing about information design. They were teaching painters and were interested in colour as an experience, in relationships that are moving or discordant for reasons no luminance calculation captures. That work is real and this lecture has ransacked it for the parts that transfer, which is a legitimate thing to do and also a narrowing. A reader who finds the subject interesting should read Interaction of Color for what it actually is, rather than for the two demonstrations that survive the trip into a design token specification.

Common failure Learning about colour vision deficiency prompts people to run their design through a simulator once, confirm it passes, and consider the matter closed. The simulator only tests deception four out of the four in step 1. Run the greyscale test as well, which catches a different failure, and check the contrast numerically rather than by eye, which catches a third. Three checks, all of them fast, and no one of them substitutes for the others.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a chart you made that uses three or more colours. Produce three versions: desaturated to greyscale, run through a deuteranopia simulator, and printed on the nearest office printer in mono. Note for each version which comparisons the chart was built to support and whether they still work. Then redesign it so that the most important comparison survives all three, and record what you had to give up.
  2. State Albers's claim in one sentence, and give one practical rule that follows for evaluating a colour.
  3. Why is lightness described as the dimension that survives? Name the failure modes it survives and the one it does not.
  4. Explain why pure yellow and pure blue both read as 50 per cent lightness in HSL, and why that makes HSL unsuitable for generating a palette.
  5. A colleague says their palette is accessible because it passed a colour-blindness simulator. What has it not been tested for, and what is the ten-second test?
  6. Of Itten's seven contrasts, which three survive both greyscale and colour vision deficiency? What design instruction follows?
  7. Explain the projector failure mode in terms of what a projector physically cannot do, and give the three consequences.
  8. In a committee of twelve, eight of them men, roughly how many people would you expect to have difficulty distinguishing a red bar from a green one? Show the arithmetic.
  9. Step 5 claims colour should be chosen last. Make the strongest case for choosing it first, then say what would have to be true for that case to win.
  10. The Okabe-Ito orange and sky blue have luminances of 0.42 and 0.41. Give two different ways to make a chart using both of them survive mono printing, and say which you would prefer.
