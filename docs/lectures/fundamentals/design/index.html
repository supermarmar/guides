Fundamentals

Design

Twenty-eight lectures on making things that are read. Not visual identity, not product design, not art direction: the design of documents, in the widest sense of that word. A blog post, a validation report, a Word memo, a board deck, a conference poster, a risk dashboard, a newsletter. Eleven lectures establish how perception, type, colour and layout actually work, then each of seven mediums gets its own treatment. Every module ships with a machine-readable token specification, so the principles land as building blocks rather than advice.

Why this exists

Most people who write professionally were never taught to design, and most design teaching is aimed at designers. The gap in the middle is where almost all real documents live. An actuary writing a model validation report, a consultant building a deck, an analyst shipping a dashboard: each of them makes a hundred design decisions and has been given a vocabulary for none of them.

The course takes the position that this is a solvable problem, because a surprising amount of what looks like taste is not. The measure at which reading speed peaks is a measurable quantity, and Bringhurst, Butterick, Rutter and the GOV.UK design system all land within the same narrow band. Cleveland and McGill established an accuracy ranking for visual encodings in 1984, and it decides most chart questions before taste is consulted. Richard Mayer's multimedia learning research gives effect sizes for slide design decisions, and the largest of them says that putting your narration on the slide actively harms recall. Where evidence exists, the lectures use it and say so. Where it does not, they say that too.

The second position is that a house style beats a good eye. A designer with taste produces good work; a system produces consistent work, which for a knowledge base compounding over years is worth more. That is what the token specifications are for.

How the modules fit together

The eleven foundation lectures are cumulative and the order is not negotiable. Perception comes first because everything after it is an argument about what a reader's visual system does before they have consciously read anything. Typography and colour follow, because they are the two systems where the amateur's instinct is most reliably wrong: type wants to be smaller and more spaced than feels right, and colour wants to be used a tenth as much. Grid, whitespace and hierarchy then say how the elements relate, and the final pair covers data graphics and a diagnostic protocol for looking at a page and naming what is wrong with it.

The seven medium modules can be taken in any order and each assumes all eleven foundations. They are sequenced by how much they constrain you rather than by importance, running from the near-total freedom of a web page to the near-total imprisonment of an HTML email. That progression is instructive in itself. The email module is last because it is the clearest demonstration that a design system is mostly a set of decisions about what you cannot do.

Reports, presentations and dashboards get three lectures each; blogs, documents, posters and emails get two. The split reflects where the professional stakes are highest and where the failure modes are most expensive, not where the material is most interesting.

How to use this

Status badges show what is built (built) or planned. All twenty-eight lectures and all eight token specifications are written.

The token files are the usable half of this course today. Start with the house specification, which defines the shared foundation every medium inherits: one warm neutral ramp, one accent, three semantic colours with separate fill and text variants, the Okabe-Ito data palette, a hand-tuned type scale and a 4px spacing base. Each medium file then states what it inherits, what it overrides and why. A medium file may change a value; it may not change a token name, because a file that renames a token has forked the system.

The separation that matters most Interface colour and data colour are different systems and never mix. The accent never appears in a chart; a data colour never styles a button. The moment they blur, a reader can no longer tell whether a colour is decoration or information, which is the failure Few, Stone and Brewer all independently warn against.

Modules

Foundations

Cumulative. Take these in order; nothing in the medium modules makes sense without them. See the module landing page. Token spec: house style.

  • builtLecture 01: What design is. Design as the assignment of meaning to visual difference, the two failure modes every bad page reduces to, and why "undesigned" is not on offer.
  • builtLecture 02: Perception and Gestalt. Preattentive processing, the grouping principles in strength order, and the experimental result that proximity overrides colour.
  • builtLecture 03: Typography I, anatomy and choosing a face. Legibility against readability, the five measurements that predict how a face behaves, and the serif question answered honestly.
  • builtLecture 04: Typography II, measure, leading and the scale. The return sweep, the 66-character target, why leading and measure are locked together, and why geometric scales fail at both ends.
  • builtLecture 05: Colour I, why colour deceives. Simultaneous contrast, the four reasons the specified value is not the perceived one, and why colour-blind safe is not the same as greyscale safe.
  • builtLecture 06: Colour II, building a palette. The build order, the neutral ramp with contrast recorded at definition, the three data families, and WCAG against APCA answered.
  • builtLecture 07: The grid. Field, module and grid; why twelve columns; the Van de Graaf canon and when it applies; and the honest answer about the golden ratio.
  • builtLecture 08: Whitespace. Space as relative rather than absolute, why uniform spacing says nothing, the nesting rule, and defending whitespace to someone who wants it removed.
  • builtLecture 09: Hierarchy and contrast. The squint test, the tools ranked with colour fifth, three levels as the budget, and hierarchy as an argument rather than formatting.
  • builtLecture 10: Data graphics. The accuracy ranking demonstrated, the pie chart and zero-baseline questions settled precisely, and the experiment that partly refutes Tufte. The most transferable lecture in the course.
  • builtLecture 11: A diagnostic protocol. Ten checks in order, five needing no knowledge of the subject, with a worked pass on a validation report and an explicit repair triage.

Blogs and long-form web

The least constrained medium, which is why it is first: every decision is yours and therefore every failure is too. See the module landing page. Token spec: blogs.

  • builtLecture 12: Reading on a screen. The three differences that matter, the reading column as the decision that fixes the rest, why body size does not scale fluidly, and dark mode as a designed palette.
  • builtLecture 13: The long-form page. The three scanning patterns, headings that carry the argument, the three-width system, sidenotes against footnotes, and the layered page.

Reports

Print-first, A4, PDF. The medium with the highest professional stakes in this collection. Token spec: reports.

  • builtLecture 14: The report page. Deriving A4 margins backwards from the 66-character target, the 15.4pt baseline and what vertical rhythm is actually worth, and the page-break checklist a fixed page forces.
  • builtLecture 15: Tables. A worked before-and-after, alignment by data type, the zebra-striping argument resolved, ordering as a design decision, and the table as disclosure rather than presentation.
  • builtLecture 16: Exhibits and business notation. The four parts of an exhibit, the IBCS scenario notation and variance layout, direct labelling, the shipping gate, and why a half-adopted notation is worse than none.

Working documents and notes

The everyday medium, and the one where the design system has to survive contact with someone else's laptop. See the module landing page. Token spec: documents.

  • builtLecture 17: The Word document as a design system. Normal as the root and every other style as a delta, direct formatting as technical debt, Word's line-spacing quirk, and the one place this course overrules Butterick.
  • builtLecture 18: Memos, briefing notes and the page limit. Three formats defined by what they forbid, the argument against bullet points stated properly, and notes as a bet on your future self.

Presentations

The medium with the most advice and the least evidence, except in the places where the evidence is unusually strong. See the module landing page. Token spec: presentations.

  • builtLecture 19: Why most decks fail. Mayer's effect sizes charted, Tufte's critique stated fairly and then argued with, and the read-alone deck treated as a request for a different artefact.
  • builtLecture 20: The slide as a designed object. Canvas zones, two type scales for two rooms, the seven layouts, the four chart overrides, and why "make it bigger" is the wrong instruction.
  • builtLecture 21: The action title. The ghost deck worked as a before-and-after, Minto's vertical and horizontal logic, Zelazny's message-to-chart mapping, and a decision rule for deck against report.

Posters and one-pagers

A single sheet, no speaker, no scroll, read by someone walking past. See the module landing page. Token spec: posters.

  • builtLecture 22: Designing for distance. Three readers at three distances, the legibility table and why the circulating formulae should not be used, what the Better Poster actually redistributes, and the word budget as a token.
  • builtLecture 23: Isotype and the one-pager. Why scaling a symbol fails in two directions at once, the Isotype rules and their political origin, what the method cannot do, and the one-pager block structure.

Dashboards

A screen someone returns to, scanning for whether anything needs attention. See the module landing page. Token spec: dashboards.

  • builtLecture 24: What a dashboard is for. Few's definition and what each clause excludes, the failures grouped into four kinds, and the scenario brief that kills dashboards worth killing.
  • builtLecture 25: Choosing the encoding. The nine FT families as a reference figure, the pie chart and zero-baseline questions settled, and the underrated case for discrete encodings.
  • builtLecture 26: Building the screen. The KPI tile and bullet graph exploded, gridlines and number formatting, the dark variant as a re-pick, and provenance as the medium's unsolved problem.

Emails and newsletters

Last, because it is the clearest demonstration that a design system is mostly a set of decisions about what you cannot do. See the module landing page. Token spec: emails.

  • builtLecture 27: The most constrained medium. The three-client support matrix, why six per cent of opens sets the whole specification, the bulletproof button, and dark mode as a degradation you design for.
  • builtLecture 28: The newsletter as composition. Where the newsletters people finish actually sit, the plain-text evidence with its caveats, the legal footer, and the closing argument of the whole course.

What is and is not covered

The course covers the design of things made to be read: documents, screens and sheets carrying argument and evidence. It does not cover brand identity, logo design, illustration, motion, product or interaction design beyond what a dashboard needs, or the craft of a designer working to a client brief. It does not teach a tool. Figma, InDesign, PowerPoint and Tableau appear only where a specific constraint of theirs shapes a decision.

Two things it does that most design teaching avoids. It separates the evidence-backed claims from the conventions, and labels which is which. Measure, encoding accuracy and the multimedia learning principles are empirical; caption placement, the twelve-column grid and the choice of a warm neutral over a cool one are conventions, defensible but not proven, and the lectures say so rather than dressing convention as law. And it teaches the disagreements: Butterick's 90-character line against Bringhurst's 75, Tufte's position that the deck should not exist against the practical need for one, Butterick's insistence on proper typefaces against the reality of a colleague's laptop. Where the authorities conflict, the lecture argues a position instead of averaging them.

Source material

On typography: Robert Bringhurst, The Elements of Typographic Style, which supplies the measure rule and the proportional approach to the page; Matthew Butterick, Practical Typography, which is the only major source that gives hard numbers for everything and is freely readable online; Jost Hochuli, Detail in Typography; Richard Rutter, Web Typography. On colour: Josef Albers, Interaction of Color; Johannes Itten, The Elements of Color; Maureen Stone, A Field Guide to Digital Color; Cynthia Brewer's ColorBrewer schemes; and the Okabe-Ito colour-universal-design palette, popularised by Bang Wong in Nature Methods (2011).

On layout and perception: Josef Müller-Brockmann, Grid Systems in Graphic Design; Jan Tschichold, The Form of the Book, and the Van de Graaf canon it documents; Max Wertheimer's Gestalt papers; Colin Ware, Information Visualization. On data graphics: Edward Tufte, The Visual Display of Quantitative Information and Envisioning Information; William Cleveland and Robert McGill's 1984 graphical-perception experiments, which are the empirical backbone of the whole subject; Stephen Few, Show Me the Numbers and Information Dashboard Design; Alberto Cairo, The Functional Art and The Truthful Art; Steve Wexler, Jeffrey Shaffer and Andy Cotgreave, The Big Book of Dashboards; the Financial Times Visual Vocabulary; and the International Business Communication Standards, now ISO 24896.

On presentations: Nancy Duarte, Slide:ology and Resonate; Garr Reynolds, Presentation Zen; Edward Tufte, The Cognitive Style of PowerPoint; Richard Mayer, Multimedia Learning and the Cambridge Handbook, which supply the only effect sizes in the course; Barbara Minto, The Pyramid Principle; Gene Zelazny, Say It With Charts. On documents and information design: Karen Schriver, Dynamics in Document Design; Otto and Marie Neurath on Isotype with Gerd Arntz's pictograms; Mike Morrison's Better Poster; Nigel Holmes on explanatory graphics; Craig Mod's essays on margins and reading.

On systems and the web: Oliver Reichenstein's writing at iA; the published design systems of IBM (Carbon), Google (Material 3), the UK Government Digital Service, Apple and Radix, which are read as evidence of practice rather than as authorities; Litmus and Can I Email for the email rendering landscape; and the W3C's WCAG 2 contrast criteria, alongside APCA, the candidate replacement that has not yet replaced anything.
