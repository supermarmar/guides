Lectures / Design / Emails

The most constrained medium

One of the three clients that matter renders your HTML through Microsoft Word. Another rewrites your colours in dark mode and ignores what you asked for. Design accordingly.

Lecture 27 of 28 Reading time, 30 minutes Sources: Litmus; Can I Email; MJML documentation
Step 1

The 30-second version

Three clients account for the overwhelming majority of opens: Apple Mail, Gmail and Outlook. They agree on very little. Layout is HTML tables, because flexbox, grid, float and positioning all fail somewhere that matters. The column is 600px, a convention that has survived twenty years because it fits every reading pane without horizontal scroll. Type is Arial or Helvetica, because a web font that fails in Outlook is worse than a system font everywhere.

The defining fact is that Outlook on Windows renders HTML through Microsoft Word's engine. That is not a quirk to work around, it is the reason the whole specification looks the way it does: no max-width, no border-radius, no background images without VML, no padding on an anchor tag, and no media queries at all.

The floor that is genuinely not negotiable is 16px body text. iOS automatically enlarges anything smaller, which breaks the layout you designed around the smaller size. Setting 16px is easier than fighting the enlargement, and it happens to be the accessible floor anyway.

Feynman test You are writing a letter that will be photocopied by three different machines before anyone reads it, and one of them is thirty years old and jams on anything but plain paper. You do not send your best watercolour. You send something that survives all three machines, and you accept that the constraint is the oldest machine.
Where this dates Microsoft has signalled that the Word-based desktop Outlook is being retired, currently pointed at late 2026. Do not drop the VML fallbacks on the strength of a signal: corporate estates lag, and the client you are designing for is the one on the recipient's machine rather than the one Microsoft ships. The reasoning in this lecture will outlast the specific figures.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that email failures are silent and total. A page with a CSS bug looks slightly wrong; an email with a CSS bug can arrive as a single unstyled column of stacked images, or as nothing at all, and the sender sees the version that rendered correctly in their own client. You do not get told, and the recipients who saw the broken version do not report it, they delete it.

The second is audience skew. The client shares quoted here are global averages, and yours are not global. A mailing list of bank employees will be far more Outlook-heavy than the average, which means the client with the worst rendering is the one most of your readers use. Check your own analytics before trusting any published distribution, including the one in this lecture.

The third is that the constraints are genuinely instructive. Working in a medium where almost nothing is available forces you to discover how little you needed, and readers who spend time here usually find their web and document work gets simpler afterwards. That is the pedagogical reason this module is last rather than first.

The fourth is legal. Unlike every other medium in this track, email carries statutory requirements: an unsubscribe mechanism, a physical postal address, clear sender identification. These are not design preferences and lecture 28 covers them.

Step 3

The mental model

Three clients, and what each one takes away.

Two consequences follow.

The first is that the design target is the intersection rather than the union. You build for what all three support and treat everything else as progressive enhancement: rounded corners that Apple Mail shows and Outlook squares off, a web font that loads for some readers and falls back for the rest. Nothing may depend on a feature that any of the three lacks.

The second is that this reverses the usual relationship between market share and influence. Six per cent of opens determines the entire specification, because that six per cent is where the design breaks rather than merely degrades. The right question is never what most readers support, it is what the least capable reader will do with it.

Progressive enhancement
Building so the base version works everywhere and better clients get more. In email this is the only viable strategy, because there is no way to detect the client and serve different code.
Conditional comment
The <!--[if mso]> syntax, which only Microsoft's engine parses. The one client-targeting mechanism available in email, and the reason bulletproof buttons are possible at all.
VML
Vector Markup Language, a pre-SVG Microsoft format that the Word engine still understands. Used inside conditional comments to produce rounded buttons and background images that Outlook would otherwise refuse.

The bulletproof button

The padding sits on the <a>, which Outlook ignores, which is exactly why the VML version exists alongside it. One primary call to action per email. Dark mode, honestly

You control one of the three clients. Declare intent anyway.

<meta name="color-scheme" content="light dark"> <meta name="supported-color-schemes" content="light dark"> Apple Mail honours prefers-color-scheme. You get the palette you designed. Gmail ignores the query and applies its own partial inversion. You get whatever it decides. Outlook no transform at all. Your light design renders on a dark OS. DESIGN SO A NAIVE INVERSION DOES NOT DESTROY IT [ ] No logo on a baked-in white background. Transparent PNG with a light stroke, so it survives either ground. [ ] No text inside images. It cannot invert, cannot be read aloud, and cannot be translated. [ ] Never rely on a background colour to make text legible. The inversion may change one and not the other, leaving grey on grey. [ ] Test in all three. There is no substitute.

Always set width as an attribute as well as in CSS. Always set alt. Many clients block images by default, so a meaningful fraction of your readers see only the alt text, which makes it content rather than metadata.

Never put essential text inside an image. It fails when images are blocked, is invisible to a screen reader, cannot be translated, and inverts unpredictably in dark mode. This single rule prevents more email failures than any other.

Responsive, singular

@media only screen and (max-width: 600px) { .container { width: 100% !important; } .section { padding: 20px !important; } .cta { width: 100% !important; display: block !important; } .stack { display: block !important; width: 100% !important; } .h-hero { font-size: 26px !important; line-height: 1.25 !important; } } Outlook desktop does not fire this. So the 600px layout must be acceptable at any width on its own, which means designing MOBILE-TOLERANT rather than mobile-first: one column that simply gets narrower works everywhere. This is also why two columns are usually a mistake. They stack correctly in Apple Mail and Gmail and remain two narrow columns in Outlook's reading pane.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Designing for the worst client is the correct decision

Six per cent of opens setting the specification looks like the tail wagging the dog and is not. The reasoning is worth making explicit because the same structure appears elsewhere.

The distribution of failures is not the distribution of readers. In Apple Mail and Gmail a design that ignores Outlook renders perfectly; in Outlook it can arrive unusable. So the expected cost is not six per cent of the value, it is six per cent of your readers receiving something that damages your credibility, and you cannot identify which six per cent in advance. In a corporate audience it is also, frequently, the most senior six per cent.

The general principle: when failures are severe and unevenly distributed, design for the failing case rather than the average one. This is the same logic as the colour-blind palette in lecture 5, where eight per cent of men cannot receive a hue distinction and the response is not to accept an eight per cent loss but to encode differently. Accessibility arguments and this one are the same argument.

What the constraint teaches

Working here for a while produces a specific realisation, and it is the reason this module closes the track.

You lose flexbox, grid, web fonts, rounded corners, shadows, background images and media queries, and the emails you produce are not noticeably worse. They are more constrained, plainer, and in the aggregate they perform better, which lecture 28 covers. A very large fraction of what the other modules treat as available technique turns out to have been optional.

That is uncomfortable and it is worth sitting with rather than resolving. It does not mean the techniques are worthless: a well-set report is genuinely better than a badly set one and the difference is not decoration. It does mean that the proportion of design value coming from structure, hierarchy, restraint and knowing what to leave out is much higher than the proportion coming from technique, and email is where you find that out because everything else has been removed.

Common failure Testing in one client and shipping. Rendering differences here are not cosmetic and cannot be reasoned about from the code; a pattern that looks safe can collapse in ways nobody predicts. Litmus and Email on Acid exist for this, and the free minimum is to send yourself a copy and open it in Apple Mail, Gmail on the web and Outlook on Windows. Twenty minutes, and it is the only part of this lecture that cannot be substituted with care.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take an email your organisation sent and open it in Outlook on Windows, Gmail on the web and a phone. Note every difference. Then look at the source and identify which construct caused each one. Most people find at least one failure they had never seen.
  2. Why is layout done with tables rather than with CSS?
  3. What is the single fact about Outlook that explains most of the specification?
  4. Why is 16px the body floor, and what happens below it?
  5. Explain why the design target is the intersection of client support rather than the union.
  6. Why does role="presentation" matter, and where does it go?
  7. Why must a bulletproof button carry both a VML block and an anchor tag? Which one does Outlook use and why is the other insufficient?
  8. You control dark mode in one of three clients. Give the three behaviours and the four design rules that make a naive inversion survivable.
  9. Why is a two-column email usually a mistake, given that the media query stacks it?
  10. Step 5 argues that designing for six per cent of readers is correct. State the reasoning, and name another place in this track where the same structure appears.
