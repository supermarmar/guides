Tables

The most misdesigned object in professional writing, and the one your reports contain most of. Almost everything that is wrong with a typical table is something that was added to it.

Lecture 15 of 28 Reading time, 30 minutes Sources: Tufte (1983); Few (2004); Butterick
Step 1

The 30-second version

Three rules do most of the work. Horizontal rules only, never vertical: alignment already separates the columns, and every vertical rule is ink the reader must filter to reach the numbers. Align by data type: text left, numbers right or on the decimal, dates left, and each header aligned to match its own column. Mark the total, because a total is a different kind of number from the rows above it and looking identical to them is a collision.

Underneath all three sits one requirement without which none of them works: tabular figures. In a proportional numeral set the digit 1 is narrower than the digit 8, so a column of right-aligned numbers will not actually align, it will wobble. This is a one-line fix, almost nobody applies it, and it defeats every other improvement you make.

The instinct this lecture argues against is addition. Faced with a table that is hard to read, most people add gridlines, shading, borders and colour. The table was hard to read because a reader cannot follow a row across or a column down, and both of those are fixed by removing things and by getting the alignment right.

Feynman test A spreadsheet has gridlines because it is a workspace and you need to know which cell you are clicking in. A printed table is not a workspace; the reader is not editing anything. Keeping the gridlines is like printing the ruler along with the drawing.
Why this matters more in your work than in most In regulated modelling work the table frequently is the evidence. A chart shows a claim about the numbers; the table shows the numbers, and it is what a reviewer, an auditor or a supervisor actually interrogates. That gives table design a status it does not have in most fields: it is not presentation of the analysis, it is disclosure of it.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that a table exists to support a specific reading motion, and a badly designed one prevents exactly that motion. Almost every table is read down a column, comparing values, or across a row, assembling a record. If the numbers do not align, the down-column read fails. If the rows are too tall or too tightly packed, the across-row read fails. Everything else about a table's appearance is secondary to whether those two motions work.

The second is volume. A validation report might contain thirty tables and four charts. Whatever fraction of design attention tables receive, it is smaller than the fraction of the document they occupy, and improvements to the table style propagate across the whole document from one change.

The third is that tables are where the reader checks you. A sceptical reviewer does not scrutinise the prose; they go to the table and try to reconcile it with something they already know. A table that is hard to read makes that reconciliation slow, and a reviewer who cannot easily verify a number is a reviewer who starts wondering what else is hard to check.

The fourth is that this is the one area where the authorities substantially agree. Tufte, Few and Butterick disagree about plenty and converge almost completely on tables: fewer rules, align by type, no decorative shading, let whitespace do the separating. When three people who argue about everything else agree, the advice is probably right.

Step 3

The mental model

The same eight numbers, twice.

Two consequences follow.

The first is that alignment creates implicit columns, and lecture 2 explains why: continuity is a Gestalt grouping cue, so a set of numbers sharing a right edge reads as a column without any rule being drawn. The vertical rules in the left-hand table are not merely unnecessary, they are asserting something the alignment was already asserting, and the redundant assertion costs ink and attention.

The second is that the total row demonstrates the collision failure from lecture 1 in its purest form. A total is a genuinely different kind of number, it exists in the author's head as different, and nothing on the left-hand table conveys that. The fix is one rule and one weight change, and it converts a table where the reader must find the total into one where the total announces itself.

Tabular figures
A numeral set in which every digit occupies the same width, so columns align regardless of which digits appear. Reached by font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums in CSS, or the corresponding OpenType feature in a layout tool. Required in every table; wrong in running prose.
Decimal alignment
Aligning numbers on the decimal point rather than on the right edge. Identical to right alignment when every value has the same number of decimals, and superior when they do not. The default choice for any column of mixed precision.
Rule weight hierarchy
Three weights, three jobs: a heavier rule above the header and below the last row to bound the table, a lighter rule under the header to separate it, and hairlines between body rows only if the table is long enough to lose your place in.

Centred numbers are the most common error and the most damaging, because centring destroys the property that makes a numeric column readable. When digits align by place value, a longer number is visibly a larger number and the reader gets magnitude for free from length. Centre them and every comparison becomes an act of reading.

Zebra striping: the argument, resolved

Tufte classifies alternating row shading as chartjunk and would remove it. Some usability work suggests it helps readers track across wide tables. Both positions have something to them, and the resolution is to identify what the striping is actually fixing.

The problem it solves is losing your place while reading across a wide or tall table. That problem is real. But striping solves it by adding a fill to half the rows, which puts ink behind the numbers, and there are two cheaper fixes for the same problem: reduce the row height so the row is a more compact visual unit, or add a hairline every five rows, which gives the eye a rail without shading anything.

So the working rule is: no striping under about eight rows, because there is no problem to solve. Above that, try tighter rows first, then grouped hairlines, and reach for striping only if the table is genuinely wide as well as long. If you do use it, use {color.neutral-05} rather than something you can see clearly, because the stripe only needs to be perceptible, not visible.

Ordering, which is a design decision

The order of rows and columns determines which comparisons are easy, and the default of alphabetical or source order is almost always wrong.

ORDERING A TABLE ROWS Sort by the variable the reader cares about, descending, unless there is a natural order (time, rating grade, maturity bucket) in which case use that. Alphabetical is correct only when the reader will look up a known name, as in a glossary. COLUMNS Put columns that will be compared ADJACENT. Comparing two columns separated by three others is a rank-2 comparison in Cleveland and McGill terms rather than rank 1, and the accuracy drop is real. Worse: Segment | PD | Exposure | Tenor | LGD Better: Segment | PD | LGD | Exposure | Tenor (PD and LGD are the pair being compared) TOTALS Bottom for a column of values the reader accumulates. Top if the total is the headline and the rows are the breakdown, which is the consulting convention and is right when the reader mostly wants the total. Pick one and use it throughout the document.

Numbers

Consistent precision within a column
Every value in a column carries the same number of decimals, even where a particular value would look tidier with fewer. 22.0% rather than 22% when its neighbours are 31.4%. Inconsistent precision is the noise failure from lecture 1: a visible difference encoding nothing.
Precision that is honest
Do not report more decimals than the estimate supports. A PD of 0.4173% from a model with a wide confidence interval claims a precision that is not there, and a reviewer who notices will discount everything else. Round to what you can defend.
Units in the header, not the cell
"EAD (GBPm)" in the header, and bare numbers below. Repeating "GBPm" in every cell adds ink forty times to say something once, and it breaks the numeric alignment.
Thousands separators, and negatives
Separate thousands, always. For negatives, choose a minus sign or parentheses and use it everywhere; parentheses are the accounting convention and read better in a dense column, but a mix of the two in one document is worse than either.

When a table should be a chart, and when it should not

Few's framing is the useful one. A table is right when the reader needs individual values, when the values will be looked up rather than compared as a set, when precision matters, or when the units differ between rows. A chart is right when the message is a pattern, a comparison across many items, or a shape over time.

The practical test: write down the sentence the reader should take away. If it contains a specific number, the table is doing the work. If it contains a comparison or a direction, the chart is. If it contains both, you may genuinely need both, and the honest layout is a small chart above a table rather than a chart with the numbers crammed onto it.

One case runs the other way and matters in regulated work: a chart that presents a conclusion should frequently be accompanied by the table it came from, in an appendix if not in the body. The chart carries the claim and the table carries the evidence, and a reviewer needs both.

Long tables

TABLES THAT SPAN PAGES [ ] Header row repeats on every page. Set as a table property, never by retyping the row. [ ] No row breaks across a page. A row split in half is unreadable and looks like an error, because it is one. [ ] Group hairline every 5 rows if over about 15 rows. {color.neutral-30} at 0.25pt. Enough for the eye to rail against, not enough to read as structure. [ ] "continued" marker on the second and later pages. In the caption, not in the table. [ ] Consider whether it belongs in an appendix. A table over about 40 rows is reference material, and reference material interrupts an argument.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The table as disclosure

In most fields a table is a presentation choice. In regulated modelling work it is closer to a legal instrument, and the difference is worth being explicit about because it changes some of the trade-offs above.

A chart makes a claim about data. A table shows the data, and a reader can check it, recompute from it, and hold you to it. When a supervisor asks how a conclusion was reached, the table is the answer in a way the chart is not. That gives table design a purpose beyond readability: it is the mechanism by which your work is auditable.

Two consequences follow that a pure design perspective would not reach. First, do not aggregate away detail for visual tidiness. A table with fourteen rating grades is uglier than one with three risk bands and it is also the one that lets a reviewer see the shape of the transition. Second, the rounding decision is substantive rather than cosmetic. Rounding to a precision the model does not support overstates; rounding away a difference that matters conceals. Neither is a formatting question.

This is also why the "should it be a chart" test resolves differently here than it would in journalism. A newspaper replaces a table with a chart because the reader wants the pattern. A validation report keeps both, because one reader wants the pattern and another wants to check the arithmetic, and the second reader is the one who signs.

Where Tufte and Few diverge, and who to follow

These two agree on almost everything about tables and diverge on one thing worth knowing about.

Tufte's instinct is maximal subtraction: remove rules until only the data remains, and if the table can survive with no rules at all, that is the correct table. Few is more permissive, treating a small number of rules as a legitimate aid to a reader who has to track across a wide table under time pressure, and being generally more interested in what works for a business audience than in what is minimal.

For the documents this course is about, Few is the better guide, and the reason is the reading conditions rather than the principle. Tufte's ideal reader is attentive and unhurried, looking at a well-printed page. Yours is on page nine of eleven papers, on a Sunday, on a laptop, at eighty per cent zoom. Three rules cost almost nothing and buy real robustness under those conditions.

Where Tufte is unambiguously right, and where the business world routinely ignores him, is on decorative ink: fills, shading, three-dimensional effects, coloured banding and boxed borders. None of those aids anything and all of them are common. The subtraction argument holds completely there and this lecture's before-and-after is mostly an illustration of it.

Common failure Fixing the table style and leaving the numbers. A perfectly set table of fourteen columns, none of which the reader needs, is still a bad table. Before styling, ask which columns support the sentence the reader should take away, and delete the others into an appendix. Table design has the same relationship to table content that typography has to writing: it can make good content usable and it cannot rescue bad content.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a table from a report you produced. Count the rules and fills, then rebuild it with three horizontal rules, correct alignment by data type, tabular figures, consistent decimals and a marked total. Put the two side by side and count the ink removed. Then check whether the columns actually align by holding a straight edge against the right edge of a numeric column.
  2. State the three main rules and the requirement that makes all three work.
  3. Why are vertical rules unnecessary? Answer in terms of a Gestalt principle rather than by restating the rule.
  4. Why is centring numbers worse than left-aligning them?
  5. Give the problem zebra striping solves, and two cheaper fixes for the same problem.
  6. A column contains 0.4%, 1.85%, 12.3% and 6%. State two things wrong with it and give the corrected column.
  7. You are comparing PD and LGD across five segments, and the table also carries exposure and tenor. Give the column order and the reason, referring to the perceptual ranking.
  8. Give Few's test for table against chart, and describe the case where a regulated document should carry both.
  9. Step 5 says rounding is substantive rather than cosmetic. Give one example of rounding that overstates and one that conceals.
  10. Tufte and Few diverge on how many rules a table should have. State the divergence, say who this module follows, and give the reason in terms of reading conditions rather than principle.
