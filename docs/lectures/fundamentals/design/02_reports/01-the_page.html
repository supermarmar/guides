The report page

Everything on an A4 page follows from one number: how many characters you want on a line. Get that right and the margins, the type size and the leading fall out of it. Get it wrong and no amount of subsequent care recovers the page.

Lecture 14 of 28 Reading time, 30 minutes Sources: Butterick; Bringhurst (1992); Tschichold (1975)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Do not choose margins. Derive them. Pick the body size, work out how wide 66 characters is in that face at that size, subtract from the page width, and distribute what is left. On A4 with an 11pt serif this gives a text block of about 160mm and therefore margins of about 25mm, which is where the report specification's numbers come from.

The second decision is the baseline. Fix a single vertical unit, 15.4pt here, being 11pt at 1.4 leading, and make every vertical measurement on the page a multiple of it: space above headings, space below, the height of an exhibit block, everything. In print this is worth doing properly, and lecture 4 explained why it is not worth doing on screen.

The third is the type pairing: serif body, sans headings. Not because serifs are more readable, which lecture 3 established they are not, but because the categorical contrast between the two gives you a hierarchy cue at a glance for free, and because a serif at 11pt on paper is what a professional document has looked like for four hundred years and register is real.

Feynman test You do not decide how wide a road should be by looking at the map and picking something that seems balanced. You work out how many lanes of traffic it has to carry and how wide a lane is, and the road width comes out of that. A margin is whatever is left over once the text block is the right width. It is the consequence, not the decision.
The Word default that is actually right Word's default A4 margins are 25.4mm all round, which lands almost exactly where the derivation above ends up. This is the one Word default worth keeping, and it is worth knowing which one it is, because the temptation once you start adjusting a template is to change everything.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that page geometry is the decision with the longest reach. It applies to every line on every page, it cannot be varied locally without looking like a mistake, and it is nearly impossible to change late because everything reflows. A report is one of the few artefacts where the first decision genuinely constrains all the others, and it is usually made in the first thirty seconds by opening a template.

The second reason is that reports are read under conditions the author never experiences. Yours is one of eleven papers in a committee pack, read on a Sunday evening, probably printed, possibly at 90 per cent scaling to fit a duplex printer, and skimmed for the two paragraphs that concern the reader's own area. A page that is comfortable when you have all afternoon can be genuinely hard work under those conditions, and margin and measure are the settings that decide it.

The third is that the fixed page is a constraint the web does not have and it changes what design has to do. There is no scrolling, no expanding, no hover, no link. If a caveat needs to reach the reader, it has to be on the page they are looking at, at a size they will notice. Every technique the blogs module used to let readers choose their own depth is unavailable here, and the compensation is that you control exactly what a page contains.

The fourth is that this is where a specification pays for itself most obviously. Once the geometry, baseline and type scale are fixed, producing the next report is largely mechanical, and the attention goes into the analysis rather than into re-deciding what a heading looks like. Every hour spent on the template is recovered several times over across a year of reports.

Step 3

The mental model

The derivation, run forwards.

Two consequences follow.

The first is that the numbers in the specification are outputs and must be recomputed if an input changes. Substituting Cambria for Source Serif changes the set width, which changes the millimetres per 66 characters, which changes the margin. A specification that states 25mm without stating the face and size it was derived for is incomplete, which is why the report token file names both.

The second is that the bottom margin being deeper than the top is deliberate and worth defending, since it looks like an error. A text block centred by measurement appears to sit slightly low, because the eye places the optical centre of a rectangle above the geometric one. Making the bottom margin about 10 per cent deeper corrects for it. This is a real perceptual effect and the correction is in every serious book design, though the exact amount is convention rather than measurement.

Text block
The rectangle within which all body text sits, defined by the margins. Its width is the measure and its height determines how many lines a page carries. Everything else on the page is positioned relative to it.
Baseline
The single vertical unit to which all spacing resolves. Here 15.4pt, being 11pt body at 1.4 leading. Every heading space, exhibit block and paragraph gap is a whole multiple of it.
Optical centre
The point a viewer perceives as the middle of a rectangle, which sits above the geometric centre. The reason the bottom margin is set deeper than the top.

Note the space-before to space-after ratios: 4:1 at section level, 3:1 at subsection, 4:1 at minor. That is lecture 2's heading rule implemented, and it is the single change that most improves a report produced from a default template.

Body at 11pt sits in the middle of Butterick's 10 to 12pt range and is a deliberate choice against the 12pt that word processors default to. Eleven point with 15.4pt leading fits more on the page and reads better than 12pt at Word's default spacing, which is a genuinely uncomfortable setting that most people have simply stopped noticing.

Paragraphs: space or indent, never both

Two conventions exist and both work. Space between paragraphs with no indent is the modern default and is right for a report with frequent headings, because the space reinforces the structure. A first-line indent of one em with no space is the book convention, saves a line per paragraph, and reads better in a long continuous narrative section.

Using both signals the paragraph break twice and wastes page. Choose per document rather than per paragraph, and if in doubt use space, because a report is a document a reader enters and leaves repeatedly rather than one they read straight through.

Running heads, folios and the furniture

Running head
8pt sans in {color.text-secondary}, 12mm from the trim edge, with a hairline rule below. Left: the document's short title. Right: the current section. Suppressed on the cover and on section-opening pages, because a reader who has just been told the section name does not need telling again.
Folio
The page number. 8pt sans, centred or outer-aligned in the footer. Roman numerals for front matter, arabic from the first body page. Format "7" or "7 of 34". Never "Page 7 of 34", which spends four words saying what two characters already said.
Section numbering
Number to two levels (3.2), rarely three (3.2.1), never four. A reference to "section 4.3.2.1" is a reference nobody can hold, and a document needing that depth needs breaking into parts. Numbers set in {color.accent} so they are findable when someone is hunting a cross-reference.
Footnotes
Separated from the body by a 0.25pt rule about 40mm wide, left-aligned. Number continuously through the document rather than restarting each page, so "footnote 14" is unambiguous. Superscript markers after punctuation.

One column, and when two

A single column is right for almost every report, because at 11pt on A4 a 66-character measure already fills the page width. Two columns on A4 gives a measure around 40 characters, which is at the short end of the band and needs the type dropped to about 9.5pt to work, which is too small for a document read on screen.

Two columns earn their place in exactly one situation: a dense appendix of reference material that will be consulted rather than read, where the shorter measure is an advantage because the reader is scanning for an entry rather than following an argument. Glossaries, parameter tables and data dictionaries qualify. The main body does not.

What a fixed page forces you to decide

THE PAGE-BREAK CHECKLIST Run before any report ships. Every item is invisible to the author working in a scrolling editor and obvious to the reader holding the paper. [ ] No heading is the last element on a page. A heading whose section starts overleaf has been orphaned from its content. Keep-with-next. [ ] No single line of a paragraph sits alone at the top or bottom of a page. Widow and orphan control on. [ ] No table row breaks across a page. And every table that does span pages repeats its header row, as a table property rather than a retyped row. [ ] No exhibit appears before its first mention. Readers who meet a figure they have not been prepared for read it wrongly and then reread it. [ ] No exhibit is separated from its caption. [ ] The executive summary fits on one page. If it does not, it is not a summary. Every one of these is a setting rather than a manual fix. If you are dragging things to make them fit, the template is doing it wrong and will do it wrong again next quarter.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Vertical rhythm, and whether it is worth the trouble

Lecture 4 argued for strict vertical rhythm in print and against it on screen. This is the medium where the claim has to be cashed, so here is the honest accounting.

What you get: text on facing pages aligns, which matters if the document is ever printed duplex and read as a spread. Show-through on lightweight paper lands in register rather than as a blur. And, less tangibly, the document acquires a steadiness a reader will not identify and would notice if it were absent.

What it costs: every element with an intrinsic height, which means every exhibit, table and code block, has to be padded to a whole number of baselines. That is arithmetic, and in Word it is arithmetic you do by hand, repeatedly, and that breaks whenever anyone edits the text above.

So the defensible position is narrower than lecture 4 implied. Enforce the rhythm on the type: heading spaces, paragraph spaces, list spacing, all multiples of 15.4pt. That is where most of the benefit is and it costs one careful pass on the style definitions. Do not fight to force exhibits onto the grid unless the document is being typeset in a system that does it for you, because the effort is real and the marginal gain is small. A reader notices misaligned body text; nobody has ever noticed an exhibit sitting 4pt off a baseline.

The template is the deliverable

The thing worth building is not a good report. It is a template that makes the next twenty reports good without anyone thinking about it, and the difference matters because the person producing the next one will be under time pressure and may not be you.

This changes what "done" looks like. A well-designed report where the formatting was applied directly is a one-off, and the next author will approximate it and drift. A template with the styles defined, the margins derived, the heading ratios set, the table style built, and the page-break settings switched on is an asset. Every finding from the lecture 11 protocol should be fixed at the style level, and any finding that cannot be is itself a finding about how the document was built.

The corollary is that the effort is front-loaded and the payoff is not visible in the first document. This is a genuinely difficult thing to justify in an organisation that measures output per week, and the argument that works is the arithmetic: an hour on the template against four hours of formatting per report, across however many reports the team produces a year.

Common failure Building the template around the first report rather than around the type system. The symptom is styles named after content ("Risk Finding", "Model Caveat") rather than after level, and a set of one-off formats that do not generalise to the next document. Name styles by level and let content decide which level it is. Word's built-in Heading 1 to 3 are the right names precisely because they are content-free.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take the last report you produced. Measure the actual character count of three body lines, then run the derivation in step 3 backwards: what margin would that measure imply, and what is the margin actually set to? Then set the margins the derivation gives and read a page of each version side by side.
  2. Why are margins described as an output rather than a decision? State what the inputs are.
  3. What happens to the derived margin if you change the typeface but not the point size?
  4. Why is the bottom margin deeper than the top? Is the specific amount a measurement or a convention?
  5. Give the ratio of space-before to space-after at section level, and say which foundations lecture it comes from.
  6. Body is set at 11pt rather than 12pt. Give the argument, and say what has to move with it.
  7. Under what single condition does a two-column A4 layout earn its place? Explain why the main body never qualifies.
  8. Pick three items from the page-break checklist and state, for each, what the reader experiences when it fails.
  9. Step 5 narrows the case for vertical rhythm. State the narrower position and the reasoning behind it.
  10. A colleague has produced a beautifully formatted report using direct formatting throughout. Explain what is wrong, in terms of what happens next quarter rather than in terms of best practice.
