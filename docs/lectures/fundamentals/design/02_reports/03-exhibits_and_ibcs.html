Exhibits and business notation

Two ideas. An exhibit is an object with four parts, one of which is a claim. And a standardised notation, learned once by the reader, is free for every exhibit after the first.

Lecture 16 of 28 Reading time, 35 minutes Sources: IBCS (ISO 24896); Minto (1987); Few (2004)
Step 1

The 30-second version

An exhibit has four parts and most reports supply two of them. The label (Exhibit 3) lets it be referred to. The title should be a complete sentence stating what the exhibit shows, not a topic label. The graphic is the part everyone thinks about. The source line says where the numbers came from and what has been done to them.

Of those, the title carries the most and is the most consistently wasted. "Exposure by segment" names a topic. "Two segments carry four fifths of the book" states a finding, and a reader who reads only the exhibit titles of a well-built report has the argument. This is the same habit as the action title in a consulting deck and the informative heading on a web page, and it is probably the single highest-value practice in this whole track.

The second idea is notation. The International Business Communication Standards define a semantic vocabulary for business charts: actual is a solid fill, plan is an outline, forecast is hatched, the previous year is light grey. The reader learns it once, in your first exhibit, and every subsequent exhibit in every subsequent report is free. Critically, the encoding is by fill pattern rather than by hue, which means it survives the photocopier and the colour-blind reader, and leaves the colour channel available for variance.

Feynman test Map symbols work because they are the same on every map. Nobody prints a key explaining that blue means water, and if a mapmaker decided blue meant motorways on their map only, the map would be useless however carefully drawn. A charting notation is the same bargain: it costs the reader one act of learning and then pays out forever, and it pays nothing at all if you only half-adopt it.
The test that changes how you work Try to write the sentence title before you build the exhibit. If you cannot, you do not yet know what the exhibit is for, and building it will not tell you. This turns a formatting convention into an analytical discipline, which is why step 5 argues it is the place where design and analysis actually meet rather than merely coexist.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that exhibits are what senior readers actually consume. A committee member with eleven papers reads the executive summary and looks at the exhibits, and forms a view from those two things. The body text exists to be available rather than to be read, and a report whose exhibits do not carry the argument has communicated its argument to almost nobody who matters.

The second is that notation compounds and ad hoc encoding does not. If every chart in every report from your team uses solid for actual and hatched for forecast, a reader develops fluency and stops decoding. If each analyst picks their own colours, every chart costs the reader a fresh act of interpretation, and the cost is paid on every exhibit forever. The saving is not per chart, it is per chart per reader per reading.

The third is that the source line is where credibility is either established or quietly lost. An exhibit with no provenance is an assertion. An exhibit that states its extract date, its population, its currency and its treatment of exclusions is evidence. In regulated work this is not a stylistic nicety, and a reviewer who has to ask where a number came from has already formed a view about the rest of the document.

The fourth is that exhibits are where the ethics problem from lecture 9 is most easily exercised. A truncated axis, a chosen base period, a stacked chart that prevents comparison, a scale that differs between two exhibits showing the same measure: each is defensible individually and each shapes a conclusion. Notation constrains this, which is a point in its favour that its advocates rarely make.

Step 3

The mental model

The notation, and the layout it makes possible.

Two consequences follow.

The first is that encoding scenario by fill rather than by hue is not a stylistic quirk. It is what frees the colour channel, and once colour is free it can carry the one thing in a management chart that is actually a judgement, which is whether a variance is good or bad. A chart that spends colour on distinguishing actual from plan has nothing left to say about whether the gap matters.

The second is that consistent scaling is part of the notation and is the rule most often broken. If two exhibits show the same measure, they use the same scale, so that a bar of a given length means the same quantity in both. Exhibits that autoscale independently make a small portfolio look like a large one, and the reader comparing across pages is misled by a default nobody chose.

Semantic notation
A fixed mapping from meaning to visual property, applied consistently across every exhibit. Distinguished from a legend, which explains one chart's arbitrary choices. Notation is learned once; a legend must be read every time.
Variance chart
A layout showing level, target, absolute gap and relative gap side by side, so the reader does no arithmetic. IBCS's most distinctive contribution and the format most worth adopting even if you adopt nothing else.
Consistent scaling
Using the same axis scale for every exhibit showing the same measure, so lengths are comparable across pages. Requires overriding the autoscaling every charting tool applies by default.

Titles: the discipline behind the convention

Topic labelFinding
Exposure by segmentTwo segments carry four fifths of the book
PD trend, 2023 to 2026PD has been flat since the 2024 recalibration
Backtesting resultsThe model underpredicts in the lowest two grades
Sensitivity analysisThe result holds unless LGD moves more than 8 points
Data quality summaryThree fields drive 90 per cent of the exclusions

Two rules keep them honest. Maximum two lines, roughly fifteen words, because a title longer than that is a paragraph pretending. And it must be a claim someone could disagree with: "Exposure varies by segment" is a sentence and is not a finding, because no possible data would have contradicted it.

The convention has a cost worth acknowledging. A sentence title commits you, and a reader who disputes the exhibit now has something specific to dispute. Some authors find this uncomfortable and retreat to topic labels for exactly that reason. That retreat is the thing to resist: an exhibit whose claim cannot be stated is an exhibit whose purpose has not been decided.

IBCS, in outline

The standard is organised around the acronym SUCCESS, and knowing the seven gives you the shape of it even if you never adopt the full ruleset.

RuleIn one line
SayConvey a message. Every exhibit makes a point
UnifyApply the semantic notation consistently
CondenseIncrease information density; do not spread thin data over a page
CheckEnsure visual integrity: consistent scales, no truncated bars
ExpressChoose the visualisation that suits the message
SimplifyRemove clutter, which is Tufte's argument by another name
StructureOrganise the content logically, usually MECE

The parts worth adopting first, in order of payoff: the sentence title (Say), the variance layout, the scenario notation (Unify), and consistent scaling (Check). Those four can be adopted in an afternoon and deliver most of the benefit. The full standard is considerably larger and, as step 5 notes, is not freely available.

Legends, and why to avoid them

A legend asks the reader to hold an arbitrary colour-to-name mapping in working memory while looking somewhere else. Direct labelling puts the name where the mark is and removes both costs. This is the highest-value single change to most report charts and it takes a minute.

DIRECT LABELLING, BY CHART TYPE Line chart label at the right end of each line, vertically aligned with its final value Bar chart value inside or above each bar; series name on the axis Stacked bar label the segments in the first bar only, or abandon the stack Scatter label the points that matter; leave the rest unlabelled rather than labelling all Where a legend is genuinely unavoidable, put it above the plot and left-aligned with it, in the order the series appear in the chart. Never to the right, which is the furthest point from where the eye is reading, and never alphabetically, which fights the chart's own order.

Placement, numbering and sizing

Placement
Top or bottom of a page, never floating mid-column with text above and below. Never before the first textual mention: a reader who meets an unheralded exhibit interprets it wrongly and then has to reread it.
Numbering
Figures and tables numbered in separate sequences, continuously through the document rather than restarting per section, unless the document is long enough for section numbering (Exhibit 4.2). Referred to by number in the text, never as "the chart below", which breaks the moment anything reflows.
Sizing
Size to the claim, not to the space available. A chart making a simple point can be a third of the text width; the reflex to fill the measure produces charts with vast empty plot areas. An exhibit wider than the text block rotates to landscape on its own page rather than shrinking below legibility.
Aspect ratio
Cleveland's banking result: slopes are judged most accurately when the average line segment is near 45 degrees. Practically, a very wide line chart flattens trends and a very tall one exaggerates them, so the ratio changes what the reader concludes and should be chosen rather than inherited from the container.

The shipping gate

BEFORE ANY EXHIBIT SHIPS [ ] Title is a sentence stating a finding, under 15 words [ ] Source line present and reproducible from [ ] Print it in mono. Does the point survive? [ ] Any series distinguished by hue alone? Add a second cue [ ] Legend replaced by direct labels, or justified [ ] Same measure as another exhibit? Same scale? [ ] Bar chart? Axis starts at zero [ ] Line chart? Axis range labelled and not hidden [ ] Sign carried by more than colour on every variance [ ] Exhibit appears after its first mention in the text The mono test is the one people skip and the one that catches most. It takes ten seconds and finds the failure that no reader will ever report to you.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

A notation is an organisational commitment, not a design choice

Everything in step 4 is straightforward for one person producing one report. The value of a notation, however, is entirely in its consistency, and consistency across a team of analysts over several years is not a design problem. It is a governance problem wearing a design costume.

The failure mode is partial adoption. Three analysts use the notation, two do not, and the reader now faces charts where a hatched bar sometimes means forecast and sometimes means whatever the fifth analyst thought looked good. That is measurably worse than no notation at all, because the reader has been trained to expect a meaning that is not reliably there. A notation half-adopted has all of the cost and none of the benefit.

What actually makes adoption stick is not persuasion but infrastructure: a chart template, a theme file, a function in the plotting library that produces a compliant exhibit by default. If producing a compliant chart is harder than producing a non-compliant one, the notation will not survive its first deadline. This is the same argument the previous lecture made about templates, and it applies with more force here because charts are usually produced in code by people who will reasonably use whatever the default is.

An honest note on IBCS itself

The scenario encodings and the SUCCESS structure in this lecture were reconstructed from published summaries and from third-party implementations such as Zebra BI and Inforiver, rather than from the standard document. The full IBCS ruleset is proprietary, now published as ISO 24896, and is not freely readable.

That matters in two ways. Practically, if you are in a context where compliance with the standard is claimed or required, verify the details against the source rather than against this lecture. Intellectually, it is worth noticing that a standard whose entire value proposition is universal adoption is not freely available, which is a real tension and one its advocates tend not to address.

The reasonable position for most readers: adopt the four high-payoff conventions in step 4, which are well documented in the public summaries and which cost nothing, and treat full IBCS compliance as a question for organisations that have decided to buy into it wholesale.

Where design meets analysis

Most of this course has been about presenting work that already exists. The sentence title is the exception and is worth ending the module on, because it runs the other way.

You cannot write "the model underpredicts in the lowest two grades" unless you have established that it does. The convention forces the finding into the open at the moment the exhibit is made, rather than leaving it implicit in a chart labelled "backtesting results" that the reader is expected to interpret. Authors who adopt it consistently report the same thing: it changes the analysis, not just the report, because it surfaces exhibits that turn out to have no finding in them.

Those exhibits are the real discovery. A chart you cannot title is a chart that is in the document because the data was available rather than because it says anything, and every report has some. Removing them is the largest single improvement available to most reports, and the titling convention is what makes them visible.

Common failure Adopting the notation and then overriding it for one exhibit because that one looks better in colour. The whole value of a notation is that the reader can stop checking, and a single exception restores the checking permanently, because the reader now knows exceptions exist. If an exhibit genuinely needs to break the notation, say so in its source line rather than leaving the reader to notice.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take the last report you produced and list every exhibit title. Rewrite each as a sentence stating a finding, under fifteen words. Note which ones you cannot rewrite, and for each of those decide whether the exhibit had a point you failed to state or no point at all. Delete the second kind.
  2. Name the four parts of an exhibit and say which two most reports omit.
  3. Why does IBCS encode scenario by fill rather than by hue? Give both consequences.
  4. What four questions does the variance layout answer, and which of them does a clustered actual-against-plan bar chart leave the reader to compute?
  5. State the consistent-scaling rule and explain what a charting tool does by default that violates it.
  6. Give the two rules that keep a sentence title honest, and explain why "exposure varies by segment" fails the second.
  7. List the four things a source line must contain, and state the test for whether it is complete.
  8. Why should a legend not sit to the right of a plot? Give the ordering rule too.
  9. Step 5 argues a half-adopted notation is worse than none. Explain the mechanism, then say what actually makes adoption stick.
  10. The sentence title is described as the place where design meets analysis. Explain what it surfaces that a topic label conceals, and say what you would do with what it surfaces.
