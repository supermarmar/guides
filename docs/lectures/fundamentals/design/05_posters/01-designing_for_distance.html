Designing for distance

Three readers, one sheet. Someone at two metres deciding whether to stop, someone at one metre giving you thirty seconds, and someone at half a metre who is genuinely interested. Serve only the third and you reach none of them.

Lecture 22 of 28 Reading time, 30 minutes Sources: Morrison (2019); institutional poster guidance; Tufte (1990)
Step 1

The 30-second version

A poster has to work at three distances simultaneously. At two metres, in about three seconds, the passer-by gets the title and the finding and decides whether to stop. At one metre, in about thirty seconds, someone who stopped gets the problem, the finding and why it matters. At half a metre, over three to five minutes, the interested reader gets method, results and caveats.

The design consequence is that the finding must be the largest thing on the sheet, stated as a plain sentence in type readable from two metres. Not the title, not the institutional logo, not a chart: the finding. This is the whole of Mike Morrison's Better Poster argument and it is why his template gives the centre of the sheet to one sentence at around 100 to 125pt.

The second consequence is that word count is a design token. Eight hundred words is the ceiling for a poster and five hundred is better; four hundred for an A4 one-pager and three hundred is better. Whitespace is budgeted at 25 to 40 per cent of the sheet before anything is placed. Both numbers are constraints you design within rather than outcomes you discover.

Feynman test A shop has a sign saying what it is, a window showing what it sells, and shelves with the detail. You use all three in about ten seconds and never notice you did. Nobody would design a shop whose only signage was the price labels on the shelves, which is exactly what a conference poster made of abstract, methods, results and references is.
What Morrison actually redistributed The Better Poster template is usually described as a layout, and its load-bearing idea is not the 25/50/25 split. It is that everything which traditionally filled a poster gets pushed to the edges so that a single plain-language sentence can occupy the middle half. Change the proportions and it still works. Put the abstract back in the centre and it does not.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the traditional academic poster fails at a task nobody states. Its layout, abstract then introduction then methods then results then discussion then references, is the structure of a paper, transplanted onto a wall and read by someone standing up in a noisy hall for ninety seconds. It was never designed for that situation; it inherited a structure designed for a different one.

The second is that the reader's decision to stop happens before any of the content matters. A poster session has fifty posters and a visitor will engage with perhaps six. Everything about which six is decided from two metres away, in the time it takes to walk past. A poster whose content is excellent and whose two-metre read is a title and a wall of grey has lost before its content was consulted.

The third is that this transfers directly to the business one-pager, which is the version most readers of this course will actually produce. A one-page summary circulated before a meeting is read at three depths too: the person who glances at it in the lift, the person who reads it properly, and the person who takes it into the meeting to argue from. Same problem, smaller sheet.

The fourth is that the constraints here are the hardest in the track and therefore the most instructive. Eight hundred words on a sheet a metre tall is very little, and the discipline of choosing them teaches more about what a piece of work is actually claiming than any other exercise in this course.

Step 3

The mental model

Three readers, three distances, three budgets.

Two consequences follow.

The first is that the finding has to be written before the poster is laid out, in twenty words or fewer, in language a non-specialist can parse. If it will not fit in twenty words, the analysis has not finished, which is the same discipline as the exhibit title in lecture 16 and the action title in lecture 21, now applied with a much harder limit.

The second is that the traditional structure is not merely suboptimal, it inverts the priority. Abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion puts the finding fourth out of five, in the same type as everything else, at the point where a passer-by has already gone. The Better Poster's contribution is to notice that the paper's order and the poster's order are different problems.

The billboard
The two-metre read: title, authors, and the finding as one plain sentence. Morrison's term, and the element his template gives the middle half of the sheet to.
Word budget
A hard ceiling on total words, treated as a design token rather than an outcome. 800 for a poster, 500 preferred; 400 for an A4 one-pager, 300 preferred. Enforced before layout, not discovered after.
Whitespace budget
25 to 40 per cent of the sheet, reserved before anything is placed. Below 25 per cent a sheet reads as cramped regardless of what is on it, and above 40 it reads as under-filled.

Design the body for one metre and the title for two. Those are the two distances that decide anything.

Two rules of thumb circulate widely and should not be used: text height in centimetres equals viewing distance in metres times 0.84, and point size equals viewing distance in inches times 0.75. They disagree with the table and with each other, by large factors, and neither appears to have a traceable empirical basis. The table above is practitioner consensus drawn from institutional poster guidance, which is a weaker warrant than a measurement and a considerably stronger one than a formula nobody can source.

Resolution falls as the sheet grows, which looks wrong and is correct: viewing distance grows with the sheet, and at two metres 150 DPI is more resolution than the eye resolves. Print files sized for A0 at 300 DPI are enormous, slow to handle and no better.

The type scale does not scale proportionally between sheet sizes. A1 body is not 71 per cent of A0 body; it is re-derived from the 1.5m row of the legibility table, because viewing distance does not shrink in proportion to the paper.

The Better Poster layout

MORRISON'S THREE ZONES, ACROSS THE SHEET | 25% | 50% | 25% | |--------|------------------------|--------| | intro | TITLE | results| | method | | detail | | | THE FINDING | | | | in one plain sentence | | | | at 100 to 125pt | refs | | | | | | | one key graphic | [QR] | THE PROPORTIONS ARE THE TEMPLATE'S DEFAULT, NOT A LAW. Adapt them. What must not change is that the finding occupies the centre and the middle half. THE QR CODE 100 to 125mm square at A0, scans from about a metre. Always labelled with what it links to; an unlabelled QR code is scanned by nobody. It is the mechanism that lets the poster stop trying to be the paper. WHERE THE VENUE FORBIDS IT Use three or four conventional columns, and put a full-width band under the title carrying the finding as a sentence. That band alone recovers most of the benefit and violates no rules.

Charts on a large sheet

The most common poster failure after word count is a chart exported at report settings and enlarged. At A0 that produces hairline strokes and 6pt axis labels sitting on a sheet whose body text is 28pt, and the mismatch is visible from across the room.

Stroke weight
2 to 3mm at A0, not the 0.5pt a report chart exports. Every line, axis and rule must be re-weighted for the sheet.
Label size
Axis and data labels at the body size for the sheet, so 28pt at A0. If they will not fit, the chart has too many categories, not labels that are too big.
No legend
Same rule as the presentation module and for the same reason with more force: at poster scale the physical distance between key and marks can be thirty centimetres.
One chart, usually
A poster with one excellent chart supporting the finding beats one with five. The extra four belong behind the QR code, where they can be looked at properly.

Proofing

BEFORE SENDING TO PRINT [ ] Print an A4 crop at 100 per cent scale. Not the whole poster shrunk to A4, which tells you nothing. A section, at final size. [ ] Read it at the design distance, scaled. Pin the A4 crop up and stand back proportionally: an A4 crop of an A0 poster read at 2m is read at 2m. [ ] Check the finding sentence from across the room. If you cannot read it, nothing else matters. [ ] Convert one chart to greyscale and check it survives. [ ] Confirm bleed and safe margins with the printer. Large-format printers vary and 5mm is a convention, not a guarantee. [ ] Check the QR code with an actual phone, from a metre.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Why the Better Poster spread and what that tells you

Morrison published a video and a template in 2019 and academics adopted it at a speed almost no design intervention achieves. That is worth examining, because the reason is not that the layout is beautiful.

It spread because it solved a problem everyone had and nobody had named. Poster sessions are widely experienced as unrewarding by both presenters and visitors, and the shared explanation was that posters are a bad format. Morrison's contribution was to identify that the format was fine and the content model was wrong: posters were being built as compressed papers rather than as invitations to a conversation.

The second reason is that it came with a template. A design argument with a downloadable implementation is adopted; the same argument as an essay is admired. This is the same point the reports and presentations modules made about templates and themes, and the Better Poster is probably the clearest natural experiment in this course: the idea and the artefact were released together and the artefact is what travelled.

The honest caveat is that the specific proportions have no evidential support. Whether 25/50/25 beats 30/40/30 is untested and probably does not matter. What has been tested, informally and at very large scale, is the principle underneath, and it survived.

The constraint teaches more than the layout

Eight hundred words on a sheet a metre tall is the tightest budget in this track and the exercise of meeting it is more valuable than the poster.

Deciding which eight hundred words forces a question that a paper never forces: what is the one thing this work established? Papers can carry three findings and a methodological contribution and some interesting negative results. A poster cannot, and choosing which one goes in the middle is an act of judgement about your own work that most researchers and most analysts never have to perform.

This is why the poster module belongs in a track aimed at people who mostly will not make posters. The one-pager is the same exercise on a smaller sheet, and the ability to say what a body of work claims in twenty words is the transferable skill. It also, reliably, exposes work that has no single claim, which is a useful thing to discover before a committee discovers it.

An admission about the evidence base

This is the weakest-evidenced lecture in the module and it should say so plainly.

The legibility table is practitioner consensus. The three-depth model is a widely repeated heuristic with no measurement behind the specific times or distances. The word budgets are conventions. Morrison's proportions are a template default. Compared with lecture 10's perceptual ranking or lecture 19's effect sizes, almost nothing here is measured.

Two things follow. Treat the numbers as starting points and proof at size, which step 4 makes a required step rather than a suggestion. And notice that the underlying principles do have support even where the numbers do not: the three-depth model is the hierarchy argument from lecture 9, the word budget is the coherence principle from lecture 19, and the legibility question is ultimately about visual acuity, which is well characterised even if these particular tables are not derived from it.

Common failure Adopting the Better Poster layout and filling the sidebars with the entire paper at 14pt. The template then reads as a poster with a large heading, and the sidebars are unreadable at any distance a visitor will stand. The layout is not a container for the same content rearranged; it works only if the content was cut to the budget first.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a piece of work you have done and write its finding in twenty words, in language someone outside your field could parse. Then write the thirty-second version in 150 words. Then count the words in the last poster or one-pager you produced. Most people find the twenty-word version is the hard part and that they have never written one.
  2. Name the three depths with their distances, times and word budgets, and state the trap in how the budgets combine.
  3. Why does the traditional academic poster structure fail? Answer in terms of where the finding sits.
  4. What is the load-bearing idea in the Better Poster template, as distinct from its proportions?
  5. Why does print resolution fall as sheet size rises?
  6. Why is A1 body type not simply 71 per cent of A0 body type?
  7. Two rules of thumb for type size against distance are in circulation. What is wrong with them, and what should you use instead?
  8. Give the four chart overrides needed at A0 and say which one has more force here than in a presentation.
  9. Why is proofing an A4 crop at 100 per cent more useful than viewing the whole poster shrunk to A4?
  10. Step 5 says this is the weakest-evidenced lecture in the module. Say what is unevidenced, and explain why the underlying principles are on firmer ground than the numbers.
