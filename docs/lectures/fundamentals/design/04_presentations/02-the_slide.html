The slide as a designed object

A fixed canvas, a reader several metres away, and a projector that cannot produce black. Three constraints that change almost every number you would use on paper.

Lecture 20 of 28 Reading time, 30 minutes Sources: Duarte (2008); Reynolds (2008); consulting deck practice
Step 1

The 30-second version

The canvas is 16:9, 13.333 by 7.5 inches, designed at 1280 by 720 and exported at twice that. Everything sits inside a safe margin of about 0.6 inches, because projectors crop and video calls letterbox. The canvas divides into three zones: a title band at the top, a content region, and a footer strip for the source line.

The type scale changes more than anything else. Body text starts at 24pt for a projected deck, against the 11pt of a report, and 18pt is the absolute floor reserved for a source line that only matters to someone who walks up afterwards. The specification carries two columns, projected and shared, because a conference room and a screen-share on a call are genuinely different problems.

Seven layouts cover everything: title, section divider, content, two-column, chart, full-bleed and closing. A deck that needs an eighth usually needs an editor. And every element snaps to a twelve-column grid, because misalignment on a two-metre projected surface reads as carelessness at a scale paper never achieves.

Feynman test Road signs are enormous and say almost nothing. Not because sign designers lack ambition, but because you are reading at seventy miles an hour from two hundred feet. A slide is a road sign. The size of the type is set by the distance, and the amount of content is set by the size of the type. Those are not two decisions.
The failure that produces every other failure Content does not fit, so the type gets smaller. It fits, the slide ships, and the back three rows cannot read it. The correct response to a slide that does not fit at 24pt is to split it or cut it, never to shrink it. Everything in this lecture assumes you have accepted that, and almost nobody has.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the defaults are calibrated for the wrong room. PowerPoint's default body text sits around 18 to 20pt and its chart exports produce axis labels near 10pt, both of which are perfectly readable on the laptop where the deck was built and illegible from the fourth row. The author never experiences the failure, which is why it persists.

The second is that a projected surface amplifies everything. A misalignment of three pixels on a 1280-wide canvas becomes about half a centimetre on a two-metre screen. A contrast ratio that is adequate on a monitor collapses under ambient light. Errors that would be invisible on paper are conspicuous, and the audience registers them as carelessness even when they cannot name what is wrong.

The third is that a deck is built under time pressure, usually the night before, and the only thing that survives that is structure decided in advance. A theme with seven layout masters means the deck assembles itself; a blank deck means fifty individual layout decisions at eleven at night. This is the template argument from the reports module, and it bites harder here because the deadline is sharper.

The fourth is that the previous lecture's evidence only translates into practice through these specifications. "Reduce cognitive load" is not actionable. "24pt floor, 75 words, one idea, direct labels, grey everything but the subject" is, and each of those is the coherence and redundancy findings turned into a number you can check.

Step 3

The mental model

The canvas, its zones, and what goes in each.

Two consequences follow.

The first is that the type scale sets the content budget rather than the other way round. At 24pt on a 1280-wide canvas, roughly 75 words fill the content zone comfortably. That is where Duarte's limit comes from: it is not an independent rule about attention, it is what the geometry permits once the type is large enough to read. This is worth knowing because it means the limit cannot be negotiated by finding a cleverer layout.

The second is that the two type columns in the specification, projected and shared, are a genuine fork rather than a range. A conference room with a back row at six metres and a screen-share where everyone is at laptop distance have different floors, and a deck built for one is wrong for the other. Decide which before you start, and if a deck must do both, build for projection and accept that it is sparse on a call.

Safe margin
The border inside the canvas that no content crosses, 0.6 inches here. Protects against projector cropping, letterboxing and screens smaller than the projected image. Full-bleed images are the single deliberate exception.
Layout master
A reusable slide template defining placeholder positions and their styles. The presentation equivalent of a Word style: change the master and every slide using it updates. Building slides without them is direct formatting by another name.
De-emphasis
Setting every element except the subject to a muted grey, so the subject is the only coloured thing on the slide. The most useful single technique in this module: it directs attention more reliably than an arrow, needs no annotation, and survives being photographed.

Two typefaces maximum and four colours maximum. Both caps are lower than a document's because projection compresses: subtle distinctions that read on paper disappear in a lit room, so every distinction on a slide has to be a large one, and you can only afford a few large ones.

The seven layouts

Charts at distance

A chart exported at report settings and dropped onto a slide is the most common single failure in professional decks, and it is invisible on the laptop where it was made. Four overrides are needed.

Label sizes
Axis and tick labels at 18pt minimum. A default chart export produces something near 10pt, which is below the absolute floor for anything on a slide and unreadable past the second row.
Line weight
3px at 1x, up from the 1.5px that suits a report. Thin strokes are the first thing a projector loses, and a hairline gridline disappears entirely, which is convenient because it should not be there anyway.
No legend
A hard rule here rather than a preference. At projection distance the eye's journey from the key to the marks is long enough that the reader loses the mapping on the way. Label series directly at the end of a line or inside a bar.
De-emphasis
Grey every series except the one under discussion. On a slide with a speaker this is doubly valuable: it directs attention without an annotation, and it means the same chart can appear on three consecutive slides with a different series highlighted each time, which is the cleanest way to walk a room through a comparison.

Tables on slides

Five columns and eight rows, maximum. Type at the small-body size with headers at label size, horizontal rules only, and the cells that carry the finding given a tint. A table where everything is equally prominent communicates nothing at reading distance, let alone across a room.

A table that will not fit those limits belongs in the appendix, with the two or three numbers that matter lifted onto the main slide. This is not a compromise: a twenty-row table on a projected slide is not being read by anybody, so the choice is between showing the numbers that matter and showing none of them.

Colour under projection

A projector cannot produce black. It produces the absence of light, which in a lit room is mid-grey, so the dark end of the palette compresses upward and contrast collapses from the bottom. A palette measuring 4.5:1 on a monitor may deliver closer to 2:1 on a screen with the lights on.

Three consequences. Never light text on a mid-tone background. Never hairline weights. And treat the contrast minima as floors to exceed rather than targets, which is why the specification sets slide text at near-black rather than at a comfortable dark grey.

Animation

One legitimate use: the build, where a complex slide is revealed in stages so the audience is looking at the part being discussed. That is the segmenting principle from lecture 19 implemented, and it has evidence behind it.

Everything else is decoration and falls under coherence, which has an effect size of 0.86 pointing the other way. Flying text, spinning transitions and dissolves between slides add nothing and cost attention. If a transition is used at all, use one, the same one throughout, at 150 to 200ms.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Build the theme, not the deck

The same argument as the Word template and the report style sheet, with one difference that makes it more urgent: decks are produced faster and by more people. A team of six analysts producing decks weekly will, without a theme, produce six visual dialects, and the drift is invisible until someone assembles a pack from all of them.

A theme in PowerPoint or Google Slides is a real mechanism, not a metaphor. It carries the type scale, the colour palette, and the layout masters with their placeholder positions. A slide built from a master inherits its geometry, so the title is always in the same place and the content region always starts at the same height, which is what produces the calm of a well-made deck. Slides built by dragging text boxes onto blank canvases cannot achieve that, however carefully each one is placed.

The practical instruction is to spend the first hour of any recurring deck series building the master set rather than the first slide. Seven layouts, the type scale applied to the placeholders, the palette in the theme colours, the chart defaults overridden. It is an hour, it is recovered in the first deck, and it is the only mechanism by which the specification actually reaches the work.

Why "make it bigger" is the wrong instruction

The single most common piece of feedback on a deck is that the text is too small, and acting on it directly makes the deck worse. Enlarging text on a full slide produces overflow, which produces a smaller line height, then a narrower margin, then a second column, and the slide ends up denser than it started.

The type size is not an independent variable. It is set by the room, and once it is set the content budget follows from it. So the correct response to "too small" is not to change the size but to reduce the content until it fits at the size the room requires. That is a harder conversation because it means deciding what to drop, which is exactly why the easier response is the popular one.

There is a diagnostic in this. If you find yourself adjusting type size to make content fit, you are letting the content set the size, which means the room is not a constraint in your process. Fix the size first, treat it as immovable, and let it do the editing.

Test on the hardware, or accept the risk knowingly

Everything about colour and contrast in step 4 is an inference from how projectors work rather than a measurement of your projector. The honest position is that you cannot know how a deck will look in a room until you have seen it in that room.

For a deck that matters, get into the room and look at it from the back. It takes ten minutes and it catches the two failures that no amount of specification prevents: contrast collapse under the actual lighting, and type that is technically 24pt on a screen that turns out to be smaller than expected. For a deck that does not matter, apply the specification and accept the residual risk, which the specification is designed to minimise.

Common failure Building the deck at full-screen on a large monitor and never viewing it at thumbnail size. The slide sorter view is the closest thing to how the deck will feel to an audience seeing each slide for a few seconds, and it exposes both inconsistency between slides and slides with no clear entry point. Look at the sorter before you present, every time.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a deck you presented. Set every body text element to 24pt without changing anything else, and note which slides now overflow. Those are the slides that were never readable from the back. Fix three of them by cutting rather than resizing, and record what you removed and whether the slide lost anything.
  2. Give the canvas dimensions in inches and in pixels, and say why the safe margin exists.
  3. Explain why the 75-word limit is a consequence of the type scale rather than an independent rule about attention.
  4. Why does the specification carry two type scales rather than one range? What should you do if a deck must serve both contexts?
  5. Name the seven layouts and say what the appendix is for.
  6. Give the four overrides a chart needs on a slide, and say which one is a hard rule here but only a preference in a report.
  7. Describe the de-emphasis technique and give the specific presentation advantage it has over an annotation.
  8. Explain in physical terms why a projector collapses contrast, and give the three design consequences.
  9. Which single use of animation is defensible, and which lecture-19 principle supports it?
  10. A reviewer says the text is too small. Explain why acting on that directly makes the deck worse, and give the correct response.
