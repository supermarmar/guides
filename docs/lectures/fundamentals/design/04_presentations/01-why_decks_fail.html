Why most decks fail

There is more advice about slides than about anything else in this track and almost none of it is tested. There is also a body of experimental work with measured effect sizes, and it says something most professional decks are built to contradict.

Lecture 19 of 28 Reading time, 35 minutes Sources: Mayer (2001 onwards); Tufte (2003); Duarte (2008)
Step 1

The 30-second version

A slide deck has a speaker attached, which makes it the only medium in this track where the artefact carries part of a message rather than all of it. Richard Mayer's multimedia learning research measured what happens when that division goes wrong, and three of its largest findings all point the same way. Coherence: removing extraneous material improves learning. Redundancy: the same words as both on-screen text and narration is worse than either alone. Modality: spoken words beat printed words when a graphic is present.

Put together they say something blunt. A slide displaying the sentences you are about to speak is not neutral and not merely inefficient. It is worse than a blank slide, because the audience cannot read and listen at the same time and will do neither well. That is not a stylistic preference and it is the single most-violated finding in professional communication.

Edward Tufte goes further. His argument is that the medium is defective rather than misused: bullet hierarchies fragment reasoning, the resolution is too low to carry real evidence, and the format conceals uncertainty. His prescription is to write a technical report instead. This lecture takes the diagnosis seriously and declines the prescription, for reasons given in step 5.

Feynman test Try reading a paragraph while someone reads a different paragraph aloud to you. You cannot do both. Now try it where they read the same paragraph you are reading, slightly out of sync with your eyes. That is worse, and it is exactly what a slide full of your own script does to a room.
The objection this lecture has to answer "Our decks have to work after the meeting, when people read them without me." That is a real and reasonable requirement, and it is a requirement for a different artefact. A document that must carry its own narration is a document, and step 5 argues you should write one rather than degrading the deck into a bad version of both.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this is the only place in the course where the evidence is strong enough to overrule taste. Everywhere else the honest position has been that a convention is defensible or that authorities converge. Here there are controlled experiments with effect sizes, replicated across decades and across subject matter, and when experimental evidence contradicts your instinct about slide design, your instinct is the thing that should move.

The second is the stakes. Decks are where decisions get made in most organisations. A report is read by two people and a deck is seen by twenty, including the ones who decide. If the format systematically degrades comprehension, it degrades it at the point of maximum consequence.

The third is that the failure is invisible to the presenter. You know what your slide says because you wrote it, and you experience the room as attentive because they are looking at the screen. The mechanism the research describes, attention split between two channels carrying the same content, produces no visible symptom during the meeting. The symptom appears a week later when nobody remembers the second point.

The fourth reason is Tufte's, and it deserves its own weight. The Columbia Accident Investigation Board criticised NASA's practice of using briefing slides in place of technical papers, and the specific slide Tufte analyses had the critical information present, buried four levels down a bullet hierarchy in the smallest type on the page under a reassuring headline. Nobody lied. The format did the work. This module's ethics argument sits there.

Step 3

The mental model

The effect sizes, sorted. This chart is also a demonstration of lecture 10: sorted descending, direct labels, no gridlines, zero baseline, one colour used to mark a threshold rather than to decorate.

Two consequences follow.

The first is that the biggest single improvement available to almost any deck is deletion. Coherence at 0.86 says that removing extraneous material improves learning, and "extraneous" in the experiments included things that were interesting, attractive and topical. Not just clip art: relevant detail that was not needed for the point being made. Most decks fail on coherence before they fail on anything a design lecture would normally cover.

The second is that redundancy and modality together define the division of labour. The slide carries the visual; the speaker carries the words. When a graphic is on screen, the words that explain it should be spoken rather than printed. This is why the specification caps a slide at 75 words and why the layout set in lecture 20 is built around graphics with short labels rather than around text blocks.

Coherence principle
People learn better when extraneous material is excluded. The word "extraneous" covers decorative images, background music, tangential detail and interesting-but-unnecessary facts. Effect size around 0.86.
Redundancy principle
People learn better from graphics with narration than from graphics with narration and on-screen text saying the same thing. Effect size around 0.87. The finding that most contradicts professional practice.
Modality principle
People learn better from graphics with spoken words than from graphics with printed words. Effect size around 0.76. The mechanism is that visual and auditory processing draw on partly separate capacity, so splitting the load across both channels uses more of the available bandwidth than loading one.

Spatial contiguity deserves a note because it independently produces a rule this track already reached from a different direction. Labels belong next to what they label, which is the experimental basis for direct labelling over legends, arrived at through learning research rather than through Cleveland and McGill's perceptual work. Two literatures, one conclusion.

Tufte's critique, stated fairly

The Cognitive Style of PowerPoint is a pamphlet and a polemic, and its central claims are three.

Resolution. A slide holds perhaps forty words or a dozen numbers. A page of a technical report holds several hundred words and a table of a hundred numbers. Tufte's argument is that serious analysis needs the higher information density, and that a medium which forces material into forty-word chunks forces it to be simplified past the point of usefulness.

Bullet hierarchies. A nested list presents items as parallel and suppresses the relationships between them, which is the same argument Amazon makes about memos and lecture 18 covered. Tufte's version is sharper: the hierarchy imposes a structure that is usually not the structure of the reasoning, and reasoning that has been forced into it comes out damaged.

Concealment of uncertainty. A bullet stating a conclusion looks identical whether the underlying evidence is strong or marginal. A paragraph can hedge; a bullet cannot, or rather it can, and the hedge occupies as much visual space as the claim so the reader cannot tell which dominates.

The Columbia example is where these combine. Boeing's slide on debris impact carried a headline suggesting the existing test data was reassuring, with the crucial qualification, that the test had been conducted on a projectile several hundred times smaller than the one that actually struck the wing, present but four levels down and in the smallest type on the slide. The Accident Investigation Board's own report criticised the substitution of briefing slides for technical papers.

Where the argument is weaker than it sounds

Two objections are worth making, because Tufte's rhetoric tends to foreclose them.

The first is that the Columbia slide is evidence about a hierarchy failure, not about the medium. Everything Tufte identifies could have happened in a memo: a reassuring opening paragraph, the qualification in a footnote, the reader taking the headline. Lecture 9 treated exactly this as a hierarchy problem and gave a testable standard for it. The slide format made it easier, and "easier" is not "caused by".

The second is that his alternative assumes a reader who will read. A technical report is better than a deck for a reader who reads it. Much of the time the actual alternative is not a well-read report but an unread one, and a meeting where twelve people look at the same thing together and argue is doing something a document cannot do. Tufte's model of communication is one person transmitting analysis to another; a deck is frequently an instrument of collective decision-making, which is a different job.

What Duarte and Reynolds add

The three-second glance test (Duarte)
If the audience cannot grasp a slide's point in three seconds, they will spend the next thirty working it out instead of listening. An operational form of the coherence principle, and a test you can actually run on a colleague.
The 75-word ceiling (Duarte)
Beyond roughly 75 words a slide has become a document. Her framing is that at that point you should hand it out and stop projecting it, which is the same conclusion this lecture reaches in step 5 from the evidence side.
Restraint, simplicity, naturalness (Reynolds)
Presentation Zen's three principles, and the instruction that follows from them: subtract rather than add, and where a sentence could be a picture, use the picture. Aesthetically argued rather than evidenced, and pointing the same way as the coherence finding.
10-20-30 (Kawasaki, not Reynolds)
Ten slides, twenty minutes, thirty-point minimum font. Guy Kawasaki's rule, pitched at venture pitches, and very frequently misattributed to Garr Reynolds. The 30pt floor is stricter than this module's 24pt and is a defensible choice for a large room.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The read-alone deck

Here is the objection this module has to answer honestly, because in banking it is not an edge case. Decks are circulated. They go to people who were not in the room, they are filed, and six months later someone reads the slides with no speaker. A deck built to the redundancy principle is useless to that reader, because the words that carried the argument were spoken and are gone.

The tempting resolution is a compromise deck: enough text to stand alone, not so much that it competes. This does not work, and the reason is that the two requirements are not on a spectrum. A slide that carries the narration triggers the redundancy effect for the live audience whether or not it is a mild version. There is no setting at which it stops.

The resolution that does work is to accept that these are two artefacts. Duarte's term is a slidedoc: a document laid out on slide-shaped pages, denser than a deck and lighter than a report, designed to be read and never projected. Reynolds makes the same split three ways, into slides for the audience, notes for the speaker, and a handout carrying the detail. Tufte's version is the technical report.

All three are saying the same thing. The artefact that is projected and the artefact that is read are different objects, and the widespread practice of making one object do both jobs produces something that does neither. The organisational obstacle is real, because producing two things costs more than producing one, and the honest answer is that it costs more because it is two jobs.

The pragmatic version, if you cannot get two artefacts: build the deck properly and write a one-page summary that goes with it. Not speaker notes appended to the slides, which nobody reads, but a separate page that states the argument in prose. That is about twenty minutes of work and it serves the absent reader far better than a deck degraded into a document.

Taking the diagnosis without the prescription

Tufte is right that a deck cannot carry a serious analysis and wrong that this means you should not make one. The two claims come apart once you separate what a deck is for.

A deck is not a vehicle for transmitting analysis. It is an instrument for getting a room to a shared understanding and a decision, in a fixed period, with a person present who can be interrogated. For that job the low information density is a feature, because the constraint is the audience's attention rather than the page. The analysis lives in the report, and the deck's job is to make the room able to discuss it.

So the working position is: when the deliverable is the analysis, write the report and do not present it. When a room has to reach a decision together, build the deck, accept the constraints in this module, and make sure the report exists too. Most of the failures Tufte catalogues come from organisations that made the deck and never wrote the report, which is a governance failure that the deck then gets blamed for.

Common failure Reading this lecture and concluding that slides should have no text. Modality says prefer spoken words when a graphic is present; it does not say a slide must be wordless. Labels, the action title, axis text and a source line are all necessary and none of them is the narration. The thing to remove is the sentence you are about to say, not every word on the slide.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a deck you presented recently. For each slide, count the words and mark whether any of them duplicate something you said aloud. Then rebuild three slides so the visual is on screen and the sentences are in your notes. Present both versions to a colleague on different days and ask each time, a day later, what the three slides showed.
  2. State the redundancy principle and explain the mechanism, not just the prescription.
  3. Why does modality work? Give the account in terms of processing capacity.
  4. Coherence has an effect size around 0.86. What counted as "extraneous" in the experiments, and why is that broader than most people assume?
  5. Give Tufte's three central claims about the medium.
  6. Explain why the Columbia slide is arguably evidence about hierarchy rather than about PowerPoint. What would the memo version of the same failure look like?
  7. Give the second objection to Tufte's prescription, the one about what a deck is actually for.
  8. Why can a deck not be tuned to serve both a live audience and an absent reader? Explain why this is not a matter of finding the right balance.
  9. What is the pragmatic fallback when you cannot produce two artefacts, and why is it better than appending speaker notes?
  10. The 10-20-30 rule is routinely misattributed. Whose is it, and how does its font floor compare with this module's?
