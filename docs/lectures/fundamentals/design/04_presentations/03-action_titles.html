The action title

Write the takeaway as the headline, not the topic. Read the headlines alone, in order, and you should have the argument. It is a formatting convention on the surface and an analytical discipline underneath.

Lecture 21 of 28 Reading time, 30 minutes Sources: Minto (1987); Zelazny (1985); consulting deck practice
Step 1

The 30-second version

An action title is a complete sentence at the top of a slide stating what the slide shows. "Default rates rose 40 basis points in the second half" is one. "Default rate trends" is a topic label and is what most slides carry. The convention comes out of Barbara Minto's work at McKinsey in the 1960s and it is the consulting tradition's one genuinely transferable contribution.

The test that makes it operational is the ghost deck. Extract every slide title into a flat list and read it. If the list is an argument, the deck has one. If the list is a table of contents, the deck describes its own organisation and concludes nothing, and a reader who scans it, which is most of them, takes away nothing.

The reason this belongs in a design course rather than a writing one is that the title is a position on the page as much as a sentence. It occupies the top-left, which lecture 9 established as the strongest location available, at the largest type on the slide. Whatever goes there is what the slide claims, and putting a topic label in the strongest position means spending your best real estate on a filing category.

Feynman test A newspaper headline says "Bank raises rates to 5.5%", not "Interest rate news". The headline is the story, compressed, and you can read a whole front page and know what happened without reading an article. Slide titles can work the same way and almost never do.
Why it is uncomfortable A topic label commits you to nothing. A sentence commits you to a claim, and a claim can be wrong, disputed, or quoted back at you in six months. That is the real reason the convention is resisted, and it is also precisely the reason it improves the work: a deck of topic labels is frequently a deck whose author has not decided what they think, and the labels are what conceals that.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the title is the most-read element in the deck by a very wide margin. Everyone reads every title. A minority read the body of any given slide, and almost nobody reads all of them. Whatever fraction of your effort goes into titles, it is smaller than the fraction of your audience's attention they receive.

The second is that decks get skimmed after the meeting by people who were not there, and skimming a deck means reading the titles. This is the same problem the previous lecture handled by recommending a separate document, and the action title is the part of the solution that lives inside the deck: a deck with real titles degrades gracefully into something an absent reader can extract an argument from.

The third is that it makes the deck's logic auditable before it is presented. A ghost deck is a page of text you can read in ninety seconds, share with a colleague, and rearrange. Finding that slide 7 does not follow from slide 6 is trivial in the list and nearly impossible when you are clicking through fifty slides in presentation mode.

The fourth is the one step 5 develops. Writing the titles first, before building anything, forces the analytical decision to happen at the start rather than emerging from whatever charts turned out to be available. That reverses the usual order of deck production, and it is the reason the convention changes output quality rather than just appearance.

Step 3

The mental model

The same eight slides, titled two ways. Read each column top to bottom.

Two consequences follow.

The first is that the ghost deck is a working document, not just a test. Write the titles as a list before building anything, rearrange them until they follow, delete the ones that turn out not to be steps in the argument, and only then start making slides. Each remaining title is now a brief for one slide: whatever evidence supports that claim is what goes on it.

The second is that the exercise reliably finds slides with nothing in them. In the left column, "segment analysis" sounds necessary. In the right, its claim has to be stated, and if the honest statement is "segments differ somewhat", the slide is a chart that was available rather than a step in the argument. Deleting those is the largest single improvement available to most decks and the titling convention is what makes them visible.

Action title
A complete sentence at the head of a slide stating the slide's takeaway. Maximum roughly fifteen words and two lines. Never ends in a colon, because a colon promises a completion the slide body is not going to supply.
Ghost deck
The list of all slide titles, extracted and read in sequence. Both a test of whether the deck has an argument and the artefact you should write first.
Governing thought
Minto's term for the single claim a whole document supports. In a deck it is the title of the summary slide, and every other title should be a step toward it or a support for it.

Minto's pyramid, in the amount a deck needs

Barbara Minto's structure has two orthogonal parts, and knowing both is what lets you diagnose a deck that has good titles and still does not work.

Vertical logic is the relationship between a claim and the claims beneath it. Each level answers the question the level above provokes. If the summary says "we should recalibrate before Q4", the reader's next question is "why", and the slides below must answer exactly that. Vertical logic fails when a supporting slide answers a question nobody asked, which is the commonest structural fault in a deck built from available material.

Horizontal logic is the relationship between claims at the same level. They are either deductive, where each follows from the last and the order cannot change, or inductive, where they are parallel members of a single category and the order is arrangeable. Mixing the two within one level is what produces a deck that feels like it is going somewhere and never arrives.

MECE, mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive, applies to the inductive case. If the three drivers of the increase overlap, the reader cannot add them up. If they omit a fourth, the reader who knows about it stops trusting the rest. Both failures are visible in the ghost deck and invisible in the slides.

SCQA, for the opening

ElementWhat it doesExample
SituationSomething the audience already acceptsThe SME PD model has been in production since 2022
ComplicationWhat changed, or what is wrongIts last calibration predates the 2023 shock
QuestionThe question that follows, usually implicitIs it still fit for use?
AnswerYour governing thought. This is the summary slideNo. It underpredicts in the lowest two grades and should be recalibrated before Q4

The structure earns its place because it starts with agreement. Opening on something the audience already believes means the first thing you say is not contested, which buys you the standing to say the second thing. Opening on the complication, which is the instinct when you are excited about a finding, means the audience is evaluating your claim before they have accepted the frame it sits in.

Zelazny: from message to chart

Gene Zelazny's framework in Say It With Charts maps the message onto the chart type, and it is the fastest way to choose because it starts where the action title leaves off. Once you have written the title, you have named the message, and the message determines the form.

Message typeSignalled byChart
Componentshare of, percentage of totalPie (five slices or fewer), stacked bar
Itemlarger than, ranks, compared withBar, dot plot
Time seriesrose, fell, trend, sinceLine, column
Frequency distributionconcentrated in, spread across, rangeHistogram, box plot
Correlationrelates to, increases with, drivesScatter, paired bar

Read the verbs in your action title and the chart type is usually settled. "The gap is concentrated in one sector" contains a distribution word and points at a histogram or a sorted bar. "Recalibration beats an overlay on cost" is an item comparison. This is a good deal faster than browsing a chart gallery and it guarantees the chart matches the claim, which browsing does not.

The rest of the slide

Below the title, three things and no more. The content, which is the evidence for the title's claim, usually a single chart or table. An optional callout, one per slide, pointing at the specific feature of the evidence the title refers to. And the source line.

The callout is the underused element. A title claims that discrimination falls in the lowest two grades; a callout on the chart marks the two points where it does. That pairing means the audience does not have to find the evidence for the claim, which is the last remaining act of work the slide can spare them. One callout only: two means the audience has to choose which the title refers to.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The title is the analysis

Almost everything in this course improves the presentation of work that already exists. This convention is the exception, and it is worth ending the module on, because it runs backwards.

You cannot write "the model underpredicts in the lowest two grades" unless you have established that it does, to a standard you are willing to put in the largest type on a slide with your name on it. Writing the ghost deck first therefore forces the analytical decisions to the front. What is the finding? Does the evidence support it at this strength? Is this step actually necessary to the argument?

Authors who adopt it consistently report the same experience: it changes what they analyse, not only how they present it. The mechanism is that the convention makes an absence visible. A slide labelled "sensitivity analysis" looks complete. The same slide with the title "we ran a sensitivity analysis" is obviously not a finding, and you are now looking at the question of what the analysis actually showed, which you may not have asked.

So the practical instruction is stronger than "write good titles". Write the ghost deck before you open the deck tool, treat each title as a hypothesis, and let the ones you cannot support honestly either change or disappear. What survives is the deck, and the building of it is mechanical.

The question this module has been deferring

Lecture 19 argued that a deck and a read-alone document are different artefacts. Lecture 20 gave the constraints of the deck. This lecture has given the argument structure. The question left is when you should be making a deck at all, and it deserves a direct answer rather than a shrug.

DECK OR REPORT? WRITE A REPORT WHEN the deliverable is the analysis itself the reader needs to check your working the material will be referred back to it must satisfy a reviewer, auditor or supervisor the reasoning is a chain that cannot be sampled there is no meeting BUILD A DECK WHEN a group must reach a shared understanding together a decision is being taken in a fixed period you will be present and can be interrogated the audience needs the argument, not the evidence DO BOTH WHEN a committee decides in a meeting and the decision has to be defensible afterwards -> which, in regulated work, is most of the time THE FAILURE TO AVOID Making the deck, presenting it, and never writing the report. Most of what Tufte catalogues as PowerPoint's crimes are cases of exactly this. The medium then gets blamed for a governance gap.

The last row is the important one. Tufte's Columbia analysis is frequently read as an argument that slides are dangerous. A closer reading is that NASA had substituted slides for technical papers, which the Accident Investigation Board said explicitly, and the slide was doing a job it was never capable of. The deck was not the problem. The missing report was.

The limits of the convention

Two honest caveats, because the consulting tradition tends to present this as universally correct.

It suits argument and fits exploration badly. A deck presenting a finding benefits enormously. A deck opening a genuinely undecided question, where the purpose is to think with the room rather than to persuade it, is distorted by titles that assert conclusions, and forcing them produces false confidence. In that case, honest topic labels with an explicit statement that the deck is exploratory is the better choice, and it is rarer than people claim when they are avoiding the discipline.

And it can be gamed. A deck of confident sentence titles supported by weak evidence is more persuasive than the same evidence with honest labels, which means the convention increases the rhetorical force of the presenter regardless of whether they are right. That is a real cost, and the only defence is the one lecture 9 offered: material that would change the decision belongs at the level of the finding it qualifies. A deck whose titles are all confident and whose caveats are all on slide 23 has used the convention against its own reader.

Common failure Writing action titles at the end, as a formatting pass over a finished deck. It produces sentences, and it produces them by describing whatever each slide happens to contain, which means the deck's structure is still whatever the available charts dictated. The value is in writing them first. Retrofitted titles get you the appearance of the discipline and none of the benefit.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a deck you built and extract every title into a flat list. Read it. Then rewrite each as an action title, and note which slides you cannot write one for. For each of those decide whether it had a finding you failed to state or no finding at all, and delete the second kind. Count how many slides survive.
  2. Why is the action title a design question rather than only a writing question? Answer in terms of position.
  3. State the ghost deck test and say what it is testing for.
  4. Give the five failure modes in step 4 and an example of each from your own work.
  5. Distinguish vertical from horizontal logic in Minto's structure, and give the failure that each produces when it breaks.
  6. What does MECE guard against? Name the two distinct failures.
  7. Why does SCQA open on the situation rather than the complication?
  8. Your action title says "the gap is concentrated in one sector". Which of Zelazny's message types is this, and which chart follows?
  9. Give the decision rule for deck against report, and state the failure mode that Tufte's Columbia analysis is really evidence of.
  10. Step 5 says the convention can be gamed. Explain how, and give the defence.
