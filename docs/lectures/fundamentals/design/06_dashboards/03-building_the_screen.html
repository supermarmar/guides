Building the screen

A number without a comparison is trivia. A gauge is an angle encoding occupying six times the space it needs. And a dashboard that does not say when it was refreshed is an assertion with no provenance.

Lecture 26 of 28 Reading time, 30 minutes Sources: Few (2006); IBCS; Carbon and Datawrapper guidance
Step 1

The 30-second version

Four things do most of the work on a built dashboard. The KPI tile must carry a comparison, because a bare number tells the reader nothing about whether it is good. The bullet graph replaces the gauge, converting an angle encoding into a position one and using a fraction of the space. Gridlines are the largest source of unnecessary ink on most screens and should be absent by default. And number formatting must be consistent per measure across the whole dashboard, which almost no dashboard achieves.

Underneath these sits the thing nobody builds: provenance. A tile shows 1,247. Filtered to one region? Computed over a partial extract because the overnight load failed? Defined the same way as last quarter? The screen usually says none of this, and somebody acts on the number anyway. Step 5 argues this is the most consequential unsolved problem in dashboard design.

The dark variant, if you build one, is a re-pick rather than an inversion. A colour verified at 6:1 on white will typically fail on near-black, and saturated colours read brighter on a dark ground and need desaturating. Every accent and semantic value is chosen again from scratch.

Feynman test A thermometer reading 38 degrees means something because you know 37 is normal. Take away everything you know about human body temperature and the number is just a number. Most dashboard tiles are that number with the shared knowledge removed, and the reader is expected to supply it from memory every morning.
The cheapest large improvement Add a comparison line to every KPI tile: against target, against prior period, or against a peer. It costs one line of text and converts a display the reader must interpret into one they can read. Few classifies missing context as one of his thirteen mistakes and it is by some distance the most common.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is repetition. The KPI tile is the most-reused component in the medium: a dashboard might carry six, and an organisation's dashboards might carry hundreds, all built from whatever the first one looked like. Getting the component right once propagates further than getting any individual screen right.

The second is that number formatting inconsistency is both trivial to fix and genuinely damaging. When the same measure appears as 12%, 12.3% and 0.123 across three tiles, a reader cannot tell whether they are the same measure. Worse, they start checking, which is precisely the work the dashboard existed to remove.

The third is staleness, which is unique to this medium. Every other artefact in this track is fixed at the moment it ships. A dashboard is a window onto a pipeline, and the pipeline fails, lags, and changes definition. A report that is six weeks old announces this by being dated. A dashboard that is six hours stale looks exactly like one that is current.

The fourth is that these are the decisions most often left to the tool. Gridlines, axis ranges, number formats and legend positions all have defaults, and the defaults are applied uniformly to data they know nothing about. Overriding them is configuration rather than design, which is why it gets skipped and why it is worth an explicit lecture.

Step 3

The mental model

Two components, exploded. Between them they cover most of what a dashboard is made of.

Two consequences follow.

The first is that the comparison line is the component, not an addition to it. A KPI tile with a label, a value and a sparkline is still missing the thing that makes the value legible, which is what it should be compared against. If you cannot state a target, a prior period or a peer, that is a signal the measure has not been thought through rather than a reason to omit the line.

The second is that the bullet graph demonstrates the discrete-encoding argument from lecture 25 in miniature. The qualitative bands turn "is this good?" from an estimation task into a classification task: the reader sees which band the bar has reached, exactly, with no judgement of magnitude required. The precise value is still available from the axis for anyone who wants it.

Comparison line
The line beneath a KPI value stating what it is measured against. Carries direction three ways: arrow, sign and colour. Two of the three survive greyscale and colour vision deficiency, satisfying WCAG 1.4.1 by construction.
Bullet graph
A horizontal bar showing a measure, over two or three greyscale qualitative bands, with a vertical marker for the target. Few's replacement for the gauge and dial.
Sparkline
A word-sized line chart with no axes, showing shape rather than value, placed beside the number it describes. Tufte's invention. Answers "is this moving?" in the space of a few characters.

The tile's external gap must exceed its internal padding, which is the nesting rule from lecture 8. At 16px padding the gutter between tiles must be more than 16px, or the enclosure and proximity cues fight and the tiles read as one continuous surface with lines drawn on it.

Gridlines are the largest source of unnecessary ink on most dashboards and the default in every tool. The test for whether one is needed: does the reader have to read a value off the axis? If the number matters that much, label the point directly and delete the gridline; if it does not, the gridline is serving nothing. In practice most charts on most dashboards need none.

The zero line is the exception and is always shown where the scale crosses it, because it is the only reference on the chart that means something absolutely rather than relatively.

Three rules govern all of these. Consistency beats precision: the same measure carries the same decimals everywhere on the dashboard, even where a particular value would look tidier with fewer. Never abbreviate inside a column where some values would abbreviate and others would not, because the column stops aligning and stops being comparable. And never write a date as 01/08/2026, which means two different days depending on where the reader is.

Tabular numerals throughout, without exception. A refreshing dashboard where the digits change width makes the whole tile jump on every update, which is distracting in a way nobody diagnoses correctly.

The dark variant

If you build one, build it. Do not invert.

WHY INVERSION FAILS 1 CONTRAST DOES NOT SURVIVE THE FLIP The house accent #5B47E0 measures 6.1:1 on white and is far too dark on #151514. Every accent and semantic colour must be re-picked and re-measured. 2 SATURATION READS BRIGHTER ON DARK The same saturated colour appears more intense against a dark ground. Chart series need desaturating by roughly 10 to 20 per cent, or the screen vibrates. 3 SEMANTIC COLOURS ARE THE HARDEST Red and green must lighten to stay legible, and lightening them naively pushes them toward pastel where they stop reading as red and green. Each is chosen by eye and then checked. 4 NEAR-BLACK, NOT BLACK #151514 rather than #000000. Pure black maximises halation and, on OLED, makes the tile edges disappear entirely. Provenance on the screen

Refresh timestamp
Absolute, top-right of the header. "Updated 14:32, 1 Aug 2026". Never "2 hours ago", because a reader investigating an event needs to know whether the data covers it.
Active filters
Always visible, as removable chips in the header. A dashboard showing a filtered subset without saying so is not a design flaw, it is a source of wrong decisions, and it is the most dangerous defect in the medium.
Stale state
When the last load failed or is late, the screen must say so prominently and not simply show yesterday's numbers as though they were today's. A banner, not a footnote.
Partial state
When a measure is computed over an incomplete population, mark the tile. A partial number that looks complete is worse than a missing one, because a missing number prompts a question and a wrong one does not.
Definition link
Every measure should be one click from its definition. "Accounts in arrears" means at least four different things in a typical bank, and the reader comparing two dashboards needs to know which.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

The unsolved problem

Every artefact in this track except this one is fixed at the moment it ships. A report is dated. A deck was presented on a day. A poster was printed. A dashboard is a window onto a living pipeline, and the pipeline degrades in ways the window does not show.

The failure modes are ordinary and none of them is visible on a well-designed screen. The overnight load fails and the dashboard shows yesterday. One source system is late and a measure is computed over 80 per cent of the population. A definition changes upstream and the series has a discontinuity nobody flagged. A filter is left applied from the previous session. In every case the numbers render perfectly and confidently.

This is a design problem and not only an engineering one, because the engineering fix, alerting the owner, does not help the reader looking at the screen right now. What helps the reader is the screen saying what it knows about itself, and almost no dashboard does. The token file's timestamp, filter chips and stale-state banner are a partial answer and they are genuinely partial: they cover the cases the pipeline knows about and not the ones it does not.

The comparison worth drawing is with the report module's source line. A report exhibit states its extract date, its population and its exclusions, and a reviewer can reconstruct it. A dashboard tile states a number. The report convention is four hundred years old and the dashboard has not yet acquired an equivalent, which is the clearest sign of how young the medium is.

Where Few's austerity needs qualifying

This module has followed Few closely and he is right about almost everything. Two qualifications are worth making.

His position was formed when the alternative to a dashboard was a printed report, and his single-screen rule is stricter than interactivity now warrants. A monitoring view that fits one screen with a well-marked drill-down one click away satisfies the spirit of the rule and does something his framing does not anticipate. The defensible version is that the monitoring view is single-screen and everything else is clearly elsewhere.

And his aesthetic austerity, like Tufte's, is a strong position supported by argument rather than experiment. Lecture 10 covered the finding that some embellishment improves recall without costing accuracy. On a dashboard, where the reader returns daily and fluency develops, the memorability argument is weaker than it is for a one-off chart, so Few's austerity is on firmer ground here than Tufte's is generally. But it is still a position, not a finding, and it should be held as one.

Where he is unambiguously right, and where the industry has comprehensively ignored him for two decades: gauges, decoration, missing context and colour used without meaning. Those four account for most of what is wrong with most dashboards, and none of them has a defence.

Common failure Building the light version, shipping, and adding a dark mode later by inverting. It produces a screen where the semantic red and green are indistinguishable from each other, the accent has vanished into the background, and the charts vibrate. Either build the dark palette deliberately at the same time as the light one, or do not offer dark mode. A bad dark mode is worse than none, because the reader who prefers dark will use it anyway.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a dashboard you use. For every KPI tile, check whether it carries a comparison; for every chart, count the gridlines and ask whether the reader reads values off the axis. Then find every instance of the same measure and check the decimals match. Most dashboards fail all three.
  2. Why is a bare number described as trivia rather than as incomplete?
  3. Give the three ways a comparison line carries direction and say why three rather than one.
  4. State four reasons a bullet graph beats a gauge.
  5. How does the bullet graph illustrate the discrete-encoding argument from lecture 25?
  6. Give the test for whether a gridline is needed, and say which gridline is always shown.
  7. Why must tabular numerals be used on a refreshing dashboard specifically?
  8. Give the four reasons a dark palette cannot be produced by inverting a light one.
  9. Name the five provenance elements and say which one is described as the most dangerous defect in the medium.
  10. Step 5 compares the dashboard with the report's source line. State the comparison and say what it suggests about the medium.
