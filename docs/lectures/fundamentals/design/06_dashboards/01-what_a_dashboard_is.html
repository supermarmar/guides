Lectures / Design / Dashboards

What a dashboard is for

Most dashboards are collections of charts that were available rather than instruments for a decision. The failure is invisible because every chart on them is correct.

Lecture 24 of 28 Reading time, 30 minutes Sources: Few (2006); Wexler, Shaffer & Cotgreave (2017)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Stephen Few's definition: a visual display of the most important information needed to achieve one or more objectives, consolidated on a single screen so it can be monitored at a glance. Every clause excludes something. "Single screen" excludes anything you scroll. "At a glance" excludes anything you have to study. "To achieve objectives" excludes anything nobody can name the purpose of, which is most of them.

The definition matters because it converts a vague ambition into a set of tests you can fail. Does it fit on one screen at 1440 by 900? Can a reader who knows the domain extract the state of things in five seconds? Can you name the decision it supports and the person who takes it? A display failing any of those is something else, possibly something useful, and calling it a dashboard imports expectations it will not meet.

The design method that follows is to work backwards from the decision. Who acts on this, what do they decide, what would change their mind, what measure carries that, and only then what chart. Almost every bad dashboard was built in the other direction, starting from what the warehouse contained.

Feynman test A car dashboard shows speed, fuel and a few warning lights. It does not show engine temperature history, tyre wear trends or a breakdown of fuel consumption by journey, all of which the car knows. Somebody decided what a driver needs while driving, and everything else went somewhere you look at deliberately. That decision is the design.
The uncomfortable diagnostic Ask, of any dashboard: what decision does this support, and when was it last made differently because of something on this screen? A surprising number of production dashboards have no answer. They are monitored, they are maintained, they are cited in status meetings, and nothing on them has changed an action in a year. That is a dashboard that has become a ritual.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the tools removed the friction that used to enforce discipline. Producing a chart used to be slow enough that you thought about whether you wanted it. Now a chart is three clicks, and the constraint that used to do the editing has gone. Nothing replaced it except judgement, which is exactly the thing under time pressure.

The second is that dashboards are consumed differently from every other medium here. A report is read once and a deck is presented once, but a dashboard is looked at repeatedly by the same person, which means its failures compound. A tile that takes four seconds longer to parse than it should costs four seconds every day for two years. It also means fluency develops, so a dashboard that is initially confusing can become usable to its regular audience while remaining impenetrable to everyone else, which conceals the defect.

The third is that dashboards carry decision weight without carrying evidence. A report shows the working; a dashboard shows a number. When the number is filtered, stale, defined differently from last quarter, or computed over a partial population, the screen usually does not say so, and someone acts on it. Lecture 26 handles this specifically, and the underlying point belongs here: a dashboard is an assertion with no visible provenance unless you build the provenance in.

The fourth is that this is the medium where the profession's own habits are weakest. There is a genuine literature on report typography going back centuries and on slide design going back decades. Dashboard design is about twenty years old, most practitioners learned it from tool documentation, and tool documentation teaches features rather than judgement.

Step 3

The mental model

Two directions of travel. Only one of them produces a dashboard.

Two consequences follow.

The first is that the hardest work happens before any chart exists and is not design work at all. Establishing what decision a screen supports means talking to whoever takes it, and they frequently cannot articulate it either. That conversation is the deliverable of the first week and skipping it is why the forwards direction is so popular.

The second is that the backwards direction supplies a deletion criterion, which the forwards direction cannot. If a tile does not bear on the named decision, it goes, regardless of how interesting it is or how much work it took. Without that criterion nothing is ever removed, and a dashboard's natural trajectory is to accumulate until it no longer fits on a screen, at which point it has stopped being a dashboard by definition.

At a glance
Few's criterion: the state of things is extractable in a few seconds by someone who knows the domain. Distinguishes a dashboard from an analytical tool, which is studied, and from a report, which is read.
Single screen
No scrolling. The working test is 1440 by 900, which is the laptop most people actually use. A dashboard that scrolls has a bottom half nobody sees, and the material below the fold is not deprioritised, it is absent.
Scenario
Wexler and colleagues' unit of design: a named audience, a named decision, and the questions they need answered. A dashboard serves one scenario. Two scenarios means two dashboards, however much data they share.
Step 4

The detail

Few's catalogue of failures

Few enumerates thirteen common dashboard mistakes. They group into four kinds, which is more useful than the list because the grouping tells you what to look for.

Failures of scope
Exceeding the boundaries of a single screen; fragmenting data so a comparison is split across tiles; including excessive detail that belongs in a drill-down. All are cases of not having deleted enough, and all follow from working forwards.
Failures of context
Displaying a measure with nothing to compare it against. A number without a target, a prior period or a peer is not information. This is the most common single defect and lecture 26 handles it in the KPI tile specification.
Failures of encoding
Choosing a display medium poorly, encoding quantities inaccurately, using meaningless variety for its own sake, and misusing colour. Lecture 25 is entirely about this group.
Failures of arrangement
Poor placement of what matters, ineffective highlighting, cluttering with useless decoration, and an unattractive display. Arrangement failures are hierarchy failures, and lecture 9's tools apply directly.

The one worth dwelling on is inadequate context, because it is the least visible. A tile reading "1,247" is complete-looking and useless: the reader cannot tell whether that is good, whether it moved, or whether it is close to a threshold. Adding "up 12.3 per cent on last month, against a target of 1,300" turns the same number into something actionable, and costs one line.

What a dashboard is not

ArtefactConsumed howConfused with a dashboard because
Analytical toolExplored, over minutes or hoursBuilt in the same software, looks similar
ReportRead once, linearly, in fullIncreasingly delivered on screen
Data catalogueSearched for a known itemAlso a screen of numbers
Status ritualDisplayed in a meeting, not acted onGenuinely was a dashboard once

Each of these is a legitimate thing to build and each has different design requirements. An analytical tool should be dense, offer filtering and drill-down, and reward exploration, all of which are wrong for a dashboard. A report should be complete and paginated. The failure is not building them; it is building one and calling it a dashboard, because then you inherit the single-screen and at-a-glance constraints without the purpose that justifies them.

The last row is the awkward one. Dashboards decay into rituals: built for a real decision, the decision changes or stops being taken, and the screen persists because it is on a wall and someone maintains it. Nothing on the display indicates this has happened. The only detection is the question in step 1, asked periodically and honestly.

The scenario brief

BEFORE BUILDING ANYTHING AUDIENCE Who looks at this? Name a role, ideally a person. "Senior management" is not an audience. DECISION What do they do differently depending on what they see? If the answer is "they are informed", stop. Being informed is not a decision and does not need a dashboard. CADENCE How often is the decision taken? Daily, weekly, quarterly? This sets the refresh rate and, more importantly, the time window the measures should span. THRESHOLD What value would change the decision? This is the single most useful question and almost nobody asks it. The answer tells you what the target line on every chart should be. QUESTIONS List the three to five questions the audience asks. Each becomes at most one tile. If you have twelve questions you have more than one scenario. WHAT IT IS NOT FOR Write this down. It is what you point at when someone asks for a fourteenth tile.

The threshold question does the most work and is the one most often skipped. A dashboard where every chart has a target line is a dashboard where the reader can see at a glance whether anything needs attention, which is the definition. A dashboard of unreferenced levels requires the reader to remember what good looks like, which is exactly the cognitive work the display was supposed to remove.

Reading order

The eye starts top-left in a left-to-right reading culture and moves in an F-shaped scan, which lecture 13 covered for web pages and which applies unchanged here. The most important measure goes top-left. Importance declines to the right and down. This is nearly free and is routinely ignored in favour of arranging tiles by whatever fits.

Filters and controls belong in a header strip, together, never scattered among the tiles. A control adjacent to a chart implies it filters that chart, which may not be true, and a reader who has misunderstood the scope of a filter is reading a subset while believing they see the whole.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Dashboards accumulate, and nothing in the process resists it

Every other medium here has a natural forcing function. A report gets finished and shipped. A deck gets presented and is over. A poster is printed. A dashboard is never finished, and each request to add something arrives individually, is individually reasonable, and is granted.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. A dashboard gaining one tile a quarter has doubled in three years, at which point it has certainly stopped fitting on a screen and has probably stopped being looked at. Nobody decided this and no single decision was wrong.

Two mechanisms actually help. The first is the "what it is not for" line in the scenario brief, written at the start, which converts each addition request from a favour into a scope change. The second is a standing rule that adding a tile requires removing one, which sounds arbitrary and works, because it forces the comparison that the incremental process never makes: is this new thing more useful than the least useful thing already here?

Neither is a design technique. Both are governance, and this is the medium where the distinction matters least, because a dashboard's design is continuously renegotiated in a way a report's is not.

The honest limits of "at a glance"

Few's definition is the sharpest available and it is worth noting where its sharpness costs something.

The single-screen constraint was formulated when the alternative was a printed report, and interactivity has genuinely changed what is possible. A screen with a well-designed drill-down can serve at-a-glance monitoring and support the follow-up question, and insisting that the second capability disqualifies it is pedantry. The defensible version of the rule is that the monitoring view fits on one screen, and everything else is one click away and clearly marked as elsewhere.

The "at a glance" criterion also carries an assumption about expertise that is worth surfacing. A domain expert extracts the state of a familiar dashboard in about two seconds; a newcomer cannot do it at all. Both are looking at the same screen. This means the criterion cannot be tested on yourself and is only meaningful relative to a named audience, which is another reason the scenario brief comes first.

Where Few is unambiguously right, and where the industry mostly ignores him, is on decoration and on gauges. Neither has any defence, both are ubiquitous, and lecture 26 replaces the second with something strictly better.

Common failure Building the dashboard the requester asked for rather than the one their decision needs. The request usually arrives as a list of metrics, because that is what the requester can articulate. Taking it literally produces a chart collection with a stakeholder's name on it. The scenario brief is the tool for converting a metric list into a decision, and the conversation is uncomfortable precisely because it asks someone to say what they actually do with the information.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a dashboard you use or maintain and complete the scenario brief for it retrospectively: audience, decision, cadence, threshold, questions. Note which fields you cannot fill. Then count the tiles that bear on the named decision and the tiles that do not.
  2. State Few's definition and say what each of its three clauses excludes.
  3. Why does working forwards from the data produce a chart collection even when every chart is correct?
  4. Which three questions in the backwards method are not about data at all, and why do they determine whether the thing is worth building?
  5. What deletion criterion does the backwards direction supply, and why can the forwards direction not supply one?
  6. Give the four groups of failure and name the one that is least visible. Why is it least visible?
  7. A tile reads "1,247". State what is missing and what the minimum fix is.
  8. Explain how a dashboard decays into a status ritual and why nothing on the display indicates it has happened.
  9. Give the two governance mechanisms that resist accumulation, and say why a design technique cannot do this job.
  10. Step 5 qualifies the single-screen rule. State the defensible version, and explain why "at a glance" cannot be tested on yourself.
