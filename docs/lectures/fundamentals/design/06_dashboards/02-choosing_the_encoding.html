Choosing the encoding

Name the relationship first, then apply the accuracy ranking within the candidates that can express it. Two steps, in that order, and most chart arguments dissolve.

Lecture 25 of 28 Reading time, 30 minutes Sources: Cleveland & McGill (1984); FT Visual Vocabulary; Few (2004)
Step 1

The 30-second version

Choosing a chart is two decisions and people conflate them. First, what relationship am I showing? The Financial Times' Visual Vocabulary gives nine families: deviation, correlation, ranking, distribution, change over time, magnitude, part-to-whole, spatial and flow. Naming the family eliminates most chart types in one step. Second, within that family, which encoding is most accurate? That is Cleveland and McGill, and it settles the remainder.

Done in that order the process is fast and the answers are defensible. Done in the other order, or done by browsing a chart gallery, you end up matching a picture you liked to data that does not suit it, which is how a dashboard acquires a donut chart.

Two long-running arguments get settled here rather than gestured at. Pie charts are acceptable under three stated conditions and not otherwise, which is a narrower prohibition than "never" and a more useful one. Truncated axes are dishonest on bars and often correct on lines, and the rule that covers both is one sentence: zero the axis when the mark's size encodes the value, not when its position does.

Feynman test If someone asks you to draw a map, your first question is what of. A street map, a tube map and a rainfall map are all correct and all useless in place of each other. Nobody starts by choosing a map style and then deciding what it shows, and yet that is exactly what choosing a chart from a gallery does.
The thing this lecture will argue that others do not Discrete encodings are systematically underrated. Counting is exact and estimating is not, so a display that lets the reader count beats one that asks them to judge a continuous magnitude, whenever the data permits. Isotype's repetition rule, IBCS's fill patterns and the bullet graph's qualitative bands are all the same move, and the reason it stays unfashionable is that discrete encodings look less sophisticated.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that a dashboard's encodings are consumed thousands of times. A chart in a report is read once by each reader; a tile on a dashboard is read every morning for two years. A choice that costs the reader half a second of extra decoding costs several hours over the life of the screen, and a choice that produces a systematic misreading produces it every single time.

The second is that encoding errors are the failure class most invisible to their author. A misaligned tile is visible. A pie chart where the reader cannot tell whether the second slice is larger than the third looks completely fine to the person who made it, because they know the numbers. This is the collision failure from lecture 1 in its most consequential form.

The third is that dashboards are where encoding decisions get made fastest and with least deliberation. A report chart gets reviewed; a dashboard tile gets dragged into place during a build. The tools default to encodings that are frequently wrong, and the default survives because nothing in the workflow asks the question.

The fourth is that the two-step method is genuinely quicker than the alternative, which matters more than being correct for whether anyone adopts it. Naming the relationship takes ten seconds and eliminates about forty of the fifty chart types your tool offers. Browsing the gallery takes several minutes and produces a worse answer.

Step 3

The mental model

The nine families. Name yours, and the candidates are the two or three in that cell.

Two consequences follow.

The first is that the family is determined by what you are claiming, not by what the data looks like. The same table of monthly exposures by segment supports a change-over-time chart, a part-to-whole chart, a ranking and a distribution, and which one is right depends entirely on the sentence you are trying to support. This is why the action-title discipline from lecture 21 precedes the chart choice rather than following it.

The second is that within a family the ranking arbitrates, and it usually arbitrates in favour of the boring option. Part-to-whole offers stacked bar, treemap, waffle and pie; the stacked bar puts the first segment on a common baseline and is the most accurate of the four. Magnitude offers column, bar, isotype and waffle; the column and bar win. The families are where the interesting variety lives and the ranking is what stops you using it.

Relationship family
The kind of claim a chart makes, independent of chart type. Nine of them in the FT's taxonomy. Naming it first is the step that makes chart selection fast.
Discrete encoding
An encoding the reader counts rather than estimates: repeated symbols, filled squares in a waffle, qualitative bands, distinct fill patterns. Exact by construction, at the cost of resolution.
Common baseline
A shared reference line from which lengths are measured. Its presence is what moves an encoding from rank 3 to rank 1. In a stacked bar only the bottom segment has one, which is the whole limitation of the form.
Step 4

The detail

The pie chart, settled

A pie encodes on angle (rank 4) and unavoidably on area (rank 5), has no common baseline, and degrades as slices multiply. It is acceptable when all three of the following hold.

A PIE CHART IS ACCEPTABLE WHEN 1 The question is coarse. "Roughly a half, or roughly a quarter?" is answerable from angle. "Is A larger than B, and by how much?" is not. 2 Part-to-whole is genuinely the point. The reader wants to know how the whole divides, not to compare the parts with each other. 3 There are no more than about five slices. Beyond that the small slices become indistinguishable and the labels stop fitting. OUTSIDE THOSE CONDITIONS, USE A BAR. Same question, position on a common scale, sortable, labels fit, and it scales to thirty categories. THE DONUT A donut removes the centre, which removes the angle, leaving only arc length and area. It is a pie with one of its two encodings deleted. There is no case in which a donut beats the pie it was made from, and the pie was already the weaker option.

"Never use pie charts" is worse advice than the above, for a practical reason: it is obviously too strong, so it gets dismissed along with the reasoning behind it. A stated set of conditions is defensible in a meeting and produces the right answer in the cases that matter.

The zero baseline, settled

ChartEncodes onZero the axis?Why
Bar, columnLengthAlwaysThe reader reads the ratio of lengths as the ratio of values. Truncate it and the encoding is false
LinePosition, slopeNot necessarilyThe reader reads the shape. Forcing zero on a series moving between 3.10 and 3.40 per cent flattens the signal
AreaLength, filledAlwaysThe fill makes it a length encoding, whatever the top edge suggests
ScatterPositionNoZeroing both axes usually pushes all points into one corner
Dot plotPositionNot necessarilySame reasoning as the line

The single rule: zero the axis when the mark's size encodes the value, not when its position does. Either way, label the range clearly. A truncated line chart is honest; a truncated line chart with the axis labels removed is not, and the difference is the label rather than the truncation.

This module takes a position on the disputed case. Line charts do not require a zero baseline, on the grounds that a line encodes change through slope and that forcing zero on a tightly ranged series destroys the only thing the chart exists to show. That is the majority practitioner position and it is not universal; some financial reporting conventions require zero throughout, and where a house convention exists it wins over this argument.

The case for discrete encodings

Three techniques already met in this track are the same move and it is worth naming the pattern.

Isotype repeats a symbol rather than scaling one, so the reader counts. IBCS encodes scenario as a fill pattern rather than a hue, so the reader identifies rather than distinguishes. A bullet graph puts qualitative bands behind the measure, so the reader classifies rather than estimates. In each case a continuous judgement has been replaced by a discrete one.

The reason this works is that counting and classifying are exact and estimating is not. Every entry in the Cleveland and McGill ranking is an estimation task with an associated error. A discrete encoding sidesteps the ranking entirely: there is no error in counting four symbols or in seeing that a bar has crossed into the third band.

The cost is resolution. Discrete encodings quantise, so they cannot express 34,000 against 36,000 if the unit is 10,000. That is a real limit and it is why the technique suits monitoring, where the question is "is anything wrong", far better than analysis, where the question is "by exactly how much".

The reason it stays unfashionable is not technical. Discrete encodings look simple, and simple reads as unsophisticated in a professional context where effort is expected to be visible. A dashboard of counted symbols and banded bars looks like less work than one of gradient-filled bubbles, and it is better. That is worth knowing about your own instincts as much as about your stakeholders'.

Encoding choices that recur on dashboards

Stacked bars
Fine for the total and for the bottom segment. Poor for everything above it, because those segments float on varying baselines and drop to rank 3. If the reader needs to compare the middle segment across categories, use small multiples or a line per segment.
Dual axes
Two series on different scales sharing a plot. The crossing point is an artefact of the scales chosen and readers interpret it as meaningful. Avoid; use two stacked panels sharing an x-axis, which is rank 2 and honest.
Gauges
Angle encoding, rank 4, occupying the space of about six bullet graphs. There is no situation in which a gauge is the best available choice. Lecture 26 gives the replacement.
Treemaps
Area, rank 5, and correctly chosen when the data is hierarchical and has many items, because nothing better shows hierarchy at that scale. Accept the imprecision and label the values. This is the case where a weak channel is the right answer.
Small multiples
Rank 2, scales to dozens of series where colour scales to about six, and turns comparison into scanning. The most underused technique on dashboards, largely because tools make it awkward.

The selection procedure

1 WRITE THE CLAIM One sentence. "Sub-prime deteriorated faster than prime after Q2." 2 NAME THE FAMILY Read the verbs. "Deteriorated faster after" is change over time. 3 LIST THE CANDIDATES IN THAT FAMILY Line, column, slope, area. 4 APPLY THE RANKING All four use position or length. Line and slope both work; area adds a fill that implies the space beneath means something. Column suits few periods, line suits many. 5 CHECK THE SPECIAL CASES Zero baseline? Line, so not required; label the range. Legend? No: label the two series directly. Colour? Grey both, then colour only sub-prime. Elapsed: under a minute, and every decision has a reason you can state.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

What the ranking is not telling you

Cleveland and McGill measured the accuracy of magnitude judgements between two encoded values. That is one thing a chart does and it is not the only one, and treating the ranking as a chart-type league table produces bad decisions.

A treemap is rank 5 and is right for a hierarchy of three hundred items, because the alternatives cannot show the hierarchy at all. A choropleth is rank 7 and is right when the question is geographic, because no better encoding preserves the geography. In both cases the ranking is correctly warning you that precise comparison will be poor, and the correct response is to accept that and label the values, not to abandon the chart.

The general form: the ranking tells you the price of an encoding, not whether to pay it. Sometimes the expensive channel is the only one that carries the structure you need to show. Know the price and pay it deliberately, which is a different thing from not knowing.

Tool defaults are a design decision someone else made

Every charting tool ships a palette, a set of gridline weights, a default legend position and a chart-type recommendation engine. None of these was chosen for your data, and several of them are actively wrong: categorical palettes applied to ordered data, gridlines as dark as the marks, legends on the right, and in several tools a chart-suggestion feature that optimises for variety rather than accuracy.

Replacing them is configuration rather than design, and it is the highest-leverage hour available on a dashboard project. A theme file in Power BI, a workbook default in Tableau, a matplotlib style sheet or a Plotly template: one file, applied everywhere, and every subsequent chart starts from the right place. Without it the specification in the token file is advice that each analyst individually fails to follow under deadline pressure.

This is the same argument as the Word template and the slide master, and it is the third time this track has made it, which is deliberate. The recurring lesson of the medium modules is that a design system only reaches the work through a mechanism, and the mechanism is always a default that someone configured once.

Common failure Applying the ranking and converting everything to bar charts. Bars are excellent and cannot show a distribution, a correlation, a flow or a geography. The ranking operates inside a family, after the family has been named, and using it to choose the family produces a dashboard that answers only magnitude questions because those are the ones bars are good at.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Imitate before you compose. Take a dashboard you use. For each chart, write the claim it supports in one sentence, name the family, and check whether the chart type is the most accurate option in that family. Note how many charts you cannot write a claim for; those are the ones to delete.
  2. State the two steps of the selection method and say why the order matters.
  3. Give the three conditions under which a pie chart is acceptable, and explain why a donut is worse than the pie it came from.
  4. Why is "never use pie charts" worse advice than a stated set of conditions?
  5. State the zero-baseline rule in one sentence covering both bars and lines, and say what must accompany a truncated axis either way.
  6. Name three techniques from this track that are all the same move, and state what that move is.
  7. Why are discrete encodings exact where continuous ones are not? What do they cost?
  8. Explain why only the bottom segment of a stacked bar is on rank 1, and what to do if the reader needs to compare a middle segment.
  9. A treemap is rank 5 and is sometimes right. Explain when, and state the general principle about what the ranking is telling you.
  10. Step 5 argues that replacing tool defaults is the highest-leverage hour on a dashboard project. Give the mechanism for a tool you use, and say what happens without it.
