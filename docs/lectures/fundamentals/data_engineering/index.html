Course companion

Data engineering

Twenty lectures on the layer beneath every model: how data gets generated, stored, ingested, transformed, and served, and what has to be true about it before a PD model or an IFRS 9 impairment run is defensible. The spine is the data engineering lifecycle of Reis and Housley, with SQL and Python as the working languages, DuckDB as the local engine, Spark as the distributed one, and the modern stack (Iceberg, dbt, Dagster, Kafka) treated as the industry default rather than a novelty. Written for the analyst who already models well and now has to own the pipeline that feeds the model.

The data engineering lifecycle

Data engineering is the discipline of moving data from where it is produced to where it can be used, at the required quality and on a schedule someone depends on. The canonical framing splits that job into five sequential stages and six concerns that cut across all of them. The five stages give the course its four content parts; the six cross-cutting concerns, the undercurrents, give it the fifth.

How to use this

The lectures are sequenced bottom up and each assumes the one before it. Part I settles what data is and where it sits. Part II is the long spine of the course, SQL and data modelling, because the single highest-return skill in this field is still writing correct SQL against a well-designed schema. Part III covers getting data in, from clean batch extracts to PDFs and event streams. Part IV covers transformation at scale and the modern lakehouse and dbt patterns that dominate industry practice. Part V covers the undercurrents, and is the part that separates a script that ran once from a production data asset. Status badges show what is built (built), planned, or a gap where the source note is a stub or absent. All twenty are written.

The running dataset Worked examples use the same credit risk loan dataset as the data analysis course, plus a small synthetic banking schema (customers, accounts, loans, arrears, collateral, payments) that runs locally in DuckDB. Synthetic throughout: no client data, ever.

Part I: foundations

What the field is, and what data is

  • builtLecture 01: What is data engineering? The lifecycle, the undercurrents, and the boundary with analytics and modelling
  • builtLecture 02: Data and file storage: structured, semi-structured and unstructured data; delimited text, JSON, BLOBs, and the columnar formats (Parquet, ORC, Avro)
  • builtLecture 03: Databases and query engines: OLTP versus OLAP, relational versus NoSQL, and the warehouse, lake, and lakehouse lineage

Part II: SQL and data modelling

SQL, in three passes

  • builtLecture 04: SQL I, definition: DDL, types, keys, constraints, indexes, and views
  • builtLecture 05: SQL II, querying: joins, aggregation, subqueries, common table expressions, and window functions
  • builtLecture 06: SQL III, manipulation: insert, update, delete, merge and upsert, transactions and the ACID guarantees

Designing the schema

  • builtLecture 07: Normalisation and relational design: unnormalised form through third normal form, functional dependencies, and entity-relationship diagrams as code (DBML)
  • builtLecture 08: Analytical modelling: Kimball star schemas, facts and dimensions, slowly changing dimensions, Inmon, Data Vault 2.0, and the medallion (bronze, silver, gold) convention
  • builtLecture 09: DuckDB: the in-process analytical engine, and why a laptop now handles what needed a cluster in 2015

Part III: ingestion

Getting data in

  • builtLecture 10: Batch ingestion patterns: full versus incremental loads, change data capture, watermarks, idempotency, late-arriving data, and backfills
  • builtLecture 11: Extraction from awkward sources: REST APIs, pagination and rate limits, spreadsheets, and structured extraction from PDFs with pdfplumber
  • builtLecture 12: Streaming and event-driven data: Kafka topics and partitions, Flink stateful processing, event time versus processing time, delivery semantics, and when streaming is genuinely warranted

Part IV: transformation at scale

Distributed compute and the lakehouse

  • builtLecture 13: Distributed processing with Spark: the three Vs, RDDs and lazy evaluation, DataFrames and Spark SQL, partitions, shuffles, broadcasts, and reading the query plan
  • builtLecture 14: Open table formats and the lakehouse: Iceberg and Delta Lake on top of Parquet, ACID on object storage, schema and partition evolution, time travel, and compaction
  • builtLecture 15: Analytics engineering with dbt: ELT rather than ETL, models and refs, tests and documentation, incremental materialisations, and treating SQL as software

Part V: the undercurrents

Quality, orchestration, and trust

  • builtLecture 16: Data quality and validation: the quality dimensions, schema enforcement with pandera, expectation suites, reconciliation to the general ledger, and quarantine over silent failure
  • builtLecture 17: Orchestration: Airflow task DAGs versus Dagster software-defined assets, scheduling, sensors, retries, dependency management, and why every task must be idempotent
  • builtLecture 18: Observability, lineage, and data contracts: freshness, volume, schema and distribution monitors; column-level lineage; catalogues; and the producer-consumer contract as an engineering artefact

Governance and delivery

  • builtLecture 19: Governance, security, and privacy: catalogues and role-based access control, PII classification and masking, retention, GDPR and POPIA obligations, and what BCBS 239 demands of risk data aggregation and lineage
  • builtLecture 20: DataOps and delivery: version control and CI for pipelines, containers, infrastructure as code, environment promotion, testing a pipeline, and engineering for cost as well as correctness

Prerequisites and adjacent courses

The course assumes working Python and no SQL. The software engineering track supplies the version control, testing, CI, and packaging habits that lectures 15 to 20 lean on heavily, and lecture 20 deliberately does not repeat them. The data analysis track sits immediately downstream: it consumes the typed, validated dataframes this course produces. Downstream of that, the machine learning track and the credit risk modelling material in application/banking depend on every undercurrent in Part V, since the data engineering stage of an A-IRB or IFRS 9 model build is exactly this course applied to one portfolio.

What is and is not covered

The course covers the design and operation of analytical data systems at the scale of a bank's risk function: source extracts, a modelled warehouse or lakehouse, transformation logic under version control, and the quality and governance apparatus that makes the output usable in a regulatory submission. It is vendor-aware but not vendor-specific: the patterns are taught once and illustrated in whichever engine makes them clearest, usually DuckDB locally and Spark for the distributed case, with Snowflake, BigQuery, and Databricks referenced as the platforms where these patterns are most often met in practice.

It does not teach cloud administration, Kubernetes, networking, or database internals as disciplines in their own right, and it does not attempt certification coverage for any single platform. Real-time machine learning serving, feature stores, and vector databases are mentioned where they intersect with ingestion and serving but are left to the machine learning track. The reading spine is Reis and Housley (2022) for the lifecycle, Kimball and Ross (2013) for dimensional modelling, Linstedt and Olschimke (2015) for Data Vault, Kleppmann (2017) for the distributed systems underneath, and BCBS 239 for the supervisory expectations that make all of this non-optional in a bank.
