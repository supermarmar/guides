Lectures / Data engineering

What is data engineering?

A model is a recipe for turning data into a number. Data engineering is the water utility: the reservoirs, the treatment works, the mains, and the meter that together mean the tap runs clean at eight in the morning without anyone thinking about it. This lecture defines the field, gives it a lifecycle with five stages and six undercurrents, and draws the boundary between the data engineer, the analyst, and the modeller.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Data engineering is the discipline of moving data from the systems that produce it to the people and models that use it, at a known quality, on a schedule someone depends on, in a way that can be explained afterwards. It is not analysis and it is not modelling. It is the supply chain underneath both.

Two analogies get used, and both are useful because they fail in different places. The first is plumbing: a data engineer delivers a vital resource without leaks or contamination, and builds the taps that let other people control the flow. The analogy captures the invisibility of good work. Nobody thanks the plumbing. The second is librarianship: a data engineer will not do your research for you, but will make sure the resource you need exists, is catalogued, and is findable. That analogy captures the part the plumbing metaphor misses, which is that data has meaning, and meaning has to be curated rather than merely conveyed. A pipe does not care what flows through it. A data platform that does not know whether default_flag means ninety days past due or written off is not a working platform, however clean its pipes.

The reason the field exists as a distinct role is economic. Between roughly 2005 and 2020 the cost of storing a byte and the cost of scanning it both fell by orders of magnitude, and the two became purchasable separately. Once storage is effectively free and compute is rented by the second, the binding constraint on an analytics function stops being "can we afford to keep this?" and becomes "do we know what we have, and can we trust it?". That question is a full-time job, and the people who do it are data engineers.

Feynman test A model is a recipe. Data engineering is the water utility. You can write a superb recipe and still poison the guests if the water is contaminated, and nobody at the table will blame the water, because water is the thing nobody looks at. The utility's job is reservoirs, treatment, mains, pressure, and a meter, so that the tap runs clean at eight in the morning and there is a record of what came out of it. When a PD model fails validation, the recipe is almost never the problem. The water is.
Why the role appeared when it did Before the mid-2000s the data engineer was a database administrator plus an ETL developer, and the work fitted inside the warehouse team because there was only one warehouse and one nightly batch. Three things broke that. Data volumes outgrew a single machine, so the work became distributed-systems work. Semi-structured data (JSON from applications, event logs, documents) stopped fitting the relational schema you had to declare up front. And the cloud separated storage from compute, so architecture became a continuous choice rather than a five-year procurement. Each change added engineering, and eventually the engineering was the job.
Step 2

Why this matters

Three arguments should be enough to take the discipline seriously, and the third is the one that makes it non-optional in a bank.

The defect cost curve, again

Software engineering has Boehm's curve: the cost of fixing a defect rises by roughly an order of magnitude for each lifecycle stage it survives. Data has the same shape and a steeper tail, because a data defect does not merely break something, it silently produces a plausible wrong answer. The commonly cited version is the 1-10-100 rule: it costs one unit to prevent a bad record at entry, ten to correct it later, and a hundred to live with the consequences of having used it. The units are folklore; the ordering is not.

The modelling effort is mostly data work

Every practitioner survey for the last decade has found the same thing: the majority of the elapsed time on an analytical project goes on acquiring, cleaning, joining, and reconciling data, not on fitting the model. This is usually reported as a complaint. It is better read as a statement about where the leverage is. If sixty to eighty per cent of a model build is data work, then a platform that halves the data work nearly doubles the modelling capacity of the team, and no improvement in model form comes close to that. The reason a credit risk function with good data infrastructure can rebuild a scorecard in six weeks and one without takes nine months is not modelling talent.

In a bank, the supervisor has an opinion

The 2007 to 2009 crisis produced a specific finding: several large banks could not tell their supervisors, quickly and consistently, what their aggregate exposure to a single counterparty was. The problem was not that the models were wrong. The problem was that the data could not be aggregated across systems, legal entities, and risk types fast enough to answer the question. The Basel Committee's response was BCBS 239, "Principles for effective risk data aggregation and risk reporting", published in January 2013 with fourteen principles. The first eleven apply to banks and cover governance, data architecture, accuracy and integrity, completeness, timeliness, and adaptability. In plain terms, BCBS 239 makes the undercurrents of this course a supervisory expectation: lineage, reconciliation, single definitions of key terms, and the ability to produce ad hoc aggregations under stress.

Compliance has been reported as persistently weak by supervisors in the decade since, which tells you the requirements are hard rather than trivial. Model risk management standards point the same way: both the US Federal Reserve's SR 11-7 and the Prudential Regulation Authority's model risk principles treat data quality and lineage as part of the model, not as an environmental condition around it. A model whose inputs cannot be traced is, for supervisory purposes, an unvalidated model.

The consequence for a modeller If you build A-IRB or IFRS 9 models, the data engineering in your development is inside the scope of validation. Every one of the following has failed a validation somewhere: a default definition that differed between the PD and LGD datasets, a snapshot that silently reused stale collateral values, a currency conversion applied twice, a "cured" account counted as both good and bad, and a monthly extract that dropped accounts closed mid-month. None of these are modelling errors. All of them are Part V errors.
Step 3

The mental model

The organising frame for this course is the data engineering lifecycle of Reis and Housley (2022): five sequential stages through which data passes, and six undercurrents that must hold at every stage. The stages tell you how the data got here. The undercurrents tell you why anyone should believe it.

The five stages

GenerationData is produced by a source system you usually do not control: a core banking platform, a collections workflow tool, a market data feed, a spreadsheet a human maintains. The engineering question is what the source guarantees and what it merely happens to do today. Covered in Lecture 02.
StorageWhere the data rests, in what format, under what schema, with what guarantees about concurrent readers and writers. Storage is not one stage in practice: it underlies every other stage, because every stage reads from and writes to it. Covered in Lectures 02 to 09 and 14.
IngestionGetting data from the source into your storage: batch or stream, full or incremental, push or pull. This is where most pipelines break, because it is the boundary you share with a system whose owners did not agree to your schedule. Covered in Lectures 10 to 12.
TransformationTurning raw arrivals into something with business meaning: typed, deduplicated, conformed, joined, aggregated, modelled. This is where the definitions live, and therefore where most of the arguments happen. Covered in Lectures 13 to 15.
ServingDelivering the result to a consumer: a BI dashboard, an analyst's dataframe, a model's feature table, a regulatory return. A pipeline with no named consumer is not a data product, it is a hobby. Covered in Lectures 15 and 16.

The six undercurrents

The undercurrents are the reason a pipeline that runs is not the same thing as a pipeline you can put in front of a model validator. Each is a cross-cutting concern: it applies at every stage, and it cannot be added at the end.

Three ideas worth holding at once

Beyond the lifecycle, three claims shape how modern platforms are built. They are not competing theories; they are complementary descriptions of the same practice.

The pipeline is software. A transformation is a function from tables to tables, and everything the software engineering discipline knows about functions applies: version control, tests, review, small deployable units, no editing production in place. The historical reason data work escaped these disciplines is that it grew up in GUI ETL tools and stored procedures where none of them were possible. The analytics engineering movement, which Lecture 15 covers through dbt, is essentially the project of putting data transformation back inside software engineering.

Data is a product, with consumers who have expectations. Zhamak Dehghani's data mesh argument (2019, book 2022) is often reduced to an org chart, but the durable part is smaller and harder to argue with: a dataset should have an owner, a documented interface, a stated quality guarantee, and a consumer who is entitled to complain. That is the data contract of Lecture 18. Whether you decentralise the teams is a second and much more contested question.

Storage and compute are separate, and this changed the architecture. In a classical warehouse the disks and the query engine were the same appliance, so capacity planning meant buying both together and the schema had to be decided before the data landed. In cloud object storage they are separate purchases, so you can land data first and decide its schema later, and you can point several engines at the same files. Everything distinctive about the modern stack, the lakehouse, open table formats, the resurgence of "just query the files" tools such as DuckDB, follows from this single fact.

Schema on write versus schema on read The old warehouse enforced the schema at write time: nothing landed unless it fitted the declared table, so the warehouse was always clean and always late, because a source change meant a change request. The data lake enforced it at read time: everything landed immediately and the interpretation was the reader's problem, so the lake was always current and frequently a swamp. The lakehouse of Lecture 14 is the attempt to have both, by keeping cheap open storage and adding back the transactional guarantees and schema enforcement that made the warehouse trustworthy. Read the history of this field as a pendulum between those two positions, with each swing keeping something from the last.
Step 4

The detail

A short history, in five movements

The intellectual foundation is Codd's relational model (1970), which gave us tables, keys, and a query language that says what you want rather than how to get it. Everything since is a response to volumes, formats, or economics that the relational database of 1980 could not absorb.

The warehouse era, roughly 1990 to 2005. Bill Inmon's Building the Data Warehouse (1992) and Ralph Kimball's The Data Warehouse Toolkit (1996) established that analytical data belongs in a separate, purpose-modelled store rather than in the transactional system, and disagreed productively about how to model it. Nightly ETL jobs moved data from operational systems into the warehouse, transforming it on the way, because warehouse storage was the expensive part. Lecture 08 covers both schools.

The big data era, roughly 2005 to 2015. Google's papers on the Google File System (2003) and MapReduce (2004) described how to compute over data that does not fit on one machine, and Hadoop (2006) made the pattern available to everyone. The promise was scale at commodity cost; the delivery included a great deal of Java, a cluster to babysit, and the discovery that most organisations did not have big data. Spark (Zaharia and colleagues, 2010) replaced MapReduce with something faster and far more pleasant, and remains the default distributed engine today. Lecture 13.

The cloud warehouse era, roughly 2012 to 2020. Redshift (2012), BigQuery, and Snowflake (founded 2012) made a fast columnar warehouse a credit card purchase, with storage and compute billed separately. Because storage was now cheap and the warehouse could scan quickly, the transformation step moved after the load: ELT replaced ETL for most workloads. dbt (from 2016) gave that transformation step version control, tests, and documentation, and the "modern data stack" (ingest, warehouse, transform, BI) became the default architecture for analytics.

The lakehouse era, roughly 2019 onward. Open table formats, Delta Lake, Apache Iceberg (from Netflix), and Apache Hudi (from Uber), put ACID transactions, schema evolution, and time travel on top of Parquet files in object storage. The consequence is that the warehouse's guarantees no longer require the warehouse's storage, and that several engines can share one copy of the data. In parallel, DuckDB (CWI Amsterdam, 2019) made the opposite bet, that a single modern laptop is enough for most analytical work, and won it more often than anyone expected. Lectures 09 and 14.

ETL and ELT: the same three letters, a different bill

The order of the letters encodes where the transformation happens and therefore who pays for it.

ELT is the default for analytical work today, but not universally. Transform before loading when the source data cannot legally or safely land in your platform in raw form (unmasked PII crossing a jurisdiction boundary), when the volume you would land is dominated by data nobody will ever use, or when the transformation needs a capability the warehouse does not have. Those exceptions are narrower than they were in 2010, and the burden of proof has moved: you now justify ETL, not ELT.

The boundary that matters most for this audience is the one between the analytics engineer and the modeller, because in credit risk it is usually invisible. When a modeller writes the SQL that derives months_in_arrears from a payment history table, that modeller is doing analytics engineering, and the question of whether that derivation is version controlled, tested, and shared with the LGD team is a data engineering question with direct validation consequences.

From notebook to pipeline

The most common transition in practice is the promotion of exploratory work into production. A notebook is an excellent instrument for thinking and a poor one for running unattended: cells can execute out of order, state persists invisibly, there is no schedule, no retry, no log, and no test. Turning a notebook into a pipeline means extracting the logic into importable functions, adding tests, adding logging, parameterising the dates and paths that were hard-coded, making the whole thing idempotent so that a rerun produces the same result rather than a double count, and handing it to an orchestrator. Lecture 20 does this end to end; the software engineering track has already made the case in general.

Worked example: the data supply chain behind a single PD model input

Take one field, the default flag on a retail mortgage account, and trace it from where it is created to where it is used. Every hop is owned by a lecture in this course.

1. Generation. The core banking system records a missed direct debit. Nothing labelled "default" exists yet; there is a payment due, a payment received, and a date. Lecture 02, because whether that arrives as a fixed-width extract, a JSON event, or a database table changes everything downstream.

2. Ingestion. A nightly extract lands the payments table. It is incremental, keyed on account and value date, and last month someone reprocessed three days of payments, so the same business day exists twice with different amounts. Lecture 10, because the answer is a watermark and an idempotent load, not a manual deletion.

3. Storage. The raw arrival is kept as Parquet in the bronze layer, partitioned by load date, and the conformed payment history is a silver table keyed on account and month. Lectures 02, 08, and 14.

4. Transformation. Ninety days past due is computed from consecutive unpaid instalments, with a materiality threshold, and forbearance flags from the collections system are joined in to distinguish a technical arrears position from a genuine default. This is where the regulatory default definition becomes SQL, and where the PD and LGD teams must use the same SQL. Lectures 05, 08, and 15.

5. Quality. A test asserts that the arrears count never decreases by more than one month per month without a cure or a write-off event, that no account is simultaneously defaulted and closed-good, and that the monthly default rate stays inside a tolerance of its twelve-month average. Failures quarantine the batch rather than publishing it. Lecture 16.

6. Serving. The monthly default flag lands in the modelling dataset used for PD estimation, and simultaneously in the IFRS 9 staging engine, which needs the same flag for a different purpose. One definition, two consumers, one contract. Lectures 15, 16, and 18.

7. Lineage. When validation asks "where does this number come from?", the answer is a column-level lineage graph from the return back to the core banking extract, plus the git history of every transformation that touched it. This is the BCBS 239 requirement, and it is the deliverable that distinguishes a documented model from an audited one. Lectures 18 and 19.

Notice what is absent from the list: the model. Steps one to seven happen before anyone fits anything, and they determine whether the fit means what the modeller thinks it means.

Step 5

Synthesis and what's next

You now have a definition (data engineering is the supply chain that delivers usable data to models and people), a lifecycle with five stages and six undercurrents, the economic reason the field exists (separated storage and compute), the reason the undercurrents are not optional in a bank (BCBS 239 and model risk standards), and the two orderings of the same three letters. The rest of the course fills in each stage and each undercurrent.
