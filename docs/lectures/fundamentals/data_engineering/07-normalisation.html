Normalisation and relational design

If a customer's address is written on each of their twelve letters, there are twelve opportunities for the address to disagree with itself, and a change of address is twelve edits of which someone will forget one. Write it once on a card and let the letters reference the card. That is the whole of normalisation; the normal forms are just a numbered procedure for finding the cards.

Lecture 7 of 20 Reading time, 45 minutes Part II: SQL and data modelling
Step 1

The 30-second version

Normalisation organises data so that each fact is stored exactly once, by splitting wide tables into narrower ones related by keys. The purpose is not tidiness. It is the elimination of three specific defects that redundancy makes inevitable.

An update anomaly: the same fact appears in many rows, so changing it means changing all of them, and any missed row now contradicts the others. An insertion anomaly: a fact cannot be recorded because the table demands unrelated information alongside it, so a customer cannot exist until they have an account. A deletion anomaly: removing one thing destroys another, so deleting a customer's last account also deletes the customer.

The normal forms are a ladder of successively stricter conditions, each removing one kind of unwanted dependency. First normal form demands atomic values. Second removes dependencies on part of a composite key. Third removes dependencies between non-key columns. Third normal form is the practical target for a transactional schema, and reaching it is mostly a matter of asking, for each column, "what does this actually depend on?" and moving it to the table whose key answers.

Feynman test Twelve letters, each with the customer's address typed at the top. The customer moves. Now you must find twelve letters and retype twelve addresses, and the one you miss becomes the version that gets used. The alternative is one card holding the address, with each letter carrying only the customer's reference number. One edit, no contradictions possible. The cost is that reading a letter now requires fetching the card as well, which is exactly the join you pay for normalisation with.
And then analytics deliberately undoes it Normalisation optimises for writing correctly, which is a transactional concern. Analytical stores optimise for reading quickly, and they deliberately reintroduce redundancy so that a query answers without traversing six tables. That is the star schema of Lecture 08, and it is not a failure to normalise; it is a different objective function on a copy of the data whose only writer is a pipeline. The rule of thumb: normalise where humans and applications write, denormalise where analysts and models read, and never confuse the two copies.
Step 2

Why this matters

Redundancy is how definitions drift apart. A bank's product terms, the margin, the reference rate, the early repayment charge, stored once per account rather than once per product means two million copies of a dozen facts. When the product changes, the copies diverge, and after two years nobody can say which version of the terms applied to which account. The same argument applies with more force to derived definitions: if the default definition is implemented separately in the PD extract and the LGD extract, the two will eventually disagree, and normalising it into one referenced table or view is the structural fix rather than a procedural one.

Understanding functional dependency is what makes you able to read someone else's schema. Arriving at an unfamiliar bank's data model, the useful question is not what the tables are called but which column determines which. Once you can say "the arrears count depends on account and month, the interest margin depends on product and effective date, the segment depends on customer and date", you know what the tables should be, and any divergence from that tells you where the model will cause trouble.

Keys are where banking data models fail in practice. Account numbers are reused after closure in some systems, so an account number alone is not a stable identifier across time. Customer identifiers differ between the core banking platform, the collections system, and the card platform, so joining them requires a mapping table that somebody must own. Sort code and account number pairs change when a bank reorganises. Every one of these is a key design problem, and every one of them shows up eventually as a modelling dataset with the wrong number of rows.

Step 3

The mental model

Functional dependency

Write X -> Y to mean "X determines Y": knowing X fixes Y. Then the entire normalisation procedure is a search for dependencies whose left-hand side is not the table's key.

account_id                  -> customer_id, product_code, origination_date
account_id, reporting_date  -> balance, arrears_months          (the snapshot grain)
product_code                -> product_name, reference_rate
customer_id                 -> surname, date_of_birth
customer_id, effective_date -> segment, risk_grade              (it changes over time)

Every line is a candidate table, keyed by its left-hand side. If a column sits in a table whose key does not determine it, the column is in the wrong table, and the anomalies follow mechanically. That is the whole diagnostic.

The ladder

Keys: natural, surrogate, composite

A natural key is an identifier the business already uses: an account number, an ISIN, a company registration number. A surrogate key is a meaningless integer or hash the platform generates. A composite key is several columns taken together.

Natural keys are readable and require no lookup, which is why they win in simple cases. They fail in three situations that all occur in banking. They change: a customer reference is reassigned during a migration, and every child row now points at the wrong parent. They are reused: some core banking systems recycle closed account numbers, so the same number means two different accounts at two different times. And they are not shared: the same customer has three identifiers in three systems, none of which is authoritative.

Surrogate keys solve all three by decoupling identity from any business attribute, at the cost of an extra join to see anything human-readable and of a mapping step at load time. The standard compromise, and the one a risk platform should adopt: surrogate primary keys on any dimension whose natural key may change or be reused, natural keys retained alongside as attributes, and a documented mapping table for cross-system identifiers with a named owner.

Step 4

The detail

Walking the ladder on a real extract

Start where extracts actually arrive: one wide row per account, with customer details repeated, product terms repeated, and collateral flattened into numbered columns.

-- UNF: as received
account_id | customer_name | customer_dob | product      | ref_rate | margin |
           | collateral_1_type | collateral_1_value | collateral_2_type | collateral_2_value

The defects. The collateral columns are a repeating group, so an account with three charges cannot be represented and an account with one wastes two columns. The customer name repeats on every account the customer holds. The product terms repeat on every account of that product. Renaming a customer means finding every row; changing a margin means finding every row; a customer with no accounts cannot exist; deleting an account deletes the only record of its collateral.

-- 1NF: atomic values, no repeating groups
-- collateral moves to its own rows, keyed by account and charge sequence
accounts(account_id, customer_name, customer_dob, product, ref_rate, margin)
collateral(account_id, charge_seq, collateral_type, collateral_value)

First normal form has fixed the repeating group. An account may now have any number of charges. Redundancy across accounts is untouched.

-- 2NF: remove dependencies on part of a composite key
-- in collateral, keyed (account_id, charge_seq), nothing depends on account_id alone,
-- so collateral is already fine. In a table keyed (account_id, reporting_date),
-- product and origination_date depend on account_id alone and must move out:
account_snapshots(account_id, reporting_date, balance, arrears_months)
accounts(account_id, customer_name, customer_dob, product, ref_rate, margin, origination_date)

This is the step that matters most in risk data, because the monthly snapshot table is keyed on account and date, and static attributes have no business being repeated sixty times. Second normal form is what separates the slowly-changing attributes of an account from the monthly measures about it.

-- 3NF: remove dependencies between non-key columns
-- customer_name and customer_dob depend on the customer, not the account
-- ref_rate and margin depend on the product, not the account
accounts(account_id, customer_id, product_code, origination_date)
customers(customer_id, surname, date_of_birth)
products(product_code, product_name, ref_rate, margin)
account_snapshots(account_id, reporting_date, balance, arrears_months)
collateral(account_id, charge_seq, collateral_type, collateral_value)

All three anomalies are now gone. A customer's name lives in one row, so it cannot contradict itself. A margin change is one edit. A customer can exist with no accounts. Deleting an account leaves the customer and the product intact. The price is that a report on exposure by customer segment now joins four tables, which is precisely the price Lecture 08 refuses to pay on the analytical side.

Where 3NF is not enough for a bank Third normal form says nothing about time. The products table above holds the current margin, so once it changes, the margin that applied at origination is gone, and any recalculation of a historical cashflow is wrong. The same applies to customer segment, risk grade, address, and every other attribute that moves. Normalising correctly and then storing only the current value is one of the most common serious modelling errors in a risk platform. The fix is to add the time dimension to the key, products(product_code, effective_from) -> margin, which is the slowly changing dimension formalised in Lecture 08.

Entity-relationship diagrams, as code

A schema is a graph, and a graph is easier to review as a picture. Keeping the picture as text, in version control, next to the DDL, means it can be reviewed in a pull request and cannot silently drift. DBML, the language behind dbdiagram.io, is the most convenient of these formats.

Table customers {
  customer_id   varchar [primary key]
  surname       varchar [not null]
  date_of_birth date    [not null]
}

Table products {
  product_code   varchar [primary key]
  effective_from date    [primary key]
  product_name   varchar
  ref_rate       varchar
  margin         decimal
}

Table accounts {
  account_id       varchar [primary key]
  customer_id      varchar [ref: > customers.customer_id]
  product_code     varchar [ref: > products.product_code]
  origination_date date
}

Table account_snapshots {
  account_id     varchar [ref: > accounts.account_id]
  reporting_date date
  balance        decimal
  arrears_months int
  indexes { (account_id, reporting_date) [pk] }
}

Table collateral {
  account_id      varchar [ref: > accounts.account_id]
  charge_seq      int
  collateral_type varchar
  collateral_value decimal
  indexes { (account_id, charge_seq) [pk] }
}

Read the relationship markers as cardinality. > is many-to-one, < is one-to-many, - is one-to-one. In the traditional crow's foot notation the same information is drawn at the line ends: a fork for many, a single stroke for one, a circle for optional. The reason to care is the fan-out warning from Lecture 05: every one-to-many relationship in the diagram is a place where a careless join inflates a total, and reading the diagram before writing the query is how you know which ones they are.

Mermaid's erDiagram is an alternative that renders directly in Markdown and in these lecture notes, covered in the diagrams as code lecture. Either is fine. What matters is that the diagram is generated from text that lives with the schema, rather than drawn once in a slide deck that is wrong within a quarter.

Worked example: the wide extract that arrives every month, and what to do with it

A collections system sends a monthly CSV with one row per account in arrears and 180 columns. Among them: customer name and address repeated on every account, the current product margin, three sets of numbered collateral columns, four sets of numbered contact-attempt columns, and the current arrears position. Two teams already consume it by reading it directly into pandas. The question is what to build.

Step one: land it exactly as it arrives. Do not normalise on ingestion. The raw arrival is evidence, per Lecture 02, and any normalisation is a transformation that can be wrong and will need rerunning. Bronze layer, Parquet, partitioned by reporting date, schema recorded.

Step two: identify the dependencies, not the tables. Ask what determines what. Customer attributes depend on the customer. Margin depends on product and effective date, and note that the extract gives you today's margin rather than the margin at the reporting date, which is a gap to record and raise with the source owner rather than paper over. Collateral depends on account and charge. Contact attempts depend on account and attempt timestamp, which means they are events, not attributes, and they belong in their own table at their own grain.

Step three: build the 3NF silver layer. Five tables emerge: customers, accounts, products keyed with an effective date, account_snapshots at the account-month grain, and collateral and contact_attempts at their own grains. The 180 columns become five tables averaging fifteen columns, and every one of the original anomalies is gone. Crucially, the unnesting of the numbered collateral columns now reveals what the wide format hid: eleven accounts have four charges, which the extract truncated at three. That is a data quality finding the wide format made invisible.

Step four: do not ask the two existing consumers to join five tables. They were reading one wide file for a reason. Build the analytical layer of Lecture 08 on top of the normalised layer and let them read that. The normalised layer exists to make the transformation correct and the definitions single-homed; the denormalised layer exists to make consumption easy. Both, not either.

What this buys. A customer name change is one row. The collateral truncation is visible and monitorable. Contact attempts, which were unusable at four columns, are now a queryable event history that supports an entirely new class of collections analysis. And the definition of arrears lives in one transformation rather than in two teams' notebooks.

Step 5

Synthesis and what's next

Normalisation stores each fact once, which eliminates the update, insertion, and deletion anomalies that redundancy guarantees. The procedure is a search for functional dependencies whose left-hand side is not the table's key, and the mnemonic covers it: every non-key column depends on the key, the whole key, and nothing but the key. Third normal form is the working target for a transactional or conformed layer. Two caveats carry into the rest of the course: third normal form says nothing about time, so historised attributes need the date in the key, and the analytical layer will deliberately denormalise a copy for read performance.
