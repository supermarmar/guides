Colour and accessibility

The previous lecture flagged colour as one of the least perceptually accurate encodings available, and that it can be actively distorted by surrounding context. This lecture develops colour properly: the physical and perceptual colour spaces every modern palette is built from, the prevalence and mechanism of colour vision deficiency, and redundant encoding, the specific, reliable technique that protects a chart's message when colour perception fails for any reader, for any reason.

Lecture 8 of 11 Reading time, 35 minutes Part III: visualisation design
Step 1

The 30-second version

Colour on a screen is physically produced by mixing red, green, and blue light in varying intensities, the RGB colour space; this is a natural space for a display to emit light in, but a poor space for a human to reason about colour in, since equal steps in RGB values do not correspond to equal steps in perceived colour difference. HSV and HSL (hue, saturation, value or lightness) reorganise the same information into a more intuitive space for choosing colours by hand, still without solving the perceptual-uniformity problem. Perceptually uniform spaces, the Munsell colour system historically and CIELUV and HCL (hue, chroma, luminance) in modern practice, are specifically constructed so that equal numerical steps correspond, as closely as achievable, to equal steps in perceived colour difference, exactly the property RGB and HSV lack; a well-designed sequential or diverging chart palette (viridis is the most widely adopted modern example) is built in one of these perceptually uniform spaces for precisely this reason. Colour vision deficiency, most commonly a reduced ability to distinguish red from green, affects a meaningful minority of the population, overwhelmingly men, and a chart relying on a red-green distinction to carry its entire message is, for that population, carrying no message at all in the colour channel. Redundant encoding, using a second channel (shape, line style, direct labelling, position) alongside colour to carry the same distinction, is the standard, reliable fix: a chart's message survives colour vision deficiency, greyscale printing, and projector colour distortion alike only if colour is never the sole channel carrying it.

Feynman test Imagine designing a set of traffic lights using only shades of red, with no positional convention (top, middle, bottom) at all, distinguishing "stop" from "go" purely by exact shade. A driver with any degree of red colour vision deficiency, and there are more of them than most designers assume, would be left guessing. Real traffic lights work regardless of colour vision precisely because position, not colour alone, carries the actual instruction, colour is a helpful redundant cue on top of a channel (position) that works for everyone. Every chart relying solely on colour to distinguish categories is making exactly the traffic-light mistake the position convention was invented to avoid.

Step 2

Why this matters

Three reasons colour deserves this level of technical attention rather than being left to a plotting library's default palette.

First, colour vision deficiency affects a genuinely large minority of the population, not a negligible edge case: red-green colour vision deficiency affects roughly one in twelve men and a much smaller fraction of women, meaning any chart distributed to a mixed audience of any meaningful size will, with near certainty, reach at least one reader for whom a red-versus-green distinction is invisible or unreliable.

Second, a chart's colour palette rarely survives its journey unchanged: the same figure is routinely printed in greyscale, projected under variable lighting and colour calibration, or viewed on a screen with different colour rendering, and a palette that relies on colour alone, without redundant encoding, can lose its entire message under any one of these ordinary, unavoidable transformations.

Third, a perceptually uniform colour space is a genuine technical requirement for an honest sequential or diverging encoding, not a stylistic preference: a palette built in a non-uniform space (raw RGB interpolation, for instance) can introduce apparent visual "steps" or discontinuities in a genuinely smooth underlying variable, misleading a reader into perceiving a threshold or boundary that does not exist in the data, exactly the kind of accidental artefact a rigorous, evidence-based design discipline is meant to prevent.

Vignette from the field A risk-reporting team built a heatmap of regional risk scores using a red-to-green diverging palette, red for high risk and green for low risk, a conventional and seemingly intuitive choice. Feedback from a colleague with red-green colour vision deficiency revealed that the entire heatmap was, for him, close to unreadable: the red and green regions were nearly indistinguishable, and he could not reliably tell high-risk regions from low-risk ones without consulting the underlying numbers directly. The team rebuilt the palette using a perceptually uniform, colour-vision-deficiency-safe diverging scheme (blue to orange, a standard accessible substitute for red-green), and separately added direct numeric labels to each cell as a redundant channel, so that the chart's message survived both a colleague's colour vision deficiency and, as a side benefit the team had not initially anticipated, the office printer's poor colour reproduction on a black-and-white handout.
Step 3

The mental model

Hold one picture: a red-green sequential palette that looks perfectly ordered to typical colour vision collapsing into a nearly uniform grey band under a simulated red-green colour vision deficiency filter, against a blue-orange palette that remains clearly ordered under the identical simulation.

Two slogans. A palette that only works for typical colour vision does not work. The risk-reporting vignette's colleague was not an edge case to be accommodated after the fact; accessible colour choice is the baseline, not an optional enhancement layered on afterwards. Colour should never be the only channel carrying a chart's message. Redundant encoding is the general-purpose insurance policy against colour vision deficiency, greyscale printing, and projector distortion all at once, developed in full below.

Step 4

The detail

RGB, HSV, and HSL

RGB represents a colour as an additive mixture of red, green, and blue light intensities, the space a screen's own hardware emits light in, and consequently the space almost every image file format stores colour in. RGB is a poor space for a human to reason about colour relationships in directly: it is not obvious, working purely in RGB, how to construct a colour that is "the same hue, slightly darker" or "the same lightness, a different hue" without trial and error. HSV (hue, saturation, value) and HSL (hue, saturation, lightness) reorganise the identical underlying colour information into a cylindrical space with hue as an angle and saturation and value (or lightness) as radial and vertical coordinates, considerably more intuitive for a human deliberately choosing or adjusting a colour by hand, though still not perceptually uniform: equal numerical steps in HSV or HSL do not correspond reliably to equal steps in how different two colours actually look to a human observer.

Perceptually uniform spaces: Munsell, CIELUV, and HCL

The Munsell colour system, developed in the early twentieth century, was among the first systematic attempts to organise colour by direct, empirically calibrated human perception of hue, value, and chroma, rather than by the physical mixture of light. Modern perceptually uniform spaces, most notably CIELUV (and the closely related CIELAB) and the derived HCL (hue, chroma, luminance) space, are constructed so that a fixed numerical distance between two colour coordinates corresponds, as closely as achievable given the underlying complexity of human colour vision, to a fixed perceived difference, regardless of where in the space the two colours sit. This property, perceptual uniformity, is exactly what a well-constructed sequential or diverging chart palette needs: a palette that is not perceptually uniform can introduce apparent visual "jumps" partway through an otherwise smooth range of values, misleading a reader into perceiving a threshold that the underlying data does not actually contain. Viridis, now a default palette in several major plotting libraries, is a widely adopted, empirically validated example of a sequential palette constructed specifically to be perceptually uniform and, as a direct further benefit, robust under the most common forms of colour vision deficiency.

Colour vision deficiency

Colour vision deficiency arises from a reduced or absent function in one or more of the eye's colour-sensitive cone cell types. Protanopia and deuteranopia (reduced sensitivity to red and green respectively, together the most common forms) affect roughly one in twelve men and a much smaller fraction of women, a genetic prevalence difference arising because the relevant genes sit on the X chromosome. Tritanopia (reduced blue-yellow sensitivity) is considerably rarer. Because red-green deficiency is by far the most prevalent form, a red-green palette (developed directly in the vignette) is the single most common accessibility failure in chart design, and testing a palette under a colour vision deficiency simulation (a standard feature of most modern design and plotting tools) before publication is cheap, quick, and directly actionable due diligence.

Redundant encoding

Redundant encoding uses a second, independent visual channel, shape, line style, direct data labelling, or spatial position, alongside colour to carry the identical categorical or ordinal distinction colour is also encoding. A line chart distinguishing three series by colour alone loses its entire distinguishing information under colour vision deficiency or greyscale printing; the same chart distinguishing the identical three series by colour and line style (solid, dashed, dotted) together survives both. This is the single most reliable, general-purpose accessibility technique available to a chart designer, and it is the direct chart-design analogue of the traffic-light position convention in this lecture's opening Feynman test: a second, colour-independent channel is what makes the chart's message robust to any single channel's failure.

The greyscale test

A simple, practical due-diligence check, converting a finished chart to greyscale and confirming the intended distinctions remain legible, catches both colour vision deficiency failures and the separate, more mundane problem of a chart printed or photocopied without colour at all, a genuinely common occurrence for regulatory submissions and printed reports; a chart that fails this simple test has, by definition, relied on colour as its sole encoding channel somewhere.

Step 5

Pitfalls

Using a red-green diverging palette without checking colour vision deficiency safety Developed directly in the vignette: this is the single most common and most avoidable accessibility failure in chart design, given how prevalent red-green colour vision deficiency is; a blue-orange or similarly tested, colour-vision-deficiency-safe diverging scheme is the standard, low-cost substitute.
Building a sequential palette by naive RGB interpolation Interpolating directly in RGB space between two colours does not produce a perceptually uniform gradient, and can introduce visible, misleading "jumps" partway through an otherwise smooth range of values; a palette built in a perceptually uniform space such as HCL or CIELUV, or a pre-validated palette such as viridis, avoids this artefact.
Relying on colour as the sole channel distinguishing categories in a chart A chart distinguishing series or categories purely by colour loses its message entirely under colour vision deficiency, greyscale printing, or poor projector colour rendering; redundant encoding via shape, line style, or direct labelling is the standard, reliable insurance against all three failure modes at once.
Skipping the greyscale test before publication A finished chart converted to greyscale should still communicate its intended distinctions; skipping this cheap, fast check is the single easiest way to miss both a colour vision deficiency failure and a genuine, practical printing or photocopying failure before a chart reaches its actual audience.
Step 6

Synthesis and what's next

RGB is the space a screen emits light in but a poor space for a human to reason about colour directly; HSV and HSL are more intuitive for manual colour selection but still not perceptually uniform. Munsell historically, and CIELUV and HCL in modern practice, are specifically constructed so that equal numerical steps correspond to equal perceived colour differences, exactly the property a well-built sequential or diverging chart palette needs; viridis is the standard modern example. Colour vision deficiency, overwhelmingly red-green and overwhelmingly affecting men, is common enough that any chart reaching a meaningfully sized audience will almost certainly reach an affected reader, making redundant encoding, a second channel carrying the identical distinction colour carries, the standard, reliable insurance against colour-based failure of every kind, including plain greyscale printing.
