Chart taxonomy and grammar

Two competing ways of organising the universe of possible charts have emerged. A taxonomy classifies existing chart types into named families; a grammar instead treats a chart as a small set of composable building blocks that can be assembled into a chart type nobody has yet named. This lecture develops both, the static-to-immersive spectrum of visualisation media that sits alongside either, and makes the case that the grammar of graphics, not the taxonomy, is the more powerful mental model for a working analyst.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Visualisations sit along a spectrum of medium: static (a fixed image, print or PDF), dynamic (a chart that updates automatically as underlying data refreshes, but with no reader interaction), interactive (a reader can filter, zoom, or drill down, the defining feature of a business-intelligence dashboard), and immersive (virtual or augmented-reality visualisation, a genuinely different medium still finding its footing in mainstream analytical practice). A chart taxonomy (the widely used datavizproject scheme, and Tableau's visual-vocabulary reference, are two common examples) organises existing, named chart types by attributes such as family (comparison, distribution, relationship, composition), required input data shape, and communicative function, giving a practical, browsable catalogue to choose from. The grammar of graphics, formalised by Leland Wilkinson and implemented directly in Hadley Wickham's layered grammar underlying ggplot2 (and its Python descendant, plotnine), takes a structurally different approach: rather than naming finished chart types, it decomposes any chart into a small number of composable layers, data, an aesthetic mapping (which variable controls which visual channel), a geometric object (points, bars, lines), a statistical transformation, a coordinate system, and facets, letting a chart type nobody has named yet, or an ordinary bar chart, be constructed from the identical small vocabulary of parts. The case against taxonomies is not that they are wrong, but that they are a closed, finite list, while the grammar is a generative system that produces the taxonomy's chart types as special cases and can construct novel ones the taxonomy does not (and cannot) anticipate.

Feynman test A taxonomy of vehicles lists "car", "van", "lorry", and stops there, a genuinely useful catalogue for someone choosing among existing options, but of no help at all to someone designing a genuinely new kind of vehicle. A grammar of vehicles instead names the composable parts, an engine, a chassis, wheels, a cargo bay, a passenger cabin, and lets any of those existing named vehicle types be reconstructed from the parts, while also allowing an engineer to combine the parts in a way nobody has built before. The grammar of graphics is exactly this second kind of system: it happens to be able to produce a bar chart or a scatter plot as special cases, but it is not limited to the finite list of chart types any taxonomy names.
Where this sits in the ML world This lecture is where every earlier lecture in this sub-module, perceptual accuracy (lecture 2), accessible colour (lecture 3), audience-appropriate narrative (lecture 4), gets applied to the actual, structural question of which chart family, or which composed set of grammar layers, best serves a given task. It also gives a direct, principled reason ggplot2 and plotnine are taught as the modern default in serious analytical work rather than a stylistic alternative to matplotlib and seaborn's more taxonomy-oriented, named-chart-type interface.
Step 2

Why this matters

Three reasons the taxonomy-versus-grammar distinction is a genuinely practical concern rather than an academic classification exercise.

First, a genuinely novel comparison sometimes has no named chart type in any existing taxonomy, and a designer working purely from a taxonomy's fixed catalogue is left improvising outside their own framework at exactly the moment a principled tool would matter most, while a designer thinking in grammar-of-graphics layers can construct the needed chart directly from first principles rather than searching a list for the closest available match.

First and second, the choice of dynamism (static, dynamic, interactive, immersive) is itself a genuine design decision with real consequences, not an afterthought made by whichever tool happens to be at hand: a static chart embedded in a regulatory PDF and an interactive dashboard serving the same underlying data genuinely serve different purposes and different audiences, and conflating the two, presenting a static screenshot of what was designed to be interactively explored, routinely loses the exact functionality that made the dashboard useful in the first place.

Third, understanding the grammar of graphics directly explains why ggplot2-style tools compose so cleanly compared with a purely named-chart-type interface: adding a facet, changing a geometric object, or layering a statistical smooth on top of raw points are all small, local changes to one grammar layer at a time, rather than requiring an entirely different named chart function altogether, a genuine practical advantage this lecture's grammar framing explains directly rather than leaving as an unexplained stylistic preference.

Vignette from the field A model-monitoring team needed a chart showing, simultaneously, a portfolio's predicted-versus-actual default rate over time, split by risk grade, with a confidence band around the predicted line and a separate marker style flagging periods of known data-quality issues, a genuinely unusual combination with no single named chart type in any standard taxonomy reference. Working from a taxonomy-based reference guide, the team's first attempts tried to force the requirement into the closest-looking named chart type (a standard line chart with error bars), losing the risk-grade facets and the data-quality flags in the process. Rebuilding the chart in a grammar-of-graphics tool, composing a line geometry for the predicted rate, a ribbon geometry for the confidence band, a point geometry with a distinct shape for the flagged periods, and a facet for risk grade, each added as an independent layer, produced exactly the required chart directly, with no need to search for, or compromise towards, a pre-named chart type that did not exist.
Step 3

The mental model

Hold one picture: a taxonomy as a fixed, finite shelf of pre-assembled, named chart types, against a grammar as a small box of composable parts that can build every item already on the shelf and, further, items the shelf has no room, or no name, for at all.

Two slogans. A taxonomy is a catalogue; a grammar is a construction kit. The catalogue is faster to browse for a familiar need; the construction kit is the only option once the need is genuinely novel. Every named chart type is a special case of the grammar, but not every useful chart is a named type. This is the precise, structural reason the grammar of graphics is the more powerful mental model for a working analyst, even though the taxonomy remains a useful, quick-reference catalogue.

Step 4

The detail

The static-dynamic-interactive-immersive spectrum

Static visualisations are fixed images, the standard medium for a printed report or a PDF regulatory submission, offering no reader interaction at all but guaranteeing every reader sees the identical, fully controlled view. Dynamic visualisations update automatically as underlying data refreshes (a live operational dashboard's headline figures, say) but still offer no reader-driven interaction. Interactive visualisations let the reader filter, zoom, hover for detail, or drill down into underlying detail, the defining feature of a genuine business-intelligence dashboard and a fundamentally different design task from a static chart, since an interactive chart must anticipate and support a reader's own exploratory path rather than presenting a single, fixed narrative. Immersive visualisation, virtual or augmented reality, remains the least mature of the four in mainstream analytical practice, genuinely useful for specific spatial or three-dimensional data but not yet a default choice for ordinary business reporting. Choosing a point on this spectrum is a genuine design decision, not a default determined by whichever tool happens to be at hand, and conflating two points on the spectrum (a static screenshot of an interactive dashboard, say) routinely loses the specific value the original medium provided.

Chart taxonomies

A chart taxonomy classifies existing, named chart types by attributes such as chart family (comparison, distribution, relationship, composition, over time), the shape of input data required, and the communicative function served. The datavizproject taxonomy and Tableau's visual vocabulary reference are two widely used, practical examples, each organised as a browsable catalogue that a designer can search by task ("I need to compare three categories over time") to find a small set of candidate, pre-named chart types. Taxonomies are genuinely useful precisely because they are fast to browse and require no deep theoretical understanding to use productively, a real practical advantage for routine, familiar comparison tasks.

The case against taxonomies

The structural limitation of any taxonomy is that it is, definitionally, a closed, finite list: a genuinely novel combination of requirements, as in this lecture's monitoring vignette, has no slot to occupy, and a designer working purely from a taxonomy is left either compromising towards the nearest available named type (losing genuine requirements in the process) or improvising entirely outside the taxonomy's own framework. This is not a claim that taxonomies are wrong or useless, only that they are closed where the actual space of possible, useful charts is open.

Wilkinson's grammar of graphics and Wickham's layered grammar

Leland Wilkinson's grammar of graphics formalised chart construction as a composition of a small number of independent, orthogonal components rather than a choice among finished, named chart types. Hadley Wickham's layered grammar of graphics, the direct theoretical foundation of ggplot2 (and its Python descendant, plotnine), refined this into a specific, practical layer structure: data (the underlying dataframe), an aesthetic mapping (which data variable controls which visual channel, position, colour, size), a geometric object (points, bars, lines, ribbons, the actual visual mark drawn), a statistical transformation (a smooth, a count, a binning, computed from the raw data before or alongside plotting), a coordinate system (Cartesian by default, but polar coordinates transform an ordinary bar chart's geometry directly into a pie chart, a fact the grammar makes explicit and the taxonomy treats as two unrelated chart types), and facets (small multiples splitting the same chart across a categorical variable). Composing different choices at each layer reproduces every one of a taxonomy's named chart types as a special case, and, as the monitoring vignette shows, composes just as readily into chart types no taxonomy names at all.

Why ggplot2-style tools compose cleanly

Because each grammar layer is independent, changing one layer (swapping a point geometry for a line geometry, adding a facet, layering a statistical smooth on top of raw points) is a small, local, additive change, not a switch to an entirely different, differently-parameterised chart function. This is the direct, structural explanation for why ggplot2 and plotnine feel more composable in practice than a purely named-chart-type interface: composability is not a stylistic property of the specific library, it is a direct consequence of building the tool around the grammar rather than around a taxonomy of finished chart types.

Step 5

Pitfalls

Forcing a genuinely novel requirement into the nearest named chart type Developed directly in the vignette: compromising towards a pre-named chart type when the actual requirement does not fit it routinely loses genuine, needed information (the risk-grade facets and data-quality flags, in the vignette's case); a grammar-based tool avoids this by constructing the exact requirement from composable parts rather than searching a finite catalogue.
Presenting a static screenshot of a chart designed to be interactive An interactive dashboard's value comes specifically from reader-driven filtering and drill-down; a static screenshot of the same view loses precisely that value while retaining none of a genuinely static chart's advantage of guaranteeing every reader sees an identical, fully controlled view; choosing the medium deliberately, rather than defaulting to whatever export format is convenient, avoids this mismatch.
Treating a taxonomy as a complete or theoretically grounded system A taxonomy is a practical, browsable catalogue, genuinely useful for familiar tasks, but it is not a generative theory of what charts are possible; treating its absence of a named category for a specific need as evidence the need cannot be met, rather than as evidence the taxonomy is simply closed, is a common and avoidable misunderstanding.
Assuming grammar-based tools are strictly better for every task The grammar's compositional power comes at some cost in the speed of producing a single, familiar chart type quickly; for a routine, well-understood comparison with no novel requirements, a taxonomy-oriented tool's named, one-call chart functions can genuinely be faster in practice, and the choice between the two families of tool is a real trade-off, not a strictly dominant preference for one over the other.
Step 6

Synthesis and what's next

Visualisations sit along a static-dynamic-interactive-immersive spectrum, each point a genuine design choice serving a different reader relationship with the data. Chart taxonomies, the datavizproject scheme and Tableau's visual vocabulary among them, organise existing, named chart types into a fast, practical, but structurally closed catalogue. The grammar of graphics, Wilkinson's original formulation and Wickham's layered refinement underlying ggplot2 and plotnine, instead decomposes any chart into composable layers, data, aesthetic mapping, geometry, statistical transformation, coordinate system, and facets, reproducing every taxonomy chart type as a special case while remaining open to genuinely novel combinations a fixed taxonomy has no slot for at all.

The closing lecture of this sub-module turns from the structural question of how a chart is built to the plain, practical discipline of how it earns its place in a finished report or presentation: what makes a figure a genuine figure rather than a decoration, the caption and cross-referencing conventions every figure needs, and the specific, different readability demands of print, a projector, and a phone screen.
