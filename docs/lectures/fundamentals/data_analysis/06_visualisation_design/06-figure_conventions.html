Figure conventions

Every idea developed across this sub-module, perceptual accuracy, accessible colour, audience-appropriate narrative, structural choice, ultimately has to survive the plain, unglamorous discipline of actually appearing correctly in a finished report or presentation. This closing lecture develops that discipline directly: what makes an image a genuine figure rather than a decoration, the captioning and cross-referencing conventions every figure needs, and the different readability demands of a printed page, a projector, and a phone screen.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A figure is a numbered object with a caption, referenced by that number somewhere in the surrounding text, not merely an image dropped into a document at the point it happens to become relevant; this distinction matters because a genuine figure can be found, cited, and discussed independently of its exact position on the page, while an unnumbered, uncaptioned image cannot. Every figure must be referenced and explained in the surrounding prose: a figure that appears with no accompanying text telling the reader what to look for and why it matters is asking the reader to do the analytical work the author was actually hired to do. A figure's caption should tell the reader what a reader needs to interpret it: the variables shown, the units, the time period or population covered, and, where relevant, the specific conclusion the figure is meant to support, not merely a restatement of the chart's title. Readability genuinely differs across medium: a figure legible on a full-page printed report can become illegible once shrunk onto a presentation slide or squeezed onto a phone screen, and font size, line weight, and label density all need to be checked against the medium the figure will actually be viewed in, not just the medium it was designed in. The presentation-specific discipline is stricter still: an important figure warrants no more than two to three minutes of live discussion, and a slide is not a report, a genuinely different medium with a genuinely different, much shorter attention budget than a printed page.

Feynman test Imagine handing someone a photograph with no caption, no title, and no mention of it anywhere in the accompanying letter, and expecting them to understand why you sent it. They would have to guess at what it shows, why it matters, and what you want them to conclude from it, exactly the position an uncaptioned, unreferenced figure puts a reader in. A properly captioned, properly referenced figure is the analytical equivalent of writing "here is the photograph, taken at this specific moment, showing this specific thing, and here is why it matters", removing every one of those guesses before the reader even has to make them.
Where this sits in the ML world This lecture closes the visualisation-design sub-module, and the data analysis course as a whole, by returning every idea developed across the previous five lectures to the plain, practical test of whether a figure actually does its job once it leaves the analyst's own screen and enters a printed report, a slide deck, or a regulator's review. It is the least glamorous lecture in the sub-module and, in genuine day-to-day practice, the one whose neglect causes the most avoidable, easily fixed embarrassment.
Step 2

Why this matters

Three reasons this lecture's plain conventions earn a dedicated closing treatment rather than being left as an assumed, obvious afterthought.

First, an unreferenced figure is functionally invisible in a document of any real length: a reader working through a long regulatory submission or technical report relies on cross-references to find and connect the specific figure being discussed at any given point in the text, and a figure that exists but is never explicitly pointed to from the prose is effectively lost, however carefully it was designed.

Second, a caption is the single most cost-effective piece of writing in an entire report, because it is read disproportionately often relative to its length: a reader skimming a document typically reads captions far more consistently than body text, making a caption's clarity and completeness a genuinely high-leverage piece of communication, not an afterthought to be written quickly once the "real" writing is done.

Third, medium-specific readability failures are entirely avoidable and entirely common: a chart built and checked only on a wide monitor routinely fails once printed on A4, projected in a dim conference room, or viewed on a phone during a follow-up email thread, and each of these failure modes is caught by a specific, cheap check rather than requiring any redesign of the underlying chart.

Vignette from the field An actuarial team submitted a detailed regulatory report containing a dozen carefully designed, accessibility-checked charts, each correctly built and internally reviewed on a wide desktop monitor. The regulator's written feedback flagged several of the charts as "difficult to interpret", not because of any flaw in the underlying design principles, but because the charts had been built with label text sized appropriately for a monitor and had become genuinely too small to read once printed at standard A4 resolution, the actual medium the regulator reviewed them in. The team's fix was a specific, standing pre-submission check: print every figure at its actual intended print size and read it at arm's length before submission, catching exactly this failure mode directly rather than relying on how the chart looked on the screen it was built on.
Step 3

The mental model

Hold one picture: the identical chart rendered at three different scales, one comfortably readable on a monitor, one becoming marginal once printed at A4, and one becoming genuinely illegible once shrunk to a typical presentation-slide thumbnail, with only the smallest of the three actually checked and adjusted for its real destination.

Two slogans. A figure is only as good as its worst destination. Designing and checking a chart exclusively on the medium it was built on, rather than the medium it will actually be read on, is precisely the gap the vignette's team closed with a standing pre-submission check. An unreferenced figure might as well not exist. Numbering, captioning, and explicitly citing a figure from the surrounding text is what makes it findable and usable, not an optional finishing touch.

Step 4

The detail

What makes an image a figure

A figure is a numbered object with a caption, distinguished from a merely decorative or illustrative image by the fact that it can be found, cited, and discussed independently of its exact position in a document, exactly as a numbered table or equation can be. Numbering a figure and giving it a caption is not a stylistic convention inherited from academic writing for its own sake; it is what makes the figure a genuine, addressable object in the document rather than an unreferenceable decoration that happens to sit near some relevant text.

Every figure must be referenced and explained

A figure appearing in a document with no accompanying sentence explaining what it shows and why it matters is asking the reader to reconstruct the analyst's own reasoning from the raw image alone, exactly the failure this lecture's opening Feynman test illustrates with the uncaptioned photograph. Every figure in a serious report should be explicitly cited from the surrounding prose ("Figure 3 shows..."), with the accompanying text stating, at minimum, what the reader is looking at and what conclusion it is meant to support; a figure that never receives this treatment has, in practical terms, not actually been included in the analysis at all, regardless of how carefully it was designed.

What a reader needs to interpret a figure

A complete caption tells the reader the variables shown, their units, the population or time period covered, and, where the figure is making a specific analytical point rather than simply presenting descriptive data, the conclusion it is meant to support. A caption that merely restates the chart's title, without any of this additional context, fails to do the actual job a caption exists to do; given how disproportionately often captions are actually read relative to body text, developed in "why this matters" above, this is a genuinely high-leverage piece of writing to get right.

Readability in print, on a projector, and on a phone

A chart's readability is not a fixed, medium-independent property; it depends directly on the physical or effective size the chart will actually be viewed at. Print readability depends on label and text size relative to the final printed dimensions, not the screen the chart was designed on, exactly the gap the vignette's regulatory-submission failure exposed. A projector introduces its own further degradation: ambient room lighting, variable projector colour calibration, and viewing distance from the back of a room all reduce effective contrast and legible detail well below what the identical file shows on a design monitor. A phone screen, increasingly the medium a figure is actually first viewed on when shared over email or a chat platform, is both physically small and often viewed at an angle or under poor lighting, demanding larger, simpler label text and fewer simultaneous data series than either print or a full presentation screen comfortably support. The standing discipline that catches all three failure modes is the same: check the actual figure at its actual intended destination size and viewing condition, not merely at the size and condition it was designed and reviewed in.

The presentation-specific discipline

A slide is not a report, and a figure destined for a live presentation faces demands neither print nor a static PDF share: an important figure warrants roughly two to three minutes of live spoken discussion, no more, meaning the figure itself needs to make its central point immediately, legible and interpretable within a few seconds of first appearing, with any further supporting detail relegated to an appendix slide or a backup document rather than crammed onto the primary slide. A figure requiring a full minute of the audience silently reading labels before the presenter can even begin discussing it has, in practical terms, already failed the presentation medium's specific time budget, regardless of how much genuine analytical value the underlying chart contains.

Step 5

Pitfalls

Including a figure with no explicit reference or explanation in the surrounding text An unreferenced figure is functionally invisible to a reader working through a document of any real length; every figure needs an explicit citation and a sentence or two of explanation, not just physical placement near the relevant discussion.
Writing a caption that merely restates the chart's title A complete caption states the variables, units, population or time period, and the specific conclusion the figure supports; a caption that only repeats the title wastes the single most disproportionately-read piece of text in the entire report.
Checking a figure's readability only on the screen it was designed on Developed directly in the vignette: a chart that reads comfortably on a wide monitor can become marginal in print and illegible on a slide thumbnail or phone screen; checking the figure at its actual intended destination size and viewing condition before submission is the specific, cheap fix.
Treating a presentation slide as if it were a printed report page A slide has a genuinely different, much shorter attention budget than a printed page; a figure crammed with the same density of detail appropriate for a report, then given only two to three minutes of live discussion, will not be absorbed by the audience regardless of the underlying chart's analytical quality; simplifying for the medium, not merely shrinking the report figure to fit, is the correct response.
Step 6

Synthesis and what's next

A figure is a numbered object with a caption, referenced explicitly from the surrounding prose, not merely an image placed near relevant text. Every figure needs its accompanying explanation, and a complete caption states the variables, units, population or period, and the conclusion the figure supports, not just a restatement of its title. Readability genuinely differs by medium, print, projector, and phone screen each degrade a chart's legibility in a different way from the monitor it was designed on, and the standing discipline of checking a figure at its actual destination size catches this before submission rather than after. A presentation slide is a genuinely different, much shorter-attention-budget medium than a printed report, demanding a figure that makes its point within seconds and reserves supporting detail for elsewhere.
