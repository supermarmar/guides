The history of data graphics

Every chart type a modern analyst reaches for without thinking, the bar chart, the line chart, the choropleth map, was invented by a specific person solving a specific, urgent problem, usually generations before computers existed to draw it automatically. Knowing that lineage is not decoration: several of the mistakes this sub-module warns against were first diagnosed, and first fixed, by the same people who invented the chart type in question.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The modern statistical graphic was not invented all at once; it accumulated across roughly three centuries, each addition solving a specific communication problem the previous state of the art could not. John Graunt's seventeenth-century mortality tables were the first systematic attempt to turn raw parish burial records into a structured, tabular picture of population risk, the direct ancestor of every actuarial life table since. William Playfair, in the late eighteenth century, invented the line chart, the bar chart, and the pie chart within a handful of years of each other, arguing explicitly that a well-drawn picture could convey a trend an equivalent table of numbers left buried. John Snow's 1854 cholera map placed individual cases as dots directly on a street map of London, and the spatial clustering the dots revealed around a single water pump was a genuine, first-of-its-kind piece of visual epidemiological reasoning, not merely an illustration of a conclusion reached some other way. Charles Joseph Minard's 1869 map of Napoleon's Russian campaign is still cited as one of the finest statistical graphics ever produced, encoding six variables (army size, position, direction of travel, temperature, dates, and geography) in a single, coherent image. Florence Nightingale's polar area diagram, presented to the British Parliament, made an argument about preventable military deaths so visually forceful that it changed army sanitary policy, an early, deliberate example of a chart built specifically to persuade a resistant audience rather than merely to inform a willing one.

Feynman test Imagine explaining to a sceptical colleague why a hundred-year-old chart is still worth studying when today's software can produce a chart in seconds. The answer is that the software did not solve the actual hard problem, which was never "how do you draw a line", it was "how do you decide what to show, and how do you make an audience that does not want to be convinced actually change its mind". Playfair, Snow, Minard, and Nightingale each solved that harder problem once, by hand, under real institutional resistance, and the specific choices they made (a line instead of a table, a map instead of a summary statistic, six variables instead of one) are still the choices a modern analyst has to make today, just with better tools for executing them.
Where this sits in the ML world This lecture opens the visualisation-design sub-module by establishing that the perceptual and rhetorical problems developed in the rest of this sub-module (illusions, colour, audience bias, narrative structure) are not new problems modern data volumes have created; they are old problems, already diagnosed and partially solved by specific historical figures, that a modern analyst inherits along with the tools.
Step 2

Why this matters

Three reasons a working analyst benefits from this history rather than treating chart design as a purely contemporary software skill.

First, Graunt's mortality tables are the direct methodological ancestor of the actuarial life table, and recognising that lineage situates the modern actuary's own work inside a continuous, centuries-long discipline of turning raw, messy vital-event records into structured risk information, rather than treating actuarial science and data visualisation as unrelated modern specialisms that happen to share some techniques.

First and second, Playfair's specific justification for inventing the line and bar chart, that a picture reveals a trend a table buries, is the same justification a modern analyst needs to be able to give for choosing a chart over a table today, and it is a justification that needs to be argued afresh for each specific dataset, not assumed automatically true simply because a chart looks more modern than a table.

Third, Snow's cholera map and Nightingale's polar diagram are the clearest historical illustrations that a well-designed chart can be a genuine act of analysis, not merely an illustration of an analysis already completed by other means, exactly the standard this sub-module holds every subsequent chart to: a chart earns its place in a report by doing analytical or persuasive work no table or paragraph could do as efficiently, not by decorating a conclusion already reached elsewhere.

Vignette from the field A junior analyst, asked to present a portfolio's regional loss experience to a committee that had previously been unmoved by a dense table of regional loss ratios, initially proposed simply reformatting the identical table with better typography. A senior colleague, invoking Snow's cholera map directly, suggested plotting each region's loss ratio directly onto a geographic map of the operating territory instead, using colour intensity to encode severity. The resulting map immediately made visually obvious a geographic clustering of high-severity regions along a specific transport corridor, a pattern the table had technically contained but that no committee member had previously noticed buried in fifty rows of numbers, and the committee approved a targeted regional pricing review within the same meeting, a decision the identical data, in table form, had failed to produce across two previous meetings.
Step 3

The mental model

Hold one picture: a timeline of five historical graphics, each adding one genuinely new capability (structured tabulation, the line and bar, spatial reasoning, multivariate encoding, deliberate persuasion) that a modern analyst still draws on, whether or not the software being used today makes the lineage visible.

Two slogans. A chart is a technology, and every technology has an inventor solving a real problem. Treating chart types as arbitrary defaults in a plotting library, rather than deliberate historical solutions to specific communication problems, is how the same old mistakes get made fresh. A chart can be an act of analysis, not just a report of one. Snow's map is the clearest possible historical case that this is true, and the standard every chart in this sub-module is held to.

Step 4

The detail

Graunt: the first structured mortality tables

John Graunt's 1662 Natural and Political Observations Made upon the Bills of Mortality took London's weekly parish burial records, an unstructured, largely administrative record with no analytical purpose of its own, and organised them into structured tables by cause of death and age, allowing the first systematic estimates of life expectancy and disease prevalence at the population level. This is the direct methodological ancestor of the actuarial life table developed in the contingencies course, and Graunt's core innovation, that raw administrative records become genuinely useful only once deliberately structured into a table designed to answer a specific question, is the same discipline the data analysis course's earlier lectures on data wrangling and exploratory analysis are built around.

Playfair: the line, the bar, and the pie

William Playfair's Commercial and Political Atlas (1786) and Statistical Breviary (1801) introduced, within a fifteen-year span, the line chart, the bar chart, and the pie chart, three chart types so thoroughly absorbed into ordinary practice today that their common origin in a single innovator's work is easy to overlook. Playfair's explicit justification, argued directly in his own writing, was that a well-constructed graphic conveys a trend or comparison that an equivalent table of numbers, however accurate, leaves the reader to reconstruct laboriously; this is precisely the justification a modern analyst still needs to be able to articulate for any specific dataset before reaching for a chart over a table.

Snow: the chart as an act of analysis

John Snow's 1854 map of cholera deaths in the Broad Street area of London plotted each individual death as a mark directly on a street map, rather than presenting an aggregate count or rate. The spatial clustering of deaths around a single water pump, made visible only by the map's spatial encoding, was the direct evidence that led Snow to the (at the time contested) conclusion that cholera spread through contaminated water rather than "miasma" (bad air), the dominant theory of the period. The map was not an illustration of a conclusion Snow had already reached by other means; the spatial pattern the map itself revealed was the evidence, a genuine historical instance of a chart functioning as the analysis rather than merely reporting one, exactly the standard invoked in this lecture's vignette.

Minard: six variables in one coherent image

Charles Joseph Minard's 1869 map of Napoleon's 1812 Russian campaign encodes, in a single static image, the army's geographic position and route, the direction of travel (outward and retreating, distinguished by colour), the army's size at each point (encoded by the band's width, shrinking dramatically as losses mounted), the ambient temperature during the retreat, and the corresponding dates, six distinct variables represented coherently without the image becoming cluttered or difficult to read. It remains a standard reference example, still cited by Tufte and others, of packing genuine multivariate complexity into a single graphic without sacrificing clarity, a direct historical counter-example to the assumption that more variables automatically require more chart panels or a less legible result.

Nightingale: the chart built to persuade

Florence Nightingale's polar area diagram (sometimes called a "rose diagram"), presented as part of her advocacy for British Army sanitary reform following the Crimean War, used wedge area to represent the proportion of army deaths attributable to preventable disease versus battle wounds, month by month. The diagram was constructed deliberately for an audience (Parliament and military leadership) that had shown limited appetite for engaging with the underlying statistical tables directly, and its visual argument, that preventable disease vastly outweighed combat as a cause of death, is credited with contributing directly to subsequent sanitary reforms. This is an early, explicit historical instance of a chart engineered specifically for its audience's resistance, the exact concern the audience-and-storytelling lecture later in this sub-module develops in full.

Venn and Euler: set relationships made visual

John Venn's diagrams (building on earlier work by Leonhard Euler) gave logical set relationships, overlap, containment, mutual exclusivity, a direct visual encoding, still the standard way to communicate overlapping categories or population segments today. Their inclusion in this history is a reminder that not every foundational chart type addresses a quantitative comparison; some, like the Venn diagram, address a purely logical or categorical relationship, a genuinely different communicative task from the quantitative trend-and-comparison charts Playfair pioneered.

Step 5

Pitfalls

Treating chart-type choice as an arbitrary default rather than a deliberate historical solution Every chart type on this lecture's timeline was invented to solve a specific communicative problem; reaching for a chart type purely because it is a plotting library's default, without asking whether it actually solves the problem in front of you, is exactly the discipline this history is meant to prevent.
Assuming a chart is neutral simply because it reports real data Nightingale's diagram is proof that a chart can be simultaneously entirely accurate and deliberately, effectively persuasive; treating "the data is real" as equivalent to "the chart is neutral" ignores every deliberate design choice a chart maker makes about what to emphasise, exactly the territory the audience-and-storytelling lecture develops next.
Underestimating how much analytical work a well-chosen chart can do Developed directly in the vignette: a table technically containing the same information as a chart is not equivalent to the chart in practice, since a table requires the reader to do the pattern-recognition work the chart does automatically; dismissing a proposed chart as "just a nicer version of the table" misses this genuine difference.
Assuming more variables always require more chart panels Minard's map is a direct historical counter-example to the assumption that six variables necessarily demand six separate small-multiple panels; a single, carefully designed encoding can sometimes communicate multivariate structure more clearly than a grid of simpler panels, though this requires real design skill to pull off well, developed further in the chart-taxonomy lecture.
Step 6

Synthesis and what's next

The modern statistical graphic accumulated across three centuries, each addition solving a specific problem: Graunt's tables gave raw records structure, directly ancestral to the actuarial life table; Playfair's line, bar, and pie charts gave shape to a trend a table buries; Snow's map turned spatial encoding into a genuine act of epidemiological analysis; Minard's map packed six variables into one coherent, still-celebrated image; and Nightingale's diagram was built deliberately to persuade an audience resistant to the underlying tables. Every one of these historical innovations is still a live design choice in a modern chart, whether or not a modern analyst is aware of the lineage.

The next lecture moves from history to the perceptual science underneath it: why the human visual system reads certain encodings (position, length) more accurately than others (colour, area), the specific illusions that can mislead even a careful reader, and the empirical Cleveland-McGill ordering of encoding accuracy that gives a rigorous, testable foundation to design choices this lecture has so far only illustrated historically.
