Perception and illusions

Not every visual encoding is equally readable, and this is not a matter of taste. A body of empirical research, running from nineteenth-century optical illusions through Stevens' psychophysical scaling laws to Cleveland and McGill's rigorously tested accuracy ranking, gives chart design an evidence base as concrete as any of this course's statistical methods. This lecture develops that evidence base directly.

Lecture 7 of 11 Reading time, 35 minutes Part III: visualisation design
Step 1

The 30-second version

The human visual system does not read every encoding of a numeric value with equal accuracy. Stevens' power law formalises the relationship between a physical stimulus's true magnitude and its perceived magnitude, $\psi = k\phi^n$, with the exponent $n$ varying by the type of encoding: length is read close to linearly ($n \approx 1$), while area and volume are systematically under- or over-estimated ($n$ typically well below or above one), meaning a circle's area scaled "correctly" to represent a doubled value is routinely perceived by a reader as representing something closer to a 1.5-fold increase, a genuine, measurable perceptual distortion, not a design flourish to be tolerated. Cleveland and McGill's landmark 1984 empirical study directly tested reader accuracy across a ranked set of encoding types, establishing that position along a common scale (as in a bar or dot chart) is read most accurately, followed by length, then angle and slope, with colour saturation, area, and volume trailing well behind, an ordering with direct, testable implications for which chart type is objectively the right choice for a given comparison, not merely a stylistic one. A family of classic geometric and colour illusions (Müller-Lyer, Ponzo, café wall, simultaneous contrast) demonstrates that the visual system's perception of length, size, and even colour is not a neutral measurement but an active, context-dependent construction, capable of being systematically fooled by surrounding visual context, exactly the mechanism a poorly designed chart can inadvertently trigger. The Gestalt laws (proximity, similarity, closure, continuity) describe the automatic, pre-attentive grouping principles a viewer's visual system applies to any image, chart included, whether or not the chart's designer intended that grouping.

Feynman test Ask someone to compare two bars in a bar chart and they will tell you, almost instantly and almost always correctly, which is taller. Ask the same person to compare two circles by area, and their answer is measurably less reliable, not because they are being careless, but because the human visual system genuinely processes length along a shared baseline more accurately than it processes area, a documented, replicated perceptual fact rather than a matter of individual skill or attention. Choosing a bar chart over a bubble chart for a value comparison is not a stylistic preference: it is choosing the encoding the evidence says a reader's own visual system can measure more accurately.
Where this sits in the ML world This lecture supplies the empirical, testable foundation for design choices the previous lecture's history could only illustrate by example: why Playfair's bar chart, not a pie or bubble chart, became the default comparison tool, and why a modern chart-selection decision should be grounded in the Cleveland-McGill ordering developed here rather than in aesthetic preference alone.
Step 2

Why this matters

Three reasons this lecture's perceptual science is a working tool, not academic trivia.

First, the Cleveland-McGill ordering gives an objective, evidence-based way to settle a genuinely common chart-selection disagreement: when a colleague proposes a pie chart or a bubble chart for a comparison task, the ordering supplies a direct, empirical answer (position and length are read more accurately than angle or area) rather than leaving the decision to competing aesthetic preferences.

Second, Stevens' power law explains, quantitatively, why area-based encodings of a value routinely mislead a reader even when the underlying calculation is mathematically correct: a chart maker who scales a bubble's area correctly in proportion to a value has still produced a chart the reader's visual system will systematically misread, because the reader's perception of area does not track true area linearly, a distinction between "the encoding is mathematically correct" and "the encoding is perceptually accurate" that matters directly for regulatory and audit-facing charts.

Third, the Gestalt grouping laws operate automatically and pre-attentively, whether or not the chart designer intended them to, meaning an unintentional visual grouping (three bars placed slightly closer together than the rest, say) will be read by the viewer as a meaningful cluster whether or not the underlying data supports that grouping, a genuine risk of accidentally implying a pattern the data does not contain.

Vignette from the field A pricing team built a bubble chart comparing the relative size of several product segments' premium income, with bubble area scaled proportionally to premium income, a mathematically correct encoding. In a review meeting, several committee members independently and confidently estimated that the largest segment's premium income was roughly double the second-largest, when the true ratio, confirmed against the underlying table, was closer to three-to-one; every reviewer had underestimated the true ratio in the same direction, exactly the systematic perceptual compression Stevens' power law predicts for area-based encodings. Rebuilding the identical comparison as a simple horizontal bar chart, encoding the same values by length rather than area, produced correct ratio estimates from every reviewer on first viewing, without a single reviewer needing to consult the underlying numbers.
Step 3

The mental model

Hold one picture: the Cleveland-McGill ranking as a ladder of encoding types, position and length at the top, colour saturation and volume near the bottom, with the height of each rung on the ladder representing genuine, measured reader accuracy, not designer preference.

Two slogans. Perceived magnitude is not physical magnitude. Stevens' power law says this precisely, with a different distortion exponent for every encoding type, and the vignette's bubble-chart failure is exactly this gap made visible. Grouping happens automatically, whether or not you intended it. The Gestalt laws describe a pre-attentive process, not a conscious reading choice, which is exactly why an accidental visual grouping in a chart is read as meaningful regardless of the designer's intent.

Step 4

The detail

Stevens' power law and the Weber-Fechner law

Stevens' power law relates a stimulus's physical magnitude $\phi$ to its perceived magnitude $\psi$ via $\psi = k\phi^n$, for a constant $k$ and an exponent $n$ specific to the type of stimulus (and, by direct extension, the type of chart encoding). Length is perceived close to linearly, $n \approx 1$, which is precisely why a bar chart's length encoding produces close to accurate perceived ratios. Area is perceived with an exponent typically well below one (commonly cited estimates cluster around $n \approx 0.7$ for area), meaning perceived differences in area systematically compress relative to the true underlying ratio, exactly the effect the vignette's bubble chart demonstrated. This power-law relationship supersedes the older Weber-Fechner law, which proposed a logarithmic rather than a power relationship between stimulus and perception; both describe the same underlying phenomenon, a non-linear, encoding-dependent gap between physical and perceived magnitude, with Stevens' formulation now the more widely used and more empirically supported of the two.

The Cleveland-McGill accuracy ordering

Cleveland and McGill's 1984 study directly and rigorously tested how accurately human subjects could estimate the ratio between two encoded quantities across a range of encoding types, producing the empirically-grounded ranking illustrated in the mental model above: position along a common, aligned scale first, then position along unaligned scales, then length, then angle and slope, then area, then colour and volume, roughly in that order of decreasing accuracy. This ordering is the single most directly actionable piece of evidence this lecture supplies: given a genuine value-comparison task, the ordering identifies which chart type (bar or dot plot over pie chart or bubble chart, for instance) a reader can actually read more accurately, not merely which looks more polished.

Geometric illusions

A family of classic geometric illusions demonstrates that perceived length and size are not read in isolation, but are actively distorted by surrounding visual context. The Müller-Lyer illusion (two equal-length lines with inward- or outward-pointing arrowheads) makes one line appear systematically longer than the other. The Ponzo illusion (two equal-length horizontal lines placed across converging perspective lines) makes the line nearer the convergence point appear longer. The café wall illusion makes parallel, perfectly straight horizontal lines appear to tilt, purely as a function of an offset checkerboard pattern surrounding them. Each of these illusions has a direct chart-design analogue: a chart with an inconsistent baseline, unnecessary decorative elements near an axis, or a busy background pattern behind a data series risks triggering the identical perceptual distortion the illusion demonstrates in isolation, distorting the reader's perception of the actual data being encoded.

Colour illusions

Simultaneous contrast (the identical grey square appears lighter against a dark background and darker against a light one) and the related Mach bands effect (perceived brightness banding at the edge of a gradient, where none physically exists) demonstrate that colour and brightness perception depend heavily on immediate surrounding context, not on the absolute value of a colour or shade in isolation. This has a direct, practical consequence for any chart using a sequential colour gradient to encode a continuous value: the same colour can read as meaningfully different depending on what colours surround it elsewhere in the same figure, a consideration developed further in the colour-and-accessibility lecture that follows this one.

The Gestalt laws

The Gestalt laws describe automatic, pre-attentive grouping principles the human visual system applies to any image. Proximity groups elements that are close together; similarity groups elements sharing colour, shape, or size; closure perceives an incomplete shape as complete; continuity perceives smoothly continuing lines or curves as a single element even where they are visually interrupted. These principles operate whether or not a chart's designer intended them, meaning an unintentional visual cue (bars accidentally spaced slightly differently, a colour repeated across unrelated categories) will be read by a viewer as a deliberate, meaningful grouping, exactly the risk flagged in "why this matters" above.

Step 5

Pitfalls

Encoding a value comparison by area when a bar or dot plot would do Developed directly in the vignette: a mathematically correct area encoding still produces a systematically compressed perceived ratio, exactly as Stevens' power law predicts; the Cleveland-McGill ordering gives a direct, evidence-based reason to prefer position or length whenever a genuine ratio comparison is the point of the chart.
Assuming a "correct" encoding is the same thing as a perceptually accurate one The gap between physical magnitude and perceived magnitude is a property of human perception, not a flaw in the chart's underlying data or calculation; a chart maker cannot fix this gap by double-checking the maths; the fix is choosing a different encoding entirely.
Introducing an unintentional visual grouping via inconsistent spacing or colour The Gestalt laws apply automatically regardless of intent; a chart with accidentally uneven bar spacing or an accidentally repeated colour across otherwise unrelated categories will be read by a viewer as implying a grouping the underlying data does not support.
Ignoring surrounding visual context when choosing a colour gradient Simultaneous contrast means the same colour can read as meaningfully different depending on adjacent colours elsewhere in the figure; testing a colour gradient in isolation, without its actual surrounding context in the final figure, risks missing a distortion that only appears once the full figure is assembled.
Step 6

Synthesis and what's next

Stevens' power law formalises a genuine, measurable gap between a stimulus's physical magnitude and its perceived magnitude, with the distortion varying systematically by encoding type; area and volume are the most distorted, length close to accurate. Cleveland and McGill's empirical ranking, position first, then length, then angle and slope, then area, then colour and volume, translates this directly into an evidence-based chart-selection rule. Classic geometric and colour illusions demonstrate that perception of length, size, and colour is actively, context-dependently constructed rather than a neutral measurement, and the Gestalt laws describe the automatic, pre-attentive grouping every image, chart included, is subject to whether or not the designer intended it.
