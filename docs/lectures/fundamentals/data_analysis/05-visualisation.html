Visualisation with matplotlib and seaborn

A plot is an answer to a question. The question dictates the plot, not the other way around. This lecture is a decision tree from variable types to plot families to the seaborn calls that produce them, plus the accessibility and honesty discipline that separates a chart you can put in a regulatory submission from one you cannot. The same dataset can support five plots and five different verdicts; the discipline is choosing the one that does not lie.

Lecture 5 of 11 Reading time, 70 minutes Part II: looking at data
Step 1

The 30-second version

A plot is honest compression: a small number of pixels chosen to represent a much larger number of values, in a way that preserves the structure that matters and discards the rest. Three things determine which plot is right: the variable types (categorical or continuous, one variable or several), the question being asked (distribution, comparison, relationship, change-over-time), and the audience (an analyst reading code, a regulator reading a PDF). The Python ecosystem provides two complementary libraries: matplotlib is the low-level engine; seaborn is the high-level layer that gives you a publication-ready chart in one call. Almost every analytical chart in this lecture is one seaborn call.

The discipline this lecture trains is refusing to put up a chart that does not survive a sceptic's first question. A bar chart with a non-zero baseline; a colour scheme that fails for colour-blind readers; a time-series with the x-axis sorted alphabetically rather than chronologically; a heatmap with a sequential palette where a diverging one belongs: each is a plot a regulator catches first and worries about second. Visualisation is the most public part of a data analyst's work and the easiest to get subtly wrong.

Feynman test A good plot lets you explain it in one sentence and that sentence is true. A bad plot needs a paragraph to explain or, worse, makes one true sentence and lets the reader infer a different one. The five most common chart sins are all forms of letting the reader infer something not warranted by the data. Pick the plot that resists inference of anything beyond what the data supports.
Where this sits Lecture 04 (EDA) covered the verdicts you reach by looking at data; this lecture covers the pictures that back those verdicts. Together they form Part II of the course. A useful rule: never produce a number without considering whether a picture would be more honest, and never produce a picture without considering whether a sceptical reader would draw a different conclusion than you intend.
Step 2

Why this matters

Three reasons to take visualisation seriously, particularly in a regulated environment. First, most of the people who read your analysis read figures, not code. The model development document goes to credit risk committees, audit teams, and regulators. None of them read the Jupyter notebook. They read the executive summary, the methodology pictures, and the validation charts. A chart that suggests a different conclusion than the prose is a chart that wastes the reader's time and earns the document a closer read.

Second, visualisation is the cheapest available diagnostic for the bugs your numerical EDA missed. Anscombe's quartet (1973) is the canonical demonstration: four datasets with identical means, variances, regression lines, and Pearson correlations to two decimal places, plotted as a noisy line, a parabola, a perfect line plus one outlier, and a vertical strip plus one outlier. Numerical summaries cannot distinguish them; the eye can in seconds. Every credit-risk dataset has its own private Anscombe pattern, and only a plot will find it.

Third, a misleading plot is harder to retract than a misleading number. A wrong number in a paragraph can be corrected with a sentence; a wrong chart sits in a slide deck and is remembered visually long after the prose is forgotten. The investment is twenty minutes of choosing axes, colours, and annotations carefully; the payback is every reader of every version of the document trusting what they see.

Step 3

The mental model

The plot-selection decision tree starts with the variable types and the question. The branches below cover the questions that come up in 95 per cent of analytical work.

A second mental model worth keeping. Matplotlib and seaborn complement each other: seaborn produces the chart with one statistically-aware call, matplotlib customises it (titles, axis labels, legend placement, colours, scales). The typical analytical chart is one seaborn line followed by three or four matplotlib lines. Almost no working credit-risk analyst writes raw matplotlib for the chart itself; almost every analytical chart needs some matplotlib customisation.

Step 4

The detail

The standard imports and a sensible default style

Setup

import pandas as pd import numpy as np import matplotlib.pyplot as plt import seaborn as sns sns.set_theme( context="paper", # "paper" / "notebook" / "talk" / "poster"; affects sizes style="whitegrid", # "darkgrid" / "whitegrid" / "ticks" / "white" / "dark" palette="colorblind", ) plt.rcParams["figure.dpi"] = 110

The context scales font sizes and line widths for the medium: "paper" for journal-style figures, "talk" for slide decks. The palette="colorblind" argument ensures the default colours pass colour-blindness tests; this is the simplest accessibility improvement available and worth setting once at the top of every notebook.

Single-variable distributions

Five plots cover the distribution of one variable: histogram, KDE, boxplot, violin plot, and ECDF. The statistics course's descriptive statistics lecture covers when each is the right choice; below are the seaborn idioms.

Histograms and KDEs

# histogram with KDE overlay sns.histplot(data=df, x="loan_amnt", bins=50, kde=True) plt.xlabel("Loan amount, £") plt.ylabel("Frequency") plt.title("Distribution of loan amounts") plt.show() # split by a category sns.histplot( data=df, x="loan_amnt", hue="loan_grade", element="step", stat="density", common_norm=False, )

Three arguments earn their keep on every histogram. bins: 30-50 is a sensible default; too few smooths out shape, too many is noise. stat="density": when comparing groups of different sizes, normalise each group to a density so the curves are comparable. common_norm=False: with hue and density, each group is normalised separately rather than to the joint distribution, which is what you usually want when comparing distributional shapes.

Boxplots and violin plots

# boxplot for distribution + outliers sns.boxplot(data=df, x="loan_grade", y="loan_amnt", order=list("ABCDEFG")) # violin: shape + quartiles together sns.violinplot(data=df, x="loan_grade", y="loan_amnt", order=list("ABCDEFG")) # with an explicit order, you control the categorical axis order_by_dr = ( df.groupby("loan_intent")["loan_status"].mean() .sort_values(ascending=False).index ) sns.boxplot(data=df, x="loan_intent", y="loan_amnt", order=order_by_dr)

The order argument is the most important and the most often omitted. By default seaborn orders categorical axes alphabetically; for an ordinal categorical (loan grade A-G) you want the natural order, for a nominal categorical (loan intent) you usually want order by something meaningful (frequency, default rate). The third example sorts intents by default rate before plotting; the reader can read the chart top-to-bottom as worst-to-best.

Categorical bar charts

Two functions: countplot for raw counts of one categorical column, barplot for a summary statistic of one numerical column by category.

Count and bar plots

# counts: how many loans in each grade? sns.countplot(data=df, x="loan_grade", order=list("ABCDEFG")) plt.ylabel("Number of loans") # summary by category: mean default rate by grade sns.barplot( data=df, x="loan_grade", y="loan_status", order=list("ABCDEFG"), errorbar="ci", # bootstrap 95% CI on the mean ) plt.ylabel("Default rate") # grouped by two categoricals sns.barplot( data=df, x="loan_grade", y="loan_status", hue="loan_intent", order=list("ABCDEFG"), )

The errorbar="ci" argument adds bootstrap confidence intervals for the mean; errorbar=("ci", 95) sets the level, errorbar=None turns them off, errorbar=("se", 2) shows ±2 standard errors. For credit-risk reports, ±2 SE on a default rate is a more standard convention than 95 per cent CI; pick one and stay consistent across the document.

The zero-baseline rule Bar charts use length to represent value. Length only encodes a ratio if the baseline is zero. A bar chart whose y-axis starts at 0.15 instead of 0 will look like the second bar is twice the height of the first when it is in fact 5 per cent larger. The same chart as a line plot or as a points-and-error-bars plot does not have this problem and is the right move when zero is too far away to be useful. The exception is when you explicitly mark the truncation; do this only when no honest alternative exists.

Two-variable relationships

The relationship between two variables depends on their types. For numerical-numerical, the natural plot is a scatter plot, optionally with a regression line. For categorical-numerical it is a boxplot or violin by category, covered above. For categorical-categorical it is a heatmap of the contingency table.

Scatter and regression plots

# straight scatter sns.scatterplot(data=df, x="person_income", y="loan_amnt", hue="loan_status", alpha=0.4) plt.xscale("log") # income is right-skewed; log scale is honest # scatter with a regression overlay sns.regplot(data=df, x="person_income", y="loan_amnt", scatter_kws={"alpha": 0.3}) # pointwise; with a categorical hue sns.lmplot( data=df, x="person_income", y="loan_amnt", hue="loan_grade", col="cb_person_default_on_file", scatter_kws={"alpha": 0.3}, height=4, )

Three points. The alpha argument lets the eye see density when points overlap; on a 30,000-row scatter, the default solid points produce a black blob and lose every pattern. The log scale on income is the right call whenever a variable spans more than two orders of magnitude; the linear scale wastes the right half of the chart on the long tail. lmplot is regplot with built-in faceting by hue and column: useful when the question is "does this relationship hold within each subgroup".

Heatmaps for contingency and correlation

# default rate by grade x intent (a pivot) heat = df.pivot_table(values="loan_status", index="loan_grade", columns="loan_intent", aggfunc="mean") sns.heatmap(heat, annot=True, fmt=".1%", cmap="RdYlGn_r", center=0.2) plt.title("Default rate by grade and intent") # correlation heatmap corr = df.select_dtypes("number").corr() sns.heatmap(corr, annot=True, fmt=".2f", cmap="RdBu_r", center=0, vmin=-1, vmax=1) plt.title("Pearson correlation matrix")

For correlation heatmaps, use a diverging palette (RdBu_r, coolwarm) centred at zero, with explicit vmin=-1 and vmax=1; this makes positive and negative correlations symmetrically readable and the colour at any cell honest. For frequency or rate heatmaps where zero is the lower bound, use a sequential palette (Blues, YlOrRd, viridis); for rate heatmaps where a portfolio average is the natural midpoint, use diverging centred at the average.

Time-series plots

The natural plot for change-over-time is a line plot, with an optional rolling mean overlay to smooth short-term noise.

Time-series lineplots

# build a monthly default rate ts = ( df.set_index("issue_date").sort_index() ["loan_status"] .resample("ME").mean() .to_frame("monthly_dr") ) ts["rolling_12m"] = ts["monthly_dr"].rolling(12, min_periods=6).mean() fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 4)) sns.lineplot(data=ts, x=ts.index, y="monthly_dr", ax=ax, label="Monthly", alpha=0.5) sns.lineplot(data=ts, x=ts.index, y="rolling_12m", ax=ax, label="12-month rolling") ax.set_ylabel("Default rate") ax.set_xlabel("Issue date") ax.set_ylim(bottom=0) # zero baseline for a rate ax.set_title("Monthly default rate, with 12-month rolling")

The monthly line is noisy; the rolling overlay shows the trend. The set_ylim(bottom=0) call enforces the zero baseline that is honest for a rate. The alpha=0.5 on the monthly line lets the rolling line read as the headline; the eye picks up the smoother line first and uses the noisy line as context.

Multi-variable plots

Three plots cover the case of more than two variables. The pairplot is a grid of bivariate scatters with marginal histograms on the diagonal; useful for spotting structure across a small number of numerical variables. The FacetGrid takes a base plot and replicates it across the values of one or two categorical variables; useful for showing how a relationship varies across groups. The correlation heatmap (covered above) condenses pairwise relationships into one figure.

Pairplot and faceting

# pairplot of the four most important numerical predictors sns.pairplot( df[["loan_amnt", "person_income", "loan_int_rate", "loan_percent_income", "loan_status"]], hue="loan_status", diag_kind="kde", height=2.2, plot_kws={"alpha": 0.4}, ) # faceted histogram of loan amount by intent g = sns.FacetGrid(df, col="loan_intent", col_wrap=3, height=3) g.map_dataframe(sns.histplot, x="loan_amnt", bins=30) g.set_titles("{col_name}")

The pairplot is expensive on large datasets; on more than 5 columns or 100,000 rows it is slow and visually unreadable. The right move is to subset to the top 4-6 predictors and a sample of 5,000-20,000 rows. The FacetGrid is the manual equivalent: it lets you replicate any seaborn call across categories with one extra line.

Customisations: titles, labels, scales, legends

Every analytical chart needs the same four customisations: a title that states the question, axis labels with units, a legend in a position that does not overlap the data, and an appropriate scale (linear, log, or otherwise). Five matplotlib lines cover all four.

The customisation block

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4)) sns.lineplot(data=ts, x=ts.index, y="rolling_12m", ax=ax) ax.set_title("Twelve-month rolling default rate, 2019-2024") ax.set_xlabel("Issue date") ax.set_ylabel("Default rate, %") ax.yaxis.set_major_formatter(lambda x, _: f"{x:.0%}") # format y as percent ax.set_ylim(0, 0.5) ax.grid(True, linestyle=":", alpha=0.5) ax.legend(loc="upper left", frameon=False) fig.tight_layout() plt.savefig("figures/dr_rolling.pdf", bbox_inches="tight") # for the report

tight_layout prevents labels from being cut off; bbox_inches="tight" on save does the same for the saved file. Save figures as PDF for documents and as PNG (with dpi=200) for slide decks. The percent formatter on the y-axis (f"{x:.0%}") is the cleanest way to convert 0.15 to "15%" in tick labels; the alternative of multiplying the data by 100 and labelling the axis "%" is a frequent source of off-by-100 bugs downstream.

Step 5

Accessibility and honesty

Five rules separate a chart that survives sceptical reading from one that does not. None of them are taste; all of them are caught by anyone who looks for them.

First, colour-blind-safe palettes. Roughly 8 per cent of men and 0.5 per cent of women have some form of red-green colour blindness. The default matplotlib palette has been colour-blind-safe since 2.0, but many lecture decks still use the old jet or rainbow palettes, neither of which work. Set palette="colorblind" at the top of the notebook and the problem disappears. For diverging palettes prefer RdBu_r over RdYlGn_r; the green-red axis fails for the most common form of colour-blindness.

Second, sequential vs diverging vs categorical palettes, matched to the data. Sequential palettes (Blues, viridis, YlOrRd) are for variables that have an order from low to high. Diverging palettes (RdBu_r, coolwarm) are for variables that have a meaningful midpoint and signed deviation (positive or negative). Categorical palettes (tab10, Set2, colorblind) are for nominal variables with no order. Using the wrong family is the most common chart sin and the easiest to catch: a categorical palette on an ordinal variable looks unordered, a sequential palette on a diverging variable hides the zero, a diverging palette on a strictly positive variable wastes half the colour range.

Third, the zero baseline rule for bar charts, covered above. Bar length encodes ratio; if you cannot use a zero baseline, switch to a different chart type (a points-and-error-bars plot, a line plot, a Cleveland dot plot). The exception is a deliberately truncated chart with an explicit break or annotation; do this only when no honest alternative works.

Fourth, category ordering by value, not by alphabet. Default alphabetical ordering on a categorical axis is the second most common chart sin. The right order is whatever encodes the question: for nominal categories, order by the y-axis value (sorted bars are much easier to read); for ordinal categories, the natural order; for time, chronological. Seaborn's order= argument takes a list and is the cure.

Fifth, chart-as-evidence, not chart-as-decoration. Every chart in a report should be there because it answers a specific question. A chart that does not answer a question is decoration and should be cut. The discipline is to write the caption first (one sentence stating what the chart shows) and only then produce the chart that supports the caption. A caption you cannot write is a chart you do not need.

What is wrong with this chart A bar chart of default rate by region with the y-axis starting at 18 per cent (so the tallest bar is 23 per cent and the shortest is 19 per cent). The visual impression is that one region is roughly 5x worse than another; the actual ratio is 1.2x. The chart is technically correct (every bar's height is its value relative to the baseline) and rhetorically dishonest (the reader is led to a wrong conclusion). The fix: zero baseline, or switch to a Cleveland dot plot, or annotate every bar with its exact value and label the truncation explicitly. The bar chart with hidden truncation is the textbook example of a misleading visualisation and the most common sin in junior-analyst slide decks.
Step 6

Worked example: a six-figure portfolio dashboard

A typical credit-risk monitoring deck opens with a six-figure summary of the loan book. Each figure answers one question and uses the appropriate plot from the decision tree. The full code is below; together it is roughly thirty lines for six publication-quality figures.

Figure 1: composition of the book

Question: how is the portfolio distributed across grades?

sns.countplot(data=df, x="loan_grade", order=list("ABCDEFG"), color="steelblue") plt.title("Number of loans by grade") plt.ylabel("Number of loans") plt.xlabel("Loan grade")

A bar chart with a zero baseline (countplot starts at zero by construction) and an explicit grade order. One question, one chart.

Figure 2: default rate by grade

Question: does the grade actually rank-order risk?

sns.barplot( data=df, x="loan_grade", y="loan_status", order=list("ABCDEFG"), errorbar=("se", 2), color="firebrick", ) plt.title("Default rate by grade, with ±2 SE") plt.ylabel("Default rate") plt.gca().yaxis.set_major_formatter(lambda x, _: f"{x:.0%}")

The error bars matter here: for sparsely-populated grades (F, G) the confidence around the default rate is wide, and the chart should show that. Ignoring uncertainty produces a misleading picture of how well-distinguished the worst grades are.

Figure 3: distribution of loan amounts by purpose

Question: is loan amount substantially different across purposes?

order = df.groupby("loan_intent")["loan_amnt"].median().sort_values().index sns.boxplot(data=df, x="loan_intent", y="loan_amnt", order=order, fliersize=2) plt.xticks(rotation=30, ha="right") plt.title("Loan amount by purpose") plt.ylabel("Loan amount, £")

Boxplot rather than barplot because the question is about the distribution, not just the average. Categories ordered by median, smallest on the left. The rotated x-tick labels avoid overlap.

Figure 4: income vs loan amount, coloured by default

Question: is there a region of (income, loan-amount) space where defaults concentrate?

sns.scatterplot( data=df.sample(5_000, random_state=0), x="person_income", y="loan_amnt", hue="loan_status", alpha=0.4, palette={0: "steelblue", 1: "firebrick"}, ) plt.xscale("log") plt.title("Income vs loan amount, by default status") plt.xlabel("Income (£, log scale)") plt.ylabel("Loan amount, £")

A sample of 5,000 is plotted rather than the full 32,000 to keep the scatter readable. The log x-axis lets the right tail of income breathe rather than crowding everything against the left edge. Defaulted loans (firebrick) cluster at high loan-to-income ratios, visible as a band of red above the blue cloud.

Figure 5: correlation heatmap

Question: which predictors are redundant?

corr = df.select_dtypes("number").corr() sns.heatmap(corr, annot=True, fmt=".2f", cmap="RdBu_r", center=0, vmin=-1, vmax=1, square=True) plt.title("Pearson correlations between numerical features")

Diverging palette centred at zero with hard limits at ±1, so a cell of colour 'medium red' has the same magnitude wherever it appears. The annotation overlay is essential for any heatmap that will end up in a report.

Figure 6: default rate over time

Question: is the default rate trending up or down?

ts = ( df.set_index("issue_date").sort_index() ["loan_status"].resample("ME").mean().to_frame("monthly_dr") ) ts["rolling_12m"] = ts["monthly_dr"].rolling(12, min_periods=6).mean() fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4)) sns.lineplot(data=ts, x=ts.index, y="monthly_dr", ax=ax, label="Monthly", alpha=0.4, color="grey") sns.lineplot(data=ts, x=ts.index, y="rolling_12m", ax=ax, label="12-month rolling", color="firebrick") ax.set_ylim(0, None) ax.yaxis.set_major_formatter(lambda x, _: f"{x:.0%}") ax.set_title("Default rate over time") ax.set_ylabel("Default rate") ax.set_xlabel("Issue date") ax.legend(frameon=False)

The pair of monthly and rolling lines is the standard pattern: noisy line in grey at low alpha, smoothed line in a stronger colour over the top. The eye reads the rolling line as the headline and the monthly as supporting detail. The zero baseline is enforced; the percent-formatted y-axis is the convention.

The six figures together fit on one slide and answer the six questions any portfolio review opens with. The discipline of asking the question before drawing the chart is what makes the deck readable: a reader can skim the titles and know what the analysis claims without reading a word of the methodology section.

Step 7

Synthesis and what's next

You now have the visualisation toolkit. The plot-selection decision tree takes you from question and variable types to seaborn call. The five common plot families (distribution, comparison, relationship, change-over-time, multi-variable) cover almost every analytical chart you will produce. Customisations come last and follow a standard pattern: title, axis labels with units, legend placement, scale, zero baseline where applicable, category order by value. The five accessibility-and-honesty rules (colour-blind palettes, palette type matched to data, zero baseline for bars, ordering by value, chart-as-evidence) are the discipline that separates a chart you can put in a regulatory submission from one you cannot.

This is the practical, API-level half of the course's visualisation coverage; you have the dataframe layer (Lectures 01-03), the descriptive discipline (Lecture 04), and this lecture's communication discipline. The visualisation design sub-module that follows takes the "which seaborn call" decisions made here and develops the reasoning underneath them: the perceptual science behind why some encodings are read more accurately than others, colour and accessibility, audience and narrative, and the taxonomy-versus-grammar structural choice. Beyond that sub-module, the natural next courses are: the statistics track to turn descriptive verdicts into inferential ones, the machine learning track to turn descriptive features into predictive models, and the business intelligence track to turn one-off charts into recurring dashboards. All three rely on the data analysis layer this course teaches; none of them substitute for it.
