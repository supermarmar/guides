Multi-armed bandits

The simplest possible reinforcement-learning problem strips away almost everything: one state, a fixed set of actions, and no transitions between states at all, action taken, reward received, repeat. Even at this level of simplicity, one genuinely new tension appears that no earlier lecture in this course has had to confront: whether to keep doing what has worked so far, or to spend effort finding out whether something else might work better.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A multi-armed bandit is a decision problem with $k$ possible actions ("arms"), each generating a reward drawn from its own, unknown distribution; there is a single state, no transitions, and the only question is which arm to pull at each of a sequence of independent rounds. The action-value $q_*(a)$ of an arm $a$ is the true expected reward from pulling it, unknown to the agent and estimated from experience as $Q_t(a)$, the sample average reward observed from $a$ up to round $t$. The greedy action at any point is $\arg\max_a Q_t(a)$, the arm with the highest current estimate; always taking the greedy action is called exploitation. But $Q_t(a)$ is only an estimate, and an arm that looks worse purely by chance early on may never get pulled again under a purely greedy strategy, permanently locking in a wrong belief; occasionally choosing a non-greedy arm anyway, to improve the estimates, is called exploration. $\varepsilon$-greedy is the simplest resolution: act greedily with probability $1-\varepsilon$, and pick a uniformly random arm with probability $\varepsilon$, guaranteeing every arm keeps getting sampled forever while still exploiting the best-known arm most of the time.

Feynman test Imagine choosing which of several coffee shops to visit each morning, with no way to know in advance which one makes the best coffee, only your own accumulating experience of how good each cup turned out to be. Always returning to whichever shop happened to make the best cup so far is tempting, but if a genuinely excellent shop simply had an off day the very first time you tried it, you would never give it a second chance under a policy that always returns to the current favourite, and you would never find out you were missing a better option. Occasionally trying a shop you are not currently favouring, purely to keep your information about it up to date, is exploration, and the multi-armed bandit is the simplest possible mathematical version of exactly this everyday trade-off.
Where this sits in the ML world This lecture and the next build the two core ideas of reinforcement learning, exploration-exploitation and incremental value updating, inside the simplest setting where they can be studied in complete isolation, with no states, no transitions, and no delayed consequences at all. Every later lecture in this sub-module reintroduces one additional piece of complexity (multiple states, unknown transition dynamics, function approximation) on top of the foundation built here.
Step 2

Why this matters

Three reasons the bandit setting earns a dedicated lecture rather than being folded directly into the full MDP framework.

First, the explore-exploit trade-off is a genuinely new concept that nothing earlier in this course required: every supervised model assumed the training data was already given, with no notion of the model itself choosing which data to collect next, while a bandit agent's own choices determine which arms get more data and which get starved of it, a feedback loop worth understanding in complete isolation before any additional complexity is added.

Second, bandit problems are directly, immediately applicable to a recurring actuarial and business scenario: A/B testing a handful of candidate pricing rules, marketing messages, or claims-handling procedures against each other is exactly a multi-armed bandit, and a bandit algorithm that adapts allocation towards the better-performing option during the test itself is a genuine improvement over a fixed 50/50 split held rigidly for the full test duration, since it spends less of the testing budget on the option turning out to perform worse.

Third, every genuinely harder problem in this sub-module still contains an explore-exploit trade-off buried inside it, and having a name and clean intuition for it from the simplest possible case makes it immediately recognisable when it resurfaces, considerably more entangled, inside the full Markov decision process framework two lectures from now.

Vignette from the field A digital-channel team running an A/B test of three candidate quote-page layouts used a fixed, equal three-way traffic split for the entire planned test duration, a standard and defensible experimental design. Partway through the test, one layout was already showing a clearly, statistically significantly worse conversion rate than the other two, yet the fixed-split design continued sending a full third of all traffic to it for the remainder of the test purely to preserve the pre-specified design, a real, quantifiable cost in foregone conversions. A subsequent test on a similar decision used an $\varepsilon$-greedy allocation instead, gradually shifting more traffic towards whichever layout was currently estimated best while still reserving a small, fixed exploration probability for the others; the team estimated, from the difference in aggregate conversion between the two testing approaches, that the adaptive allocation recovered a meaningful fraction of the fixed-split design's foregone conversions without materially compromising the statistical confidence in the final result.
Step 3

The mental model

Hold one picture: several arms with unknown, overlapping reward distributions, current estimates that are noisy approximations to the truth, and an $\varepsilon$-greedy policy that mostly pulls the current best-estimated arm but keeps a standing, permanent probability of sampling any other arm to keep every estimate honest.

Two slogans. A greedy policy can lock in a wrong belief forever. Once an arm stops being pulled, its estimate stops updating, and nothing in a purely greedy rule ever revisits that decision. Exploration is the price paid for keeping every estimate honest. $\varepsilon$-greedy's random exploration probability is not wasted effort, it is the specific mechanism that guarantees the agent's beliefs eventually converge to the truth rather than to whatever the first few rounds happened to suggest.

Step 4

The detail

Action-values and the sample-average estimate

Each arm $a$ has a true, unknown expected reward $q_*(a) = \mathbb{E}[R \mid A = a]$. The agent's estimate after $t$ rounds is the sample average of rewards actually observed from that arm,

$$Q_t(a) = \frac{\text{sum of rewards when } a \text{ taken prior to } t}{\text{number of times } a \text{ taken prior to } t}.$$

By the law of large numbers, $Q_t(a) \to q_*(a)$ as the number of times $a$ is pulled grows, provided $a$ continues to be pulled; an arm that stops being pulled has its estimate frozen at whatever value it last happened to reach, which is precisely the mechanism behind the mental model's locked-in wrong belief.

Greedy action selection

The greedy action at round $t$ is $A_t = \arg\max_a Q_t(a)$, always choosing whichever arm currently has the highest estimated value. Acting greedily forever is pure exploitation: it makes the best use of current information but never deliberately gathers more, and, since $Q_t(a)$ is only ever a noisy estimate of $q_*(a)$, a purely greedy policy risks converging permanently to a suboptimal arm whenever early noise happens to favour the wrong one, exactly the risk the mental model illustrates.

$\varepsilon$-greedy exploration

The $\varepsilon$-greedy policy resolves this by acting greedily with probability $1-\varepsilon$ and choosing an arm uniformly at random (including, with some small probability, the currently-best arm again) with probability $\varepsilon$, for a small fixed $\varepsilon \in (0,1)$, commonly in the region of 0.01 to 0.1. Because every arm has a guaranteed, non-zero probability of being sampled at every round forever, $Q_t(a) \to q_*(a)$ for every arm $a$, not just the ones the greedy rule happens to favour, which is exactly the guarantee pure greedy action selection does not provide. This is also the source of the persistent trade-off: a larger $\varepsilon$ corrects wrong early beliefs faster but sacrifices more reward to exploration that turns out, in retrospect, to have been unnecessary once the true best arm is well identified; a smaller $\varepsilon$ exploits more efficiently once beliefs are accurate but risks a slower, more fragile correction of an unlucky early estimate.

Decaying $\varepsilon$

A fixed $\varepsilon$ continues sacrificing a constant fraction of rounds to exploration even after the agent's estimates have become highly accurate, which is wasteful once little remains to be learned. A decaying $\varepsilon$ schedule, shrinking $\varepsilon$ towards zero as the number of rounds grows (for instance $\varepsilon_t = 1/t$ or a similar decreasing schedule), front-loads exploration early, when estimates are least reliable and the value of new information is highest, and shifts towards pure exploitation later, when estimates are already close to the truth and further exploration has comparatively little left to offer; this is the standard refinement used whenever the total number of rounds is large enough for the distinction to matter in practice.

Initialisation effects

The value each $Q_1(a)$ is initialised to before any arm has been pulled at all has a genuine, sometimes large, effect on early behaviour. Optimistic initialisation, starting every $Q_1(a)$ deliberately higher than any reward the agent expects to actually observe, encourages exploration automatically and without any explicit $\varepsilon$ at all: every arm's first few pulls will, almost by construction, disappoint relative to its optimistic initial estimate, driving the agent to keep trying under-sampled arms until their estimates have been dragged down to something realistic, a simple and elegant alternative exploration mechanism worth knowing alongside $\varepsilon$-greedy.

Diagnosing an undecided algorithm

A bandit algorithm whose arm-selection frequencies remain roughly equal across all arms for a long stretch of rounds, with no arm's selection frequency clearly pulling away from the others, is a direct, visible diagnostic that the algorithm has not yet meaningfully distinguished between the arms' true values, either because the true values are genuinely close together (in which case the algorithm is behaving correctly, there is little to distinguish) or because $\varepsilon$ is too large relative to how quickly the true differences would otherwise become apparent. Plotting cumulative arm-selection counts over the course of a run is the standard, cheap diagnostic for telling these two cases apart.

Step 5

Pitfalls

Running a purely greedy policy and calling it "optimised" A purely greedy policy exploits perfectly given its current beliefs but has no mechanism at all for correcting a wrong belief formed from unlucky early rounds; the fixed-split A/B test in the vignette is the safer, if less efficient, alternative precisely because it does not suffer this specific failure, and an adaptive bandit algorithm needs its own explicit exploration mechanism to avoid it.
Choosing $\varepsilon$ without considering the total number of rounds available A fixed $\varepsilon$ that is sensible for a short test can waste a large fraction of a long test's reward on unnecessary continued exploration well after the best arm is already clear; a decaying schedule is the standard fix whenever the total round count is large enough for this to matter.
Ending a fixed-allocation A/B test rigidly once a clear winner has emerged mid-test Developed directly in the vignette: preserving a pre-specified fixed split for its own sake, once one option is already showing a clear and statistically significant disadvantage, has a real, quantifiable opportunity cost; an adaptive bandit-style allocation is a genuine, principled improvement precisely in this situation, though it does require care to preserve valid statistical inference on the final comparison.
Treating a bandit's stationary-reward assumption as always safe to assume The basic bandit setup assumes each arm's true reward distribution $q_*(a)$ is fixed over time; if the underlying process is genuinely non-stationary (a competitor's pricing changes mid-test, say), a sample average computed over the whole history mixes old and new information inappropriately, and a recency-weighted estimate (an exponentially weighted average rather than a plain sample mean) is the standard adjustment, directly foreshadowing the incremental-update machinery of the next lecture.
Step 6

Synthesis and what's next

A multi-armed bandit is the simplest possible reinforcement-learning problem: one state, a fixed set of arms, no transitions, only the accumulating question of which arm's true expected reward is highest. Action-values $Q_t(a)$ are sample-average estimates of the true, unknown $q_*(a)$, and a purely greedy policy risks locking in a wrong belief formed from unlucky early rounds, since an arm that stops being pulled never gets its estimate corrected. $\varepsilon$-greedy resolves this by keeping a standing, permanent exploration probability, with a decaying schedule as the standard refinement once enough rounds are available, and optimistic initialisation as an elegant alternative exploration mechanism requiring no explicit $\varepsilon$ at all.

The next lecture develops the incremental-update machinery hinted at in this lecture's non-stationarity pitfall, rewriting the sample-average update as a running, constant-memory computation and introducing the temporal-difference idea that becomes the backbone of every algorithm in the rest of this sub-module, before lecture 4 finally introduces multiple states and the transitions this lecture has deliberately avoided.
