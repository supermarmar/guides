Transformers

The previous lecture closed with two specific RNN failures: an imperfect long-range memory and a sequential computation that cannot be parallelised across time. The transformer answers both with a single mechanism, self-attention, that lets every position in a sequence look directly at every other position, computed for every position simultaneously. This is the architecture behind every modern large language model and, in its tabular adaptation, an increasingly credible alternative to gradient boosting for actuarial and credit pricing data.

Step 1

The 30-second version

The attention mechanism computes, for every position in a sequence, a weighted average of every other position's information, with the weights themselves learned from the data rather than fixed in advance. Formally, three matrices are derived from the input, a query $Q$, a key $K$, and a value $V$, and the scaled dot-product attention is $H = \operatorname{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{q})\,V$: the query at each position is compared (by dot product) against every position's key to produce a set of attention weights, normalised by softmax to sum to one, and those weights determine how much of each position's value contributes to the output at the querying position. A transformer layer wraps this attention head in a residual (skip) connection, a normalisation step, and a position-wise feed-forward layer, and stacks several such layers, optionally with multiple attention heads operating in parallel to capture different kinds of relationships simultaneously. Because the mechanism has no inherent notion of sequence order (permuting the input positions permutes the output identically), a positional encoding is added to the input before the first layer, and a causal mask (setting attention weights to zero for future positions) is used whenever the model must not see ahead, as in language generation. The critical computational property, relative to the previous lecture's RNN, is that every position's attention output can be computed simultaneously, in parallel, rather than one step at a time, at the cost of a computation that scales quadratically, rather than linearly, in sequence length.

Feynman test Imagine every word in a sentence holding up a question ("what modifies me?"), a label describing itself ("I am a noun, I am about a location"), and a piece of information it carries. Attention lets every word compare its question against every other word's label, decide how relevant each other word is, and then blend in a weighted mixture of everyone's information accordingly, all at once, for every word simultaneously. An RNN, by contrast, could only pass information along one word at a time, forgetting a little at each step. Attention is direct, parallel access from every position to every other position, with the model itself learning which positions are worth attending to.
Where this sits in the ML world This is the single most consequential architecture in the sub-module and the direct prerequisite for the large-language-model material later in this course. It builds on the word embeddings from lecture 5 (the standard input to a sequential transformer) and closes the general-purpose architecture arc that began with CNNs and RNNs; the next lecture in this sub-module adapts the identical mechanism to ordinary tabular covariate rows.
Step 2

Why this matters

Three reasons the transformer earns the depth of treatment this lecture gives it.

First, it is, without serious competition, the dominant architecture in modern deep learning, underlying every major language model, most modern image models, and an increasing share of tabular and time-series models; a modeller who cannot read a transformer's architecture diagram is locked out of reading the large majority of current deep-learning literature.

Second, the parallelism is not a minor implementation detail, it is the property that made the current scale of language modelling economically feasible. An RNN's inherently sequential computation caps how much a larger GPU cluster can speed up training; a transformer's parallel-across-position computation lets training throughput scale with available hardware in a way recurrence never could, which is the direct, practical reason model sizes and training corpora grew as fast as they did once transformers displaced RNNs.

Third, the tabular adaptation genuinely competes with gradient boosting on actuarial pricing data, and knowing the attention mechanism in enough depth to understand what a "feature tokeniser transformer" is actually doing, rather than treating it as an unexplained black box, is a prerequisite for evaluating whether a vendor's tabular-transformer pricing model deserves the same scrutiny a GLM or GBM would receive.

Vignette from the field A vendor proposed replacing an insurer's incumbent GBM pricing model with a transformer-based tabular model, presenting an attention-weight visualisation as an interpretability feature: for a given policy, the visualisation showed which other covariates the model "attended to" most strongly when producing its prediction. The insurer's validation team, familiar with the mechanics developed in this lecture, correctly identified that raw attention weights are not, on their own, a rigorous feature-attribution method (a high attention weight does not guarantee a large causal contribution to the output, a distinction the model-interpretation sub-module's SHAP material makes precise for exactly this reason) and required a proper SHAP-based explanation alongside the attention visualisation before the model could proceed to a full validation cycle.
Step 3

The mental model

Hold one picture: every query position compares itself against every key position via a dot product, producing a score matrix that softmax turns into attention weights, which then blend the value vectors.

Two slogans. Attention is a learned, content-based lookup, not a fixed rule. Which positions matter to which other positions is discovered from data via the query and key projections, not hand-specified the way a convolution's local window or an RNN's sequential order is. Every position sees every other position, all at once. This is the direct structural fix for both of the previous lecture's RNN limitations: no information has to survive a long chain of sequential updates to reach a distant position, and every position's computation can proceed in parallel.

Step 4

The detail

Scaled dot-product attention

Given three matrices $Q, K, V \in \mathbb{R}^{t \times q}$ (the query, key, and value, each a linear projection of the input sequence, derived via a time-distributed feed-forward layer applied identically at every position), the attention head is

$$H = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{q}}\right) V \in \mathbb{R}^{t \times q},$$

where the softmax is applied row-wise to the matrix $A = QK^\top/\sqrt{q} \in \mathbb{R}^{t\times t}$ of raw attention scores, so each row of the resulting weight matrix sums to one. Writing $Q = [q_1, \ldots, q_t]$ and $K = [k_1, \ldots, k_t]$ with $q_u, k_s \in \mathbb{R}^q$, the score between query position $u$ and key position $s$ is $a_{u,s} = \langle q_u, k_s\rangle / \sqrt{q}$: if the query at position $u$ points in a similar direction to the key at position $s$, the two share a large dot product and $s$ receives a large attention weight from $u$, meaning the value at position $s$ contributes heavily to the output at position $u$. The $1/\sqrt{q}$ scaling keeps the dot products from growing too large in magnitude as the embedding dimension $q$ increases, which would otherwise push the softmax into a saturated regime with vanishing gradients, echoing the saturation concern lecture 1 raised for ordinary activation functions.

The transformer layer

A full transformer layer wraps the attention head in a residual connection and follows it with a normalisation step and a position-wise feed-forward layer, itself wrapped in a second residual connection:

$$z^{\text{skip}}(X_{1:t}) = X_{1:t} + H(X_{1:t}),$$ $$z^{\text{trans}}(X_{1:t}) = z^{\text{skip}}(X_{1:t}) + z^{\text{t-FNN}}\big(z^{\text{norm}}(z^{\text{skip}}(X_{1:t}))\big),$$

where $z^{\text{t-FNN}}$ is a time-distributed feed-forward layer (the identical small FNN applied independently and identically at every sequence position, exactly as a convolutional filter is applied identically at every spatial position) and $z^{\text{norm}}$ is a normalisation step. Layer normalisation, the standard choice inside a transformer, normalises across the feature dimension for each sequence position independently (as opposed to batch normalisation, which normalises across the batch dimension for a fixed feature), a choice that matters specifically because sequence lengths vary across a batch in a way that makes batch statistics awkward to define consistently, a problem layer normalisation sidesteps by never needing to look across the batch at all. The residual (skip) connections, directly analogous to the identity shortcut in a residual network, give the gradient a direct path around the attention and feed-forward sub-layers, mitigating the vanishing-gradient concern that motivated much of the previous lecture.

Multi-head attention

A single attention head learns one notion of "relevance" between positions; multi-head attention runs several attention heads in parallel, each with its own independently learned query, key, and value projections, so different heads can specialise in different kinds of relationships (one head might learn to track short-range local dependencies, another long-range thematic ones). For $n_h$ heads, each producing $H_j \in \mathbb{R}^{t\times q}$,

$$H^{\text{MH}}(X_{1:t}) = \operatorname{Concat}\big(H_1(X_{1:t}), \ldots, H_{n_h}(X_{1:t})\big)\, W^O,$$

with an output projection $W^O$ combining the concatenated heads back down to the model's working dimension. Multiple transformer layers are then stacked sequentially (each processing the output of the one before), while multiple attention heads within a layer operate in parallel on the identical input, a distinction worth keeping straight: depth (stacked layers) and width (parallel heads within a layer) are two separate architectural knobs.

Positional encoding

The attention mechanism as described has no inherent notion of sequence order: permute the rows of $X_{1:t}$, and the attention head's output rows permute identically, with no information about which position was originally first, second, or last. This is a genuine structural gap for any task where order matters (word order in a sentence, the temporal order of a claims-development history), and it is closed by adding a positional encoding, a vector representing each position's location, to the input embeddings before the first transformer layer. Vaswani et al.'s original construction used fixed sine and cosine functions of the position at different frequencies; many subsequent architectures instead use a learned positional embedding, a dense vector per position learned during training exactly as an entity embedding is learned per categorical level (lecture 4), which the tabular-transformer lecture next in this sub-module adopts for exactly that reason.

Causal masking

For tasks where the model must not be allowed to see future positions when producing an output at a given position, most obviously generating text one word at a time, a causal mask is applied to the raw attention scores before the softmax, setting $a_{u,s}$ to $-\infty$ (so that, after softmax, the corresponding attention weight is exactly zero) whenever $s > u$: the query at position $u$ is prevented from attending to any key at a later position $s$. This is essential for auto-regressive generation (developed further in the large-language-model material later in this course) and is simply omitted for tasks (most standard tabular applications, most sentence-classification tasks) where the entire input is available at once and no positional causality constraint applies.

An actuarial analogy for causal masking Causal masking is directly analogous to the upper-left triangle of an actuarial claims-development triangle: when projecting future development, an actuary uses only the observed, upper-left entries and never the lower-right entries that have not yet been observed. A causally masked transformer, predicting the next token in a sequence, is restricted in exactly the identical way, to information that has already "developed" by the position it is currently predicting, and the analogy extends naturally: just as an error in an early development factor propagates through the rest of a claims triangle, an error in an early generated token can compound through everything a language model generates afterwards.

Attention is not, by itself, a rigorous explanation method

It is worth being precise about a point the vignette in section 2 raised. A large attention weight from position $u$ to position $s$ means the model found the content at $s$ useful, via its learned query-key comparison, for computing the output at $u$; it is a legitimate diagnostic signal, but it is not the same statement as "position $s$ caused a large share of the output at $u$" in the additive, axiomatically grounded sense SHAP (model-interpretation sub-module) provides. Attention weights can be high for positions whose value contribution is nonetheless small, and vice versa, because the attention weight and the value magnitude are two separate quantities multiplied together in the final output; a rigorous attribution claim needs to account for both, which is exactly what a properly applied SHAP analysis does and a raw attention-weight visualisation, on its own, does not.

Step 5

Pitfalls

Treating raw attention weights as a validated explanation As the vignette illustrates, a high attention weight is suggestive but is not, by itself, a rigorous causal or additive attribution of the model's output to a specific input position; pairing an attention visualisation with a proper SHAP analysis, rather than presenting the attention weights alone, is the standard a serious model validation should hold a vendor to.
Omitting positional encoding and expecting order-sensitive performance Without a positional encoding, a transformer genuinely cannot distinguish "the claim was paid, not disputed" from a scrambled version of the identical words, because the attention mechanism itself is permutation-equivariant. Any application where order carries meaning, essentially all natural-language and time-series applications, needs positional encoding as a first-class architectural component, not an afterthought.
Forgetting the causal mask in a generative setting Training a next-token-prediction model without a causal mask lets the attention mechanism see the very token it is being asked to predict, appearing later in the identical sequence; the model then trivially "cheats" during training and fails catastrophically at inference time, when the future tokens genuinely do not exist yet. This is a correctness bug, not a performance nicety, and it is specific to auto-regressive generation; ordinary classification or regression tasks over a complete, already-observed sequence do not need a causal mask at all.
Ignoring the quadratic cost of attention on long sequences The $O(t^2)$ cost of computing the full $t \times t$ attention matrix becomes the binding computational constraint for long sequences (long documents, long claims-development histories), a cost an RNN's linear-in-$t$ computation does not share; for genuinely long sequences, sparse-attention or windowed-attention variants, or simply truncating the input, are standard mitigations worth considering deliberately rather than assuming a standard transformer scales to arbitrary sequence length.
Step 6

Synthesis and what's next

Scaled dot-product attention lets every sequence position query every other position directly, via a learned comparison of query and key vectors, and blend in a weighted combination of every position's value; a transformer layer wraps this in residual connections, layer normalisation, and a position-wise feed-forward layer, and stacks several such layers, optionally with multiple parallel attention heads per layer. Positional encoding restores the order information the attention mechanism itself discards; causal masking enforces that a position cannot see the future, essential for auto-regressive generation. The mechanism directly addresses both of the previous lecture's RNN weaknesses, an imperfect long-range memory (every position has direct access to every other, with no sequential decay) and a sequential computation bottleneck (every position's attention output computes in parallel), at the cost of a quadratic-in-sequence-length computational bill.

The next, closing lecture of this sub-module adapts the identical mechanism to ordinary tabular covariate rows, where there is no natural sequence order at all, via the feature tokeniser transformation and a classification token that summarises the row for prediction, the architecture this course's actuarial-applications module later extends into the credibility transformer.
