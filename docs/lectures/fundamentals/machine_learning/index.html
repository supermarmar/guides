Machine learning

A sixty-two-lecture course on machine learning for the working quantitative modeller. Twelve sub-modules trace one full pipeline, a calibration stage, and a closing arc on generative AI, language models, and decision-making: prepare the features, split the data, fit a model, tune it, evaluate it, explain it, check that its predicted levels are correct, model the data's own distribution well enough to sample from it and reason about what a foundation model can and cannot be trusted to do, and, finally, learn to act in a way that changes the process itself rather than merely forecasting it. Theory is derived in full but the centre of gravity is practice: every algorithm gets a worked example, every metric a credit-risk reading, every modelling choice a clear trade-off. Assumes the statistics track (in particular descriptive statistics, probability, inference, GLMs, and the exponential dispersion family) as a prerequisite; assumes Python at the level of the data analysis track.

The ML world map

Machine learning is not one subject. It is a pipeline of six stages, with the modelling stage itself fanning out into three algorithmic families. Every lecture in this course can be located on the map below, and every later lecture should be read as "the stage to the left, with one extra concern bolted on". Putting the map up front is the difference between memorising algorithms and understanding why the workflow is shaped the way it is.

Three observations follow from the map. First, the pipeline is sequential and leakage flows downstream. A leak at the feature stage (a target-encoded variable computed without holding out the test set) corrupts every later stage; an honest evaluation in stage 5 is the only way to detect it, and by then it is expensive to fix. Second, the three modelling families are interchangeable for the same problem. A credit decision can be made by a logistic regression, a gradient-boosted tree ensemble, or a neural network; choosing between them is a trade between interpretability, performance, and the price of a wrong answer. Third, the bias-variance trade-off is the only universal trade-off in this entire course. Every algorithmic choice, every hyperparameter, every regularisation knob is a position on that single axis. Hold the axis in your head; the rest of the course is local detail.

Companion tracks

Machine learning sits between three other tracks in the fundamentals curriculum. The language engineering course is the applied continuation of Part V: where sub-modules 10 and 11 derive what a generative model is and why scale changes its behaviour, that track builds things with one, covering tokenisers, retrieval-augmented generation, agents, and the fine-tuning methods (LoRA, QLoRA, DPO) this course only names. The statistics course is a prerequisite for everything from lecture 02 onwards: feature transformations rely on distribution-shape concepts from descriptive statistics, train and test split relies on sampling theory from probability and inference, and every supervised model is a statistical model at heart. The credit risk modelling track in the applied curriculum is where these methods are operationalised under regulatory constraints (Basel A-IRB, IFRS 9): the techniques here are the universal toolbox, the credit-risk track is the specific application.
