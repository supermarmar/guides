Medoids and mixtures

K-means, from lecture 1 of this sub-module, has two hidden assumptions worth surfacing: it needs squared Euclidean distance specifically, because that is the only dissimilarity whose cluster centre reduces to a simple average, and it draws a hard boundary around every cluster, as if every observation belonged unambiguously to exactly one group. K-medoids relaxes the first assumption; Gaussian mixture models relax the second. Together they are the standard answers whenever k-means' two silent commitments turn out to be wrong for the problem at hand.

Step 1

The 30-second version

K-medoids solves the identical fixed-$K$ partitioning problem as k-means, minimising total within-cluster dissimilarity, but restricts the cluster centres, the medoids, to be genuine observations from the dataset rather than an averaged, possibly nonsensical point: $\arg\min_{(\mathcal{X}_k)_k;\, c_k \in L} \sum_k \sum_{i \in \mathcal{X}_k} L(c_k, X_i)$, with the minimisation running over medoids drawn from the learning sample $L$ itself, not over any point in the ambient space. Because a medoid never needs to be averaged, K-medoids works with any dissimilarity function (lecture 3), not only squared Euclidean distance, at the cost of a harder combinatorial search; the standard algorithm, partitioning around medoids (PAM), searches by repeatedly proposing to swap a current medoid for a non-medoid observation and keeping the swap only if it reduces total dissimilarity. Gaussian mixture models (GMMs) address the opposite limitation: rather than assuming clusters have hard, mutually exclusive boundaries, a GMM models the population as a weighted mixture of $K$ Gaussian distributions and gives every observation a soft, probabilistic degree of membership in every cluster, fitted by maximum likelihood via the expectation-maximisation (EM) algorithm, which alternates between computing each observation's cluster membership probabilities (the E-step) and updating each cluster's mean, covariance, and weight given those probabilities (the M-step) until convergence to a local optimum of the likelihood.

Feynman test If you cluster policyholders by demographic features and ask k-means for the "centre" of a cluster, it hands you the arithmetic average, which might describe a policyholder who is 47.3 years old, 0.6 male, and drives a car that is 60 per cent a Toyota and 40 per cent a Ford, a description of nobody. K-medoids instead hands you an actual policyholder from the cluster, the one who is, on balance, most centrally representative, which you can point to, look up, and use as a genuine model point. Gaussian mixture models solve a different problem: instead of insisting every policyholder belongs to exactly one segment, they let you say a given policyholder is 70 per cent segment A and 30 per cent segment B, which is often closer to the truth when segments genuinely blend into each other at their edges.
Where this sits in the ML world This lecture completes the fixed-$K$, flat-partition family alongside k-means (lecture 1), having introduced the tree-structured alternative in between (lecture 4, hierarchical clustering). K-medoids and GMMs relax k-means' two silent assumptions, the dissimilarity function and the hard-boundary assumption, in that order, and the next lecture's DBSCAN relaxes a third: that the number of clusters must be fixed at all.
Step 2

Why this matters

Three reasons these two extensions earn their place beyond k-means.

First, a genuine, pointable representative observation is frequently the actual deliverable, not a mathematical convenience: the previous lecture's model-point exercise needs a real policy to stand in for a cluster, not the arithmetic average of several policies' features, because downstream valuation and reporting processes need an object that actually exists and can be audited, exactly the gap K-medoids closes.

Second, many genuinely useful dissimilarity functions are not squared Euclidean distance, and k-means simply cannot be applied to them, because its cluster-update step relies specifically on the fact that the mean minimises squared Euclidean distance to a set of points; a Manhattan-distance clustering (more robust to outliers), an edit-distance clustering of claims-narrative text, or any dissimilarity from lecture 3's broader menu needs K-medoids or an equivalent, not k-means.

Third, hard cluster boundaries are frequently a modelling fiction that a soft, probabilistic model can avoid: two risk segments that genuinely blend into each other at the margin (a mid-market small-business book that shares characteristics with both a retail and a corporate book, say) are better described by a mixture model's gradual, overlapping membership probabilities than by k-means' insistence that every policy belongs unambiguously to exactly one segment.

Vignette from the field The actuary from the previous lecture's model-point exercise, having settled on complete-linkage hierarchical clustering to fix the chaining problem, still faced a second issue: each cluster's "centre", computed as the arithmetic mean of the policies in it, was not itself a real policy, meaning the valuation team could not simply look up the model point's cash flows from an existing record and instead had to construct a synthetic policy from averaged features, a process the actuarial function's internal audit had already flagged as a documentation gap in a prior review. Re-running the exercise with K-medoids, keeping the same complete-linkage-derived number of clusters, produced representative policies that were genuine, existing records, each traceable to a real policyholder, closing the audit gap entirely without materially changing the clustering's substantive quality.
Step 3

The mental model

Hold two contrasting pictures: k-medoids' hard boundary around a real observation acting as centre, and a Gaussian mixture's soft, overlapping probability contours with no hard boundary at all.

Two slogans. A medoid must already exist; a mean never needs to. This single restriction is what lets K-medoids work with any dissimilarity function, at the cost of a combinatorial rather than an analytic centre-update step. A mixture model answers "how much", not "which one". Where k-means and K-medoids answer "which cluster does this point belong to", a GMM answers "what is the probability this point belongs to each cluster", a genuinely different and often more honest question.

Step 4

The detail

K-medoids: the double minimisation

Recall k-means solves $\arg\min_{(\mathcal{X}_k)_k} \sum_k \sum_{i \in \mathcal{X}_k} \|c_k - X_i\|_2^2$ with the cluster centres $c_k$ computed analytically as the within-cluster mean, a step that only works because the mean is the unique minimiser of total squared Euclidean distance to a set of points. K-medoids instead solves a genuine double minimisation, over both the partition and the choice of centres, with centres restricted to the observed sample:

$$\arg\min_{(\mathcal{X}_k)_k;\ c_k \in L \text{ for all } k} \sum_{k} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{X}_k} L(c_k, X_i),$$

for any dissimilarity function $L$ (lecture 3), not necessarily squared Euclidean distance. Because the minimising $c_k$ can no longer be computed in closed form (there is no formula for "the observation that minimises total dissimilarity to the rest of the cluster" the way there is for a mean), K-medoids necessarily costs more to fit than k-means, a direct, unavoidable consequence of dropping the assumption that made k-means' centre-update step cheap.

The PAM algorithm

Partitioning around medoids (PAM) solves the double minimisation by local search. Initialise by choosing $K$ initial medoids at random from $L$, and assign every other observation to its closest medoid, giving an initial total within-cluster dissimilarity $D = \sum_k \sum_{i \in \mathcal{X}_k} L(c_k, X_i)$. Then repeat until no improving move is found: select at random a current medoid and a non-medoid observation, tentatively swap their roles (the non-medoid becomes a candidate medoid, the former medoid rejoins the pool of ordinary observations), reassign every observation to its closest medoid under the new configuration, and recompute the total dissimilarity $D'$; keep the swap if $D' < D$, otherwise reject it and try a different candidate swap. This is a genuine local search, not a guaranteed global optimum, exactly the caveat k-means' own iterative algorithm already carried; running PAM from several different random initial medoid sets and keeping the best result is the standard practical remedy, identical in spirit to k-means' own multiple-restart practice.

Gaussian mixture models: the generative story

A Gaussian mixture model assumes the observations $(X_i)_{i=1}^n$ are drawn i.i.d. from a density that is a weighted combination of $K$ multivariate Gaussian components,

$$f(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k \cdot \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{q/2}|\Sigma_k|^{1/2}} \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(x-c_k)^\top \Sigma_k^{-1}(x-c_k)\right),$$

with mean vectors $(c_k)_k$, covariance matrices $(\Sigma_k)_k$, and mixture weights $(p_k)_k$ summing to one, collectively the model parameter $\theta = (c_k, \Sigma_k, p_k)_k$. Unlike k-means and K-medoids, which draw a hard partition boundary directly, a GMM is a genuine probabilistic density model, and clustering falls out of it as a secondary step: assign each observation to the component with the highest posterior probability, computed below, if a flat hard partition is still wanted for reporting purposes.

Why maximum likelihood needs help: the EM algorithm

Maximising the GMM log-likelihood directly is a harder problem than it looks: the log-likelihood of a mixture is generally unbounded (a component's covariance can shrink toward a single training point, driving that component's density, and hence the overall likelihood, to infinity, a spurious, degenerate solution rather than a genuine one), so a raw maximum-likelihood estimate does not always exist in a well-posed sense, and even where a sensible local maximum exists, it cannot generally be found in closed form the way a single Gaussian's MLE can. The standard practical solution is the expectation-maximisation (EM) algorithm, which alternates two steps until convergence to a (local) stationary point of the likelihood.

The E-step computes, for the current parameter estimate $\hat\theta$, each observation's posterior responsibility, the probability it was generated by each component given its observed value,

$$\gamma_{ik} = \frac{\hat p_k \, \mathcal{N}(X_i; \hat c_k, \hat \Sigma_k)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} \hat p_j \, \mathcal{N}(X_i; \hat c_j, \hat \Sigma_j)}.$$

The M-step then updates every parameter as a responsibility-weighted statistic across the sample,

$$\hat c_k \leftarrow \frac{\sum_i \gamma_{ik} X_i}{\sum_i \gamma_{ik}}, \qquad \hat \Sigma_k \leftarrow \frac{\sum_i \gamma_{ik}(X_i - \hat c_k)(X_i-\hat c_k)^\top}{\sum_i \gamma_{ik}}, \qquad \hat p_k \leftarrow \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \gamma_{ik}.$$

Each pass of E-step then M-step is guaranteed not to decrease the likelihood, so the algorithm converges monotonically to a stationary point, though not necessarily the global maximum, and, as flagged above, not necessarily a well-behaved one at all without further restriction; a common practical safeguard restricts every component's covariance matrix to share the identical, isotropic (spherical) form, trading some flexibility for protection against the degenerate, collapsing-covariance solutions the unrestricted model is prone to. Several random initialisations, exactly as for k-means and PAM, is standard practice here too.

Reading off a hard partition, if one is still needed

Where a flat, hard partition is required for a downstream process that cannot consume soft probabilities (a reporting system expecting one segment label per policy, say), the standard conversion assigns each observation to the component with the largest responsibility, $\hat k(X_i) = \arg\max_k \gamma_{ik}$. This discards the genuine soft-membership information the GMM computed, and should be treated as a deliberate simplification for a specific downstream constraint, not as evidence that the underlying clustering was ever really hard in the first place.

Step 5

Pitfalls

Using k-means on mixed or categorical data and treating the resulting centroid as meaningful Averaging one-hot-encoded categorical dummies produces a centroid with fractional values on every level, a description of nobody, exactly the failure the vignette's model-point exercise ran into; K-medoids, with a dissimilarity function suited to mixed data, is the direct fix, not a workaround.
Treating PAM's result as a guaranteed global optimum PAM's swap-based local search, like k-means' own iteration, can converge to different local optima from different random initial medoid sets; running several restarts and keeping the configuration with the lowest total dissimilarity is standard practice, not an optional refinement.
Fitting an unrestricted GMM and being surprised by a degenerate solution An unrestricted covariance GMM can converge to a component whose covariance has collapsed toward a single point (or a small cluster of near-identical points), driving that component's density, and the overall likelihood, toward an unbounded value; restricting covariance structure (equal, isotropic components, as a common default) or monitoring for near-singular covariance estimates during fitting is the standard safeguard.
Discarding soft membership probabilities without a stated reason Converting a GMM's genuine, informative responsibilities into a hard label by taking the arg max throws away exactly the information a GMM was chosen to capture in the first place; do this only when a specific downstream system genuinely cannot consume a probability vector, and consider whether that downstream constraint is itself worth revisiting before defaulting to a hard label.
Step 6

Synthesis and what's next

K-medoids relaxes k-means' dependence on squared Euclidean distance by restricting cluster centres to genuine observations, solved by PAM's swap-based local search, and pays for that generality with a combinatorial rather than analytic centre-update step. Gaussian mixture models relax the hard-boundary assumption both k-means and K-medoids share, modelling the population as a weighted combination of Gaussian components and computing a genuine, probabilistic degree of membership for every observation in every cluster, fitted by the EM algorithm's alternating E-step (compute responsibilities) and M-step (update parameters given those responsibilities), with degenerate, unbounded solutions a real risk the unrestricted model needs guarding against.

The next lecture relaxes the remaining assumption every method so far has shared, that the number of clusters $K$ is fixed in advance: density-based clustering, and the DBSCAN algorithm specifically, determines the number of clusters dynamically from the data's own density structure, with no $K$ chosen by the modeller at all.
