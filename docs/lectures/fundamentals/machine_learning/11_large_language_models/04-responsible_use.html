Responsible use

Every capability developed across this sub-module, scale, in-context learning, pretraining, prompting, RLHF, reasoning, is a capability, not a guarantee. This closing lecture develops the governance discipline a regulated actuarial user must apply on top of all of it: forcing outputs into a form ordinary statistical validation can grade, monitoring for drift and bias exactly as any other production model would be monitored, and being honest about four specific, structural limitations that no amount of scale or clever prompting removes.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A large language model deployed inside a regulated actuarial workflow does not get a pass on the model-risk-management discipline every earlier model in this course has been subject to; if anything, it needs more of it, precisely because its failure modes are less well characterised. The single most important governance technique is forcing a numeric score out of the model wherever a decision genuinely requires one, converting an open-ended, free-text output into a structured number or category that the calibration and evaluation machinery from earlier sub-modules can then grade using exactly the same tools (auto-calibration, Gini, backtesting) applied to any other model in this course. Robustness under small, adversarial or incidental prompt perturbation, bias and fairness monitoring across protected and proxy subgroups, and ongoing drift monitoring as the model, its deployment context, or the underlying population changes are the same three governance disciplines applied to every earlier model in this course, applied here again with no exemption. Four hard limitations are structural, not implementation bugs to be patched away: no genuine grounding in verified fact, a propensity to hallucinate (produce fluent, confident, and simply false content), no formal, guaranteed logical reasoning process, and the fixed context window developed two lectures ago.

Feynman test Imagine a brilliant new hire who writes with total, unwavering confidence regardless of whether they actually know the answer, occasionally invents a plausible-sounding but entirely fictitious source when asked to justify a claim, and has read an enormous amount but genuinely cannot see or remember anything beyond what is currently in front of them on the desk. You would not simply trust this colleague's numbers directly into a regulatory return; you would insist their conclusions be expressed as a checkable figure, cross-check their most consequential claims independently, watch for a pattern of quiet overconfidence over time, and never ask them to reason about something that requires information nobody put in front of them. Every governance technique in this lecture is exactly that ordinary professional scepticism, applied systematically rather than left to individual judgement.
Where this sits in the ML world This lecture is not a new modelling technique; it is the model-risk-management discipline from the calibration sub-module and the model-interpretation sub-module, applied without exception to the newest and least well-characterised model family in this course. Closing the sub-module here, rather than treating governance as an afterthought, is a deliberate structural choice: every capability in this sub-module is genuinely useful, and every one of them needs exactly this discipline before it is trusted with a decision that matters.
Step 2

Why this matters

Three reasons this lecture is not optional reading for a regulated actuarial practitioner, unlike some of the more exploratory material earlier in this sub-module.

First, a validator, supervisor, or auditor will ask the identical questions of an LLM-based tool that they ask of any other model: is it calibrated, is it monitored for drift, is its performance stable across sub-populations, and can its decisions be explained and reproduced; a team that cannot answer these questions for an LLM-based component has not actually completed the model-risk-management process, regardless of how impressive the tool's outputs look in a demonstration.

Second, hallucination is not a rare edge case to be caught by spot-checking, it is a structural property of how these models generate text: a model producing fluent, confident, and entirely fabricated content (a plausible-sounding but non-existent regulatory citation, a fabricated case reference) is doing exactly what its training optimised it to do, produce plausible continuations, with nothing in its architecture that distinguishes a true statement from a false but equally fluent one.

Third, the numeric-score discipline is what makes every other tool in this course's toolbox usable on an LLM's output at all: an unstructured, free-text output cannot be run through a calibration check, a Gini score, or a backtest; forcing the model to commit to a structured, checkable number or category is the specific bridge that connects this sub-module's newest tool back to every validation technique developed earlier in the course.

Vignette from the field A claims-triage team deployed an LLM-based tool to flag claims narratives likely to indicate a disputed liability, initially accepting the model's free-text explanation ("this claim appears disputed because...") as the primary output, with human reviewers reading the explanation and making a final call. Audit review found that reviewers were, in practice, anchoring heavily on a confidently worded explanation even when the underlying flag was wrong, because fluent, well-structured prose reads as more trustworthy regardless of its actual accuracy, a bias the review process had not accounted for. The team restructured the tool to output a forced numeric disputed-liability score (0 to 1) alongside the free-text explanation, then ran the numeric score through the identical auto-calibration and Gini diagnostics used for the team's conventional fraud-scoring models, discovering in the process that the score was reasonably well discriminating but meaningfully overconfident in the top decile, exactly the kind of miscalibration the calibration sub-module's tools are built to catch, and exactly the kind of finding the free-text explanation alone would never have surfaced.
Step 3

The mental model

Hold one picture: an LLM's free-text output passing through a forced-numeric-score bottleneck before it ever reaches the same validation pipeline (calibration, Gini, drift monitoring) every other model in this course has had to pass through, with the four structural limitations sitting permanently outside that pipeline as things no amount of validation removes.

Two slogans. An ungraded output is an unvalidated model, no matter how fluent it sounds. Fluency is not evidence of correctness, and the triage vignette's reviewers learned this the hard way before the numeric-score discipline was imposed. Governance reveals limitations; it does not remove them. The four structural limitations closing this lecture are not bugs a better validation process eventually patches; they are permanent features of the current generation of these models that every deployment must be designed around.

Step 4

The detail

Governance and accountability

Every earlier model in this course, from the humble GLM through the deepest transformer, has been subject to the risk-management sub-module's governance framework: a named accountable owner, a defined use test (was the model actually built for, and validated against, the decision it is now being used to support), and a documented, reproducible validation record. None of this is waived for an LLM-based component; if anything, the governance burden is higher, because the model's failure modes are, at the time of writing, considerably less well characterised than those of a GLM or a gradient-boosted tree ensemble that the industry has decades of collective validation experience with.

Forcing a numeric score

The single most load-bearing governance technique developed in this lecture is forcing a numeric score: structuring the model's task so that its output is, or reduces cleanly to, a number or a category, rather than accepting an open-ended free-text response as the final deliverable. Once a decision is expressed this way, every tool built across the calibration sub-module (actual-versus-predicted diagnostics, auto-calibration, the Gini score) and the model-evaluation sub-module (backtesting, resampling validation) applies directly and without modification, exactly as the triage vignette demonstrates. This is not merely a convenience; it is the specific technique that makes an LLM-based tool auditable in the same terms as every other model a validation team already knows how to assess.

Robustness under prompt perturbation

Robustness testing asks whether small, plausible perturbations to the input prompt, rewording a question, adding an irrelevant sentence, changing formatting, produce materially different outputs for what is substantively the identical decision. A production LLM-based tool whose forced numeric score swings meaningfully under such perturbations is not yet fit for a regulated decision, exactly the same standard applied to a conventional model's sensitivity to small, substantively irrelevant changes in input data (a rounding convention, a field's units) in the model-validation practice this course has developed throughout.

Bias and fairness monitoring

Monitoring a forced numeric score's performance and calibration across protected characteristics and their proxies is the identical discipline the actuarial-applications sub-module develops for conventional pricing and underwriting models, applied here without exception. An LLM's pretraining corpus, developed in lecture 2, can encode historical societal biases in ways that are considerably harder to trace to a specific cause than a conventional model's biased training data, which makes subgroup performance monitoring, rather than an assumption of neutrality, the only reliable check available.

Drift monitoring

A deployed LLM-based tool is subject to the identical concept-drift risk as any other production model, and to an additional risk unique to this model family: the underlying model itself can change without the deploying team's direct control, if the tool is accessed via a third-party API that is silently updated or retrained upstream. Drift monitoring for an LLM-based deployment therefore needs to track not only the usual population and relationship drift covered in the calibration sub-module, but also whether the underlying model's behaviour has itself shifted independently of anything happening in the deploying organisation's own data.

The four hard limitations

Four limitations are structural properties of the current generation of these architectures, not implementation defects a future patch removes. No grounding: nothing in the pretraining or generation process guarantees a connection between a model's output and any externally verified fact; the model produces the most statistically plausible continuation of its input, which frequently coincides with truth but is not the same claim as being anchored to it. Hallucination is the direct consequence: fluent, confident, and simply false content, including fabricated citations, case references, or numerical figures, produced with no internal signal distinguishing it from a true statement. No formal reasoning guarantee: even a reasoning model's extended chain-of-thought, developed in the previous lecture, is generated token by token from the same underlying statistical process as any other output, with no formal logical proof system underneath it ensuring the conclusion actually follows validly from the stated steps. The fixed context window, developed in full in lecture 1, remains a hard architectural ceiling regardless of any prompting or fine-tuning technique applied on top of it.

Step 5

Pitfalls

Accepting a fluent free-text explanation as evidence of a correct decision Developed directly in the vignette: fluent, well-structured prose reads as more trustworthy independent of its actual accuracy, a bias that undermines exactly the human-in-the-loop review process it is meant to support unless the review is anchored to a separately validated, structured score rather than the prose itself.
Treating an LLM-based tool as exempt from the model-risk-management framework applied to every other model A named accountable owner, a defined use test, and a documented validation record are not optional simplifications for a newer or more impressive-seeming tool; a validator will ask for exactly the same evidence regardless of the underlying architecture.
Treating hallucination as a rare failure mode to be caught by spot-checking Hallucination is a structural property of how these models generate fluent continuations, not an occasional bug; any deployment where a fabricated fact could cause real harm needs a systematic verification step against an independent, authoritative source, not an assumption that hallucinations are rare enough to catch by sampling.
Assuming a third-party API's underlying model is stable over time A model accessed via an external API can be updated or retrained by the provider without the deploying team's knowledge or consent; drift monitoring that only tracks the deploying organisation's own data misses this entire, LLM-specific source of behavioural change.
Step 6

Synthesis and what's next

Every capability developed across this sub-module needs the identical governance discipline applied to every other model in this course: a named accountable owner and use test, a forced numeric score wherever a genuine decision is required (the specific bridge that connects an LLM's free-text output to the calibration and evaluation machinery built earlier), robustness testing under prompt perturbation, subgroup bias and fairness monitoring, and drift monitoring that additionally accounts for the possibility of an unannounced upstream model change. Four limitations, no grounding, hallucination, no formal reasoning guarantee, and the fixed context window, are structural properties of the current architecture family, not defects a future patch removes; good governance reveals how much they affect a given deployment, it does not make any of them disappear.
