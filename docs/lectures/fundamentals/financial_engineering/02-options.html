Lectures / Financial engineering

Options

A forward obliges both sides to trade. An option obliges only one of them. That single change of word, from "must" to "may", turns a linear contract into a kinked one, and a contract worth zero at inception into one that costs real money up front. This lecture builds the payoff logic, the model-free bounds, and the one relationship, put-call parity, that ties options back to the forward you already know how to price.

Step 1

The 30-second version

An option gives its holder the right, but not the obligation, to trade an asset at a fixed price K on or before a fixed date T. A call is the right to buy; a put is the right to sell. Because the holder can always decline the trade when it does not suit them, the payoff is never negative, and a payoff that is never negative is worth something. Unlike a forward, an option costs a premium up front.

The payoff at maturity for a European call is $\max(S(T) - K, 0)$, and for a European put it is $\max(K - S(T), 0)$. Both are kinked at $S(T) = K$ rather than straight lines, and that single kink is the entire reason option pricing needs more machinery than forward pricing. A forward's value is pinned down by a static replicating portfolio; an option's value, in general, needs a dynamic one. Before reaching for that machinery, though, a surprising amount can be said using nothing but the absence of arbitrage: a relationship called put-call parity links the call price, the put price, and the forward price exactly, and a handful of model-free bounds constrain each price on its own, all without assuming anything about how S moves.

Feynman test You and I agree today that in three months you may buy my house from me for £300,000, if you want to. You do not have to. If house prices crash, you walk away and lose nothing but whatever you paid me today for the privilege of asking. If house prices soar, you buy at £300,000 and pocket the difference. Because the deal can only go well for you, never badly beyond what you paid upfront, I will not grant it to you for free. That upfront payment is the option premium, and the entire content of this lecture is working out what that payment has to be, or at least what it cannot be, without either of us needing to guess where house prices are going.
What's beyond the source The UP source proves a single combined bound on the call price by comparing three payoff diagrams (forward, call, share) and proves put-call parity the same graphical way, then stops: it has no American-option material at all, no dividend-adjusted parity, and no proof that the call price is convex in the strike (its butterfly spread appears only as a payoff diagram and an exercise, not a convexity argument). This lecture adds the four separate model-free bounds with explicit arbitrage trades for each, the full early-exercise argument for American calls and puts (including why it fails once dividends are introduced), the dividend-yield version of parity, and the convexity-in-strike proof via the butterfly spread. It is narrower than the source in one respect: the source also surveys digital, knockout and lookback options and the strip/strap/strangle/spread combination book, none of which appear here.
Step 2

Why this matters

The asymmetry in an option's payoff is what separates it economically from the forward in lecture 1, and that asymmetry is worth naming precisely. A forward's payoff, $S(T) - K$, is a straight line through the point $(K, 0)$: as likely to be negative as positive, symmetric in gains and losses, and consequently free to enter, since neither side has an advantage to pay for. An option's payoff is that same line, but bent flat wherever it would otherwise have gone negative. The bend removes the downside while keeping the upside, and removing downside without giving up upside is not something the market hands out for nothing. The price of that removed downside is the premium, and it is strictly positive whenever there is any chance the option finishes in the money.

Practically, options are the mechanism behind equity hedging (protective puts on a portfolio), yield enhancement (covered calls written against a holding), employee compensation (stock option grants), and every structured product with a "guaranteed floor" or "capped upside" (both are just options bolted onto a bond or a forward). Conceptually, this lecture is the last stop before the machinery gets harder. Put-call parity and the no-arbitrage bounds are exact, model-free, and provable with the same static replication technique from lecture 1. The binomial model in lecture 3 is where a genuine model, with an assumption about how the asset moves, first enters the pricing of an option in its own right, and everything you derive here becomes the sanity check that model has to satisfy.

Step 3

The mental model

Two pictures carry the lecture. The first is the family of four hockey-stick diagrams: long call, short call, long put, short put, each drawn as both a payoff (ignoring what was paid for the option) and a profit (netting off the premium). The second is the put-call parity replication, structured exactly like the cash-and-carry picture from lecture 1: two portfolios, built from different traded instruments, that turn out to have an identical payoff at maturity in every possible state of the world.

Picture one: the four hockey sticks

Every one of the four basic option positions bends at the strike K and is flat on one side of it. The solid line in each panel below is the payoff at maturity, $S(T)$ on the horizontal axis. The dashed line is the profit: the payoff minus the premium paid (for the two long positions) or plus the premium received (for the two short positions). The vertical shift between the two lines is exactly the premium, which is why a long position's dashed line always sits below its solid line, and a short position's dashed line always sits above.

Two features are worth carrying forward. First, a long call and a long put both have payoff diagrams that never go below zero, which is exactly why they cost money: nobody sells "never lose, sometimes win" for free. Second, the short positions are the mirror image reflected through the horizontal axis; the premium the writer receives is their maximum possible gain, while their downside is asymmetric and, for the short call, unbounded.

Picture two: the parity replication

Put-call parity, derived formally in section 4, says that buying a call and selling a put on the same strike and maturity produces exactly the same payoff as buying the asset outright and borrowing the present value of the strike. The picture below lays the two portfolios side by side, in the same style as the cash-and-carry figure from lecture 1: two routes, same destination.

Step 4

The detail

Definitions

A call option gives its holder the right to buy the underlying asset at the strike price K; a put option gives the right to sell at K. A European option can only be exercised at maturity T; an American option can be exercised at any time up to and including T. Every option has two sides: the long position holds the right and pays the premium; the short position (also called the writer) grants the right and receives the premium, taking on an obligation that is exercised at the long's discretion, never their own.

Premium
The price paid by the long to the short at inception for the option contract. Unlike a forward, which is constructed to have zero value at inception, an option's payoff is never negative and strictly positive with positive probability, so no-arbitrage forces the premium to be strictly positive whenever there is any chance the option finishes in the money. The premium is denoted C for a call and P for a put throughout this lecture, each implicitly a function of S(0), K, T and r.

The European payoffs at maturity are

$$ \text{Call: } \max(S(T) - K, 0), \qquad \text{Put: } \max(K - S(T), 0). $$

An option's moneyness describes where the current spot sits relative to the strike. A call is in the money (ITM) when $S(t) > K$, at the money (ATM) when $S(t) \approx K$, and out of the money (OTM) when $S(t) < K$. A put has the inequalities reversed. The premium at any time before maturity splits into two components: the intrinsic value, which is what the option would be worth if exercised immediately, $\max(S(t)-K,0)$ for a call, and the time value, the remainder, which reflects the chance that the intrinsic value grows before T. Time value is always non-negative for a European option and decays to zero as $t \to T$, the effect traders call theta decay.

Put-call parity by replication

The parity relationship follows from the same static-replication logic as lecture 1's cash-and-carry argument, applied to two portfolios rather than one. Consider two European options on the same non-dividend-paying underlying, same strike K, same maturity T.

Portfolio A, at time 0: Buy one European call, strike K, maturity T. cost +C Sell one European put, strike K, maturity T. cost -P ------- Net cost of A: C - P Portfolio B, at time 0: Buy one unit of the underlying asset. cost +S(0) Borrow K·exp(-rT) in cash (repay K at T). cost -K·exp(-rT) ------- Net cost of B: S(0) - K·exp(-rT) At time T, in every state of the world: Portfolio A pays: max(S(T)-K,0) - max(K-S(T),0) = S(T) - K Portfolio B pays: S(T) - K = S(T) - K Both portfolios pay S(T) - K at T, with no exception for whether S(T) is above or below K. Identical payoff in every state forces identical cost today, or the cheaper portfolio is a free lunch: C - P = S(0) - K·exp(-rT)

The identity that emerges,

$$ \boxed{\, C - P = S(0) - K \, e^{-rT} \,} $$

is exactly the value of a long forward contract with delivery price K, taken from lecture 1's formula $V(0) = S(0) - K e^{-rT}$. That is not a coincidence: a long call combined with a short put, both struck at K, has a payoff of $S(T) - K$ in every state, which is precisely the forward payoff. The forward is the special case of an option combination where the two option positions offset exactly and no optionality survives; buying a call and selling a put at the same strike removes the kink entirely and leaves a straight line. Put-call parity is, in this light, not a separate fact about options but the statement that "call minus put equals forward" is true regardless of what model you believe for S.

With a continuous dividend yield q, the same replication applies with the asset leg replaced by $S(0)e^{-qT}$ units bought today rather than one unit, exactly as in the dividend extension of the forward price in lecture 1. Parity becomes

$$ C - P = S(0)\, e^{-qT} - K \, e^{-rT}. $$

Model-free no-arbitrage bounds

Parity ties C and P to each other. It says nothing about either one in isolation. Four further inequalities bound each price on its own, and each is provable by exhibiting an arbitrage if it fails, without any assumption about the distribution of S(T).

Call lower bound. If $C < \max(S(0) - Ke^{-rT}, 0)$, buy the call and sell the replicating portfolio of section 3 (short the asset, lend $Ke^{-rT}$). This costs $C - S(0) + Ke^{-rT} < 0$ today, a strictly positive amount is received up front, and the position unwinds to at worst zero at T: the call pays $\max(S(T)-K,0)$, and closing the short asset against the bond proceeds pays $K - S(T)$, summing to $\max(S(T)-K,0) + K - S(T) = \max(K - S(T), 0) \geq 0$. Free cash today, no possibility of loss later, which is an arbitrage. Hence $C \geq \max(S(0) - Ke^{-rT}, 0)$. Since a call can never be worth less than zero either (the holder simply does not exercise), this is usually written with the max already inside.

Call upper bound. A call can never be worth more than the asset itself: owning the asset outright dominates owning the right to buy it, in every state, because the asset holder receives $S(T)$ unconditionally while the call holder receives at most $S(T) - K < S(T)$. If $C > S(0)$, sell the call and buy the asset, pocketing $C - S(0) > 0$ today while holding a position that is worth at least as much as the short call liability at every future date. Hence $C \leq S(0)$.

Put lower bound. By the mirror argument to the call lower bound, applied to buying the put and the opposite replicating trade (buy the asset, borrow $Ke^{-rT}$... reversed): $P \geq \max(Ke^{-rT} - S(0), 0)$.

Put upper bound. A put can never be worth more than the discounted strike: the best possible outcome for the put holder is that the asset becomes worthless, in which case the payoff is exactly K at T, worth $Ke^{-rT}$ today. If $P > Ke^{-rT}$, sell the put and invest the proceeds at the risk-free rate; the invested cash alone, at $Ke^{-rT}\cdot e^{rT} = K$, covers the worst-case payout of K owed on the put, with the premium overcharge left over as riskless profit. Hence $P \leq Ke^{-rT}$.

Monotonicity and convexity in the strike

Two further structural results have nothing to do with parity but everything to do with keeping a set of quoted option prices internally consistent. Raising the strike of a call can only reduce its payoff in every state (the holder has to pay more to exercise, or the option is further out of the money), so $C(K)$ is a decreasing function of K. The stronger, less obvious result is that $C(K)$ is convex in K.

The proof is a butterfly spread argument. Take three strikes $K_1 < K_2 < K_3$ with $K_2 = (K_1 + K_3)/2$, and build the portfolio: long one call at $K_1$, long one call at $K_3$, short two calls at $K_2$. At maturity, this portfolio's payoff is $\max(S-K_1,0) + \max(S-K_3,0) - 2\max(S-K_2,0)$, which is zero for $S \leq K_1$ or $S \geq K_3$, and strictly positive (rising to a peak of $K_2 - K_1$ at $S = K_2$) for S in between. The payoff is never negative anywhere, so the cost of the portfolio today cannot be negative either:

$$ C(K_1) + C(K_3) - 2\,C(K_2) \geq 0 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad C(K_2) \leq \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(C(K_1) + C(K_3)\bigr), $$

which is precisely the definition of convexity. The same argument applied to puts gives $P(K)$ increasing and convex in K. This result reappears, unannounced, as the no-arbitrage condition on the implied volatility surface: a set of quoted option prices across strikes that violates convexity implies a negative butterfly price, which is a static arbitrage regardless of any model, and every options desk screens for it before anything more sophisticated is fitted to the data.

American options and early exercise

An American option can be exercised at any time up to T, so it must be worth at least as much as its European counterpart: $C^{\text{Am}} \geq C^{\text{Eu}}$ and $P^{\text{Am}} \geq P^{\text{Eu}}$, since the American holder always has the European strategy of waiting until T available as one option among many. The gap $C^{\text{Am}} - C^{\text{Eu}} \geq 0$ is called the early-exercise premium, and the surprising result, for a call on a non-dividend-paying stock, is that it is exactly zero.

The argument has two parts, and both must hold for early exercise to be strictly dominated. First, exercising a call early means paying K now instead of at T; holding the option instead of exercising lets you keep that cash earning interest until T, a benefit that immediate exercise throws away. Second, exercising early converts the option into outright stock ownership, which forfeits the "insurance" the option still provided against $S$ falling below K before T: the option holder's downside was capped at zero, but the stockholder's is not. Both the interest on the deferred strike and the residual insurance value are strictly positive whenever $T > t$ and $r > 0$, so the European call is always worth strictly more than its own intrinsic value, $C^{\text{Eu}}(t) > S(t) - K$, and immediate exercise (which realises only the intrinsic value) is never optimal. Formally, using the call lower bound from this section with the American holder always free to exercise for $S(t) - K$:

$$ C^{\text{Am}}(t) \geq C^{\text{Eu}}(t) \geq S(t) - K\,e^{-r(T-t)} > S(t) - K, $$

so the American call is worth strictly more alive than dead, at every time before T, and $C^{\text{Am}} = C^{\text{Eu}}$ on a non-dividend-paying stock.

The argument does not survive for puts. Exercising a put early yields K in cash immediately, which can then earn interest until T; if the stock has fallen close to zero, the option is deep in the money, the remaining time value is small, and the benefit of receiving and investing K sooner can outweigh what little insurance value is left. It can therefore be optimal to exercise an American put early when the stock price is sufficiently low, and $P^{\text{Am}} > P^{\text{Eu}}$ is a real, not merely theoretical, possibility.

The call result also has a well-known exception: if the underlying pays a discrete dividend before T, exercising immediately before the ex-dividend date lets the holder capture the dividend, which the option itself does not pay. If that dividend is large enough relative to the remaining interest-and-insurance benefit of waiting, early exercise of an American call becomes optimal, and it is exercised, if at all, only in the instant before the stock goes ex-dividend, never at any other time.

Worked example: parity, and what to do when it breaks

A stock trades at $S(0) = 50$. The continuously compounded risk-free rate is $r = 5\%$ per annum. A six-month ($T = 0.5$) European call struck at $K = 48$ is quoted at $C = 4.20$. Put-call parity gives the fair value of the corresponding put:

$$ P = C - S(0) + K\,e^{-rT} = 4.20 - 50 + 48\,e^{-0.05 \times 0.5} = 4.20 - 50 + 48 \times 0.97531 = 4.20 - 50 + 46.81 = 1.01. $$

Suppose the market instead quotes the put at $P = 1.50$, roughly 49 pence too expensive per share. Parity is violated, so an arbitrage is available: buy the cheaper synthetic combination and sell the more expensive one.

Fair value check: K·exp(-rT) = 48 x 0.97531 = 46.81 Parity put = C - S(0) + K·exp(-rT) = 4.20 - 50 + 46.81 = 1.01 Market put quoted at 1.50 -> put is overpriced by about 0.49 Cost of Portfolio A (long call, short the market's expensive put): Pay C = 4.20, receive P = 1.50. Net cost = 4.20 - 1.50 = 2.70 Cost of Portfolio B (long asset, borrow K·exp(-rT)): Pay S(0) = 50, receive 46.81 from borrowing. Net cost = 50 - 46.81 = 3.19 Portfolio A is cheaper than Portfolio B for an identical S(T) - K payoff, so go long A and short B: Buy the call pay 4.20 Sell (write) the put at the market price receive 1.50 Short-sell the asset receive 50.00 Lend (invest) K·exp(-rT) at the risk-free rate pay 46.81 ------- Net cash received today: 0.49 At T, in every state: Long call, short put position pays: S(T) - K Short asset, closed with bond proceeds: K - S(T) ----------- Net payoff at T: 0 Result: 0.49 collected today, zero net obligation at T. Riskless profit equal to the size of the parity violation, exactly as it should be.

The arbitrage is often described informally as "selling the box": whenever a call, a put, the asset and the risk-free rate imply two different prices for the same $S(T) - K$ payoff, a trader buys the cheap combination and sells the expensive one, collecting the difference with no residual risk. Real trading desks run this check on every quoted option chain automatically, because a parity breach that survives transaction costs is one of the very few genuinely riskless profits left in a liquid market.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Parity is model-free, and that is precisely its value

Nothing in the derivation of $C - P = S(0) - Ke^{-rT}$ used a probability distribution for $S(T)$, a volatility, or a drift. It used only the payoffs at maturity and the absence of arbitrage. That is what makes parity useful for something the Black-Scholes formula, three lectures away, cannot do on its own: checking that a data set is internally consistent before any model is fitted to it. If a listed call and put on the same strike and maturity do not satisfy parity once implied dividends and repo rates are accounted for, the data is wrong, stale, or illiquid, not the model. Practitioners run the relationship in reverse for exactly this reason, backing out the market's implied dividend yield or the effective borrow cost from the observed gap between $C - P$ and $S(0) - Ke^{-rT}$, since those are frequently harder to observe directly than the option prices themselves, particularly for hard-to-borrow single-name stocks.

The bounds are exact but weak

The four inequalities in section 4 are true regardless of the model, which sounds like a strength and is also their limitation. A model-free bound has to hold under every possible model, including the most pessimistic one imaginable, so it typically leaves a wide range of prices unexplained. Knowing that $C \geq \max(S(0)-Ke^{-rT},0)$ and $C \leq S(0)$ narrows a call's fair value to a range that can easily span several multiples of the true price for an out-of-the-money option with a long maturity. Getting from "consistent with no-arbitrage" to "a specific number" requires committing to an assumption about how S evolves, which is exactly the gap the binomial model in lecture 3 exists to close: the simplest possible stochastic assumption (the asset moves to one of two prices next period) that is just rich enough to pin a single price down by replication, in the same static-hedging spirit as this lecture, but repeated over time.

Synthetic positions and the market maker's toolkit

Parity is not only a check, it is a construction tool. Rearranged, $C = P + S(0) - Ke^{-rT}$ says a call can always be synthesised from a put, the underlying, and cash; equally, a put can be synthesised from a call, a short position in the underlying, and cash. Market makers use this routinely: a desk that is asked to quote a put it does not want to warehouse can instead quote it off the call it is more comfortable pricing, hedge with the underlying, and let parity guarantee the two exposures are equivalent. This is also how a "conversion" (long stock, long put, short call, all matched) and a "reversal" (the mirror trade) function as pure arbitrage vehicles when parity drifts even slightly out of line, which is one of the oldest trades on any options floor.

Watch out Parity as stated here assumes four things that real markets bend. (1) The options are European; American exercise breaks the clean replication because the short side of the parity portfolio can be exercised against you at a time you do not choose. (2) Dividends, if discrete rather than a continuous yield, need to enter as a present value adjustment, and unexpected dividend changes between quote and expiry break it further. (3) Borrowing and shorting the underlying happen at the risk-free rate with no cost; in practice, hard-to-borrow stocks carry a specific repo spread that shows up as an apparent parity violation that is not actually an arbitrage once financing costs are included properly. (4) Bid-ask spreads on four instruments (call, put, stock, financing) have to be crossed to execute the arbitrage, which can exceed a small parity gap entirely. None of this makes parity wrong; it means the "exact" relationship holds exactly only in a frictionless market, and every deviation observed in practice is a clue about which friction is binding.

Why this is the last stop before a model is needed

Everything derived in this lecture, parity, the four bounds, the convexity result, holds for every option-pricing model ever written, from Black-Scholes to the most exotic local-volatility or jump-diffusion construction. That universality is the tell that no model has been used yet: these are constraints that any correct model must reproduce, not results a model produces. The next lecture changes that. The single-period binomial model commits to a specific, if simplified, description of how the asset can move, and in exchange it delivers something these bounds cannot: an exact price, not a range. Keep the bounds from this lecture in your pocket when you get there. They are the check that the binomial price, and every price after it for the rest of the course, has to fall inside.

  • The source's put-call parity proposition and proof cover only the non-dividend case; there is no dividend-yield version of parity anywhere in the six pages. The extension to $C - P = S(0)e^{-qT} - Ke^{-rT}$ in this lecture is new.

The convexity proof in this lecture, and its connection to the no-arbitrage screen on implied volatility surfaces, is new.
  • The source in turn covers ground this lecture omits entirely: digital options (cash-or-nothing and stock-or-nothing), knockout options, lookback options, and the combination strategies straddle, bullish and bearish vertical spread, strip, strap and strangle. None of these appear here.
