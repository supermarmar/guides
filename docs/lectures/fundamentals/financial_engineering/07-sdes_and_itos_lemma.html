Stochastic differential equations and Ito's lemma

An SDE is an integral equation wearing a differential's clothes. Once that is settled, Ito's lemma is just the chain rule for a world in which squared increments do not vanish. Everything from delta hedging to the Vasicek short-rate model is this one lemma, applied to a different function.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A stochastic differential equation is usually written $dX(t) = \mu(t,X(t))\,dt + \sigma(t,X(t))\,dW(t)$, and almost every symbol in that line is doing honest work except the differentials themselves. Brownian motion W is continuous everywhere and differentiable nowhere, established in Lecture 6, so dW(t) cannot mean "the derivative of W at t", because no such derivative exists. The equation is shorthand for its integral form,

$$ X(t) = X(a) + \int_a^t \mu(s,X(s))\,ds + \int_a^t \sigma(s,X(s))\,dW(s), $$

where the second integral is the Ito integral built in Lecture 6. Read any other way, treating dX/dt as an ordinary rate of change, the SDE is not merely imprecise, it is false: the paths it describes are nowhere differentiable, so no such rate exists. Once that is accepted, the rest of the lecture is mechanical. Ito's lemma is the chain rule adapted to this setting: it tells you the SDE satisfied by any smooth function of an Ito process, and it differs from the ordinary chain rule by exactly one extra term, a second-derivative correction that arises because $(dW)^2$ behaves like dt rather than vanishing. That single correction is responsible for the geometric Brownian motion solution having a $-\sigma^2/2$ in its drift, for convex payoffs picking up extra expected value, and for the entire structure of delta hedging in the lectures that follow.

Feynman test Ask someone what the "speed" of a coastline is at a particular point, meaning how fast its height changes per unit of horizontal distance. The question has no answer: zoom in as far as you like and the coastline is still jagged, never straight, so there is no well-defined slope anywhere. Brownian motion is the same kind of object. Writing $dW/dt$ and expecting it to mean something is exactly this mistake. What you can sensibly ask instead is: over a whole stretch of coastline, how much total wiggle has accumulated? That question has a precise answer, the quadratic variation from Lecture 6, and it is the question an SDE is actually answering when it writes $(dW)^2 = dt$. It is a statement about accumulated wiggle over an interval, never about the slope at a point.
What's beyond the source The UP source is seven pages and entirely one-dimensional: one Brownian motion, one process at a time. It builds arithmetic and geometric Brownian motion by two contrasting methods (an informed guess, and a systematic "matching of coefficients"), shows the second method failing on a genuinely non-Markovian SDE, and leaves the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process as a guided exercise. It never states a general existence and uniqueness theorem, never distinguishes strong from weak solutions by name, and never leaves one dimension. This lecture keeps every one of the source's worked derivations intact and adds: the existence and uniqueness theorem (stated, not proved), the multidimensional Ito's lemma with a cross-variation term, the product and quotient rules for two correlated Ito processes, the CIR process, a numerical worked example quantifying the mean-median gap in GBM, and a martingale check under the risk-neutral drift.
Step 2

Why this matters

Every technical result in the remaining lectures of this course is Ito's lemma applied to one specific function. The Feynman-Kac connection between conditional expectations and PDEs, the Black-Scholes PDE itself, the delta-hedging argument that makes replication dynamic rather than static, the Vasicek short-rate model: each one starts by picking a function of an Ito process, most often the value of a derivative contract as a function of time and the underlying, and applying exactly the lemma derived in this lecture. If Lecture 1's replicating portfolio was static (set it up once, walk away), everything from here on is dynamic, and Ito's lemma is the tool that tells you how the value of a dynamically rebalanced portfolio actually moves.

The practical stakes are not only mathematical. The single most consequential number in this lecture, the $-\sigma^2/2$ that separates the median growth rate of a stock from its mean growth rate, governs how leveraged exchange-traded products decay over time, how badly a naive arithmetic-mean projection overstates long-run wealth accumulation, and why the geometric mean, not the arithmetic mean, is the correct summary statistic for compounding returns. An actuary projecting a fund forty years forward for a pension liability who uses the arithmetic mean return will overstate the typical outcome by a margin that grows without bound as the horizon lengthens. Section 4 makes this precise with numbers.

There is also a bookkeeping reason to master SDEs before anything else in continuous time. Every model the rest of the course touches, a single stock, a basket of correlated assets, a short rate, a stochastic volatility factor, is specified by writing down its SDE and nothing else. The SDE is the primitive object; the PDE in Lecture 9, the risk-neutral pricing formula, the simulation scheme used to calibrate the model to market prices in Lecture 5's continuous-time analogue, are all derived from the SDE, never the other way round. Getting comfortable reading an SDE, extracting its drift, its diffusion, and (where more than one process is involved) its correlation structure, at a glance, is the single skill this lecture is training.

Step 3

The mental model

Two pictures carry the whole lecture. The first shows why a GBM's average path and its typical path are different objects that drift apart over time. The second shows, geometrically, where the extra term in Ito's lemma comes from: it is Jensen's inequality, drawn as a picture of a chord sitting above a tangent line.

Picture one: mean and median pulling apart

A GBM with drift μ and volatility σ has a median that grows at rate $\mu - \sigma^2/2$ and a mean that grows at the faster rate μ. Both are correct descriptions of the same process; they simply answer different questions. The median describes the outcome you should expect to see on any single simulated path. The mean is pulled upward by an ever-thinning population of extreme paths, and the longer the horizon, the more that thin population matters.

Picture two: the chord, the tangent, and where the correction comes from

Ito's lemma differs from the ordinary chain rule by a term $\tfrac12 f''(x)\sigma^2\,dt$. For a convex function this term is positive, and the reason is entirely geometric: diffusion spreads the value of X around its current level the way a small perturbation δ spreads a point x₀ to x₀ − δ and x₀ + δ. The average of f at those two spread-out points, the chord, sits above f evaluated at the centre, the tangent point, whenever f is convex. That gap is Jensen's inequality, and it is exactly the extra drift Ito's lemma adds.

Step 4

The detail

The SDE as an integral equation

The source considers equations of the form dZ(t) = μ(t,Z(t))dt + σ(t,Z(t))dW(t) with initial condition Z(a) = z, where z may be a constant or a random variable. This defines, by definition, an Ito process.

Ito process / SDE solution
A process Z(t) satisfying $dZ(t) = \mu(t,Z(t))\,dt + \sigma(t,Z(t))\,dW(t)$ with $Z(a)=z$ is, by definition, one for which $Z(t) = Z_a + \int_a^t \mu(s,Z(s))\,ds + \int_a^t \sigma(s,Z(s))\,dW(s)$ holds for every t ≥ a. Nothing here presumes differentiability of any path; the defining object is the pair of integrals, one ordinary (Riemann or Lebesgue) and one an Ito integral.

Strong and weak solutions, and when a solution exists at all

A strong solution fixes the probability space, the filtration, and the Brownian motion W in advance, and builds X(t) as an explicit, adapted functional of the given path of W. Every solution constructed in the source, arithmetic Brownian motion, geometric Brownian motion via the log-transform, and the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process via an integrating factor, is a strong solution in exactly this sense: each is a closed-form function of t and the already-given W(t). A weak solution asks less: only that some probability space, filtration, and Brownian motion exist on which a process with the right law can be built. The driving noise is not handed to you, it is part of what you are allowed to construct. Strong solutions are what you can simulate directly against an observed price path; weak solutions matter chiefly for proving that an SDE has a solution at all when the coefficients are not well-behaved enough to guarantee a strong one by direct construction.

The standard existence and uniqueness theorem (stated here without proof; see Øksendal, Stochastic Differential Equations, chapter 5, or Björk, Arbitrage Theory in Continuous Time, chapters 4 to 5) says: if there is a constant K such that, uniformly over t in [0, T],

$$ |\mu(t,x)-\mu(t,y)| + |\sigma(t,x)-\sigma(t,y)| \le K|x-y| \quad \text{(Lipschitz)}, \qquad |\mu(t,x)| + |\sigma(t,x)| \le K(1+|x|) \quad \text{(linear growth)}, $$

and X(0) has finite second moment, then the SDE has a pathwise unique strong solution on [0, T], continuous in t, with $\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_0^T X(t)^2\,dt\right] < \infty$. The Lipschitz condition buys uniqueness; linear growth buys existence for all of [0, T] by ruling out the coefficients exploding faster than the solution can be controlled. Arithmetic and geometric Brownian motion satisfy both trivially, since μ and σ are linear in x. The CIR process introduced later in this section has a diffusion coefficient $\sigma\sqrt{x}$ that is not Lipschitz at x = 0 (the square root has infinite slope there), so the theorem above does not apply directly; a unique strong solution nonetheless exists, by a finer pathwise-uniqueness argument (Yamada-Watanabe) that trades the Lipschitz condition for Hölder continuity of order one-half combined with a monotone drift. This is a genuine subtlety the source never raises, since it never leaves the linear (ABM/GBM/OU) setting.

Ito's lemma, one dimension

For X(t) satisfying $dX = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW$ and a function f(t,x) once differentiable in t and twice in x,

$$ df(t,X(t)) = \left(f_t + \mu f_x + \tfrac12 \sigma^2 f_{xx}\right)dt + \sigma f_x \, dW(t). $$

The source writes the same partial derivatives as $F_1$ (with respect to t), $F_2$ (with respect to x) and $F_{22}$ (the second derivative in x); the two notations mean the same thing and both appear below so the two are easy to line up. The heuristic derivation is a second-order Taylor expansion,

$$ df = f_t\,dt + f_x\,dX + \tfrac12 f_{xx}(dX)^2 + \tfrac12 f_{tt}(dt)^2 + f_{tx}\,dt\,dX + \cdots, $$

followed by substituting $dX = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW$ and applying the multiplication rules $dt\cdot dt = 0$, $dt \cdot dW = 0$, $dW \cdot dW = dt$: every term of order higher than dt vanishes, leaving $(dX)^2 = \sigma^2\,dt$ and recovering the boxed formula. The one term that changes character compared with ordinary calculus is $\tfrac12 f_{xx}(dX)^2$: in ordinary calculus this is a second-order infinitesimal that vanishes, here it survives as a first-order term because $(dW)^2$ does not vanish at the same rate as dt does.

This substitution is legitimate, not a notational trick, because of quadratic variation, established in Lecture 6: for a partition $0 = t_0 < t_1 < \cdots < t_n = t$ with mesh going to zero, $\sum_i \bigl(W(t_{i+1}) - W(t_i)\bigr)^2 \to t$ in mean square. Ito's lemma is precisely the statement that this convergence can be carried inside the Taylor expansion term by term, replacing the random sum of squared increments by its deterministic limit. Every other second-order cross term (dt·dW, (dt)²) has quadratic variation zero and disappears in the same limit.

Worked example: geometric Brownian motion, solved in full

The source solves $dX = \mu X\,dt + \sigma X\,dW$, $X(a)=b$, by two methods: an informed guess based on the deterministic ODE analogue, and a systematic "matching of coefficients" that assumes $X_t = F(t, W_t)$ and solves the resulting PDE system for F. Both recover the same answer; the guessing method is reproduced here because it shows most directly where the $-\sigma^2/2$ comes from.

Apply Ito's lemma to Y(t) = ln X(t), where dX = mu X dt + sigma X dW. f(x) = ln x f_x = 1/x f_xx = -1/x^2 dY = f_x dX + (1/2) f_xx (dX)^2 = (1/X) dX - (1/(2 X^2)) (dX)^2 Substitute dX = mu X dt + sigma X dW and (dX)^2 = sigma^2 X^2 dt: dY = (1/X)(mu X dt + sigma X dW) - (1/(2 X^2)) sigma^2 X^2 dt = mu dt + sigma dW - (1/2) sigma^2 dt = (mu - sigma^2 / 2) dt + sigma dW Y(t) is now an arithmetic Brownian motion. Integrate directly: Y(t) = Y(a) + (mu - sigma^2/2)(t-a) + sigma (W(t) - W(a)) ln X(t) = ln X(a) + (mu - sigma^2/2)(t-a) + sigma (W(t) - W(a)) X(t) = X(a) exp[ (mu - sigma^2/2)(t-a) + sigma (W(t) - W(a)) ]

Setting a = 0 gives the familiar closed form $S(t) = S(0)\exp\!\bigl[(\mu-\sigma^2/2)t + \sigma W(t)\bigr]$, which is exactly the Samuelson model the source verifies in the opposite direction (starting from this formula, apply Ito's lemma, and check that it satisfies the GBM SDE). The source's second method, matching of coefficients, arrives at the same formula from an assumed form $F(t,W_t)$ and a PDE for F; it is worth knowing precisely because it fails elsewhere. Applied to $dX_s = s\,dW_s$, matching coefficients demands $F_1(s,x) + \tfrac12F_{22}(s,x) = 0$ and $F_2(s,x)=s$, and the second equation forces $F(s,x)=sx+\phi(x)$, which substituted into the first gives $s = -\tfrac12\phi''(x)$: a function of s equal to a function of x alone, which is absurd. The SDE has no solution of the Markovian form $F(s,W_s)$; it is a genuinely non-Markovian process, $X_t = X_0 + \int_0^t s\,dW_s$, whose value depends on the entire path of W up to t, not merely on W(t). The lesson generalises: matching of coefficients works only when the SDE happens to admit a solution expressible as a function of t and the current value of W, and the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process below is a second example, noted explicitly in the source, where it fails for a subtler reason.

The $-\sigma^2/2$ term: the most commonly misunderstood result in the course

The term $-\sigma^2/2$ in the exponent is not a correction for some error; it is what makes the formula consistent with $\mu$ meaning the arithmetic growth rate of S. Because $\ln$ is concave, applying it to a random variable loses value on average: $\mathbb{E}[\ln X(t)] < \ln \mathbb{E}[X(t)]$ whenever $X(t)$ is genuinely random, which is Jensen's inequality for a concave function. Concretely, from the derivation above,

$$ \mathbb{E}[\ln S(t)] = \ln S(0) + \left(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)t, \qquad \ln \mathbb{E}[S(t)] = \ln S(0) + \mu t, $$

using the standard log-normal moment $\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{\sigma W(t)}\bigr] = e^{\sigma^2 t/2}$ (the same identity appears as the log-normal mean formula on the WTW 365 Formulas.pdf summary sheet, Theme 7). The two quantities differ by exactly $-\sigma^2t/2$, and the sign is not a coincidence: it is Jensen's inequality applied to a concave transform, the mirror image of the convex-function picture in section 3.

Because $W(t)$ is symmetric around zero, $S(t)$, being a strictly increasing transform of $W(t)$, has median equal to that transform evaluated at the median of $W(t)$, namely zero:

$$ \text{median}\bigl[S(t)\bigr] = S(0)\exp\!\left[\left(\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)t\right], \qquad \mathbb{E}[S(t)] = S(0)e^{\mu t}. $$

The mean exceeds the median by a factor $e^{\sigma^2 t/2}$, growing without bound as t increases. This is why the expected log return, $(\mu-\sigma^2/2)t$, is not the log of the expected return, $\mu t$: the two live on opposite sides of a concave transform, and no amount of algebra makes them equal except in the degenerate case $\sigma = 0$. The quantity $\mu - \sigma^2/2$ is the continuously compounded rate that actually describes the typical path; $\mu$ describes the mean, which is dominated by a shrinking population of unusually large outcomes.

Parameters: S(0) = 100, mu = 10% p.a., sigma = 30% p.a. At T = 1 year: Median[S(1)] = 100 * exp(0.10 - 0.045) = 100 * exp(0.055) = 105.65 Mean[S(1)] = 100 * exp(0.10) = 110.52 Gap: the mean sits 4.6% above the median. At T = 10 years, same parameters: Median[S(10)] = 100 * exp(0.55) = 173.33 Mean[S(10)] = 100 * exp(1.00) = 271.83 Gap: the mean now sits 56.8% above the median. The typical (median) ten-year outcome is a 73% cumulative gain. The average outcome across all possible paths, pulled up by a shrinking population of very large realisations, is a 172% cumulative gain. Both numbers are correct; they answer different questions, and mistaking one for the other is the single most common error in long-horizon return projection.

Multiple assets: the cross-variation term

For two Ito processes $dX_1 = \mu_1\,dt + \sigma_1\,dW_1$ and $dX_2 = \mu_2\,dt + \sigma_2\,dW_2$, driven by Brownian motions with $dW_1\,dW_2 = \rho\,dt$, and a function $f(t,x_1,x_2)$, the multidimensional Ito's lemma reads

$$ df = \left(f_t + \mu_1 f_1 + \mu_2 f_2 + \tfrac12\sigma_1^2 f_{11} + \tfrac12\sigma_2^2 f_{22} + \rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2\, f_{12}\right)dt + \sigma_1 f_1\,dW_1 + \sigma_2 f_2\,dW_2. $$

The extra term relative to two independent applications of the one-dimensional lemma is the cross-variation piece $\rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2\,f_{12}\,dt$, arising because $dX_1\,dX_2 = \rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2\,dt$ by the same quadratic-variation argument as before, extended to the covariation of two correlated Brownian motions. A correlated pair can always be written $W_2 = \rho W_1 + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,W_3$, for an independent Brownian motion $W_3$, so that $dW_1\,dW_2 = \rho\,(dW_1)^2 + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,dW_1\,dW_3 = \rho\,dt$, the second term vanishing because $W_1$ and $W_3$ are independent. This term is entirely absent from the source, which never drives two processes with different Brownian motions; it becomes essential the moment two assets, two rates, or an asset and a stochastic volatility factor are modelled jointly (see Björk, chapters 4 to 5, or Shreve, Stochastic Calculus for Finance II, chapter 4, for the full multidimensional treatment).

The product and quotient rules

Setting $f(x_1,x_2) = x_1x_2$ in the two-dimensional lemma ($f_1=x_2$, $f_2=x_1$, $f_{11}=f_{22}=0$, $f_{12}=1$) gives Ito's integration-by-parts formula,

$$ d(X_1X_2) = X_2\,dX_1 + X_1\,dX_2 + dX_1\,dX_2, $$

identical to the ordinary product rule except for the surviving cross-variation term $dX_1\,dX_2 = \rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2\,dt$. For two correlated GBMs, $dX_i = \mu_iX_i\,dt+\sigma_iX_i\,dW_i$, this gives $d(X_1X_2) = X_1X_2\bigl[(\mu_1+\mu_2+\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2)\,dt + \sigma_1\,dW_1+\sigma_2\,dW_2\bigr]$: the product of two GBMs is itself a GBM, but its drift is not simply $\mu_1+\mu_2$, it picks up an extra $\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2$ from the cross-variation, exactly the multi-asset generalisation of the single-asset $S^2$ result below. Setting $f(x_1,x_2)=x_1/x_2$ ($f_1=1/x_2$, $f_2=-x_1/x_2^2$, $f_{22}=2x_1/x_2^3$, $f_{12}=-1/x_2^2$) gives the quotient rule; for the same two correlated GBMs it works out to

$$ \frac{d(X_1/X_2)}{X_1/X_2} = \bigl(\mu_1-\mu_2+\sigma_2^2-\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2\bigr)\,dt + \sigma_1\,dW_1 - \sigma_2\,dW_2. $$

The source's own exercises 3.5.5 and 3.5.6 ask for exactly these two derivations, but with both GBMs driven by the same Brownian motion, the special case $\rho=1$. The general-ρ versions above are the practically relevant ones: they are the starting point for quanto adjustments and for any relative-value model involving two correlated assets.

(a) Ito applied to f(S) = S^2 under GBM dS = mu S dt + sigma S dW f_S = 2S, f_SS = 2 df = f_S dS + (1/2) f_SS (dS)^2 = 2S(mu S dt + sigma S dW) + (1/2)(2)(sigma^2 S^2 dt) = (2 mu + sigma^2) S^2 dt + 2 sigma S^2 dW d(S^2) = (2 mu + sigma^2) S^2 dt + 2 sigma S^2 dW The drift of S^2 is (2 mu + sigma^2), not 2 mu. The extra sigma^2 term is the Ito correction, positive because f is convex. The ordinary chain rule, d(S^2) = 2S dS, would have missed it entirely. (b) Verifying the discounted stock is a martingale under Q Under the risk-neutral measure, replace the real-world drift mu with the risk-free rate r: dS = r S dt + sigma S dW^Q. Discounted price: Stilde(t) = exp(-rt) S(t). d(Stilde) = -r exp(-rt) S dt + exp(-rt) dS = -r exp(-rt) S dt + exp(-rt)(r S dt + sigma S dW^Q) = sigma exp(-rt) S dW^Q = sigma Stilde dW^Q The dt terms cancel exactly. Stilde has zero drift: it is a driftless Ito process, a (local) martingale under Q. This is the defining property of the risk-neutral measure, and the reason r, not mu, appears in every pricing formula from here on: substitute mu for r above and the dt term does not cancel.

The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

The source poses the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process as a guided exercise, $dZ(t) = -\alpha Z(t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW(t)$, $Z(0)=z_0$, with the explicit hint to try the substitution $Y(t) = e^{\alpha t}Z(t)$ and apply Ito's lemma, and the explicit warning that matching of coefficients does not work here, because the solution is not expressible as $F(t,W_t)$ for any function F: it genuinely depends on the whole history of Z, not merely on the current value of W.

Ornstein-Uhlenbeck: dZ(t) = -alpha Z(t) dt + sigma dW(t), Z(0)=z0 Integrating factor: Y(t) = exp(alpha t) Z(t) p(t,z) = exp(alpha t) z p_t = alpha exp(alpha t) z, p_z = exp(alpha t), p_zz = 0 dY = p_t dt + p_z dZ + (1/2) p_zz (dZ)^2 = alpha exp(alpha t) Z dt + exp(alpha t)(-alpha Z dt + sigma dW) = sigma exp(alpha t) dW The dt terms cancel by construction: that is what "integrating factor" means. Integrate directly: Y(t) = z0 + sigma * Integral_0^t exp(alpha s) dW(s) Z(t) = exp(-alpha t) Y(t) = z0 exp(-alpha t) + sigma * Integral_0^t exp(-alpha(t-s)) dW(s)

Since W has zero mean, $\mathbb{E}[Z(t)] = z_0e^{-\alpha t} \to 0$ as $t\to\infty$: the process mean-reverts to zero. Its variance is $\sigma^2\bigl(1-e^{-2\alpha t}\bigr)/(2\alpha) \to \sigma^2/(2\alpha)$, giving a stationary distribution $N\bigl(0,\sigma^2/(2\alpha)\bigr)$. The extension to a general mean-reversion level, $dX = \alpha(\theta-X)\,dt+\sigma\,dW$, needed for the Vasicek short-rate model, follows by the substitution $Z=X-\theta$, which reduces it to exactly the equation above: $X(t) = \theta + (X(0)-\theta)e^{-\alpha t} + \sigma\int_0^te^{-\alpha(t-s)}\,dW(s)$, stationary $N\bigl(\theta,\sigma^2/(2\alpha)\bigr)$. This generalisation does not appear in the source; see Björk, chapters 4 to 5, or Øksendal, chapter 5, for the general treatment.

The CIR row has no closed-form path solution for the reason flagged earlier: the square-root diffusion is not linear in X, so no integrating factor or log-transform reduces it to an ABM in disguise the way GBM and OU both do. Its transition density is known exactly (a scaled non-central chi-squared distribution), which is enough for simulation and for pricing bonds under it, but it is a genuinely different kind of "solved" from the closed-form path expressions in the first three rows. The Feller condition, $2\alpha\theta \ge \sigma^2$, has a direct reading: the mean-reverting pull toward θ, scaled by 2α, must dominate the diffusive push near zero, scaled by σ²; when it holds, the process never reaches zero, and when it fails, zero is reached with positive probability and the square-root diffusion coefficient still keeps the solution well-defined by shutting off precisely there.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Ito's lemma is the engine of every hedging argument

Delta hedging, the technique that makes an option replicable by a continuously rebalanced portfolio, is nothing more than Ito's lemma applied to the option's value function $V(t,S)$. Expand $dV$ using the one-dimensional lemma from section 4, and the coefficient of dW in the resulting expression is $\sigma S\,V_S$: the exposure a hedger must offset by holding $V_S$ units of the underlying. That single observation, that the diffusion term of $dV$ is $\sigma S V_S\,dW$, is the entire content of Lecture 9's Black-Scholes PDE derivation and the delta-hedging argument that follows it. Every later lecture in this course that constructs a hedge is doing this same calculation for a different V.

Volatility drag: from leveraged products to actuarial projections

The gap between $\mu$ and $\mu-\sigma^2/2$ has a name in practice: volatility drag. A daily-rebalanced 2x leveraged exchange-traded product does not deliver twice the cumulative return of its underlying index over any horizon longer than a day, because leveraging multiplies σ as well as μ, and the drag term scales with σ², not σ: doubling volatility more than doubles the drag. The same arithmetic applies, with lower stakes but larger cumulative effect, to any long-horizon actuarial projection built from an assumed arithmetic-mean return. A forty-year pension fund projection that plugs in the arithmetic mean historical return, without the $-\sigma^2/2$ adjustment, is systematically projecting the mean of a right-skewed distribution as if it were the typical outcome, and the numerical example in section 4 shows how large that overstatement becomes at even a ten-year horizon.

Jensen's inequality: the static shadow of the Ito correction

Every time this lecture applied Ito's lemma to a convex or concave function, an extra term of a definite sign appeared: positive for convex $f$ (the $S^2$ example), negative for concave $f$ (the $\ln$ transform behind GBM). This is not a coincidence of the specific examples chosen. Jensen's inequality, $\mathbb{E}[f(X)] \ge f(\mathbb{E}[X])$ for convex f and reversed for concave f, is a static statement about a single random variable. Ito's lemma is its dynamic, infinitesimal-time twin: the extra drift term $\tfrac12f''\sigma^2\,dt$ is precisely the rate at which Jensen's gap accumulates over an instant. Anywhere a convex payoff appears (a call option, S², a leveraged product), expect Ito's lemma to hand back a positive drift correction that the ordinary chain rule would miss; anywhere a concave transform appears (log returns, a floor on a payoff), expect the reverse.

Watch out Do not substitute the real-world drift μ where the risk-neutral drift r belongs, or vice versa. Worked example (b) in section 4 shows that the discounted stock price is a martingale precisely because r, and only r, makes the drift term cancel after discounting. Using μ instead leaves a residual drift term of $(\mu - r)$, which means the "discounted price" carries a systematic trend and is no longer a martingale under the measure you are working in. This error surfaces constantly in practice: calibrating a pricing model to historical returns (which estimate μ) rather than to traded option prices or the risk-free curve (which pin down r) silently reintroduces a risk premium into a framework built to price under the assumption that there is none.
Step 6

Test yourself

  1. Explain why $dX(t) = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW(t)$ cannot be read as "the derivative of X is μ plus σ times the derivative of W". Write out what the expression actually asserts, in full integral form.
  2. State the existence and uniqueness theorem for SDEs under Lipschitz and linear growth conditions. Why do arithmetic and geometric Brownian motion satisfy the hypotheses immediately, while the CIR process does not, at least not without a finer argument?
  3. Derive Ito's lemma for $f(t,X(t))$ from a second-order Taylor expansion. Which single term changes character compared with ordinary calculus, and why does it survive rather than vanish?
  4. Lecture 6 shows that the quadratic variation of Brownian motion over $[0,t]$ equals t. Explain precisely how this licenses the substitution $(dW)^2 = dt$ inside Ito's lemma, as opposed to it being a notational convenience.
  5. Apply Ito's lemma to $Y(t) = \ln X(t)$ for X following a GBM, and hence derive the closed-form solution $X(t) = X(0)\exp[(\mu-\sigma^2/2)t + \sigma W(t)]$.
  6. A colleague argues that since $\mathbb{E}[\ln(S(T)/S(0))] = (\mu-\sigma^2/2)T$, the expected return on the stock must be $\mu-\sigma^2/2$. Explain what is wrong with this statement, and give the correct expected (arithmetic) return.
  7. For two GBMs driven by Brownian motions with $dW_1\,dW_2=\rho\,dt$, derive the SDE satisfied by the product $X_1(t)X_2(t)$. Where does the term $\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2$ come from?
  8. State the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck SDE, solve it using the integrating factor $Y(t)=e^{\alpha t}Z(t)$, and give its stationary distribution. Why does the source say matching of coefficients cannot solve this SDE?
  9. Why does the CIR process have no closed-form path solution, even though it is, like the OU process, mean-reverting? What replaces the closed-form path in practice?
  10. Verify, using Ito's lemma, that the discounted stock price is driftless under the risk-neutral drift r. Explain precisely why the calculation would fail to produce a driftless process if the real-world drift μ were substituted instead.

Reported gaps in source

  • The UP PDF is seven pages and entirely one-dimensional: every worked example and every exercise is driven by a single Brownian motion. It never states a multidimensional Ito's lemma or a cross-variation term, so this lecture supplies both, along with the product and quotient rules for two correlated Ito processes, which the source only poses (exercises 3.5.5 and 3.5.6) for the special case of two GBMs sharing the same Brownian motion.
  • The source never states a formal existence and uniqueness theorem. It demonstrates, by direct construction, that ABM, GBM and the OU process each have explicit solutions, and demonstrates by counterexample ($dX_s = s\,dW_s$) that not every SDE has a solution of the Markovian form $F(t,W_t)$. It does not connect either observation to the general Lipschitz/linear-growth theorem, which this lecture adds.
  • The source never names or distinguishes strong and weak solutions; "solution" is used informally to mean an explicit closed-form expression, and no measure-theoretic distinction is drawn between a solution built from a given Brownian motion and one for which the probability space is part of the construction.
  • The CIR process, needed for the Vasicek/CIR short-rate models in Lecture 12, does not appear anywhere in the source. It is included here in the standard-SDE table only, in anticipation of that lecture.
  • Notes.pdf and WTW 365 Formulas.pdf were both checked for this lecture. Notes.pdf covers mean-variance portfolio theory, CAPM, APT, utility theory and measures of investment risk; it contains no material on stochastic differential equations, and nothing from it was used. The formula sheet's Theme 7 (stochastic returns, log-normal moments) is the one useful cross-reference, and it backs up the mean-versus-median arithmetic in section 4. The formula summary that does cover this material is WTW 364 Summary.pdf, Unit 3 (stochastic calculus), which states Ito's lemma and the standard SDE solutions in the form the course examines them.
