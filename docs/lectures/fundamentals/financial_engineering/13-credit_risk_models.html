Credit risk models

A firm's equity is a call option on its own assets, struck at the face value of its debt. That one sentence, due to Merton, is the whole of structural credit risk modelling: default is what happens when the option expires worthless. The market takes a different route entirely, treating default as a bolt from the blue with a hazard rate calibrated to whatever spread it is willing to pay. This lecture builds both, and spends real time on the distinction a working credit modeller cannot afford to blur: the probability the market prices is not the probability your data measures.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Credit risk models split into two families that answer two different questions. A structural model asks what has to happen to the firm for it to default, and answers with the balance sheet: default occurs when the value of the firm's assets falls below the face value of its debt. A reduced-form model asks nothing about the firm's balance sheet at all. It asks what the market is charging for the possibility of default, and answers with a hazard rate, the instantaneous arrival probability of a default event, calibrated directly to observed bond or CDS spreads.

Merton's structural model treats the firm's asset value V_t as a geometric Brownian motion, exactly the dynamics lecture 9 gave the stock. Equity is the residual claim after debt is repaid, so at maturity T the equityholders receive $\max(V_T - K, 0)$, where K is the face value of a single zero-coupon debt tranche. That payoff is a European call on V_t struck at K, so the entire Black-Scholes machinery of lecture 9 prices the equity, prices the debt as a residual, and hands you a risk-neutral default probability, $\Phi(-d_2)$, for free. Reduced-form models skip the balance sheet and model the default time directly as the first jump of a Poisson process with hazard rate $\lambda$, from which survival probability, bond prices, and CDS spreads all follow by direct calculation.

Feynman test A bank lends a company $500{,}000$ for a year. The company's assets are worth $1{,}000{,}000$ today and bounce around with 50% annual volatility. Will the loan be repaid? You cannot answer that with a balance sheet snapshot, because the number that matters is not where assets stand today but where they might stand in a year against the $500{,}000$ hurdle. Merton's insight is that this is exactly the question a call option answers: the firm's owners get to keep the upside above the debt and can walk away from the downside, so their equity stake is a call on the firm's assets, and the same volatility that makes the option valuable is exactly what makes the loan risky. Price the option and you have priced the loan, by arithmetic rather than assumption.
What's beyond the source The UP PDF covers Merton's structural model only, sections 4.1 and 4.2, running the equity-as-a-call-option argument through to the risk premium and the real-world default probability, with a worked example and four exercises. Step 2

Why this matters

Every other lecture in this course prices a claim on an asset that is not itself in danger of ceasing to exist. Credit risk is different: the underlying event is the extinction of the obligor, and that changes what "pricing" even means. A structural model earns its keep wherever a firm's balance sheet is genuinely observable or estimable, corporate credit ratings, bank internal ratings, private-firm lending, because it explains default in terms of quantities a credit analyst already looks at: leverage, asset volatility, debt maturity. A reduced-form model earns its keep wherever a liquid market already prices the risk, corporate bond desks, CDS trading books, structured credit, because it takes the market's own valuation as given and turns it into a calibrated arrival rate without ever needing to open the firm's accounts.

For a reader who builds A-IRB PD models and IFRS 9 impairment models for a living, this lecture is not an academic detour. Every derivative-pricing default probability in this lecture, the $\Phi(-d_2)$ from Merton and the hazard rate $\lambda$ bootstrapped from CDS spreads, is a risk-neutral quantity. It is the probability that makes traded prices arbitrage-free under a measure constructed so that every asset's discounted price is a martingale. The PD you estimate for regulatory capital or accounting provisions is a real-world quantity, estimated from historical default data under the actual probability measure, whether through-the-cycle for A-IRB or point-in-time for IFRS 9. These two numbers are not two estimates of the same thing with different error bars. They are two different mathematical objects that happen to share the letters "PD", and section 4 spends real space showing exactly how large the gap between them can be and why it never closes to zero. The credit risk lecture track in this repository, starting at the credit risk overview and its treatment of PD concept at A-IRB PD concept, builds the real-world side of this picture in full; this lecture builds the risk-neutral side and draws the line between them explicitly.

Step 3

The mental model

Two pictures carry the whole of Merton's model. The first shows the same random quantity, the firm's terminal asset value $V_T$, viewed under two different probability measures, real-world and risk-neutral, each assigning a different amount of mass to the default region below the debt barrier K. The second shows what equityholders and bondholders each receive as a function of that same terminal asset value, which is the picture that makes the option decomposition impossible to un-see once you have seen it.

Picture one: two measures, one barrier, two different default probabilities

The firm's asset value at the horizon is random under both measures, but the two measures disagree about its drift: the real-world measure uses the firm's actual expected return $\mu$, the risk-neutral measure uses the risk-free rate r. Whenever the firm carries a positive risk premium, meaning $\mu > r$, the risk-neutral distribution sits to the left of the real-world one, and more of its mass falls below the default barrier K. The risk-neutral default probability is mechanically larger than the real-world one, for a reason that has nothing to do with which estimate is more accurate and everything to do with which question each measure is built to answer.

Picture two: equity and debt as two option payoffs on the same firm

At maturity T the firm's asset value $V_T$ is split between exactly two claimants, and equation (4) of the source, $E_T + P^d(T,T) = V_T$, says the split is exhaustive: nothing is created or destroyed, the firm's value merely changes hands. If $V_T \geq K$ the firm is solvent, bondholders receive the promised K in full, and equityholders keep the excess $V_T - K$. If $V_T < K$ the firm is insolvent, bondholders receive whatever is left, $V_T$, and equityholders receive nothing. Equity is a call on the firm struck at K; debt is the firm minus that call, which is the same as a risk-free bond minus a put on the firm struck at K.

Step 4

The detail

Setting up Merton's model

The source (section 4.2) posits a firm whose total asset value $V_t$ follows the same geometric Brownian motion lecture 9 assumed for a stock:

$$ dV_t = \mu V_t \, dt + \sigma V_t \, dW_t, \qquad dB_t = r B_t \, dt, $$

with $\mu$ the firm's real-world asset drift, $\sigma$ its asset volatility, and r the continuously compounded risk-free rate. The firm's only liability is a single zero-coupon defaultable bond of face value K maturing at T. Writing $E_t$ for the value of equity and $P^d(t,T)$ for the value of the defaultable bond, the balance sheet identity $V_t = E_t + P^d(t,T)$ holds at every $t \in [0,T]$.

Merton's structural model
Default is defined purely by the balance sheet at the single date T: the firm defaults if and only if $V_T < K$. If the firm survives, $V_T \geq K$, bondholders are repaid in full and equityholders keep the residual, $E_T = V_T - K$. If the firm defaults, bondholders seize the entire firm, $P^d(T,T) = V_T$, and equityholders receive nothing. Combining the two cases, $E_T = \max\{0, V_T - K\}$ and $P^d(T,T) = \min\{V_T, K\}$: equity is exactly the payoff of a European call on the firm's assets struck at K, and debt is the firm minus that call.

Equity as a call, debt as the residual

Because $E_T = \max\{0, V_T-K\}$ is a European call payoff on V struck at K, no-arbitrage forces $E_t = C_t$ for every $t \in [0,T]$, where $C_t$ is the Black-Scholes price of that call. Substituting lecture 9's boxed formula, with the firm value $V_t$ in place of the stock and r unchanged, gives the source's theorem 4.2.2 directly:

$$ \boxed{\, E_t = V_t \, \Phi(d_1) - K e^{-r(T-t)} \, \Phi(d_2), \,} $$

$$ d_1 = \frac{\ln(V_t/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)(T-t)}{\sigma\sqrt{T-t}}, \qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T-t}, $$

with $\Phi$ the standard normal CDF, written N in lecture 9; the two symbols mean the same function throughout this lecture. Debt is then the residual, $P^d(t,T) = V_t - E_t$, which a short algebraic rearrangement using $1-\Phi(d_1) = \Phi(-d_1)$ turns into the source's second formula:

$$ P^d(t,T) = \Phi(-d_1) \, V_t + K e^{-r(T-t)} \, \Phi(d_2). $$

Read the debt formula as a decomposition in its own right. The second term, $Ke^{-r(T-t)}\Phi(d_2)$, is the discounted face value weighted by $\Phi(d_2)$, the risk-neutral probability that the firm survives; the first term is the discounted value bondholders recover in the states where the firm does not. $\Phi(d_2)$ here is the exact analogue of $N(d_2)$ in the ordinary Black-Scholes call formula, the risk-neutral probability that the option, here the equity claim, finishes in the money.

The risk-neutral default probability and the distance to default

Since $\Phi(d_2)$ is the risk-neutral probability of solvency, $\Phi(-d_2)$ is the risk-neutral probability of default, exactly as $N(-d_2)$ in a plain vanilla put is the risk-neutral probability of finishing out of the money. This is the quantity the market prices when it prices the firm's debt: it costs the same as insuring against a default whose risk-neutral frequency is $\Phi(-d_2)$, whatever the true frequency turns out to be.

$$ \boxed{\, \text{PD}^{\mathbb{Q}} = \Phi(-d_2). \,} $$

Because $d_2$ measures $\ln(V_t/K)$, the log-distance between the firm's current value and the default barrier, netted against the drift and scaled by the volatility over the horizon, it is naturally read as a number of standard deviations of asset-value movement separating the firm from default. That reading is the distance to default: a firm with a large, positive $d_2$ is many standard deviations of asset-value movement away from insolvency, and a small or negative $d_2$ signals a firm within one bad quarter of breaching the barrier. Distance to default is the natural risk measure here for the same reason $d_2$ is the natural argument of a survival probability: it collapses leverage, volatility and horizon into a single comparable number, which is exactly why Moody's KMV built a commercial ratings methodology on top of it, discussed further below.

The yield spread

The source measures the compensation the bond market demands for default risk through the yield spread, following its own notation (section 4.2.1). The yield of a T-maturity bond with face value K and price $P(t,T)$ solves $P(t,T) = Ke^{-Y(t,T)(T-t)}$, so for a risk-free bond $Y(t,T) = r$ under a flat curve. The defaultable bond's yield solves the same equation with its own price, $P^d(t,T) = Ke^{-Y^d(t,T)(T-t)}$, giving

$$ Y^d(t,T) = \frac{\ln K - \ln P^d(t,T)}{T-t}, \qquad \text{yield spread} = Y^d(t,T) - Y(t,T). $$

The spread is not itself the default probability. It blends the probability of default with the loss given default implicit in the recovery $V_T$ bondholders keep when they do default, exactly the identification problem that resurfaces in reduced-form form later in this section.

Worked example: reproducing the source, then extending it

Example 4.2.3 of the source gives $V_0 = 1{,}000{,}000$, $K = 500{,}000$, $T=1$, $\sigma = 0.5$, $r=0.05$, and a firm asset drift $\mu = 0.08$. Its own worked arithmetic gives $E_0 = 534{,}579$, $P^d(0,1) = 465{,}421$, a defaultable bond yield $Y^d(0,1) = 7.17\%$ against the risk-free $5\%$, a yield spread of $2.17\%$, and, from example 4.2.5, a real-world default probability of $9.74\%$. Recomputing every one of these figures independently confirms the source's arithmetic to the last reported decimal, and confirms too that the source's $d_1$ and $d_2$ formulae match the standard Black-Scholes convention exactly, with no sign or drift anomaly of the kind flagged elsewhere in this course's source material.

To make the risk-neutral versus real-world contrast concrete with rounder numbers, take a firm with asset value $V_0 = 100$, debt face value $K=70$ maturing in $T=1$ year, asset volatility $\sigma = 25\%$, risk-free rate $r=5\%$, and real-world asset drift $\mu = 10\%$.

Real-world versus risk-neutral: the distinction that matters most for this reader

Two default probabilities have now been derived from the same firm, the same barrier, the same volatility, and the same horizon, and they disagree by half. The mechanism is exact, not approximate: both probabilities are $\Phi$ applied to a $d_2$-type quantity built from $\ln(V_0/K)$ netted against a drift term and scaled by $\sigma\sqrt{T}$, and the two calculations differ only in which drift enters that netting, r for the risk-neutral case, $\mu$ for the real-world case. Whenever the firm's assets carry a positive risk premium, $\mu > r$, the real-world drift term is larger, the real-world $\tilde d_2$ is larger, and $\Phi(-\tilde d_2)$ is therefore smaller than $\Phi(-d_2)$. The risk-neutral default probability exceeds the real-world one for every firm whose assets are expected to earn more than the risk-free rate, which in practice is essentially every firm a bank would consider lending to. The gap is not noise around a shared true value; it is the risk premium, priced.

The economic reason is the same one lecture 9 gave for options generally. A risk-neutral price is constructed so that every traded asset's discounted price is a martingale, which requires assuming every asset drifts at the risk-free rate under that measure, whatever it actually does in the world. A defaultable bond priced this way is priced to be arbitrage-free against the other traded assets in the same market, and the $\Phi(-d_2)$ that falls out of that pricing is not an estimate of how often the firm will actually default. It is the default frequency that would make the bond's price consistent with a risk-free numeraire, inflated relative to the true frequency by exactly the compensation investors demand for bearing a risk they cannot diversify away. A CDS spread or a corporate bond yield spread is, in this precise sense, always answering "what does the market charge for this risk", never "how often will this actually happen".

This is the single most consequential confusion available to a credit modeller who moves between derivatives pricing and prudential or accounting work. An IFRS 9 expected credit loss calculation asks for the real-world, unbiased probability that an exposure defaults over the relevant horizon, because IFRS 9 explicitly wants a best estimate of expected cash shortfalls, not a risk-adjusted price. An A-IRB PD model targets a long-run average of realised default rates, through-the-cycle by construction, again a real-world quantity, because the regulatory capital formula already builds in its own conservatism through the supervisory correlation and confidence level rather than through an embedded market risk premium. Feeding a CDS-implied or bond-spread-implied PD into either calculation without stripping out the risk premium first systematically overstates expected loss, sometimes by the same rough factor of one and a half seen in the worked example above, and the overstatement grows, not shrinks, for lower-quality, higher-spread names, precisely where getting the number right matters most. The two PDs answer different questions and belong to different models; treating a market-implied PD as a free, well-calibrated substitute for a real-world one is a fluent-sounding error, not a subtle one, once the mechanism is seen this plainly.

Where Merton breaks, and what came after

Four limitations of the basic model drove three decades of extensions. Default can only occur at the single maturity date T, so a firm whose assets crash and recover before T never defaults in the model even though it may have been briefly insolvent. The asset value $V_t$ and its volatility $\sigma$ are not directly observable, since firms do not trade their total assets on an exchange; both must be inferred indirectly from the equity price and equity volatility that are observable, by solving the model's own two equations, $E_t$ as a function of $(V_t,\sigma)$ and Itô's lemma relating equity and asset volatility, simultaneously. The model assumes a single, homogeneous class of debt, when real capital structures stack multiple seniorities with different recovery rates. And the model's short-maturity credit spreads come out far too low relative to what is observed in real corporate bond markets, because Brownian motion assigns vanishingly small probability to a large move over a very short horizon, while real short-dated spreads reflect a persistent risk of sudden jumps that a continuous diffusion cannot generate.

Two responses dominate practice. Moody's KMV built a commercial implementation around exactly the distance-to-default idea above: back out $V_t$ and $\sigma$ from equity data, compute distance to default, and then map that distance empirically onto an expected default frequency using a large historical default database, rather than trusting the model's own analytic normal-CDF formula, which tends to understate real-world default rates in the tails. Black and Cox's first-passage extension lets default occur the instant assets first touch a barrier at any time before T, not only at maturity, which fixes the "default can only happen at T" limitation directly and produces more realistic short-maturity spread behaviour, at the cost of a harder first-passage-time calculation in place of the simple terminal-value one used above.

Reduced-form models: default as a jump, not a balance-sheet event

A reduced-form model gives up on explaining default and instead models the default time $\tau$ directly as the first jump of a Poisson process with (initially constant) intensity, or hazard rate, $\lambda$. The defining property of such a process is memorylessness: conditional on survival to time $t$, the probability of surviving a further $u$ years is independent of how long the firm has already survived, giving the exponential survival function

$$ \mathbb{Q}(\tau > t) = e^{-\lambda t}. $$

Consider a defaultable zero-coupon bond paying $1$ at T if the firm survives, and a fixed recovery R (as a fraction of face value, paid at T) if it does not, with $\lambda$ constant and default risk independent of interest rates. Its risk-neutral price is the discounted expectation of that payoff,

$$ P^d(t,T) = e^{-r(T-t)}\Bigl[\underbrace{e^{-\lambda(T-t)}}_{\text{survives}} \cdot 1 + \underbrace{\bigl(1-e^{-\lambda(T-t)}\bigr)}_{\text{defaults}} \cdot R \Bigr] = e^{-r(T-t)}\Bigl[R + (1-R)e^{-\lambda(T-t)}\Bigr]. $$

Defining the bond's own yield spread the same way the source defines $Y^d - Y$, write $P^d(t,T) = e^{-(r+s)(T-t)}$ for spread $s$, so $e^{-s(T-t)} = R + (1-R)e^{-\lambda(T-t)}$. For small $\lambda(T-t)$, expand $e^{-\lambda(T-t)} \approx 1-\lambda(T-t)$, giving the right-hand side $R + (1-R)\bigl(1-\lambda(T-t)\bigr) = 1 - (1-R)\lambda(T-t)$, and $e^{-s(T-t)} \approx 1 - s(T-t)$ on the left for small $s(T-t)$. Matching the two linear approximations:

$$ \boxed{\, s \approx \lambda(1-R). \,} $$

Spread is hazard rate times loss given default, to leading order. This is a genuine identification problem, not a footnote: a single observed spread s pins down only the product $\lambda(1-R)$, never $\lambda$ and R separately. A market quoting a 200 basis point spread is exactly as consistent with $\lambda=4\%$ and $R=50\%$ as with $\lambda=2\%$ and $R=0\%$. Separating hazard rate from recovery requires an independent source of information on one of the two, exactly the split a bank's own PD and LGD models supply separately from historical workout data, and exactly why market-implied credit measures cannot substitute for a full PD and LGD decomposition even when they agree on the product.

Credit default swaps: mechanics and the par spread

A credit default swap (CDS) is a bilateral contract in which the protection buyer pays a periodic running premium, the CDS spread s, on a notional amount to the protection seller until the earlier of maturity or a default event on the reference entity; if a default event occurs before maturity, the protection seller pays the buyer $(1-R)$ times the notional, and the premium leg stops. The par spread is the value of s that makes the contract worth zero to both sides at inception, equivalently the spread that equates the present value of the premium leg to the present value of the protection leg.

Under the same constant-hazard, continuous-payment idealisation used above, with premium paid continuously at rate s while the reference entity survives and protection paid at the instant of default, the premium leg's present value is

$$ \text{PV(premium)} = s \int_0^T e^{-(r+\lambda)u} \, du = s \cdot \frac{1-e^{-(r+\lambda)T}}{r+\lambda}, $$

discounting at r and weighting by the survival probability $e^{-\lambda u}$ at each instant u, while the protection leg's present value, paid $(1-R)$ at the random default time with density $\lambda e^{-\lambda u}$, is

$$ \text{PV(protection)} = (1-R)\int_0^T \lambda e^{-\lambda u} e^{-ru} \, du = (1-R)\lambda \cdot \frac{1-e^{-(r+\lambda)T}}{r+\lambda}. $$

Setting the two legs equal and cancelling the common factor $\bigl(1-e^{-(r+\lambda)T}\bigr)/(r+\lambda)$ leaves

$$ \boxed{\, s = \lambda(1-R), \,} $$

exactly the bond-spread approximation above, except here it is exact in the continuous-payment, continuous-default limit rather than a small-$\lambda$ approximation. The same identification problem survives intact: a single CDS spread still identifies only the product $\lambda(1-R)$.

Real CDS markets quote a term structure of spreads at standard tenors, commonly one, three, five, seven and ten years, and a single flat hazard rate cannot fit all of them simultaneously unless the market happens to believe the hazard rate is constant. The standard technique, bootstrapping, assumes a piecewise-constant hazard rate, one flat segment per tenor bucket, and solves for each segment's hazard rate in turn: the shortest tenor's par-spread equation involves only $\lambda_1$ on $[0,T_1]$ and is solved directly; the next tenor's equation involves $\lambda_1$, now known, and the new unknown $\lambda_2$ on $[T_1,T_2]$, and is solved for $\lambda_2$ alone; and so on outward along the curve. The result is a piecewise-constant hazard curve consistent with every quoted spread simultaneously, the reduced-form analogue of bootstrapping a discount curve from a term structure of swap rates.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Why banks run both families side by side

A bank's internal rating and origination process leans structural, because a corporate credit analyst has a balance sheet, an income statement and a business plan in front of them, and distance to default gives a single, leverage-and-volatility-aware number that behaves sensibly as those fundamentals move. A trading desk marking a bond or CDS position to market leans reduced-form, because the desk's job is to match today's traded price exactly, not to explain it, and a hazard rate calibrated to the current spread curve does that by construction in a way no balance-sheet model can, since the balance-sheet model's output is a model price that may simply disagree with the tape. Counterparty credit risk and CVA desks sit between the two: they need a full, path-dependent simulation of a counterparty's exposure and default over the life of a long-dated derivative, so it is common to simulate default times using a reduced-form intensity, calibrated to the counterparty's own CDS curve where one exists, while retaining structural intuition (leverage, sector, macro factor sensitivity) to build intensities for the far larger number of counterparties with no liquid CDS quoted at all.

The Vasicek model is Merton in disguise

The single-factor Vasicek model underlying the Basel A-IRB capital formula is worth naming explicitly here, because it is structural credit risk modelling wearing a portfolio hat. Each obligor i is given a standardised asset-return proxy $R_i = \sqrt{\rho}\,Z + \sqrt{1-\rho}\,\varepsilon_i$, a systematic factor Z shared by every obligor in the portfolio and an idiosyncratic factor $\varepsilon_i$, both standard normal and independent of each other. Obligor i defaults exactly when $R_i$ falls below a threshold calibrated so that the unconditional default probability matches the obligor's PD, $\Phi^{-1}(\text{PD})$. This is the Merton default mechanism, asset value below a barrier, applied not to a single firm's continuous-time asset path but to a single-period, cross-sectional proxy for it, with the systematic factor Z standing in for the common macroeconomic driver of every firm's true (unobserved) asset return. The A-IRB risk-weight formula's conditional PD, $\Phi\bigl((\Phi^{-1}(\text{PD}) - \sqrt{\rho}\,Z)/\sqrt{1-\rho}\bigr)$ evaluated at the regulatory 99.9% confidence level for Z, is Merton's own $\Phi(-d_2)$-style default probability recomputed under a stress scenario for the shared factor. A reader who builds A-IRB PD models is, without perhaps naming it this way day to day, already working inside a structural model every time the capital formula is applied.

2008 and the limits of a single correlation number

The Gaussian copula, used to model joint default dependence across many obligors in a CDO or securitisation by coupling their individual reduced-form or structural default times through a single, constant pairwise correlation parameter, was standard market practice for portfolio credit before 2008 precisely because it let a desk fit an entire correlation structure from one traded number, the base correlation implied by index tranche prices. What the crisis exposed was that a single Gaussian correlation parameter badly underestimates the tendency of defaults to cluster in the tail: real defaults are far more likely to happen together in a systemic downturn than a Gaussian dependence structure implies, so the senior tranches of structured credit products that the Gaussian copula priced as almost risk-free absorbed losses the model had assigned near-zero probability. The lesson generalises past the specific copula: any portfolio credit model that borrows a correlation parameter fitted in ordinary times and applies it unchanged to a systemic tail event is making the same mistake, whether that correlation sits inside a Gaussian copula or inside the single asset-correlation parameter $\rho$ of the Vasicek formula just discussed.

Watch out Do not feed a CDS-implied or bond-spread-implied default probability into an IFRS 9 expected credit loss calculation or an A-IRB PD model without first stripping out the risk premium, and do not assume that stripping is a minor adjustment. Section 4's worked example showed a risk-neutral PD roughly one and a half times its real-world counterpart from identical fundamentals, and the ratio widens for riskier names, where risk premia are larger in absolute terms. A market-implied PD used directly as an accounting or regulatory input systematically overstates expected loss and understates capital efficiency, in a way that is easy to defend in a model document, because the number came from "the market", and wrong all the same.
