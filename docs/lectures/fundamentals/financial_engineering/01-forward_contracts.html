Forward contracts

The simplest derivative there is: an agreement today to trade an asset on a fixed future date at a fixed price. Two parties, no money changes hands at the start, and yet the contract has a value that moves with the market. Every later result in this course is a generalisation of the one argument used to price it.

Lecture 1 of 13 Reading time, 40 minutes Source: UP / 1 - Forward Contract.pdf
Step 1

The 30-second version

A forward contract is a bilateral agreement to trade one specific asset on one specific future date at one specific price. The price is fixed today, the trade happens later. No cash changes hands at the start, so the contract begins life worth zero to both sides. The interesting mathematics is how to choose that fixed price so it is fair to both parties, and how the value of the contract drifts away from zero as time passes and the spot price moves.

The fair price, called the forward price F(t), is uniquely determined by a no-arbitrage argument called cash and carry. The result is breathtakingly simple: on a non-income-paying asset with spot price S(t) and continuously compounded risk-free rate r, the forward price for delivery at time $T$ is $S(t)\, e^{r(T-t)}$. The spot price grown forward at the risk-free rate. Once you accept that, the rest of derivatives pricing is methodology.

Feynman test Imagine you and I shake hands today on the price at which I will sell you a barrel of oil in three months. We do not exchange any money now. The only thing we exchange is a promise. The question this lecture answers is: what is the right price to write into that promise so that neither of us would prefer the other side of the deal? The answer turns out to be the spot price today, but pushed forward in time using the interest rate, because the alternative to writing the forward is to buy the oil today and store it until delivery, financed at the risk-free rate. The forward price has to match this alternative, or someone will arbitrage the difference.
What's beyond the source The UP source covers the non-income-paying case (a stock with no dividend). This lecture extends to dividend-paying stocks, foreign currency (covered interest parity), commodities with storage costs and convenience yields, and the connection between the forward price and the risk-neutral expectation of the spot. It also draws out the replicating portfolio interpretation, which is the conceptual ancestor of every Black-Scholes proof later in the course.
Step 2

Why this matters

The forward contract is the simplest possible derivative, and that simplicity is the point. Every result later in the course is a generalisation of one technique used here: build a portfolio of traded assets that exactly replicates the payoff of the derivative, then invoke the absence of arbitrage to conclude that the derivative and the replicating portfolio must have the same price. The forward gives you that argument in its cleanest possible form: the replicating portfolio is just "buy the asset, borrow the money", and the no-arbitrage conclusion is just the spot-forward parity.

Practically, forwards are the building block of currency hedging (every multinational corporate uses FX forwards), commodity hedging (the farmer in the source PDF), and rate-locking (forward rate agreements). Conceptually, they are the prototype that teaches you the technique you will use to price options, swaps, and structured products. If you cannot price a forward by replication, you cannot price anything else.

Step 3

The mental model

Three pictures will carry you through the entire lecture. The first is the payoff diagram: a 45-degree line plotting the gain or loss at maturity against the spot price at maturity. The second is the timeline showing what passes between the two parties at the start, in the middle, and at maturity. The third, the most important, is the cash-and-carry portfolio that pins down the forward price by arbitrage.

Picture one: the payoff at maturity

At time T, the long party pays K and receives an asset worth S(T). Their payoff is therefore S(T) - K. The short party gets the opposite payoff, K - S(T). Both payoffs are linear in S(T). The long benefits if the spot at maturity ends up above the delivery price; the short benefits if it ends up below.

Picture two: the timeline

At time t (the start), the two parties agree on the delivery price K and exchange a promise. No cash. At intermediate times u between t and T, the contract has a market value that fluctuates with S(u): positive for one side, negative for the other. At time T (maturity), the trade actually happens: the long pays K and receives the asset, the short receives K and delivers the asset.

Picture three: cash and carry

This is the picture that does all the work. Suppose I want to hold the underlying asset at time T. I have two ways of getting there.

Route A. Enter a long forward at time t, with delivery price K. At time T, pay K and receive the asset.

Route B. Buy the asset on the spot market at time t for S(t), borrowing the money from the bank at the risk-free rate. Hold the asset until time T. At time T, the borrowing has grown to $S(t)\, e^{r(T-t)}$, which I repay. I now own the asset.

Both routes leave me holding the asset at time T. They cost nothing at time t in either case (the forward is free to enter; the spot purchase is fully financed by the loan). The only difference is the cash outflow at time T: K on route A, $S(t)\, e^{r(T-t)}$ on route B. If those two numbers were different, I would pick whichever route was cheaper and the counterparty on the other side of the forward would be either ripped off or refusing the trade. The only price K that makes the trade indifferent (and therefore acceptable to both sides) is

$$ F(t) = S(t) \, e^{r(T - t)}. $$

Step 4

The detail

Setting up the market

The pricing arguments assume a frictionless market with the following structure. There is one risky asset, the underlying, with spot price S(t) modelled as a random process. There is one risk-free asset, the bond, evolving deterministically as

$$ B(t) = B(s) \, e^{r(t-s)}, \quad t \geq s, $$

where r is the continuously compounded risk-free rate (the force of interest). Investors can borrow and lend in the bond at this rate without limit. They can also short sell the underlying without restriction, meaning they can hold a negative position. There are no transaction costs, no taxes, no margin requirements, and crucially no arbitrage opportunities: there is no portfolio that costs zero today and is guaranteed to be worth a non-negative amount at maturity with strictly positive probability of being worth more than zero.

Arbitrage opportunity
A portfolio Π over the interval [t, T] satisfying three conditions: (1) Π(t) = 0, (2) P(Π(T) ≥ 0) = 1, (3) P(Π(T) > 0) > 0. Costs nothing to set up, cannot lose money, might make money. In real markets such opportunities exist briefly and are competed away; in our model we assume they cannot exist at all.

The spot-forward parity by arbitrage

The argument is by contradiction. Suppose $F(t) < S(t)\, e^{r(T-t)}$ at some time $t$. We construct an arbitrage portfolio at time $t$:

Trades at time t (all execute at zero net cost): 1. Take a LONG position in the forward at delivery price F(t). cost 0 2. SHORT the underlying, receiving S(t) in cash. cost -S(t) 3. Invest the proceeds S(t) in the bond at rate r. cost +S(t) ----- Net cost: 0 At time T: Forward delivers the asset, which closes the short. payoff S(T) - F(t) Bond investment has grown. payoff +S(t)·exp(r(T-t)) Short position is now closed; no payoff. payoff -S(T) ---------- Portfolio value at T: S(t)·exp(r(T-t)) - F(t) > 0

The portfolio costs nothing at $t$, has zero risk between $t$ and $T$ (every leg is deterministic, except the short position which is exactly offset by the long forward), and is worth a strictly positive amount at $T$. This violates the no-arbitrage assumption, so the original supposition $F(t) < S(t)\, e^{r(T-t)}$ must be false.

The reverse inequality $F(t) > S(t)\, e^{r(T-t)}$ is ruled out by the mirror argument: short the forward, buy the underlying with borrowed cash, repay the loan at $T$ and pocket the difference. The two contradictions sandwich the forward price:

$$ \boxed{\,F(t) = S(t) \, e^{r(T - t)}.\,} $$

Two boundary checks. At $t = T$ the formula gives $F(T) = S(T)$, which is correct: the forward price at maturity must equal the spot price, since the "forward" with zero time to maturity is just an immediate trade. At the original contract initiation, with $t = 0$ and the delivery price $K$ chosen to make the contract fair, the formula gives $K = F(0) = S(0)\, e^{rT}$.

The value of an existing forward

Once a contract is in force with delivery price K (locked in at inception), its market value will move as time passes and the spot moves. Let V(t) denote that mark-to-market value at time t. By the same arbitrage logic applied to a replicating portfolio (one unit of the underlying, financed by borrowing $K\, e^{-r(T-t)}$ at rate r), the value is

$$ V(t) = S(t) - K \, e^{-r(T-t)}. $$

Three ways of reading this formula make it unforgettable.

Reading one. The first term is the current value of receiving the asset at T; the second is the present value of paying K at T. The long position is "long asset, short cash bond at strike K".

Reading two. Substitute $S(t) = F(t)\, e^{-r(T-t)}$ from the spot-forward parity:

$$ V(t) = e^{-r(T-t)} \bigl( F(t) - K \bigr). $$

The value of the contract is the discounted difference between the current forward price and the locked-in delivery price. If the forward price has risen since inception, the long is in the money; if it has fallen, the long is out of the money. The diagonal symmetry of the payoff diagram you saw in section 3 lives here in continuous form.

Reading three. At $t = 0$ with $K = S(0)\, e^{rT}$, the formula gives $V(0) = S(0) - S(0)\, e^{rT} \cdot e^{-rT} = 0$. The contract is worth nothing at inception, by construction, which is what allowed the parties to agree to it without any cash changing hands.

Replicating portfolio The proof of $V(t) = S(t) - K\, e^{-r(T-t)}$ works because the portfolio "one unit of asset minus $K\, e^{-r(T-t)}$ in cash" exactly reproduces the forward payoff $S(T) - K$ at maturity. This is the prototype of every pricing argument in the rest of the course. To price a derivative, find a portfolio of traded assets that replicates its payoff, then invoke no-arbitrage to equate prices. The harder cases (options, exotics) need stochastic calculus to construct the replicating portfolio dynamically, but the logic is identical.

Worked example: a three-month forward on a non-dividend stock

A stock trades at S(0) = 100. The continuously compounded risk-free rate is r = 4% per annum. A six-month forward contract is being written today. The fair delivery price is

$$ K = F(0) = 100 \cdot e^{0.04 \times 0.5} = 100 \cdot 1.0202 = 102.02. $$

Now suppose two months later the stock has risen to S(2/12) = 105 and the risk-free rate has stayed flat. The forward has four months left to maturity. Its mark-to-market value to the long is

$$ V(2/12) = 105 - 102.02 \cdot e^{-0.04 \times 4/12} = 105 - 102.02 \cdot 0.9868 = 105 - 100.67 = 4.33. $$

The long could in principle close out the position by entering an equal and opposite short forward at the prevailing forward price $F(2/12) = 105 \cdot e^{0.04 \times 4/12} = 106.41$, locking in a profit at maturity of $106.41 - 102.02 = 4.39$, with present value $4.39 \cdot e^{-0.04 \times 4/12} = 4.33$, matching the formula.

Extension one: dividends and income

If the underlying pays a continuous dividend yield q (a common assumption for indices or foreign currency), the cash-and-carry argument changes. Holding the asset between t and T now produces income, which reduces the cost of the carry route. The fair forward price becomes

$$ F(t) = S(t) \, e^{(r - q)(T - t)}. $$

If the dividend yield exceeds the risk-free rate (rare for equities, common for some currencies), the forward price is below the spot. This is called the forward trading at a discount; the opposite case is a premium.

For discrete dividends paid at known dates between t and T, the formula uses the present value I of those dividends instead:

$$ F(t) = \bigl( S(t) - I \bigr) \, e^{r(T - t)}. $$

Either way, the principle is the same: the holder of the spot earns the income, the holder of the forward does not, so the forward price must be lower by exactly the present value of that income, otherwise arbitrage is available.

Extension two: foreign exchange forwards and covered interest parity

An FX forward is a forward to exchange one currency for another at a fixed rate on a fixed date. The "asset" is a unit of the foreign currency; it earns the foreign risk-free rate r_f; the domestic risk-free rate is r_d. The dividend-yield formula applies with q = r_f:

$$ F(t) = S(t) \, e^{(r_d - r_f)(T - t)}. $$

This is the covered interest parity relationship. If it fails, an FX arbitrage is available by borrowing in the low-rate currency, converting to the high-rate currency, lending out, and locking in the future exchange rate with a forward. In normal markets the relationship holds tightly; visible deviations from it during the 2008 and 2020 crises are studied as anomalies, usually attributed to balance-sheet costs at the dealer banks.

Extension three: commodities, storage costs and convenience yield

Commodities behave differently from financial assets in two ways. First, holding them costs money (storage, insurance), which makes the carry route more expensive. Second, holding the physical commodity confers a benefit beyond mere price exposure, called the convenience yield: an oil refiner who holds oil inventory can keep its plant running through a supply disruption, which is worth something. Aggregating, the forward price is

$$ F(t) = S(t) \, e^{(r + u - y)(T - t)}, $$

where u is the per-period storage cost (as a rate) and y the convenience yield (as a rate). The market convention is to call r + u - y the cost of carry for the commodity. When r + u < y, the forward curve slopes downward (backwardation) and is a sign that the spot market is tight. When r + u > y, the forward curve slopes upward (contango) and is a sign of plentiful supply.

Watch out The convenience yield is not directly observable. In practice it is backed out from observed forward prices and treated as the residual that makes the spot-forward formula balance. This is fine as a calibration device, but it means the formula above is a tautology unless you have an independent estimate of y. Empirically, convenience yields vary with inventory levels: high inventory, low y, contango; low inventory, high y, backwardation.

The forward price as a risk-neutral expectation

One more reading of the spot-forward parity, the most important one for everything that comes after. Take logs and differences of $F(t) = S(t)\, e^{r(T-t)}$ and rearrange:

$$ S(t) = e^{-r(T-t)} \cdot F(t). $$

The current spot is the present value of the forward price at maturity. But F(T) = S(T), so this says the spot is the discounted future spot. Naively this would require that the future spot price be predictable, which it is not. The resolution is that the equality holds only under a particular probability measure, called the risk-neutral measure Q, under which the expected spot at maturity equals the current forward:

$$ F(t) = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}} \bigl[ S(T) \mid \mathcal{F}_t \bigr]. $$

This is not the real-world expectation. Under the real-world measure, the expected spot grows at the asset's actual drift (risk-free rate plus a risk premium). The risk-neutral measure rebalances probabilities so that the discounted asset price is a martingale. Forwards, being linear in the spot, are correctly priced by taking risk-neutral expectations. Options, being non-linear, will need the same machinery extended through stochastic calculus, which is where the rest of the course is going.

For the actuary The risk-neutral measure is the financial-economics analogue of the actuary's "equivalent principle" for premium calculation: an insurance premium is set so that the expected present value of claims equals the premium, with expectation taken under the assumptions in the pricing basis (which are themselves a measure choice). Financial pricing uses a measure constructed to make the risk-free rate the only drift; actuarial pricing uses a measure that captures best-estimate mortality plus margins. In both cases, the "price equals expectation under the right measure" idea is the heart of the matter.
Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Forwards versus futures: the distinction the textbook glosses over

A future is the exchange-traded cousin of the forward. Economically they look identical: both lock in a price for trading an asset on a future date. The difference is procedural and matters more than it looks. Futures are standardised, exchange-traded, and marked to market daily: gains and losses are settled in cash every day rather than accumulated until maturity. Forwards are bilateral, over-the-counter, and settle in one cash flow at maturity.

The daily settlement of futures has a subtle pricing consequence. If interest rates and the underlying are positively correlated, gains on a long future are realised on days when interest rates are higher, so the cash gains earn more interest; losses are realised when rates are lower, so the cash funding is cheaper. The result is that the long future is worth slightly more than the long forward when correlation is positive, and the futures price exceeds the forward price by a small amount. In typical equity and FX markets the effect is tiny and ignored. For interest rate futures (eurodollar futures, SOFR futures) the effect is material and a convexity adjustment is applied, which is the topic of lecture 12.

The replicating portfolio is the entire toolkit

Every result later in the course flows from the same template you saw in this lecture: find a portfolio of traded assets that exactly reproduces the payoff of the derivative, and invoke no-arbitrage to equate prices. The forward case is special because the replicating portfolio is static: you set it up at t and leave it alone until T. For options, the replicating portfolio is dynamic: you have to rebalance it continuously as the underlying moves, and the rebalancing rules are what give rise to the delta hedge. The mathematical machinery that justifies the rebalancing (Ito's lemma, the Black-Scholes PDE) is the work of the next eight lectures, but the conceptual frame is identical to what you have just seen.

What the no-arbitrage argument is, and is not

No-arbitrage gives you the forward price, but it does not tell you the spot dynamics, the drift, or the volatility of the underlying. It is a relative pricing principle: given a market with some traded assets, it tells you the prices of other claims that can be replicated by trading in those assets. It is silent on what the prices of the original assets ought to be. This is a feature, not a bug. The forward price formula is robust to whatever you think S(T) will be: it depends only on S(t) and r. Two analysts who completely disagree about the expected return on a stock will still agree about its forward price.

Why the formula breaks (and what to do about it)

Watch out The spot-forward formula assumes four things that real markets violate to varying degrees. (1) The asset can be short sold without cost or limit. (2) Borrowing and lending happen at the same rate. (3) There is a single discount rate, not a curve. (4) There is no counterparty credit risk in the contract. For exchange-traded FX between major currencies the violations are small. For OTC forwards on emerging market currencies, single-name credit, or bespoke commodity products, the violations matter and the simple formula is replaced by a more elaborate one that includes funding spreads, credit valuation adjustment (CVA), and possibly collateral effects.

Forwards in real risk management

The classic use case in textbooks is hedging: the farmer who wants to lock in a selling price for their crop. The classic use case in practice is more varied. Treasurers at multinational corporates use FX forwards to lock in conversion rates for known future foreign cash flows, removing currency exposure from earnings. Asset-liability managers at pension funds use long-dated interest rate forwards (or forward rate agreements, or swaps, which are bundles of forwards) to match the duration of liabilities. Energy producers use commodity forwards to lock in revenue. In every case, the forward is acting as a tool for transferring price risk from one party to another, with the bank in the middle warehousing the net exposure or laying it off in the futures market.

The discrete-time analogue: a preview of the binomial model

Lectures 3 and 4 build the binomial model, where the underlying takes one of two values at each step and the replicating portfolio is constructed by solving two simultaneous equations. In that setting the spot-forward parity becomes a constraint on the model parameters: the risk-neutral probabilities of "up" and "down" must average back to the forward price after one period. The forward formula you derived here is the discrete-time skeleton of the binomial tree, and the continuous version is recovered by taking the tree limit. Keep the spot-forward parity in mind when you get there; it is the consistency check that ties the binomial model to no-arbitrage.

Test yourself

  1. State the spot-forward parity for a non-dividend-paying stock. Now state it for an asset paying continuous yield q, and explain in one sentence why the q term reduces the forward price.
  2. Show that the inequality $F(t) > S(t)\, e^{r(T-t)}$ permits arbitrage. Construct the portfolio explicitly and compute its time-$T$ value.
  3. Show that F(T) = S(T). (Hint: a forward with zero time to maturity is just a spot trade.)
  4. A six-month FX forward on EUR/USD is quoted at 1.0850. Spot is 1.0900. The continuously compounded USD risk-free rate is 4% per annum. What is the implied EUR risk-free rate? Comment on the sign.
  5. Re-derive the formula $V(t) = S(t) - K\, e^{-r(T-t)}$ by constructing a replicating portfolio for the long-forward payoff. State which assets you hold and in what quantities.
  6. Show that $V(t) = e^{-r(T-t)} (F(t) - K)$, using the spot-forward parity. Interpret in one sentence.
  7. Plot the payoff function for the holder of the short position. State its slope, its intercept, and the spot price at which it crosses zero.
  8. Why is it that no-arbitrage gives you the forward price without requiring any assumption about the distribution of S(T)?
  9. Crude oil trades at 80 USD per barrel. The continuously compounded risk-free rate is 5% per annum. Storage costs are 2% per annum. The convenience yield is estimated at 4% per annum. What is the fair price of a one-year forward? Is the market in contango or backwardation?
  10. For an interest rate future on a 90-day rate, the convexity adjustment makes the futures rate higher than the forward rate. Explain in one paragraph why the marking-to-market mechanism produces this effect.
