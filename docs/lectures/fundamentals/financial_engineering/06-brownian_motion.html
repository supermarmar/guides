Brownian motion and stochastic integrals

Shrink the binomial tree's step size to zero and something has to survive the limit: a single continuous-time object that moves independently at every instant, is nowhere smooth, and yet is exactly the process every later model in this course is built from. That object is Brownian motion. Ordinary calculus cannot integrate against it, which is why this lecture builds the calculus that can.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Standard Brownian motion, written W_t, is the continuous-time limit of a random walk whose step size shrinks to zero in a very particular way: the step length shrinks like the square root of the time step, not like the time step itself. That single scaling choice is what keeps the variance of the process finite and equal to t at time t, and it is also exactly why the resulting path is continuous everywhere but differentiable nowhere. You cannot take dW_t/dt at any point, so you cannot use ordinary calculus to make sense of an expression like ∫ H_s dW_s, a quantity you need the moment any trading strategy or any model of an asset price is allowed to react continuously to a continuously moving market.

The fix is the stochastic (Itô) integral. It is built the same way every unfamiliar integral is built: define it first for simple building-block integrands where the answer is an ordinary finite sum, prove an identity that controls the size of that sum (the Itô isometry), and then extend by a limiting argument to general integrands. The reward for doing this properly is a single worked calculation, ∫_0^T W_s\,dW_s = (W_T^2 - T)/2, whose extra −T/2 term is the entire reason stochastic calculus needs a chain rule different from the one learned in a first calculus course. That different chain rule, Itô's lemma, is the subject of the next lecture; this one builds every ingredient it requires.

Feynman test Picture a speck of pollen jostled by water molecules from every side, thousands of times a second. Ask: how far does it drift in the next instant? The honest answer is not "proportional to how long you wait", the way a car's position is proportional to how long it drives at constant speed. The honest answer is "proportional to the square root of how long you wait". Wait four times as long and the pollen typically drifts twice as far, not four times as far. That single fact, variance grows linearly in time so typical displacement grows like its square root, is the whole content of Brownian motion, and it is why every calculation in this lecture keeps producing terms like √dt where a first course in calculus would expect dt.
What's beyond the source The UP source builds the random-walk limit, states the four defining properties of W_t, proves the quadratic-variation result, and constructs the stochastic integral for integrands that are functions of the current state. It stops just short of naming several things by their standard names. This lecture adds: geometric Brownian motion as the asset-price model the whole course is heading towards; the plain-language meaning of a filtration and an adapted process, which the source explicitly declines to define formally; the Paley-Wiener-Zygmund non-differentiability theorem and the self-similarity property; the Stratonovich integral by name; and the connection from quadratic variation to realised-variance estimation in high-frequency finance. References throughout are to Shreve, Björk, Øksendal, and Karatzas and Shreve.
Step 2

Why this matters

Lecture 4 showed a binomial tree's terminal distribution converging to a lognormal as the number of steps n grows, and lecture 5 calibrated the tree parameters u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}} and d = 1/u precisely so that convergence holds. Neither lecture asked what the tree is converging to, as an object in its own right rather than as a sequence of terminal distributions. That object is Brownian motion, and until it is built and its calculus is available, "continuous time" is a phrase without a rigorous referent: you cannot write down a model of a continuously trading market, let alone price a derivative on one, without first having a precise continuous-time source of randomness and a precise meaning for integrating against it.

Every model in the rest of this course is a statement about Brownian motion. Geometric Brownian motion is the asset-price process behind Black-Scholes. Interest rate models write the short rate as a stochastic differential equation driven by one or more Brownian motions. The Feynman-Kac connection between stochastic differential equations and partial differential equations, the replicating-portfolio argument behind the Black-Scholes PDE, and the credit-risk models that arrive at the end of the course all rest on the same two facts this lecture proves: that W_t exists as a well-defined continuous-time process, and that ∫ H_s\,dW_s is a well-defined random variable with computable moments. Skipping this lecture and going straight to "the stock price follows dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t" treats that equation as a slogan rather than a statement with content.

Step 3

The mental model

Two pictures carry the whole intuition. The first shows what a Brownian path actually looks like once simulated: several independent paths starting at zero, spreading out inside an envelope that widens like √t, never like t. The second shows the fact that makes the stochastic integral necessary in the first place: however wildly a single path oscillates, the running sum of its squared increments climbs in a straight line, converging to exactly t at time t. The first picture is about the process; the second is about why ordinary calculus cannot handle it.

Picture one: simulated paths and the √t envelope

Each of the three paths below is generated the same way the source builds Brownian motion: sum a large number of independent, mean-zero, equal-variance increments, one per small time step. No two paths agree after the first instant, because each is driven by an independent sequence of coin flips writ small. What they share is a common envelope: at time t the standard deviation of W_t is √t, so roughly nineteen paths in twenty stay inside the dashed band at ±2√t. The envelope narrows near t=0 and widens thereafter, tracing out a parabola turned on its side, not a cone with straight edges.

Picture two: quadratic variation accumulates in a straight line

Take one of those paths and, at each of the same 100 time steps, add up the square of the increment since the last step. Plotted against t, that running total is almost a straight line rising to T, even though the path generating it is anything but straight. This is the property that makes the stochastic integral both necessary and tractable: necessary, because a path with this much wiggle has infinite length (infinite total variation) and so cannot be integrated against in the classical Riemann-Stieltjes sense; tractable, because the wiggle is not arbitrary; it accumulates at a completely predictable rate of one unit of quadratic variation per unit of time.

Step 4

The detail

From random walk to Brownian motion

The source builds W_t the same way the mental model above described it. Start with the standard random walk S_n = \sum_{i=1}^n R_i, where the R_i are independent, identically distributed, with P(R_i = 1) = P(R_i = -1) = 1/2, so that E(S_n) = 0 and \text{var}(S_n) = n. Standardising, Q_n = S_n/\sqrt{n} has mean zero and unit variance for every n, and the central limit theorem gives Q_n \to N(0,1) in distribution.

To move to continuous time, fix a step length \tau > 0, let the particle jump at times \tau, 2\tau, 3\tau, \dots, and after any finite time t count [t/\tau] completed jumps. Define W_t^\tau = \sqrt{\tau}\, S_{[t/\tau]}. Then E(W_t^\tau) = 0 and

$$ \text{var}(W_t^\tau) = \tau \cdot \text{var}\!\left(S_{[t/\tau]}\right) = \tau \cdot [t/\tau] \approx t. $$

The step length \sqrt{\tau} is doing exactly the same job as the CRR up-factor u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}} from lecture 5: it is the unique scaling of the step size, in terms of the time step, that keeps the variance of the accumulated process proportional to elapsed time rather than to the number of steps. Refine \tau towards zero and the central limit theorem says the distribution of W_t^\tau converges to N(0,t). What it does not immediately give you is a limiting process: a single object W_t, defined for every t simultaneously, with continuous paths. The source is candid about this: "there isn't an obvious mathematical procedure in which we can show the existence of such a limit", and the fully rigorous constructions (via wavelet expansions, via the Kolmogorov extension theorem, or via Donsker's invariance principle, which makes the random-walk convergence above rigorous as a statement about paths and not just about one t at a time) are set aside as beyond the source's scope. This lecture does the same and states existence as a theorem, due to Wiener (1923), referring the construction itself to Karatzas and Shreve, Brownian Motion and Stochastic Calculus.

Standard Brownian motion
A process (W_t)_{t \geq 0}, taking values in \mathbb{R}, satisfying: (1) W_0 = 0 almost surely; (2) for any 0 \leq t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_n, the increments W_{t_0}, W_{t_1}-W_{t_0}, \dots, W_{t_n}-W_{t_{n-1}} are independent; (3) for s,t \geq 0, W_{s+t} - W_s \sim N(0,t); (4) t \mapsto W_t is almost surely continuous. Wiener's theorem asserts that a process satisfying all four exists; the construction is nontrivial and is not given here.

Path properties: where intuition fails

Two properties do most of the damage to first-course calculus intuition, and both follow from the same source: the variance of an increment over a time step h is h, so its standard deviation is \sqrt h, not h.

The difference quotient (W_{t+h}-W_t)/h has standard deviation \sqrt h / h = 1/\sqrt h, which diverges as h \to 0. That divergence is only heuristic evidence, but it is the correct heuristic: a genuine theorem, usually attributed to Paley, Wiener and Zygmund (1933), states that with probability one, t \mapsto W_t is differentiable at no point whatsoever, despite being continuous everywhere. This is stated here without proof; see Øksendal, Stochastic Differential Equations, chapter 2, or Shreve, Stochastic Calculus for Finance II, chapter 3. A path can wiggle infinitely on every scale precisely because its increments scale like \sqrt h rather than h: zoom in on any piece of the path and it looks, statistically, like the whole path again. This is self-similarity: for any c > 0, the rescaled process \tilde W_t = W_{ct}/\sqrt c is itself a standard Brownian motion, since \tilde W_0 = 0, its increments inherit independence from W, and \tilde W_{s+t} - \tilde W_s = (W_{c(s+t)} - W_{cs})/\sqrt c \sim N(0,t).

The second, and mathematically load-bearing, property is quadratic variation. Partition [0,t] into n equal pieces of length t/n, write \Delta_j W = W_{s_{j+1}} - W_{s_j}, and consider \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} (\Delta_j W)^2. The source proves (its Proposition 3.2.1) that this sum converges in mean square to t as n \to \infty. The proof is short enough to sketch: the increments are independent with E[(\Delta_j W)^2] = t/n and, since a Gaussian's fourth moment is three times its variance squared, E[(\Delta_j W)^4] = 3(t/n)^2. A direct computation of the variance of the sum, using independence across j, gives

$$ E\!\left[\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} (\Delta_j W)^2 - t\right)^{\!2}\right] = n\left(3\frac{t^2}{n^2} - \frac{t^2}{n^2}\right) = \frac{2t^2}{n} \;\longrightarrow\; 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty. $$

So \sum (\Delta_j W)^2 \to t in mean square: the quadratic variation of Brownian motion on [0,t] equals t, a deterministic number, not a random one. This single fact reconciles two statements that look contradictory at first. A Brownian path is continuous, so on any finite interval its increments shrink as the mesh shrinks; yet the same path has infinite total variation on every interval of positive length. Suppose, for contradiction, the total variation V_t = \lim \sum_j |\Delta_j W| were finite. Then

$$ \sum_{j} (\Delta_j W)^2 \;\leq\; \Big(\max_j |\Delta_j W|\Big) \sum_j |\Delta_j W| \;\leq\; \Big(\max_j |\Delta_j W|\Big) \cdot V_t. $$

Continuity of the path forces \max_j |\Delta_j W| \to 0 as the mesh shrinks, so the right-hand side would tend to zero, forcing the quadratic variation to be zero too. It is not: it is t > 0. The only way out is that V_t was never finite in the first place. A path smooth (or even merely Lipschitz) enough to have finite length has quadratic variation zero; a Brownian path has quadratic variation t precisely because it has infinite length. This is why the Riemann-Stieltjes integral, which needs a finite-variation integrator to make sense of the limiting sum regardless of where within each subinterval the integrand is evaluated, cannot be used to define \int H_s\,dW_s. Something else is needed, and that something is built in the next two subsections.

The martingale property

Let \mathcal{F}_t denote the information generated by observing W_s for s \leq t. For s < t, writing W_t = W_s + (W_t - W_s) and using that the increment W_t - W_s is independent of \mathcal{F}_s with mean zero,

$$ E[W_t \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = W_s + E[W_t - W_s \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = W_s + 0 = W_s, $$

so W_t is a martingale: its best forecast, given everything known up to s, is simply its current value. A second martingale, used repeatedly from lecture 7 onward, is M_t = W_t^2 - t. Expand W_t^2 = W_s^2 + 2W_s(W_t - W_s) + (W_t - W_s)^2 and take conditional expectations term by term. The middle term vanishes as before; the last term has conditional expectation t - s, since W_t - W_s \sim N(0, t-s) independently of \mathcal{F}_s. So

$$ E[W_t^2 \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = W_s^2 + (t - s) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad E[W_t^2 - t \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = W_s^2 - s. $$

The -t correction is what turns W_t^2, which drifts upward on average since it cannot go negative, into something driftless. It is the same correction, in embryonic form, that reappears as the worked example's -T/2 below, and again as the second-order term in Itô's lemma next lecture.

Drift, scaling, and the model for an asset price

Shifting and rescaling standard Brownian motion gives Brownian motion with drift, X_t = \mu t + \sigma W_t, with E[X_t] = \mu t, \text{var}(X_t) = \sigma^2 t, and increments X_t - X_s \sim N(\mu(t-s), \sigma^2(t-s)) independent across disjoint intervals. This is a perfectly good model for a quantity that can go negative and whose absolute changes are what matter. An asset price is neither: it cannot go negative, and it is percentage changes, not absolute ones, that are economically comparable across different price levels. Geometric Brownian motion is the fix, defined by the stochastic differential equation

$$ dS_t = \mu S_t \, dt + \sigma S_t \, dW_t, $$

with solution S_t = S_0 \exp\!\big((\mu - \sigma^2/2)t + \sigma W_t\big). Deriving that solution from the equation needs Itô's lemma, which is lecture 7's machinery, so it is stated here rather than derived. The source itself, in its own worked example on the process e^{W_t - t/2}, computes d(e^{W_t - t/2}) = e^{W_t - t/2}\,dW_t: a driftless exponential of Brownian motion, which is exactly the \mu = \sigma^2/2 special case of geometric Brownian motion with \sigma = 1. That the drift term has to be exactly -\sigma^2/2 to cancel out and leave a pure martingale is the same -t/2 correction seen in the martingale property of W_t^2 - t above, and it is the reason the risk-neutral drift of a stock in the Black-Scholes model is r - \sigma^2/2 rather than r.

Filtrations and adapted processes, in plain language

A filtration (\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \geq 0} is the flow of information available over time: \mathcal{F}_t is everything that can be known by observing the market, or the Brownian path, up to and including time t, and information is never lost, so \mathcal{F}_s \subseteq \mathcal{F}_t for s \leq t. A process H_t is adapted to that filtration if its value at time t is determined by \mathcal{F}_t: it depends only on what has happened by time t, not on anything that happens afterwards. The source avoids stating this formally, preferring the informal phrase "driven by Brownian motion", and explicitly calls the formal definition beyond its scope. The financial content of adaptedness is nevertheless the single most important idea in this lecture: a trading strategy is a rule for choosing a position at each instant, and any strategy actually implementable in a real market must be adapted, because a trader can only act on prices already observed, never on tomorrow's price today. The stochastic integral built next encodes this constraint directly into its definition.

Constructing the Itô integral

The construction mirrors the pattern used for any unfamiliar integral: define it on simple objects where the answer is obviously correct, prove an identity controlling its size, then extend by a limit.

Step one, simple predictable integrands. Fix a partition 0 = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_n = T and a process H_t that is constant on each [t_j, t_{j+1}), taking the value H_j, where H_j is known by time t_j (it is \mathcal{F}_{t_j}-measurable, fixed before the interval it is held over begins). Define

$$ \int_0^T H_s \, dW_s \;:=\; \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} H_j \big(W_{t_{j+1}} - W_{t_j}\big). $$

This is an ordinary finite sum: nothing subtle has happened yet.

Step two, the Itô isometry. For a simple H, expand the square of the sum and use that H_j is independent of W_{t_{j+1}} - W_{t_j} (which lies strictly in the future of the information used to fix H_j), and that increments over disjoint intervals are independent. Every cross term vanishes, leaving

$$ E\!\left[\left(\int_0^T H_s\,dW_s\right)^{\!2}\right] \;=\; \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} E[H_j^2]\,(t_{j+1}-t_j) \;=\; E\!\left[\int_0^T H_s^2 \, ds\right]. $$

This is the Itô isometry: the squared L^2 size of the stochastic integral equals the ordinary time-integral of the squared integrand's expectation. It says the map from integrands to stochastic integrals preserves distances, which is exactly the property needed to extend a definition by a limiting argument.

Step three, the limit. A general adapted H with E\big[\int_0^T H_s^2\,ds\big] < \infty can be approximated by a sequence of simple processes H^{(n)} with E\big[\int_0^T (H_s^{(n)} - H_s)^2\,ds\big] \to 0. By the isometry, the corresponding sums \int H^{(n)}\,dW form a Cauchy sequence in L^2, hence converge to a limit; that limit is defined to be \int_0^T H_s\,dW_s, and the isometry guarantees the limit does not depend on which approximating sequence was chosen. The resulting integral inherits two properties directly from the simple case: E\big[\int_0^T H_s\,dW_s\big] = 0, and the running integral M_t = \int_0^t H_s\,dW_s is itself a martingale, by the same independence-of-future-increments argument used throughout this section.

What is skipped, deliberately: a precise measure-theoretic definition of which processes count as adapted (or the stronger, technically necessary notion of predictable), and a fully rigorous proof that the limit in step three exists and is unique. Both require machinery from Karatzas and Shreve or from Øksendal, chapter 3, that goes beyond what either the source or this lecture attempts.

Worked example: computing ∫ W dW from first principles

Partition [0,T] into n equal pieces, s_j = jT/n, and evaluate the integrand at the left endpoint of each subinterval, exactly as the Itô construction requires:

$$ S_n = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} W_{s_j}\big(W_{s_{j+1}} - W_{s_j}\big). $$

A naive guess, reasoning by analogy with \int x\,dx = x^2/2, is S_n \to W_T^2/2. That guess is wrong, and seeing exactly where the extra term comes from is the single most instructive calculation in stochastic calculus.

Apply the algebraic identity a(b-a) = (1/2)(b^2 - a^2) - (1/2)(b-a)^2, with a = W_{s_j}, b = W_{s_{j+1}}: S_n = (1/2) * sum_j [ (W_{s_{j+1}})^2 - (W_{s_j})^2 ] - (1/2) * sum_j (W_{s_{j+1}} - W_{s_j})^2 The first sum telescopes, since consecutive terms cancel pairwise: sum_j [ (W_{s_{j+1}})^2 - (W_{s_j})^2 ] = W_T^2 - W_0^2 = W_T^2 (since W_0 = 0) So: S_n = (1/2) W_T^2 - (1/2) * sum_j (Delta_j W)^2 As n -> infinity, the mesh shrinks to zero, and by the quadratic-variation result proved above: sum_j (Delta_j W)^2 --> T (mean square) Therefore: S_n --> (1/2) W_T^2 - (1/2) T = (W_T^2 - T) / 2 (mean square) Result: INTEGRAL from 0 to T of W_s dW_s = (W_T^2 - T) / 2

The naive answer W_T^2/2 is exactly what falls out of the telescoping first term alone. It is wrong because it silently assumes \sum_j (\Delta_j W)^2 \to 0, which is true for a differentiable (or merely finite-variation) integrator, where the sum of squared increments is O(\text{mesh}) and vanishes as the partition refines. For Brownian motion that sum does not vanish: it converges to the quadratic variation T, a strictly positive number that survives into the final answer. The -T/2 is not a technicality; it is quadratic variation itself, smuggled into what looks like a first-order calculation. Every application of Itô's lemma in lecture 7 produces a structurally identical correction term.

The evaluation point matters here in a way it never does for a Riemann integral. The source's own exercises establish what happens under the other two natural conventions:

Evaluation point in [s_j, s_{j+1}]Limit of the sumName
Left endpoint, \xi_j = s_j$(W_T^2 - T)/2$Itô integral: non-anticipating, the convention used throughout finance
Midpoint, \xi_j = (s_j+s_{j+1})/2$W_T^2 / 2$Stratonovich integral: obeys the ordinary chain rule
Right endpoint, \xi_j = s_{j+1}$(W_T^2 + T)/2$Anticipating; not a standard convention, shown for contrast

Three different, verifiable numbers for what looks like "the same" integral. That would be impossible for a classical Riemann-Stieltjes integral against a finite-variation integrator, where every choice of tag point gives the same limit. It is entirely possible here because the integrator has infinite total variation, and it is exactly why the Itô convention, fixing the evaluation point at the left endpoint once and for all, has to be stated as part of the definition rather than left as a detail that washes out in the limit.

Step 5

The grandmaster's perspective

Quadratic variation is why volatility is estimable from a single path

Classical statistics estimates a variance by averaging over many independent repetitions of the same experiment. A trading day gives you exactly one realisation of the asset price path, never a thousand. Quadratic variation is the fact that rescues volatility estimation from that limitation: as a single path is sampled ever more finely, the sum of its squared increments converges, path by path, to a deterministic number, the integrated variance over that stretch of time. That is the theoretical basis of realised variance: sum the squared five-minute, or one-minute, or tick-level log returns over a trading day, and the result is a genuinely informative estimate of how much variance actually accumulated that day, built from one path rather than from a hypothetical ensemble of repeated days. The high-frequency volatility literature that followed Andersen, Bollerslev, Diebold and Labys in the early 2000s, realised variance, realised kernels, and their many refinements, rests entirely on this one convergence result.

Itô or Stratonovich: why finance almost always picks Itô

The table above showed the same limiting sum producing three different answers depending on where within each subinterval the integrand is evaluated. Stratonovich calculus, which evaluates at the midpoint, is attractive in some areas of physics and engineering because it obeys the ordinary chain rule, with no extra second-order correction term (see Øksendal, chapter 3, or Björk, Arbitrage Theory in Continuous Time, chapter 4, for the comparison). Finance almost never uses it, for a reason that is economic rather than purely mathematical: the Itô convention, evaluating at the left endpoint, uses only information available strictly before the interval it is held over. That is not a mathematical nicety, it is a restatement of what a trading strategy actually is. A hedge ratio fixed at the start of a five-minute interval cannot be set using the interval's midpoint price, because that price has not happened yet. Choosing Itô over Stratonovich is choosing a convention that rules out insider trading by construction.

Watch out The single most common error is to treat dW_t as an ordinary infinitesimal, the way dt is treated in a first calculus course, and to discard (dW_t)^2 as automatically second order. It is not. Since E[(dW_t)^2] = dt, the increment dW_t is of order \sqrt{dt}, not order dt, and (dW_t)^2 behaves, to leading order, like the deterministic quantity dt itself (its own variance is O(dt^2), negligible beside its mean). This is exactly what the Itô multiplication table dt \cdot dt = 0, dt \cdot dW_t = 0, dW_t \cdot dW_t = dt is encoding. Drop that last rule and the -T/2 in the worked example disappears, along with every drift correction that Itô's lemma produces from lecture 7 onward.
