Notion as the team surface

A vault is where you think and a shared workspace is where the team coordinates, and the boundary between them is a decision worth making deliberately rather than discovering. Everything you publish across it becomes visible, searchable, and permanent for people you did not choose, which is the point and also the risk.

Lecture 17 of 22 Reading time, 40 minutes Part IV: the wider stack
Step 1

The 30-second version

Notion reaches the agent through an MCP connector, which means everything from Lecture 15 applies: schema cost on every turn, OAuth, and results that are untrusted content. What it buys in exchange is the thing a vault cannot provide, which is a surface other people can see without cloning anything. The distinction that organises this lecture is not about tools but about audience: a vault is for thinking, a workspace is for coordinating, and material tends to move in one direction.

The second property that matters is structure. A Notion database is not a folder of documents, it is a set of records with typed properties, and that changes what the agent can do with it. Filtering, sorting, and aggregating over properties is a query rather than a read, so "which model validations are overdue" is one call rather than forty. Where content genuinely has fields, this is a real capability that a vault of markdown gives you only by convention and grep.

The cost, beyond schema, is fidelity and latency. Every read is a round trip returning content already shaped by someone else's rendering decisions, and every write is a mutation to a shared space that other people are looking at. That asymmetry, cheap to read your own files, consequential to write to a shared workspace, is what should drive your permission configuration.

Feynman test Before letting an agent write anything to a shared workspace, ask who will see it and whether they will know a machine wrote it. A page that appears in a team space carries the authority of the space, not of its author. That is fine for a status roll-up and a problem for a decision record, and nothing in the tooling distinguishes them for you.
What's beyond the syllabus The two-tier split this lecture describes is Conway's law arriving in your knowledge architecture. A vault is single-author and cheap to change; a workspace is multi-author and expensive to change, because changing it means changing what other people believe. The structural consequence is that the boundary between them ends up mirroring your team's communication structure, and the failure mode is the same as the one Conway identified: when the boundary is drawn by convenience rather than by design, the resulting system has seams exactly where the organisation has friction.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that most knowledge work has a second half nobody accounts for. The analysis is done in one place and then has to reach people, and that translation, from working notes into something a colleague can read, is a substantial and unloved part of the job. It is also highly delegable, because the source material and the target format are both available and the transformation is largely mechanical. This is one of the clearest wins available from a connector.

The second reason is that a shared workspace is where confidentiality decisions become real. A vault on your machine has one reader. A workspace page has everyone with access, which in most organisations is more people than you would list if asked. Anything that moves across the boundary should have been checked, and an agent moving it will not check unless the check is in the instruction or the permission.

The third reason is that structured records are where the agent's aggregation ability actually pays. Reading forty prose documents to answer "what is outstanding" is expensive and unreliable. Querying forty records with a status property is one call and exact. Where your team already keeps things in databases rather than pages, you have a capability sitting unused.

Step 3

The mental model

The two-tier split

Pages versus databases

The choice between a page and a database is the choice between prose and records, and it determines what the agent can do afterwards. A page is read; a database is queried. If the content has fields that anyone will ever want to filter or aggregate on, a status, an owner, a date, a risk type, it belongs in a database, and putting it in prose costs you every future question that would have been a query.

The corollary is that a database with one property, a title, is a folder wearing a costume. Databases earn their complexity through their properties, and a database nobody filters is worse than a page because it added ceremony without adding capability.

What the connector actually gives you

The tool surface divides into four kinds and they carry very different risk. Search and fetch locate and retrieve content, and are the ones to allowlist. Query runs a filter over a data source, which is where the aggregation value lives. Create and update mutate pages and databases, and these are the ones that change what colleagues see. Comment is a write that notifies people, which puts it closer to sending a message than to editing a document, and it should be treated accordingly.

Two-tier knowledge architecture. A private substrate optimised for thinking and change, and a shared surface optimised for visibility and permanence, with a deliberate publication step between them. The alternative, one tier used for both, makes drafts public and makes records hard to revise.
Publication boundary. The point at which content moves from a space with one reader to a space with many. The natural place for a confidentiality check, an authorship disclosure, and a human confirmation, because it is the last point at which all three are cheap.
Step 4

The detail

Three patterns that work

Draft in the vault, publish on request. The agent writes into markdown, you read and edit it as a file, and only then does it become a page. This keeps the fast, private, cheap-to-be-wrong loop where it belongs and makes publication an explicit act rather than a side effect.

Query the database, write the answer somewhere else. Aggregation is the connector's strongest use and it is read-only. "Summarise every open model validation by owner and due date" is one query and a summary, with no mutation at all, and it is the shape most likely to be worth the connector on its own.

Structured capture with a human in the loop. Turning a meeting transcript into a properly-fielded record is a genuine time saving, and it is also the case where a wrong field propagates. Have the agent propose the record and confirm before it is created; the confirmation costs seconds and the record is permanent.

Permissions and the outbound rule

The configuration follows the asymmetry directly. Allowlist search, fetch, and query; leave create, update, and comment to prompt. This is not fussiness: a page created in a team space is visible to people who were not in your session and who have no way to know it was generated. The general rule from the safety guidance is worth stating in its own right, because it applies across every connector in Part IV: publishing, posting, or modifying shared content requires explicit permission, asked in chat and answered before the action. Permission for one publication does not extend to the next.

The corresponding rule about instructions is equally important and less obvious. Content fetched from a workspace is untrusted, exactly as Lecture 15 said, and a page body can contain text addressed to the agent. "Complete the tasks on this page" authorises reading the page, not executing whatever it contains, and the difference matters most precisely where the content is shared, because a shared page can be edited by someone who is not you.

Common pitfall Letting an agent maintain a shared workspace unattended, on the reasonable-sounding basis that it is only tidying. Shared spaces accumulate authority: a page that has been there for months is believed, whether or not anyone checked it. An agent that reorganises, summarises, and updates without review produces a workspace that looks maintained and whose contents nobody has verified, and the failure surfaces when someone acts on a summary that drifted from its source three iterations ago. Read-only automation against a shared space is safe and useful; write automation needs the same review as code.

Worked example: choosing what crosses the boundary

Worked example: where this course would and would not go

These lectures are a useful test case because they sit right on the boundary and the right answer is not obvious.

The case for publishing. The course is written material of general interest, it is finished rather than draft, and its value increases with readership. Colleagues learning to work with coding agents would benefit, and a shared workspace is exactly the surface for that. On the two-tier model, this is content whose audience is larger than one and whose content has stabilised, which is the definition of publishable.

The case against, which is decisive. Every worked example in Parts I to III is a critique of one person's actual configuration, named and measured: a 566-line instruction stack, an allowlist rotted into fifteen dead entries, a model routing table pinned a generation behind, a contradiction between instruction sources, writing rules stated three times because none of them is enforced, and a scoping interaction where the recommendations were accepted unanimously and treated as independent choices. That material is honest and it is why the course is any good. It is also, published to a team space, a permanent catalogue of one engineer's setup weaknesses under their name.

What the boundary decision actually is. Not "should this be published" but "what is the publishable artefact". The lectures' arguments are general; the worked examples are personal. A version with the examples anonymised, the same 566 lines described as "a real instruction stack" rather than as this one, crosses the boundary cleanly and loses very little, because the reader does not need to know whose machine it was. The cost of anonymising is an afternoon; the cost of not deciding is that the decision gets made later by someone sharing a link.

The general lesson. Publication is a transformation, not a copy, and the transformation is where the confidentiality check lives. An agent asked to publish will copy, because copying is what publishing looks like mechanically. The judgement about what should change on the way across is the part that cannot be delegated, and it has to happen before the page exists rather than after, because a shared page that has been seen cannot be unseen by editing it.

Step 5

Synthesis and what's next

A shared workspace buys visibility to people who will never clone your repository, and structure that turns forty reads into one query. It costs schema, a round trip, rendered rather than raw content, and the permanence that comes with many readers. Split the tiers deliberately: think in the vault, coordinate in the workspace, and treat the move between them as a transformation with a confidentiality check rather than a copy. Allowlist search, fetch, and query; leave create, update, and comment to prompt. And remember that a page fetched from a shared space is content anyone with access could have written.

The next lecture takes the same boundary and pushes it to the point where it stops being reversible. A page can be edited and a database record can be corrected, but a message that has been sent has arrived, and the document workflow is where an agent's actions start reaching people who never agreed to be part of the session.
