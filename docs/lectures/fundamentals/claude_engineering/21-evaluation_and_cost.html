Evaluation and cost: did the agent actually help?

Almost everyone reports feeling faster. Very few measure anything, and the measurements that exist are less flattering than the feeling. The gap between the two has a specific cause, and understanding it is the difference between adopting a tool and adopting a belief about a tool.

Lecture 21 of 22 Reading time, 45 minutes Part V: judgement and governance
Step 1

The 30-second version

The thing that feels like speed is time to first draft, and it genuinely collapses. The thing that determines whether work got done is time to a verified change in production, and it does not move nearly as much, because the steps between draft and verified, review, correction, testing, integration, were already the majority of the elapsed time and have if anything grown. Measuring the first and experiencing it as the second is the central error in how people evaluate these tools, including people who are otherwise rigorous.

The empirical picture is more uncomfortable than the marketing. DORA's research programme, which has spent over a decade measuring software delivery performance, reported in its 2024 study that increased AI adoption was associated with decreases in delivery throughput and delivery stability, even while individual developers reported improved productivity and job satisfaction. The interpretation is contested and the effect sizes are modest, but the direction should give any honest practitioner pause: the perceived and the measured came apart, and they came apart in the direction that flatters the tool.

What follows is not that these tools do not work. It is that the benefit is conditional, and the conditions are the subject of the previous twenty lectures. Fast verification, small diffs, real review, tight scope, written specifications: teams with those get compounding returns, because the agent's output enters a system that can absorb it. Teams without them get more code arriving at an unchanged review capacity, which is a bottleneck with extra steps.

Feynman test Time yourself on the whole loop, not the exciting part. From "I understand what needs to change" to "this is merged and I would defend it" is the number that matters. If that number has not moved, the tool has changed where your time goes without changing how much of it there is, and the pleasant feeling of a fast first draft is measuring the wrong interval.
What's beyond the syllabus Jevons observed in 1865 that improving the efficiency of coal use increased total coal consumption, because cheapness expanded demand faster than efficiency reduced it per unit. The software analogue is already visible: making code cheap to produce has not reduced the amount of code, it has increased it, and code is a liability that must be read, maintained, secured, and eventually deleted. A team whose output doubled while its review capacity stayed constant has not become twice as productive; it has doubled the rate at which it accumulates things nobody has properly read. The scarce resource was never typing.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that unmeasured adoption cannot be steered. If you do not know which parts of your work are improved, you cannot do more of those and less of the rest, and you end up applying the tool uniformly, which guarantees you are over-applying it somewhere. The teams getting the most from this are not the ones using it most; they are the ones who know where it pays.

The second reason is that the costs are diffuse and therefore invisible in the way that matters. Token spend appears on a bill and gets attention. Review load, comprehension debt, the code nobody wrote and therefore nobody understands, and the slow erosion of the practice that lets a team debug its own system: none of these appears anywhere until it appears everywhere. A cost that is not on a dashboard is not a cost that is not being paid.

The third reason is intellectual honesty about your own experience. The feeling of productivity is real and it is not the same as productivity, and the mechanism by which they diverge, a fast first draft followed by a slower everything-else, is exactly the mechanism that makes the feeling untrustworthy. Anyone recommending these tools to colleagues owes them the measured version rather than the felt one.

Lines of code was always a bad metric and is now actively misleading, because the thing it counts has become nearly free while the thing it was proxying for, delivered value, has not. Any measure that rewards volume will be satisfied, and satisfying it is trivial.

The cost arithmetic, done honestly

Token spend is almost never the binding cost, and saying so out loud helps. An engineer's hour costs more than a substantial number of agentic sessions, so optimising model choice to save a few pounds while spending an extra hour reviewing is a straightforward loss. The costs that dominate are review time, correction rounds, and the comprehension debt of code nobody understands, and none of them appears on the bill.

Which flips the usual instinct. When quality matters, use the better model at higher effort, because the saving from a cheaper configuration is measured in pence and the cost of an extra correction round is measured in hours. Lecture 4's routing advice holds, but it is about matching configuration to task, not about frugality.

Verification bottleneck. The point after which producing more work does not produce more finished work, because the capacity to check it is saturated. Moves from writing to reviewing under agentic workflows, and does not move again.
Comprehension debt. Code in your system that nobody has ever fully understood. Accrues silently, comes due during an incident, and is not measured by any standard metric.
Step 4

The detail

Where the return is genuinely large

Four shapes, and they share a property. High read-to-conclusion work: exploration, surveys, locating things. Mechanical transformation with a specification: migrations, ports, reformatting. Work behind a fast, trustworthy check, where the loop closes without you. The unfamiliar, where the alternative was an hour of documentation and the agent's answer is checkable against the code in front of you.

The shared property is that verification is cheap relative to production. That is the whole criterion, and it has been the criterion since Lecture 1.

Where it is negative, and worth saying plainly

Three cases where reaching for an agent makes things worse. Work whose difficulty is deciding rather than doing: a fluent answer to a question you have not framed properly forecloses the thinking you needed to do. Small changes in code you know well, where writing it is faster than specifying it and reviewing it. And anything where you need to build understanding, because delegating the work delegates the learning, and the learning was the point.

That third case deserves more weight than it gets. Comprehension is acquired by doing, and a team that has delegated all its doing has a system nobody can debug. This is not a hypothetical: it is the automation irony from Lecture 19 arriving on a longer timescale, and it is invisible until an incident.

How to actually run the comparison

You do not need a controlled trial. You need one honest fortnight. Pick a handful of representative tasks, record start and end times for the whole loop rather than the writing, note where corrections happened, and compare against your own recollection of similar work. Two properties make this worth doing: it is your workload rather than a benchmark, and it measures the interval that matters. Most people who do this discover the tool helps enormously on two of their five task types and roughly not at all on the others, which is a far more useful finding than an average.

Common pitfall Measuring adoption instead of outcome. Sessions started, prompts sent, lines accepted, and percentage of code AI-assisted are all easy to instrument and all measure how much the tool is used rather than whether the work got better. Worse, they are the metrics most likely to be requested by someone who has already decided the answer, and a team that starts optimising them will use the tool more in exactly the places it helps least, because that is where the volume is available.

Worked example: what this course cost and whether it was the right artefact

Worked example: twenty-one lectures, and a harder question than cost

What was produced. Twenty-one lecture files and a landing page, roughly six thousand lines of hand-structured HTML including inline SVG figures, grounded in about a dozen documentation fetches and one very large skill invocation, across several hours of wall-clock time in a self-paced loop.

The naive comparison, which flatters. A competent writer producing this material to this standard would need weeks. Measured as time to first draft, the ratio is not close, and this is exactly the interval this lecture warns about mistaking for the real one.

The honest comparison. The loop's verification was mechanical only, as Lectures 12 and 19 both admitted: paths, classes, tags, dashes, spellings. Nothing checked that the factual claims match the documentation they were drawn from, nothing checked pedagogical soundness, and the four checks had never been observed to fail when the verification was reported. So the correct accounting is not "weeks compressed into hours" but "weeks of drafting compressed into hours, with the verification deferred". The verification work is still outstanding. Until someone reads these lectures against their sources, the cycle time to a verified artefact is unknown, and the honest number is not the one already reported.

Cost in tokens versus cost in attention. The token spend for this course is real but is comfortably less than a day of professional time, which puts it firmly in the category this lecture describes as not binding. The binding cost is review: twenty-one lectures is more than anyone will read carefully in one sitting, and material that is not read carefully is material whose errors survive. The volume that made the course feel productive to produce is the same volume that makes it expensive to check.

The harder question, which is not about cost at all. Was a 22-lecture course the right artefact? The user asked for lectures and got lectures, and the format matches the repository's existing tracks, so the answer is defensible. But the practical content of this course, the part that would change what someone does tomorrow, is perhaps two pages: sort tools by reversibility, put the check in a hook rather than an instruction, delegate on the read-to-conclusion ratio, break the triad, watch the test fail before trusting it. A two-page checklist would be read. Twenty-one lectures will be skimmed. Cheap production is exactly what makes the long version the default, and Jevons is the reason: when writing became cheap, the equilibrium length went up rather than the total effort going down.

What that implies generally. The evaluation question is not only "did this cost less than doing it myself" but "would I have chosen this deliverable if it had been expensive". That second question is the one cheap generation makes it easy to skip, and skipping it is how organisations end up with a great deal of well-produced material that nobody uses.

Step 5

Synthesis and what's next

The interval that feels faster is time to first draft; the interval that matters is time to a verified change, and the two have come apart in a way that flatters the tool. The measured research is more cautious than the felt experience, with DORA reporting associations between AI adoption and reduced throughput and stability even as developers reported feeling more productive. Measure cycle time, change failure rate, review latency, rework, and cost per merged change; do not measure volume, sessions, or subjective speed. Token spend is rarely binding, so buy quality when quality matters. And the return is large where verification is cheap relative to production, and negative where the difficulty is deciding, where you already know the code, or where you needed to learn something.

The uncomfortable conclusion is that the tool amplifies whatever practice it lands in. A team with fast tests, small diffs, and real review gets compounding returns. A team without them gets a faster route to code nobody has read. That is the argument the whole course has been making from a different angle each time, and the final lecture makes it directly, using the one system it can examine honestly: this repository, and what building it actually demonstrated.
