Reviewing output you did not write

Every heuristic you use to read code quickly was calibrated on human authorship, where fluency and understanding travel together. They have come apart. Reviewing generated work means replacing instinct with procedure, because the instinct is now measuring the wrong thing.

Lecture 12 of 22 Reading time, 45 minutes Part III: verification
Step 1

The 30-second version

Human code review works largely on surface signals. Confused naming, inconsistent style, a comment that does not match the code, an odd structure: each is weak evidence that the author was unsure, and unsure authors write bugs. The signals are cheap to read and they correlate well enough that experienced reviewers can skim most of a diff and slow down where it feels wrong. That correlation is what has broken. Generated code is fluent everywhere, including in the parts the model did not understand, so the feel-wrong signal no longer fires where the risk is.

What replaces it is a checklist, applied deliberately, aimed at the four things a test suite cannot catch: whether the change solves the problem you actually had, whether its scope matches what you asked for, what it is missing, and whether the code is code you would be willing to own. Style, syntax, and the presence of obvious bugs can all be delegated to machines and largely should be. Your attention is the scarce input and it should go where nothing else can look.

The reviewer of last resort should also not be the author. A fresh-context reviewer, from Lecture 10, sees the diff and the criteria without the reasoning that produced them, which is a genuinely different vantage point. It comes with a documented caution worth taking seriously: a reviewer asked to find gaps will find some, whether or not they exist, and chasing all of them produces over-engineering. The instruction that fixes this is to flag only what affects correctness or the stated requirements.

Feynman test The question that carries the most information is not "is this correct?" but "would I have written this?" Correctness you can partly test. Shape you cannot. A diff that solves the problem in a way you would never have chosen is either teaching you something or hiding a misunderstanding, and the only way to find out which is to be able to explain, in your own words, why it is built that way. If you cannot, you have not reviewed it, you have skimmed it.
What's beyond the syllabus Bacchelli and Bird's 2013 study of code review at Microsoft found that the stated purpose, finding defects, was not what review mainly delivered. The larger benefits were knowledge transfer, shared ownership, and alternative-solution discovery. That finding has a sharp implication now. If review was always mostly about spreading understanding through a team, then generated code that nobody has to write also means understanding that nobody automatically acquires. The reviewer is the last remaining point at which someone is forced to comprehend the change, which makes review more load-bearing than it was, not less, at exactly the moment the volume makes it harder.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is volume. The bottleneck has moved and this is where it moved to. An engineer who could write four hundred considered lines a day can now receive four thousand, and reviewing four thousand lines properly takes longer than writing four hundred did. Any workflow that does not confront this arithmetic ends in rubber-stamping, which is the same as no review with extra ceremony.

The second reason is that the failure being caught is quiet. A wrong approach compiles, passes its tests, reads well, and is discovered months later by someone trying to extend it. There is no moment at which it announces itself, so it is caught only by a reviewer who was actively looking rather than scanning for things that look off. Scanning finds nothing because nothing looks off.

The third reason is ownership, and it is the one teams discover late. Code that arrives reviewed but not understood is code with no owner. When it breaks, the person who merged it reads it for the first time under pressure, in production, with the original context gone. Review is where that understanding is supposed to be acquired, and a review that did not produce understanding has produced a merge and nothing else.

Step 3

The mental model

Three layers, and where your attention belongs

The four questions

Does it solve the problem I actually had? Not the problem as the diff understands it. Reread your own request, then read the change, and check they are about the same thing. This catches the expensive failure and it is the question most often skipped because the diff's internal coherence is persuasive.

Is the scope what I asked for? Count the files. A two-line fix touching nine files has grown a refactor. This is not pedantry: a large diff is an unreviewable diff, and unreviewable diffs are where defects survive. Send it back to be split rather than reviewing it badly.

What is missing? The hardest question, because absence has no visual presence in a diff. The migration that was not written, the caller in another service, the documentation, the error path, the test for the case the change makes newly possible. Reading for absence is a deliberate act and it is where experience earns its keep.

Would I sign this? The ownership test. If this breaks at three in the morning, are you the person who can fix it? If not, either read it until you are, or do not merge it. There is no third option that is not a bet.

Fluency decoupling. The breakdown of the correlation between surface quality and author understanding. The reason review heuristics calibrated on human code mislead on generated code, and the reason procedure has to replace instinct.
Reviewable diff. One small enough and coherent enough that a competent reader can hold all of it at once. Size is not the criterion by itself; a thousand-line rename is reviewable and a ninety-line change across four subsystems may not be.
Step 4

The detail

Using an agent as the reviewer

A reviewing agent in a fresh context is the highest-leverage addition to this workflow, and it has two documented modes. The bundled review skill checks the current diff for bugs in a fresh subagent and returns findings. For checking a change against a plan rather than for correctness, write the prompt yourself and name three things: the work to check, the plan to check it against, and what counts as a finding.

Use a subagent to review the rate limiter diff against PLAN.md. Check that
every requirement is implemented, the listed edge cases have tests, and
nothing outside the task's scope changed. Report gaps, not style preferences.

Two calibration problems pull in opposite directions and both are documented. Ask for gaps and you get gaps, including invented ones, and following all of them yields defensive code, extra abstraction, and tests for impossible cases. But over-correct with "only report high-severity issues" and a model that follows instructions literally will investigate just as thoroughly, find the bugs, and then decline to report the ones it judges below your bar. Precision rises and measured recall falls, and the drop looks like a capability regression when it is a harness effect.

The resolution documented for this is to separate finding from filtering. Tell the reviewer its job is coverage, that it should report everything including low-confidence findings, and that it should attach a confidence and a severity to each. Then filter downstream, in a second pass or by hand. A reviewer that is both finding and filtering is doing two jobs whose optimal settings conflict.

Reading a large diff without pretending

When a diff is too large to read properly, the honest options are to split it, to sample it deliberately, or to decline. Sampling has a structure worth using: read every change to a file you did not expect to be touched, every change to an interface, every deletion, and a random selection of the rest. Deletions deserve special mention because they are the least visible and most dangerous class of change: a removed check, a dropped case, a deleted test, each reads as a small green-and-red block and each can be the whole defect.

What does not work is reading top to bottom until attention fails, which is what happens by default and which concentrates all your care on the first file alphabetically.

Common pitfall Reviewing a diff more leniently because you approved the plan it came from is the specific trap Lecture 7 named, and it is worth restating here because this is where it bites. Approving the plan creates a feeling of having already checked, and the review inherits that feeling without inheriting any actual verification, since a plan is a statement of intent and a diff is a statement of fact. The two need separate attention. If anything the plan should make you more exacting, because you now have something specific to check the change against rather than a general sense of what it ought to do.

Worked example: what was and was not reviewed in this course

Worked example: a thorough mechanical review and an absent semantic one

These lectures went through a verification pass that was, on its own terms, reasonably complete. Every relative path was resolved against the filesystem. Every CSS class was cross-referenced against the stylesheet. Section and div tags were counted for balance. The files were grepped for em dashes and for a list of American spellings. Git status was checked to confirm exactly the expected files had changed. All of that was reported, and all of it was true.

What that pass could not see. Every one of those checks lives in the top band of the diagram above. Not one of them looks at whether a sentence is correct. The lectures make specific factual claims: that output tokens cost five times input tokens, that the effort default is high, that a skill's body persists for the session, that exit code 2 blocks a hook and feeds stderr back as the reason. Each was fetched from documentation before being written. None was checked afterwards against what was fetched.

Why that gap is the dangerous shape. The verification report was detailed and accurate, and detail is persuasive. A reader, including the person who wrote it, comes away with a sense that the lectures were checked. They were checked mechanically and completely, and semantically not at all, and the report did not distinguish those because the mechanical checks were the ones that existed. This is fluency decoupling operating one level up: a thorough-looking process concealing an unexamined layer.

What the middle band would have been. A fresh-context subagent given one lecture and the source documentation, asked to list every factual claim in the lecture and mark each as supported, contradicted, or not addressed by the source. That is a bounded task with a high read-to-conclusion ratio, exactly the delegation shape from Lecture 10, and it would take one pass per lecture. It was not run.

What only a human can do here. Even a claim-checking agent would not answer the questions that matter most: is this the right syllabus, is the emphasis in the right places, is a lecture that spends a third of its length on a worked example well-proportioned, and does the course as a whole teach what someone actually needs. Those are intent-band questions and they belong to the person who asked for the course. The general lesson generalises past this repository: a verification report tells you what was checked, and its most important content is what it does not mention. Read your own reports for absences with the same suspicion you read a diff.

Step 5

Synthesis and what's next

Surface fluency has stopped predicting author understanding, so the heuristics that made review fast now point at the wrong places, and procedure has to replace instinct. Push the mechanical layer onto machines and the local-logic layer onto a fresh-context reviewer, and spend your own attention on intent, where nothing else has the context to look. Ask the four questions: does it solve the problem I had, is the scope what I asked for, what is missing, and would I sign it. When using a reviewing agent, separate finding from filtering, because a reviewer that does both is running two jobs with conflicting settings. And read for deletions, which hide best.

The wider point is that review is now the place where understanding enters a team, since nobody is acquiring it by writing. That makes it the highest-leverage half hour in the workflow and the one most under pressure from volume. The next lecture takes the case where review has already failed and something is broken: how to debug systematically when the agent's strongest instinct is to pattern-match a fix onto a symptom it has not yet explained.
