Course companion

Language engineering

Eighteen lectures on building working systems out of language models. The machine learning track derives what a transformer is and why scale changes its behaviour; this track picks the story up at the point where you have a trained model in front of you and a problem to solve with it. Tokenisers, embeddings, the inference-time economics of the forward pass, five different ways to classify a document, topic modelling, the prompt-technique catalogue, chains and agents, retrieval-augmented generation and how to tell whether yours is any good, multimodal models, and finally training and fine-tuning your own: contrastive embedding models, SetFit, LoRA, QLoRA and direct preference optimisation. Assumes the deep learning sub-module of the machine learning track, in particular attention and transformers, and Python at the level of the data analysis track.

The map

Two ideas organise everything below, and both are worth holding before the first lecture. The first is that language models come in two flavours defined by what they are asked to produce rather than by architecture: representation models turn text into vectors and are the engine behind classification, clustering and search, while generative models turn text into more text and are the engine behind prompting, chaining and agents. Almost every real system uses both. The second is that the three ways of getting a model to do your task, prompting it, retrieving context for it, and training it, sit on a rising curve of cost and control. Start at the cheap end and only climb when the cheap end demonstrably fails.

How to use this

Read Part I in order, because everything later depends on it. After that the parts are largely independent: if you have come for retrieval-augmented generation you can go straight to lectures 11 and 12, and if you have come to fine-tune a model you can go straight to Part V. Each lecture names its prerequisites in the connections block at the end. Status badges show what is built (built) or planned.

Code appears throughout, as it does in the software and data engineering tracks, but it is illustrative rather than runnable end to end. Library interfaces in this field move faster than any written course can track, so the prose carries the durable idea and the snippet shows its shape. Where an interface used by the source material has since been deprecated, the lecture says so rather than reproducing dead code.

Where this sits against the neighbouring tracks

Three tracks in this curriculum touch language models, and keeping their jobs separate is what stops all three from bloating. The large language models sub-module of the machine learning track answers why it works: scaling laws, emergent capability, the theory of in-context learning, and the governance discipline a regulated user must apply. This track answers how to build with it. The Claude engineering track answers a third and narrower question, how to get useful software written by an agent you did not build, and lecture 10 here is the natural bridge into it.

Two more tracks are load-bearing prerequisites rather than neighbours. The deep learning sub-module of machine learning supplies attention, multi-head attention, positional encoding and causal masking, all of which this track assumes rather than re-derives; its word embeddings lecture supplies word2vec and GloVe, which lecture 3 builds on. The unsupervised learning sub-module supplies UMAP and HDBSCAN, which lecture 6 uses without re-deriving.

Source material

This track adapts material from Hands-On Large Language Models: Language Understanding and Generation by Jay Alammar and Maarten Grootendorst (O'Reilly Media, 2024, https://www.llm-book.com/), specifically its twelve chapters on tokenisation, embeddings, text classification, clustering, prompt engineering, agents, semantic search, multimodality, and fine-tuning. The prose, structure, worked examples and figures throughout are written fresh for this course and do not reproduce the source text or its illustrations. Readers wanting the original explanations, the authors' own diagrams, and the runnable Google Colab notebooks that accompany every chapter should consult the book directly and its companion repository, which is also the right place to look for code that still runs against current library versions.
