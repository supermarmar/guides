Lectures / Language engineering

Multimodal models

Part IV is one lecture and one idea: the transformer never cared that its inputs were words. Cut an image into squares, project each square to a vector, and the same architecture applies. What follows from that are three things worth knowing — why image resolution is quadratically expensive, why the batch size in contrastive training is the training signal rather than a throughput setting, and why a "shared" image-and-text space is less shared than the phrase suggests.

Step 1

The 30-second version

A vision transformer, from Dosovitskiy and colleagues in 2020, cuts an image into a grid of fixed squares and treats each as a token. A 224-pixel image at 16-pixel patches gives 196 patches plus a classification token, so 197 tokens, and everything downstream is the architecture from lecture 4 unchanged.

The first consequence is a hard cost curve. Attention is quadratic in the token count and the token count is quadratic in the resolution, so quadrupling the pixels multiplies the attention pairs by sixteen: 224 pixels gives 38,809 pairs and 448 gives 616,225. Vision models are fussy about input size for a reason text models are not.

CLIP (Radford and colleagues, 2021) puts images and captions in one space by contrastive training: a batch of N pairs forms an N by N similarity matrix and the correct pairing is the diagonal. So every example has one positive and N−1 negatives, and the batch size is the difficulty. CLIP used 32,768, where a random guess is right 0.0031 per cent of the time. This is one of the few settings where a larger batch produces a better model rather than merely a faster run.

And the space is less shared than advertised. Liang and colleagues measured a persistent modality gap: image and text embeddings occupy separate cones after training, so a cosine of 0.31 can be an excellent cross-modal match and a poor within-modal one, and no single threshold serves both. BLIP-2 then sidesteps retraining entirely, bridging a frozen vision encoder to a frozen language model by training a small adapter: 2.3 per cent of the parameters.

Feynman test Chop the picture into little squares, turn each square of pixels into a list of numbers, and feed them in exactly as if they were words. Then show the model millions of pictures with their captions and make it match each picture to its own caption out of thirty thousand.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that it shows what the transformer actually requires, which is a sequence of vectors and nothing else. Text needed a tokeniser because language arrives as characters; images need only a projection because pixels are already numbers. Seeing that the architecture is indifferent to the modality is the point of the lecture, and audio, video and time series follow the same route.

The second reason is that contrastive training, met here in its image-and-text form, is the same objective that trains a good retrieval embedding. Everything about batch size, negatives and the shape of the learned space carries directly into Part V, where the embedding models of lectures 11 and 12 stop being bought and start being trained.

The third reason is practical and easy to get wrong. A CLIP score is meaningful in comparison and misleading in isolation, because of the modality gap. Systems that threshold a raw cross-modal cosine behave strangely, and the fix is to rank rather than to threshold.

And a fourth reason about cost. The BLIP-2 arrangement, a frozen encoder and a frozen language model joined by a trained bridge, is the general pattern for adding a modality without paying for a new foundation model. It is the same instinct as the parameter-efficient fine-tuning in lecture 17: leave the large things alone and train the join.

Step 3

The mental model

One projection that makes an image a sequence, one objective that puts two sequences in one space, and one bridge that avoids paying for either again.

THE PATCH SIZE IS THE DIAL smaller patches see finer detail and cost quadratically more. 14-pixel patches at 224 give 257 tokens against 197 for 16-pixel, which is 70 per cent more attention for a modest gain in acuity. AND WHAT A PATCH EMBEDDING IS a 16 x 16 patch in three colour channels is 768 raw numbers, and a single linear layer maps them to a 768-dimensional vector: 590,592 parameters for the whole "tokeniser". CONTRAST WITH LECTURE 2. Text tokenisation is a lookup into a learned vocabulary, discrete, with its own training and its own out-of-vocabulary problem. Image "tokenisation" is a linear projection of a pixel block, continuous, with no vocabulary and nothing that can fail to be represented. The same word is doing two very different jobs.

CLIP: one space, learned by elimination

CLIP trains two encoders, one for images and one for text, so that a picture and its caption land near each other. The training signal is entirely comparative. Take a batch of N image-caption pairs, encode both sides, and compute the N by N matrix of cosine similarities. The correct pairing is the diagonal, so the loss is cross-entropy over each row, asking which caption belongs to this image, and over each column, asking which image belongs to this caption.

The task is hard by construction, and that difficulty is what forces the representation to be discriminative rather than merely approximate. THE FORMAL VERSION the InfoNCE objective (van den Oord and colleagues, 2018) bounds the mutual information between the two views from below by log N. So doubling the batch adds log 2 = 0.693 nats going from 256 to 32,768 adds 4.85 nats WHICH MAKES CONTRASTIVE TRAINING UNUSUAL in most training, batch size is a throughput and stability decision that leaves the objective alone. Here it IS the objective: a bigger batch is a harder task and a tighter bound, so it produces a better model rather than the same model sooner. that is also why contrastive training is memory-hungry in a specific way, and why techniques for holding very large effective batches matter more here than elsewhere. AND WHAT IT BUYS: ZERO-SHOT CLASSIFICATION once images and text share a space, classification needs no classifier. Embed the candidate labels as sentences, embed the image, and take the nearest. The label set can change at run time, which no trained classification head can do.

The modality gap

The phrase "shared embedding space" invites a picture in which images and captions are interleaved and a caption's nearest neighbour is its picture. That is not what happens. Liang and colleagues (2022) showed that image embeddings and text embeddings occupy separate narrow regions of the sphere, and that the gap persists through training rather than closing.

Two practical consequences follow, and both catch people out. Cross-modal cosines are systematically lower than within-modal ones, so a score that would be poor between two captions can be excellent between an image and a caption. And the nearest neighbour of an image, among a mixed set, is almost always another image. Which means CLIP scores must be compared, never thresholded: rank the candidate captions for this image against each other, and do not ask whether 0.31 is a good number, because in isolation it has no meaning.

BLIP-2: keep both models, train the join

Training a large vision-language model from scratch is expensive and largely redundant, because good vision encoders and good language models already exist. BLIP-2 (Li and colleagues, 2023) freezes both and trains a small module, the Q-Former, to convert what the vision encoder produces into something the language model can read.

So the language model spends 32 tokens of its context window on the image rather than 257, an eight-fold saving on a budget lecture 11 showed is tight. WHY IT WORKS AT ALL both frozen models are already excellent at their own half. The bridge is not learning to see or to write; it is learning a TRANSLATION between two existing representations, which is a far smaller problem than either. AND THE SAME INSTINCT RECURS this is the pattern of lecture 17's parameter-efficient fine-tuning: leave the large thing frozen and train a small thing next to it. Once you have noticed it here, it is visible everywhere in the field. THE LIMITATION WORTH STATING a frozen language model cannot learn anything new about vision, so everything the system knows about images must survive translation through 32 vectors. Tasks needing fine spatial detail, counting, or reading small text in an image are exactly where this shows.
Step 5

Synthesis and what's next

A transformer needs a sequence of vectors and does not care where they came from. Cutting an image into a grid of patches and linearly projecting each one supplies exactly that, so a 224-pixel image at 16-pixel patches becomes 197 tokens and the architecture from lecture 4 runs unchanged. The cost is quadratic twice over: tokens grow with the square of the resolution and attention with the square of the tokens, so four times the pixels is sixteen times the attention.

CLIP puts both modalities in one space by making each example compete against the rest of its batch, which is why the batch size is the training signal rather than a throughput setting, and why 32,768 was not excessive. The resulting space supports zero-shot classification with a label set that can change at run time, and it carries a persistent modality gap that means scores are comparable within a query and meaningless as absolute thresholds. BLIP-2 declines to retrain any of it, joining a frozen encoder to a frozen language model through a bridge that is 2.3 per cent of the parameters and compresses 257 patch tokens into 32.

What Part IV is really setting up The contrastive objective in this lecture is not a vision technique that happens to involve text. It is the general recipe for learning a space in which similarity means something, and the retrieval embeddings that lectures 11 and 12 spent their length measuring are trained the same way, with a caption replaced by a related passage. Everything here about negatives, batch size and the shape of the learned space transfers directly, which is why Part V opens with it rather than with fine-tuning.

Part V turns from using models to training them. Lecture 14 asks the question that lecture 12 left implicit: if a cross-encoder is so much more accurate, why can it not simply be the search engine? The answer is architectural rather than a matter of scale, and it leads to the three loss functions that produce a usable bi-encoder. Lectures 15 and 16 then train and adapt embedding and representation models, including the case where no labels exist at all, and lectures 17 and 18 close the course with supervised fine-tuning under quantisation, preference tuning, and the evaluation of generative systems.
