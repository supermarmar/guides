Preference tuning and evaluation

Supervised fine-tuning teaches a model to imitate one correct answer, which only works where one exists. Most of what we want from a generative model, that an explanation be full enough, that a refusal be gracious, that a summary keep the right things, is a matter of better and worse. The third training stage learns from comparisons instead. And then the course has to close on the question it has been deferring since lecture 5: how do you know whether any of this worked?

Step 1

The 30-second version

Preference tuning, also called alignment or RLHF, is the third stage. Its data is not questions with correct answers but prompts with a chosen and a rejected generation, and often both are acceptable and one is merely better.

The established route trains a reward model: take the instruction-tuned model, replace its language modelling head with one that emits a single score, and train it so the chosen generation always outscores the rejected one. Then optimise the language model against that score, classically with proximal policy optimisation, which constrains how far the model may drift. Llama 2 used two reward models, one for helpfulness and one for safety.

Direct preference optimisation removes the reward model entirely. Keep a frozen copy of the model as a reference, and for each pair measure how the trainable model's log-probabilities of the chosen and rejected generations have shifted relative to it. Optimise that shift directly. Two models instead of three, no reinforcement learning loop, and reported as more stable and more accurate.

On evaluation there is no single answer and it is important to say so. Word-level metrics such as perplexity measure confidence, not correctness. Public benchmarks such as MMLU's 57 tasks or HumanEval's 164 programming problems measure breadth and invite overfitting. Leaderboards aggregate benchmarks and inherit the problem. LLM-as-a-judge scales to open-ended output and improves as models do. Human evaluation remains the standard, and the Chatbot Arena's 800,000-plus blind pairwise votes turn it into an Elo rating. Every one is a proxy, and Goodhart's law applies to all of them.

Feynman test You cannot teach good writing by supplying the one correct essay, because there isn't one. What you can do is put two drafts side by side and say which is better, thousands of times, until the pattern in your preferences becomes learnable. Then, having trained on it, you face the harder problem: no exam measures good writing, so you use several partial measures and remember that whichever one you optimise will stop telling you the truth.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that preference tuning is what separates a model people will use from one they will not. An instruction-tuned model answers; a preference-tuned one answers at the right length, in the right register, admitting uncertainty where it should. That difference is almost entirely stage three, and it is not obtainable from more supervised fine-tuning, because the training signal it needs does not exist in the form supervised fine-tuning consumes.

The second reason is that comparison data is far cheaper to produce than gold answers, and often already exists. Asking an annotator to write the ideal response to a question is slow, expensive and inconsistent between annotators. Asking which of two responses is better is fast and much more reliable. Any product with a thumbs-up and thumbs-down is already collecting preference data.

The third reason is that DPO changed who can do this. Reinforcement-learning-based alignment required training and running a reward model alongside the policy, with the tuning difficulty that reinforcement learning brings. DPO is an ordinary supervised training loop over pairs, and it runs on the same QLoRA setup lecture 17 built.

And a fourth reason, which is why the evaluation half is not an appendix. Every number quoted in this track, the 0.85 F1, the 0.8094 Pearson, the 85 per cent recall at six, is a measurement made under assumptions, and at least one of them turned out to be contaminated. A course that taught the techniques and not their measurement would be teaching people to build things they cannot check.

Step 4

The detail

Why stage three exists

Supervised fine-tuning trains on question-and-answer pairs, so it learns to imitate the answer it was shown. That is exactly right where a correct answer exists and useless where the difference is one of degree. Asked "what is an LLM?", both "it is a large language model" and a paragraph explaining what one does are correct. One is better. Supervised fine-tuning has no way to express that, because its loss compares the model's output against a single target.

Preference data expresses it directly: a prompt, a chosen generation and a rejected one. The nuance in the framing matters and is easy to lose. This is not a good-versus-bad dataset. Both generations are frequently acceptable, and the label records only that one was preferred. That is why the data is cheap: judging which of two is better is a much easier and more consistent task for an annotator than writing the ideal answer.

Reward models

A reward model converts a preference dataset into a scoring function. The construction is neat: take a copy of the instruction-tuned model and swap its language modelling head, which projects onto the vocabulary, for a head that emits a single number. The body is unchanged, so it already understands the text; only what it produces changes.

REWARD MODEL: INPUTS, OUTPUT, OBJECTIVE IN a prompt and one generation OUT a single scalar: how good is this response to this prompt TRAINING, per preference example 1. score the chosen generation 2. score the rejected generation 3. push the parameters so that score(chosen) > score(rejected) note what the objective does NOT require: it never says what the score should BE. Only the ordering is supervised, which is exactly the information the annotators gave. THEN, PREFERENCE TUNING PROPER, IN THREE STAGES 1. collect preference data 2. train the reward model on it 3. fine-tune the LLM using the reward model as the judge stage 3 is classically PROXIMAL POLICY OPTIMIZATION, a reinforcement learning method that raises the expected reward while penalising the model for moving too far from where it started. That constraint is essential: without it, the model finds whatever degenerate output the reward model happens to score highly and produces only that. It is the training-time form of Goodhart's law, and it arrives within hours rather than years. PPO was used to train the original ChatGPT. THE IDEA EXTENDS Llama 2 trained TWO reward models, one scoring helpfulness and one scoring safety, so the two objectives could be weighed against each other explicitly rather than averaged into one number by an annotator. Where two goals genuinely conflict, and helpfulness and safety routinely do, separating them is more honest than a single scalar. THE COST three models to hold and coordinate: the policy being trained, the reward model, and a reference copy for the drift constraint. Plus reinforcement learning's own sensitivity to hyperparameters.

Direct preference optimisation

Direct preference optimisation asks whether the reward model is necessary. Its answer is that the language model already contains the information: the probability it assigns to a generation is a statement about how much it favours it, so preferences can be expressed as changes in those probabilities rather than as an external score.

DPO, MECHANICALLY keep two copies of the instruction-tuned model REFERENCE frozen. Where you started. POLICY trainable. for each preference example, compute under BOTH models log p(chosen | prompt) log p(rejected | prompt) accumulated over the tokens of each generation. then optimise the POLICY so that, relative to the reference, the chosen generation becomes MORE likely the rejected generation becomes LESS likely WHAT THE REFERENCE MODEL IS DOING two jobs at once, which is the elegant part. It supplies the baseline that makes "more likely" and "less likely" meaningful, since raw probabilities depend on length and vocabulary. And it constrains drift. A policy that moves far from the reference is penalised by the objective itself. This is the same job PPO needed an explicit penalty term for, obtained here for free. So the reference model IS the implicit reward. There is no separate scoring function anywhere in the procedure. WHAT IT COSTS AND SAVES models in play PPO 3 DPO 2 training loop RL ordinary supervised reported by authors more stable, more accurate AND ONE HONEST CAVEAT DPO's simplicity is real, and "more stable and more accurate" is the authors' finding on their setups rather than a universal law. Reward-model-based methods remain in active use at frontier labs, partly because an explicit reward model can be reused, inspected and audited, which a procedure with no reward function cannot.

In practice DPO slots into the QLoRA setup from lecture 17 with almost no change: the same quantisation, the same adapter configuration, the same trainer shape. Two differences are worth naming.

WHAT CHANGES FROM SFT TO DPO THE DATA SFT {"text": "<|user|>...<|assistant|>..."} DPO {"prompt": ..., "chosen": ..., "rejected": ...} the worked example filters a public preference set from ~13,000 examples down to ~6,000 (about 46%) by dropping ties, requiring a chosen-score of at least 8, and excluding anything present in a benchmark's training split. That last filter is the contamination discipline of this lecture applied in advance. THE LEARNING RATE SFT 2e-4 DPO 1e-5 -> 20x smaller preference tuning adjusts an already-good model, so large steps destroy what stage two built. Add warmup_ratio=0.1 so the first 10% of steps ramp the rate up from zero rather than starting at full size. everything else, the 4-bit base, the LoRA adapters, the paged optimiser, gradient accumulation and checkpointing, is unchanged from lecture 17.

Evaluating any of it: five imperfect instruments

There is no golden standard here and pretending otherwise is the main failure mode. A generative model is used across many tasks, and competence at one implies little about another: solving mathematics does not predict writing code. What follows is five instruments, each measuring something real and none measuring enough.

Word-level metrics compare generated tokens against a reference. Perplexity is the one worth understanding: it measures how surprised the model is by well-written text, so a model that assigns "target" a high probability after "when a measure becomes a" is less perplexed and scores better. BLEU, ROUGE and BERTScore compare against reference outputs with varying tolerance for paraphrase. All of them measure the model's confidence or its overlap with a reference, and none measures whether the output is true, coherent or useful. A confidently fluent wrong answer scores well.

PUBLIC BENCHMARKS MMLU 57 tasks: classification, question answering, sentiment, and more. Breadth of general knowledge and understanding. GLUE language understanding tasks across a range of difficulty. Also the source of the MNLI and STSB data used throughout Part V. TruthfulQA whether generated statements are true, and specifically resistant to common misconceptions. GSM8k grade-school mathematics word problems, written by humans. Multi-step reasoning. HellaSwag common-sense inference, four-way multiple choice. Random guessing scores 25%. HumanEval 164 programming problems, scored by whether the generated code passes tests. THREE PROBLEMS, IN ORDER OF SEVERITY CONTAMINATION. These are public, so they end up in training corpora, and a model that memorised the test set scores well without being able to do the task. Lecture 5 hit this with a hosted classifier on a 2005 dataset; lecture 15 hit it with an embedding model that had already seen MNLI. Assume contamination unless the model's training data is documented, which for most models it is not. BREADTH IS NOT YOUR TASK. A high MMLU tells you the model is broadly capable and nothing about whether it can extract sums assured from your policy documents. COST. Some benchmarks take hours of GPU time, which makes them useless for the tight iteration loop you actually want while developing. AND CHOOSE BY USE CASE for a coding assistant, HumanEval is informative and GSM8k is not. Running everything because it is available is how you end up optimising something irrelevant.

Leaderboards aggregate benchmarks, and the Open LLM Leaderboard combines six of them. They are useful for orientation and they inherit every problem above, plus one of their own: a public leaderboard built on public benchmarks creates a direct incentive to overfit to it.

LLM-as-a-judge addresses what benchmarks cannot, which is quality on open-ended output. Two models answer the same question and a third declares which answer is better, a pairwise comparison. This scales to questions with no single correct answer, and it has the unusual property of improving as the field does, since a better judge model is available every few months. Its weakness is that the judge has biases of its own, notably toward longer and more confident-sounding answers, so a judged comparison should be spot-checked against human judgement rather than trusted outright.

Human evaluation is the standard everything else approximates. The Chatbot Arena implements it at scale: a user asks a question, receives answers from two anonymous models, votes for the better one, and only then learns which was which. Over 800,000 votes turn into an Elo rating, the chess system, so beating a highly rated model moves you further than beating a weak one. Blind pairwise voting removes brand effects, and crowdsourcing removes the individual annotator's idiosyncrasy. What it cannot remove is that the aggregate preference of a large crowd is not the preference of your users on your task.

Goodhart's law, which is the closing point of the whole course "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Optimise for grammatical correctness and a model that only ever emits "This is a sentence." scores perfectly. That example is silly and the mechanism is not: it is the same mechanism as PPO's drift constraint, as benchmark overfitting, and as a leaderboard shaping what gets built. Every metric in this lecture is a proxy, and the moment a team is judged on one, it stops measuring what it measured. The defence is to hold several proxies at once, to keep an evaluation set you built yourself and never published, and to look at actual outputs regularly. You are the best evaluator of whether the thing works for your use case, and no benchmark relieves you of the job.
Step 5

Synthesis, and the end of the course

Preference tuning is the third stage, and it exists because supervised fine-tuning can only imitate a single correct answer while most of what we want is a matter of degree. Its data is a prompt with a chosen and a rejected generation, cheap to collect because comparing is easier than authoring. The classical route trains a reward model, an instruction-tuned model with its vocabulary head replaced by a scalar one, supervised only on the ordering, and then optimises the language model against it with PPO, whose drift penalty stops the model collapsing onto whatever the reward model overrates. Direct preference optimisation removes the reward model by keeping a frozen reference copy and optimising the shift in log-probabilities of the chosen and rejected generations relative to it, which supplies both the baseline and the drift constraint at once: two models rather than three, a supervised loop rather than a reinforcement learning one, and a twenty-times smaller learning rate than stage two because the model being adjusted is already good.

Evaluation has no golden standard. Word-level metrics measure confidence and overlap, benchmarks measure breadth and leak into training corpora, leaderboards inherit both problems and add an incentive, LLM-as-a-judge scales to open-ended output at the cost of the judge's own biases, and human evaluation measures the right thing slowly and expensively. Every one is a proxy, and Goodhart's law converts any of them into a bad measure the moment it becomes the target.

Eighteen lectures, three ideas Everything is a sequence of vectors. Tokenise text, project image patches, and the same architecture runs; embeddings are the common currency, and lecture 3's three kinds recur in every part of the track.

Freeze the large thing and train something small beside it. A logistic regression on frozen embeddings (lecture 5), a bridge that is 2.3 per cent of the parameters (lecture 13), one unfrozen encoder block (lecture 16), LoRA at 0.130 per cent (lecture 17). Large pretrained models are already nearly right.

Climb the cost-and-control ladder only when the rung below fails. Prompt it, then give it retrieved context, then train it. The landing page's dashed axis was the argument of the whole course, and most problems that look like they need training stop at the middle rung.

What this track has deliberately not covered is worth naming. It has not covered serving at scale, which is its own discipline. It has not covered the governance obligations a regulated user carries, which the machine learning track's responsible use lecture treats properly and which apply to everything built here. And it has not fixed the field in place: the library interfaces in these lectures will move, the model names will date, and the arithmetic will not. Where a technique here rests on a measured quantity, a token count, a parameter ratio, a memory figure, that is the part to carry forward.
