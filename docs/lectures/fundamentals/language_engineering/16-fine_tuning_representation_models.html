Lectures / Language engineering

Fine-tuning representation models

Lecture 5 kept every model frozen and got 0.85 F1 by training a logistic regression on its output. This lecture unfreezes, and the interesting result is that full fine-tuning also gets 0.85, while a method using 32 labelled examples instead of 8,530 gets 0.8364. Along the way: what freezing layers actually costs, how to adapt a model to your vocabulary with no labels, and the sub-token alignment problem that quietly breaks most first attempts at entity recognition.

Lecture 16 of 18 Reading time, 40 minutes Part V: training your own
Step 1

The 30-second version

Attach a classification head to a pretrained BERT and train both together, and the Rotten Tomatoes sentiment task from lecture 5 goes from 0.80 with a frozen off-the-shelf model to 0.85 F1, in a few minutes on one GPU.

Freezing layers trades quality for compute, and the curve is sharply non-linear. Freeze everything except the classification head and the score collapses to 0.63, a 25.9 per cent fall. Unfreeze just the top encoder block and it recovers to 0.80. About five of the twelve blocks gets close to training all of them, so the useful reading is that the last few blocks do nearly all the task-specific adapting.

SetFit handles the case where you have almost no labels. Take 16 examples per class, generate contrastive pairs by combining them within and across classes, fine-tune a sentence embedding model on those pairs, then fit an ordinary classifier on the resulting embeddings. From 32 labelled documents it manufactures 1,280 training pairs and reaches 0.8364, which is 98.4 per cent of the full fine-tune from 0.375 per cent of the labels.

Continued pretraining is the no-labels option: keep training the pretrained model on your own corpus with masked language modelling before fine-tuning it on anything. And named-entity recognition classifies every token rather than every document, which forces the sub-token alignment problem into the open: ten words become eleven sub-tokens plus two special tokens, and every label has to be placed correctly or the model learns nonsense.

Feynman test A specialist who has read widely can be adapted to your job in three ways. Let them read your files and update everything they think, which works best. Or forbid them from changing their mind about anything and only let them adjust the final judgement, which is fast and much worse. Or, when you can only show them thirty examples, have them study which pairs of examples are alike and which differ, which turns thirty examples into a thousand comparisons.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the freezing curve tells you where to spend a limited GPU budget. It is not a smooth trade where half the layers gives half the quality. Almost all the task-specific adaptation happens in the top few blocks, so freezing the bottom two-thirds costs a few points and saves most of the backward pass. Knowing the shape of that curve is the difference between an informed compromise and an arbitrary one.

The second reason is that SetFit changes what counts as a feasible project. The usual answer to "we have no labelled data" is a labelling exercise costing weeks. Sixteen examples per class is an afternoon, and it gets within two per cent of the full-data result. Any classification project should try that before commissioning annotation, in the same way lecture 5 argued for measuring the zero-shot baseline first.

The third reason is that continued pretraining is the cheapest domain adaptation available and is routinely skipped. You already have the documents. Running masked language modelling over them costs one GPU session, requires no annotation, and produces a base model that knows your vocabulary before any task-specific training begins.

And a fourth reason about correctness. Token classification is where the tokenisation of lecture 2 stops being background and becomes something you must handle explicitly. A label list aligned to words does not align to sub-tokens, and the resulting off-by-one errors do not raise exceptions. They just train the model on the wrong targets.

The detail

Full fine-tuning, and what it beats

The setup is the Rotten Tomatoes dataset from lecture 5, 8,530 training reviews, with bert-base-cased and a two-class head. The head is a small feedforward network on top of the encoder, and the difference from lecture 5 is only that gradients now flow through both.

Freezing: where the adaptation actually happens

A pretrained BERT is twelve encoder blocks over an embedding layer, and any subset can be held fixed. Freezing removes those parameters from the backward pass, which is where most of the training cost sits.

for name, param in model.named_parameters():
    param.requires_grad = name.startswith("classifier")   # head only -> F1 0.63

# or, freeze everything before encoder block 11:
for index, (name, param) in enumerate(model.named_parameters()):
    if index < 165:
        param.requires_grad = False                        # top block  -> F1 0.80
WHAT FREEZING COSTS trainable F1 change vs full ----------------------------------------------------- everything 0.85 -- top block + head 0.80 -5.9% head only 0.63 -25.9% THE NON-LINEARITY going from "head only" to "top block + head" 0.80 - 0.63 = 0.17 F1, for ONE block of gradients going from "top block + head" to everything 0.85 - 0.80 = 0.05 F1, for ELEVEN more blocks one block recovers 0.17/0.22 = 77% of what full freezing gave away. WHY with everything frozen, the encoder's representation is fixed and the head can only draw a linear boundary through a space that was never arranged for this task. One unfrozen block lets the representation itself move. Lower blocks encode general syntax and word sense, which your task does not need to change. IN PRACTICE around five of the twelve blocks gets close to training all twelve. Freeze from the bottom, unfreeze from the top, and always leave frozen layers BELOW trainable ones rather than interleaved. AND THE GAP WIDENS WITH EPOCHS these figures are one epoch. Over several epochs the compute saved by freezing grows, and so does the quality difference. Neither stays fixed.

SetFit: turning 32 documents into 1,280 comparisons

When labels are scarce, the ordinary supervised framing wastes them. Thirty-two documents give thirty-two training signals. Treating them contrastively gives every pair of documents a signal, and the number of pairs grows much faster than the number of documents.

The three steps are worth reading against the rest of Part V, because SetFit is assembled entirely from pieces already covered. Step one builds pairs by pairing documents with the same label as positives and documents with different labels as negatives, which is the contrastive data construction of lecture 14 with the class label standing in for entailment. Step two fine-tunes a sentence embedding model on those pairs, which is lecture 15's supervised fine-tuning. Step three fits a classifier on the resulting embeddings, which is lecture 5's frozen-embeddings-plus-logistic-regression pipeline. Nothing here is new; the assembly is.

THE PAIR ARITHMETIC 16 examples per class x 2 classes = 32 documents default 20 pair combinations each = 640 one positive and one negative each = 1,280 training pairs so 32 labelled documents become 1,280 supervised comparisons, a 40-fold amplification, without anyone labelling anything further. WHAT STEP 2 ACTUALLY CHANGES a general embedding model places documents by SEMANTIC similarity. After step 2 it places them by CLASS similarity: two negative reviews about entirely different films become neighbours. This is the point lecture 15 made about steering the space, applied to a classification objective. RESULT F1 0.8364 from 32 labels against 0.85 from 8,530 AND WITH NO LABELS AT ALL SetFit can also run zero-shot by synthesising examples from the label names themselves: for labels "happy" and "sad" it generates sentences like "The example is happy" and trains on those. Weaker than 16 real examples, and it costs nothing.

Continued pretraining with masked language modelling

The two-step paradigm from lecture 1 is pretrain then fine-tune, and the pretraining was done for you on general text. Insert a third step in the middle: continue the pretraining objective, masked language modelling, on your own unlabelled corpus. No annotation is involved, so the only requirement is documents.

The effect is easiest to see qualitatively. Ask the model to fill a blank before and after continued pretraining on film reviews.

"What a horrible [MASK]!" BEFORE, general bert-base-cased >>> What a horrible idea! >>> What a horrible dream! >>> What a horrible thing! >>> What a horrible day! >>> What a horrible thought! AFTER, continued pretraining on the review corpus >>> What a horrible movie! >>> What a horrible film! >>> What a horrible mess! >>> What a horrible comedy! >>> What a horrible story! The model's sense of what words go together has moved into the domain. Nothing was labelled to achieve this. WHERE IT FITS 1. general pretraining (done for you) 2. CONTINUED PRETRAINING (your corpus, no labels) <- new 3. task fine-tuning (your labels) and step 3 then starts from a model that already knows the vocabulary. This is the masked-language-modelling branch of the domain adaptation lecture 15 described; TSDAE is the other, and the choice between them is largely whether you want a better base model for a task head (MLM) or a better sentence embedding (TSDAE). THE EXAMPLE HERE IS UNDERPOWERED ON PURPOSE film reviews are close to general English, so the shift is stylistic. On genuinely specialised text, policy wordings or clinical notes, where the general model has never seen the vocabulary, the gain is much larger. That is the situation this step exists for.

Named-entity recognition, and the alignment problem

Everything so far classified whole documents. Named-entity recognition classifies every token, using AutoModelForTokenClassification rather than AutoModelForSequenceClassification: the same encoder with a head that emits one label per position rather than one per sequence.

The labels use the BIO scheme, where B- marks the beginning of an entity and I- marks a continuation inside the same one. That prefix is what distinguishes "Dean Palmer" as one person from "Dean" and "Palmer" as two. With four entity types plus the outside label, CoNLL-2003 has nine labels: O, and B-/I- for person, organisation, location and miscellaneous.

Then the problem. Your labels are one per word. The tokenizer produces sub-tokens, and adds special tokens at both ends. The two lists are different lengths, and nothing checks that you lined them up.

WHAT GOES WRONG WITHOUT THIS pass the original 10 labels against 13 positions and either you get a shape error, if you are lucky, or a silent off-by-one in which "Palmer" is labelled with what belongs to "hit" and every subsequent label is shifted. Nothing raises. The model trains happily on nonsense and the F1 is merely disappointing rather than obviously broken. TWO MORE THINGS THAT CHANGE FOR TOKEN CLASSIFICATION DataCollatorForTokenClassification, not DataCollatorWithPadding: padding must extend the LABEL sequence too. seqeval, not plain accuracy: entity-level scoring, so a two-token person found as one token counts as wrong. That is the right severity, because half an entity is not half an answer.

At inference the sub-tokens come back individually, and the BIO prefixes are what let you reassemble them.

token_classifier("My name is Maarten.")
# [{'entity': 'B-PER', 'word': 'Ma',     'score': 0.995},
#  {'entity': 'I-PER', 'word': '##arte', 'score': 0.993},
#  {'entity': 'I-PER', 'word': '##n',    'score': 0.995}]

One name, three sub-tokens, correctly chained by one B- and two I-s. Aggregating those back into "Maarten" is a post-processing step, and the pipeline can do it for you, but it is worth seeing the raw output once so the shape of the task is clear.

Step 5

Synthesis and what's next

Unfreezing a pretrained BERT and training it jointly with a classification head takes the Rotten Tomatoes task from 0.80 with an off-the-shelf model to 0.85, though it only matches, rather than beats, lecture 5's frozen embeddings with a logistic regression on top. Freezing is a dial with a sharply non-linear response: head only collapses to 0.63, one unfrozen block recovers to 0.80, and the remaining eleven are worth 0.05, because the task-specific adaptation happens near the top. SetFit sidesteps the label requirement by treating 16 examples per class as 1,280 contrastive pairs, reaching 0.8364 from 0.375 per cent of the labels, and it is assembled entirely from lecture 14's pair construction, lecture 15's embedding fine-tuning, and lecture 5's classifier on frozen features. Continued pretraining with masked language modelling adapts the base model to your vocabulary using no labels at all. And named-entity recognition moves classification to the token level, which forces you to align word-level labels onto sub-tokens explicitly, using -100 for positions the loss should ignore, or train silently on shifted targets.

The order to try things in Nothing in Part V argues for reaching straight for a full fine-tune. Measure the zero-shot baseline (lecture 5). Try frozen embeddings with a classifier, which on this task equalled the fine-tune. If labels are scarce, label sixteen per class and run SetFit. If your vocabulary is specialised, continue pretraining before anything else. Full fine-tuning is the last rung, not the first, and its main cost is not the GPU hour but that you now own a model to host, version and revalidate.

Part V's remaining two lectures move from representation models to generative ones, where the same instinct, leave the large thing alone and train something small beside it, returns as the dominant technique.
