Training embedding models

Lecture 14 trained from scratch, which is the one thing you should almost never do. This lecture covers the three realistic situations instead: you have pairs and a pretrained model to start from, you have a few thousand labels where you need hundreds of thousands, or you have nothing labelled at all. The third case is the interesting one, because on the same benchmark an entirely unsupervised method scores 0.6992 against 0.5982 for a supervised method with fifty thousand labels.

Lecture 15 of 18 Reading time, 40 minutes Part V: training your own
Step 1

The 30-second version

Start from a model somebody else has already trained on hundreds of millions of pairs. Swapping bert-base-uncased for all-MiniLM-L6-v2 and changing nothing else takes the multiple-negatives result from lecture 14 from 0.8094 to 0.8510, and MiniLM is six layers rather than twelve.

When labels are scarce, Augmented SBERT manufactures more. Fine-tune an accurate but unusable cross-encoder on the small annotated gold set, use it to label a much larger unannotated set into a silver set, then train the bi-encoder on both. With 10,000 gold labels standing in for 50,000, the score is 0.7102 against 0.7222 for the fully supervised run: 98.3 per cent of the quality from 20 per cent of the labels.

When there are no labels, TSDAE removes a fraction of the words from a sentence, encodes the damaged version to a single vector, and makes a decoder rebuild the original from that vector alone. A vector good enough to reconstruct a sentence turns out to be good enough to compare sentences. It scores 0.6992 with zero labels, which is 16.9 per cent above the softmax-loss model of lecture 14 that used fifty thousand of them.

And the combination is domain adaptation: run TSDAE or masked language modelling on your own unlabelled corpus first, so the model learns your vocabulary, then fine-tune on pairs, which may come from a different domain entirely.

Feynman test Three ways to get a specialist. Hire one who is already trained and give them a week on your files. Or take your one expert, have them mark a thousand cases, and use those markings to have a machine mark forty thousand more. Or, with nobody to teach you anything, tear words out of your own documents and practise putting them back, until you understand the documents well enough that the summary you write is enough to reconstruct them.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that the labels are the cost. The model is a download, the GPU is an hour's rental, and the training script is thirty lines. What actually stops embedding projects is that nobody has annotated fifty thousand pairs and nobody is going to. Both of the second and third methods here exist to remove that blocker, and between them they cover almost every real situation.

The second reason is that the unsupervised result is genuinely surprising and should change your default. A method using no labels at all beats a supervised method using fifty thousand. That inverts the usual assumption that unlabelled data is a consolation prize, and it means the first thing to try on a new domain is not a labelling exercise.

The third reason is that domain adaptation is the case most people actually have. A leaderboard model was trained on web text, and your corpus is policy wordings, clinical notes or claims narratives, where the vocabulary is different and the important distinctions are ones general text never had to make. Adapting a strong general model to your domain reliably beats picking a stronger general model.

And a fourth reason about honesty in measurement. One of the numbers in this lecture is contaminated, and the source says so plainly. Recognising why is a transferable skill, because the same contamination affects most published fine-tuning comparisons.

The mental model

Step 4

The detail

Supervised fine-tuning, and an honest caveat

The change from lecture 14 is one line. Where the base model was a plain bert-base-uncased that had never been trained to produce sentence embeddings, use one that has.

from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer, losses

# lecture 14:  SentenceTransformer("bert-base-uncased")             -> 0.8094
embedding_model = SentenceTransformer("sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2")
train_loss = losses.MultipleNegativesRankingLoss(model=embedding_model)
# same data, same loss, same one epoch                              -> 0.8510

Note that MiniLM is the smaller model, six transformer layers against BERT base's twelve, and still wins by four points. Parameter count is not what separated them; having already been trained on the right objective is.

This particular number should not be quoted all-MiniLM-L6-v2 was trained on over a billion pairs, and MNLI in full was among them. The evaluation therefore involves a model that has already seen the entire training set, of which our 50,000 examples are a subset. The 0.8510 is a demonstration that the code works, not a measurement of what fine-tuning gains. This is the same benchmark-contamination problem lecture 5 flagged for the hosted classifier, and it affects most published fine-tuning comparisons on public datasets. The only clean measurement is on pairs you constructed yourself and never released.

The genuine finding underneath survives the caveat: starting from a model already trained for sentence similarity is cheaper and better than starting from a plain encoder, because you are adapting a good geometry rather than building one. Fine-tuning also needs far less data than training, which is what makes the next two methods worth having.

Augmented SBERT: spend an expensive model to make cheap labels

Production embedding models are trained on hundreds of millions of pairs. You have a few thousand. Augmented SBERT bridges that gap by using the accurate-but-unusable architecture from lecture 14 as a labelling machine rather than as a search engine, which is the one job its cost structure suits: a cross-encoder run once over a fixed dataset offline is entirely affordable, whereas the same model run per query is not.

AUGMENTED SBERT, FOUR STEPS 1. TRAIN A CROSS-ENCODER ON THE GOLD SET gold = 10,000 hand-labelled pairs cross-encoder from bert-base-uncased, 2 labels, 1 epoch accurate, slow, produces no embeddings. Fine: it will never see a query. 2. ASSEMBLE UNLABELLED PAIRS 40,000 pairs with no annotation (here, the remainder of the 50,000, treated as unlabelled) 3. LABEL THEM -> THE SILVER SET cross-encoder.predict(pairs) 40,000 machine labels. Not ground truth, hence "silver". 4. TRAIN THE BI-ENCODER ON GOLD + SILVER 10,000 + 40,000 = 50,000 pairs, deduplicated cosine similarity loss, as in lecture 14 THE RESULT Augmented SBERT, 10,000 gold 0.7102 fully supervised, 50,000 gold 0.7222 -------- ratio 0.7102 / 0.7222 = 98.34% labels 10,000 / 50,000 = 20.0% 98.3 per cent of the quality for 20 per cent of the annotation. The remaining 1.7 per cent is the price of silver labels being predictions rather than truth. HOW TO CHOOSE THE UNLABELLED PAIRS (this matters more than it looks) RANDOM PAIRING is the obvious move and a poor one: take a premise from one row and a hypothesis from another and you can manufacture ten times the data, but almost every pair will be dissimilar, so the silver set is wildly imbalanced and teaches the model little. SEMANTIC SEARCH is the fix. Embed all candidate sentences with an existing model and keep each sentence's top-k neighbours as candidate pairs. The pairs are then plausible enough that the cross-encoder's judgement is informative. This is the same rough-then-precise pattern as two-stage retrieval, applied to dataset construction. HOW TO CHECK THE SILVER IS WORTH ANYTHING train once on gold alone, once on gold plus silver, and compare. The difference is what the augmentation bought. If it is zero, your candidate pairs were badly chosen.

TSDAE: training with no labels at all

Sometimes there is nothing to label with. The transformer-based sequential denoising auto-encoder assumes exactly that, and does not fabricate labels either. It sets up a task that any pile of sentences can supply on its own.

Delete a fraction of the words from a sentence. Push the damaged version through an encoder and pool it into one vector. Then hand that single vector to a decoder and require it to reproduce the original, undamaged sentence. The vector is the only channel between the two, so it must carry enough of the sentence's content to support reconstruction, and it turns out that a vector good enough for that is also good enough for comparing sentences.

WHAT THE MODEL SEES damaged "Grim jaws are." original "Grim faces and hardened jaws are not people-friendly." the encoder never sees the original. The decoder never sees the damaged input. Everything that passes between them is one pooled vector. RELATION TO MASKED LANGUAGE MODELLING MLM reconstructs specific tokens, and is TOLD where the gaps are by the [MASK] token TSDAE reconstructs the WHOLE SENTENCE, and is not told where anything was removed the harder task forces the pooled vector to carry sentence- level meaning, which is precisely what MLM never required and precisely why pooling a plain BERT fails (lecture 3). TWO CONFIGURATION POINTS THAT ARE NOT ARBITRARY POOL ON [CLS], NOT THE MEAN. The TSDAE authors found CLS pooling better here, because mean pooling discards position information and reconstruction needs word order. This is the one place in the track where mean pooling is the wrong default. TIE THE ENCODER AND DECODER WEIGHTS. One set of weights serves both, halving the parameters and stabilising training. and lower the batch size: 16 rather than 32, because the decoder is extra memory on top of the encoder. THE DATA, AND HOW LITTLE IT NEEDS 25,000 MNLI rows, premises and hypotheses flattened into one list and deduplicated: 50,000 sentences labels used: 0 THE RESULT TSDAE, no labels 0.6992 softmax loss, 50,000 labels 0.5982 (lecture 14) -------- 0.6992 / 0.5982 = 1.169 -> +16.9% An unsupervised method beats a supervised one that had fifty thousand annotations. The supervised method was handicapped by a poor loss, which is exactly the point: the objective matters more than the supervision. Against the best supervised result (MNR, 0.8094) TSDAE is 13.6 per cent behind. So unsupervised is not free quality; it is a very good starting position.
from sentence_transformers import models, losses, SentenceTransformer
from sentence_transformers.datasets import DenoisingAutoEncoderDataset

damaged_data = DenoisingAutoEncoderDataset(list(set(flat_sentences)))

word_embedding_model = models.Transformer("bert-base-uncased")
pooling_model = models.Pooling(
    word_embedding_model.get_word_embedding_dimension(), "cls")   # not "mean"
embedding_model = SentenceTransformer(modules=[word_embedding_model, pooling_model])

train_loss = losses.DenoisingAutoEncoderLoss(embedding_model, tie_encoder_decoder=True)

When training finishes, throw the decoder away. It existed only to prove the vector was good; the encoder is the artefact.

Domain adaptation: use both

The three routes above are not exclusive, and the standard arrangement combines them. An off-the-shelf embedding model was trained on general web text, which is a different domain from policy wordings, clinical notes or claims narratives: different vocabulary, different frequent constructions, and distinctions that matter to you and never mattered to the source corpus.

ADAPTIVE PRETRAINING, THEN ADAPTIVE FINE-TUNING STAGE 1 learn the vocabulary, unsupervised, ON YOUR CORPUS run TSDAE, or masked language modelling (lecture 16), over your unlabelled documents. No annotation needed, and you almost certainly already have the documents. Outcome: a model that knows how your domain's words are used. STAGE 2 learn similarity, supervised, ON WHATEVER PAIRS EXIST fine-tune with MNR loss on pairs. These may come from OUTSIDE your domain and it still works, because stage 1 has already handled the vocabulary. General NLI or question-answer pairs are enough. Outcome: a model that knows what "similar" means. WHY THE ORDER MATTERS stage 1 teaches WHAT THE WORDS MEAN HERE. stage 2 teaches WHAT SIMILARITY MEANS. Doing 2 alone leaves the model fluent in the wrong vocabulary; doing 1 alone leaves it knowledgeable and unable to compare. AND IF YOU HAVE A FEW LABELS TOO stage 1 (TSDAE on your corpus) stage 2 (Augmented SBERT on your small gold set) is the full pipeline, and needs no dataset you do not already possess.

What actually determines the outcome

Two closing observations that the numbers support and that get ignored.

The data is the constraint, not the model or the compute. Every result in the ladder used one epoch on a modest GPU. What separates 0.5982 from 0.8510 is the objective and the pairs, not the architecture, and no amount of training longer will substitute. Positive pairs are usually easy to obtain; hard negatives, as lecture 14 argued, are where the effort belongs.

And contrastive training is unstable on small datasets in a way ordinary supervised training is not. The objective depends on the distribution of negatives, so a small sample gives an unrepresentative one and results vary run to run. If you are working with a few thousand pairs, that instability is a reason to augment, as here, rather than a reason to accept the variance.

Step 5

Synthesis and what's next

Do not train from scratch. Starting from a model already trained for sentence similarity moves the lecture 14 result from 0.8094 to 0.8510 with one line changed, using a model half the depth, though that particular figure is contaminated because the pretrained model had already seen the benchmark's training set. When labels are scarce, Augmented SBERT fine-tunes a cross-encoder on a small gold set and uses it to label a larger silver set, reaching 98.3 per cent of the fully supervised score from 20 per cent of the annotation, provided the candidate pairs are chosen by semantic search rather than at random. When there are no labels, TSDAE deletes words and trains a decoder to rebuild the original sentence from the pooled vector alone, scoring 0.6992 with zero annotation, which is 16.9 per cent above the softmax-loss supervised model. Combining them gives domain adaptation: unsupervised pretraining on your corpus to learn the vocabulary, then supervised fine-tuning on any available pairs to learn similarity.

The result worth remembering An unsupervised method beat a supervised method with fifty thousand labels. That is not an argument that supervision is worthless, since the best supervised result is still 13.6 per cent ahead. It is an argument that the objective matters more than the label count, and that the correct first move on a new domain is to run an unsupervised pass over the documents you already have, not to commission an annotation exercise. Measure the unsupervised baseline before you buy labels, exactly as lecture 5 argued for zero-shot classification.
