Retrieval quality and evaluation

Lecture 11 deferred the same question three times: how good is the retrieval, actually? Answering it needs metrics that care where a result sits rather than merely whether it appeared, a second-stage model that reorders what the first stage found, and four separate measurements, because a system that scores 57 per cent end to end might need its retriever fixed or its prompt fixed and the single number cannot tell you which.

Lecture 12 of 18 Reading time, 45 minutes Part III: building systems
Step 1

The 30-second version

Recall at k counts whether the right passage appeared in the top k. It cannot see where. Two rankings holding the same three relevant chunks inside the top six have identical recall of 1.00 and normalised discounted cumulative gains of 0.9207 and 0.4423, because one puts the best chunk first and the other buries it at rank six, which is where lecture 11's "lost in the middle" finding says the model reads least reliably.

Improving the order needs a different kind of model. A bi-encoder embeds question and chunk separately, so chunk vectors are precomputed and a query is one embedding plus a nearest-neighbour lookup. A cross-encoder reads the pair together and is markedly more accurate, and it can precompute nothing, so it needs one forward pass per candidate: a second on a hundred candidates, and 223 seconds on the 22,322 chunks of a ten-million-token corpus. It cannot be the search. It can only reorder a shortlist.

That two-stage design is worth a great deal. If the first stage finds the answer somewhere in its top hundred 95 per cent of the time but places it in the top six only 60 per cent of the time, a reranker that promotes it correctly nine times in ten lifts recall at six to 85.5 per cent, a gain of 25.5 points. The ceiling is the first stage's recall at a hundred, and no reranker exceeds it.

Whole-system evaluation then splits along the same seam. Two RAG systems can both score 57.0 per cent end to end, one from 60 per cent retrieval and 95 per cent generation and the other from 95 and 60, and they need opposite repairs. Measuring four axes rather than one is what tells them apart.

Feynman test Finding the right paragraph is not enough; it has to be near the top, because the model reads the top best. So fetch a hundred cheaply, have a slower and better model put six of them in order, and then measure the fetching and the answering separately, because fixing the wrong one is the usual mistake.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that almost every disappointing retrieval-augmented system is disappointing at the retrieval step, and the team debugging it is usually adjusting the prompt. The four-axis split in step 4 exists to make that mistake impossible to sustain: it localises the fault before anyone starts editing.

The second reason is that the metric determines what you optimise, and recall is the wrong metric for a generation system. A retriever tuned to maximise recall at fifty will happily place the answer at rank forty-nine, which is useless when six chunks fit in the prompt and the model attends best to the first. Rank-sensitive measures reward what actually helps.

The third reason is that reranking is the highest-value single change available to most systems and is frequently skipped because it looks like an optimisation. It is not an optimisation; it is a correction of a structural weakness in bi-encoder retrieval, which compresses a whole chunk into one vector before it has seen the question.

And a fourth reason about honesty. These systems are usually demonstrated rather than measured, because building an evaluation set is dull and a demonstration is not. A hundred questions with known answers, assembled once, converts every subsequent argument about a change from an exchange of anecdotes into a number.

Step 3

The mental model

Two stages in the pipeline, and four measurements across it.

Bi-encoder
Encodes query and document independently into vectors compared by cosine similarity. Because the document vector never sees the query, it must summarise the whole chunk in advance for every possible question. That is what makes it fast and what limits its accuracy.
Cross-encoder
Encodes query and document jointly and outputs a relevance score. Attention runs across both, so the model can weigh the exact terms of the question against the exact wording of the passage. Nothing can be precomputed, which restricts it to shortlists.
nDCG
Normalised discounted cumulative gain, from Järvelin and Kekäläinen (2002). Sums the relevance of each result discounted by its rank, then divides by the best achievable arrangement. Rewards putting the most relevant item first, and gives one for a perfect ordering regardless of how many relevant items exist.

Why the second stage exists

A bi-encoder compresses a chunk into a vector before it has seen any question, so that vector has to serve every question that might ever be asked. A cross-encoder reads the pair together, which lets attention compare the actual words of the query against the actual words of the passage. The accuracy difference is large and the cost difference is larger.

THE ARITHMETIC THAT FORCES TWO STAGES A CROSS-ENCODER NEEDS ONE FORWARD PASS PER CANDIDATE, at roughly 10 ms per pair when batched top 6 only 6 passes -> 0.1 s top 50 50 passes -> 0.5 s top 100 100 passes -> 1.0 s top 1,000 1,000 passes -> 10.0 s the whole 10M-token corpus 22,322 passes -> 223.2 s SO IT CANNOT BE THE SEARCH. It can only reorder a shortlist, and the shortlist size is a latency budget: 100 candidates costs about a second, which most interactive systems can absorb, and 1,000 costs ten, which they cannot. WHAT THE REORDERING IS WORTH suppose stage 1 finds the answer in its top 100 for 95% of queries but places it in the top 6 for only 60%. cross-encoder promotes it correctly reranked recall@6 80% of the time 76.0% +16.0 pp 90% of the time 85.5% +25.5 pp 95% of the time 90.2% +30.2 pp AND THE CEILING IS ABSOLUTE reranking reorders; it cannot conjure. Reranked recall@6 can never exceed stage 1's recall@100. If that is 70 per cent, no reranker takes the system past 70 per cent, and the fix is a better first stage, a hybrid retriever, or different chunking, not a better reranker. SO THE DIAGNOSTIC ORDER IS FIXED 1 measure recall@100. that is your ceiling. 2 measure recall@6. the gap is what reranking can win. 3 only then decide whether to buy a reranker or fix stage 1.

Four things worth doing to the query and the results

Reciprocal rank fusion
The standard way to merge the dense and lexical rankings from lecture 11. Each document scores the sum over rankings of 1/(k + rank), with k a small constant. It needs no score calibration between systems, because it uses only positions, and it is a few lines of code. From Cormack, Clarke and Buettcher (2009).
Query rewriting
A short model call that turns a conversational question into a standalone one before it reaches the retriever. Essential in a chat setting, where "what about for contractors?" is meaningless as a search query and the previous turn holds its subject.
Hypothetical document embeddings
Ask the model to draft an answer, embed the draft rather than the question, and retrieve against that. Gao and colleagues (2022) observed that an answer resembles the passage that contains it more than a question does, which is the asymmetry a bi-encoder struggles with.
Retrieve small, send large
Index fine-grained chunks so that matching is precise, then send the parent section so that the model has enough context to use it. Decouples the retrieval unit from the reading unit, which the fixed chunk size in lecture 11 had forced together.

Evaluating the whole system: four axes

A retrieval-augmented system has two halves and each can fail on two counts, which is where the standard four-axis framing comes from, popularised by the RAGAS work of Es and colleagues in 2023. Retrieval is judged on context precision, whether the chunks sent were relevant, and context recall, whether the chunks needed were sent. Generation is judged on faithfulness, whether each claim is supported by the supplied context, and answer relevance, whether the answer addresses the question.

THE EVALUATION SET IS THE PREREQUISITE a hundred questions with known answers and known source passages, built once and versioned. Step 5

Synthesis and what's next

Recall counts presence and ignores position, which is the wrong emphasis for a system whose prompt holds six chunks and whose model reads the first one best. Normalised discounted cumulative gain fixes that by discounting each result by its rank and normalising against the ideal ordering: two rankings with identical recall score 0.9207 and 0.4423, and at three results the gap widens to 0.75 against 0.05. Mean average precision does the same job for binary relevance. Recall at k keeps one use, as a ceiling check on the first stage.

Two-stage retrieval follows from an arithmetic constraint rather than a preference. A cross-encoder is more accurate because it reads the query and the chunk together, and for exactly that reason it can precompute nothing and needs a forward pass per candidate: one second over a hundred candidates, 223 seconds over a ten-million-token corpus. So the bi-encoder shortlists and the cross-encoder orders, which lifts recall at six from 60 to 85.5 per cent on the worked figures. The ceiling is the shortlist's own recall, and measuring it first is what tells you whether to buy a reranker or fix the retriever.

The habit this lecture is really arguing for Every result here depends on having an evaluation set: a hundred questions with known answers and known source passages. Without one, no claim in this lecture can be checked on your own corpus, reranking is a purchase made on faith, and "the retrieval seems better now" is the only sentence available. With one, the four axes tell you which half is broken, recall at a hundred tells you the ceiling, and nDCG tells you whether a change helped or merely moved things about. The set is a day's work and it is the difference between engineering and decoration.
