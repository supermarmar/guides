Supervised fine-tuning with QLoRA

A base model does not answer questions; it continues them. Turning one into something that follows instructions is the second of three training stages, and doing it used to require the compute of the first. Two independent tricks removed that requirement. One replaces each weight update with a pair of thin matrices, cutting a 150,994,944-parameter update to 196,608. The other stores the frozen weights at four bits instead of thirty-two. Together they put fine-tuning a billion-parameter model on a free Colab GPU, in about an hour.

Step 1

The 30-second version

Three stages produce a usable generative model. Pretraining predicts the next token over an enormous unlabelled corpus and yields a base model that completes text and follows no instructions. Supervised fine-tuning continues next-token prediction on instruction-and-response pairs, which converts a completer into something that answers. Preference tuning, lecture 18, aligns it with human judgements about which answer is better.

Full fine-tuning updates every parameter and is expensive in compute, time and storage. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning avoids it. The earliest form, adapters, inserts small trainable modules into each transformer block: fine-tuning 3.6 per cent of BERT's parameters came within 0.4 per cent of full fine-tuning on GLUE.

LoRA is the technique that stuck. Rather than adding layers, it represents the change to a weight matrix as the product of two thin matrices. For a 12,288 × 12,288 matrix in GPT-3, that is 150,994,944 parameters replaced by two 12,288 × 8 matrices totalling 196,608, a 768-fold reduction, and it works because the useful update turns out to have very low intrinsic dimension.

QLoRA adds quantisation to the frozen base: store it in 4-bit normalised floats with blockwise and double quantisation, and a 1.1-billion-parameter model loads in about 1 GB of VRAM rather than about 4 GB. The adapters train in 16-bit on top. Afterwards the adapter is merged back into the base weights, so inference costs nothing extra.

Feynman test You want to correct a very long reference book. You could reprint every page, which is what full fine-tuning does. Or you could notice that your corrections are not scattered randomly: they follow a few consistent patterns, so a short list of rules plus where to apply them reproduces almost all of them. That list is thousands of times smaller than the book. And while you work, you keep the original on microfiche rather than in print, because you only need to read it, never to rewrite it.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is access. Full fine-tuning of a seven-billion-parameter model needs enterprise hardware, principally because the optimiser states and gradients are several times the size of the weights themselves. LoRA removes the gradients and optimiser states for almost every parameter, and quantisation shrinks what remains. The result is that the technique is available to anyone with a consumer GPU, which is a change in who can do this work rather than merely how fast it goes.

The second reason is that the artefact is small and swappable. A LoRA adapter is a few tens of megabytes rather than a few tens of gigabytes, so one base model can serve many specialisations, each stored and versioned separately. That is a materially different deployment story from holding a full copy of the model per task.

The third reason is that the choice between fine-tuning and the alternatives is a real decision and this lecture is where you can price it. Prompting is free, retrieval is cheap, fine-tuning costs a training run and gives you a model to host, version and revalidate. Fine-tuning is the right answer for changing behaviour, the format, the register, the willingness to follow a house style. It is the wrong answer for supplying facts, which is lecture 11's job.

And a fourth reason about why it works at all. LoRA is not a lossy compromise that happens to be tolerable. It rests on a finding, that the update needed to adapt a language model has very low intrinsic dimension, and understanding that claim is what tells you when to expect the technique to hold and when to be suspicious.

Step 4

The detail

Why a base model needs stage two

A base model was trained to predict the next token, and that is all it does. Give one an instruction and it will often produce more instructions, because in its training corpus a question is frequently followed by further questions rather than by an answer. It has the knowledge and none of the conversational contract.

Supervised fine-tuning does not change the objective. It is still next-token prediction; what changes is the data. Train on pairs of instruction and response, formatted in a chat template, and the highest-probability continuation of a question becomes an answer. Lecture 1 called this a thin layer over autocomplete, and this is where that layer is applied.

Full fine-tuning, adapters, and the shape of the alternative

Full fine-tuning updates every parameter, which is straightforward and expensive. The cost is not mainly the weights: it is that each trainable parameter needs a gradient and, for an optimiser like Adam, two more optimiser states, so training memory is several times the model size.

Adapters, the first widely used parameter-efficient method, insert small trainable modules after the attention and feedforward sub-layers of every transformer block, and freeze everything else. Fine-tuning 3.6 per cent of BERT's parameters reached within 0.4 per cent of full fine-tuning on GLUE. Because an adapter is a self-contained set of modules, one specialising in clinical text and another in entity recognition can be swapped into the same frozen base.

Adapters add depth, which adds inference latency. LoRA does not.

LoRA: factorise the update

The insight is to leave the original weight matrix alone and represent only the change to it. If a layer's weight matrix is W, ordinary fine-tuning learns some update ΔW of the same shape. LoRA constrains ΔW to be the product of two much thinner matrices, so instead of learning d × d numbers you learn d × r and r × d, where the rank r is small.

THE ARITHMETIC GENERAL CASE, a d x d weight matrix at rank r full update d x d = d^2 LoRA update (d x r) + (r x d) = 2dr saving factor d^2 / 2dr = d / 2r note the saving grows with d. LoRA helps MORE on larger models, which is the opposite of most efficiency tricks. TOY CASE, the 10 x 10 matrix full 10 x 10 = 100 weights rank 1 (10 x 1) + (1 x 10) = 20 weights saving 10 / 2 = 5x REAL CASE, GPT-3 at 175B parameters each of 96 transformer blocks holds a 12,288 x 12,288 matrix 12,288^2 = 150,994,944 (~151 M per matrix) at rank 8 2 x (12,288 x 8) = 196,608 (~197 K) saving 12,288 / 16 = 768x as a fraction = 0.130% WHAT THIS BUYS BEYOND SPEED no gradients or optimiser states for the frozen 99.87% an adapter file of tens of MB, not tens of GB many task adapters over one shared base model WHY IT IS NOT MERELY A LOSSY SHORTCUT "Intrinsic dimensionality explains the effectiveness of language model fine-tuning" found that language models have a very low intrinsic dimension: the useful adaptation lives in a small subspace. So a low-rank Delta-W is not an approximation of the update you wanted; for these models it is close to the update you wanted. the corollary is the honest caveat. Where the adaptation is genuinely high-rank, teaching substantially new capability rather than adjusting behaviour, low rank will not capture it, and raising r until it does eventually costs what full fine-tuning costs.

You also choose which matrices get an adapter. Targeting only the query and value projections is a common minimal configuration; targeting all attention and feedforward projections is more thorough and slower.

Quantisation, and the Q in QLoRA

LoRA shrinks what you train. Quantisation shrinks what you keep. Since the base model is frozen, it is only ever read, so it can be stored at much lower precision than training requires.

Naive quantisation fails for a specific reason: mapping a wide range of high-precision values onto few low-precision levels makes distinct weights collapse onto the same value, destroying the differences that carried the information. QLoRA's two corrections are worth knowing by name.

MAKING FOUR BITS SURVIVABLE BLOCKWISE QUANTISATION quantise in small blocks, each with its own scaling constant, rather than mapping the entire tensor onto one global scale. A block of similar weights then gets levels spread across its own narrow range, so nearby weights stay distinguishable. DISTRIBUTION-AWARE LEVELS (the "NF" in NF4) neural network weights are approximately normally distributed in [-1, 1]. Evenly spaced levels waste resolution in the sparse tails and starve the dense middle. Normalised float 4 places its 16 levels according to that distribution, so each level carries roughly equal probability mass. This also blunts the effect of outliers. DOUBLE QUANTISATION the per-block scaling constants are themselves numerous enough to matter, so they are quantised too. A second-order saving, and free. PAGED OPTIMIZERS move optimiser state between GPU and CPU memory on demand, so a transient spike does not end the run. THE MEMORY, FOR TinyLlama AT 1.1B PARAMETERS float32 1.1e9 x 4 bytes = 4.4 GB float16 1.1e9 x 2 bytes = 2.2 GB 4-bit 1.1e9 x 0.5 bytes = 0.55 GB ~1 GB as actually loaded, the difference being quantisation constants and runtime overhead AND THE PART PEOPLE GET WRONG only the FROZEN BASE is quantised. The LoRA adapters train in higher precision, because gradients at 4 bits are useless. QLoRA is a 4-bit model with 16-bit adapters on it, not a 4-bit training run. note also that loading is not training. Activations, gradients for the adapters and optimiser state all add on top of that ~1 GB, so budget several times it.

Doing it: instruction-tuning TinyLlama

The worked example takes TinyLlama at 1.1 billion parameters, a genuine base model, and teaches it to follow instructions using 3,000 conversations from UltraChat.

Templating first. The data has to arrive in exactly the chat format the model will later be prompted with, which lecture 7 established is not decoration. Use the tokenizer's own template rather than writing the markers by hand.

template_tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("TinyLlama/TinyLlama-1.1B-Chat-v1.0")

def format_prompt(example):
    return {"text": template_tokenizer.apply_chat_template(example["messages"], tokenize=False)}

dataset = load_dataset("HuggingFaceH4/ultrachat_200k", split="test_sft") \
            .shuffle(seed=42).select(range(3_000)).map(format_prompt)

The result is text with <|user|> and <|assistant|> turns and an end-of-turn marker after each. Training on that is what teaches the model both to answer and to stop.

Then quantise, then attach adapters.

from peft import LoraConfig, prepare_model_for_kbit_training, get_peft_model

peft_config = LoraConfig(
    r=64,                 # rank
    lora_alpha=32,        # scaling applied to the LoRA update
    lora_dropout=0.1,
    bias="none",
    task_type="CAUSAL_LM",
    target_modules=["q_proj", "k_proj", "v_proj", "o_proj",
                    "gate_proj", "up_proj", "down_proj"],
)
model = get_peft_model(prepare_model_for_kbit_training(model), peft_config)
A contradiction in the source worth knowing about The usual rule of thumb for lora_alpha is roughly twice the rank, and the configuration above uses r=64 with lora_alpha=32, which is half. Both appear in the source material. Since alpha scales the LoRA update relative to the frozen weights, halving it rather than doubling it makes the adaptation about four times weaker than the rule of thumb intends. Neither setting is wrong in principle, and both are defensible, but do not copy a rank and an alpha from different places and assume they compose. Set the ratio deliberately and record why.

Then train. The configuration is unremarkable except for three entries that exist because of the memory situation.

TRAINING CONFIGURATION, AND WHY per_device_train_batch_size = 2 gradient_accumulation_steps = 4 -> effective batch of 8, accumulated rather than held in memory at once. This is how you get a usable batch size on a small card. gradient_checkpointing = True -> recompute activations during the backward pass instead of storing them. Trades compute for memory. optim = "paged_adamw_32bit" -> the paged optimiser, so a spike pages out rather than crashing. learning_rate = 2e-4 -> roughly ten times a typical full fine-tuning rate, because far fewer parameters are moving. lr_scheduler_type = "cosine" -> warm up linearly from zero, then decay on a cosine. num_train_epochs = 1 -> more epochs tend to degrade instruction tuning, so keep it low. max_seq_length = 512 ON A FREE COLAB T4: about one hour.

Then merge. After training you have a base model and a separate adapter. Reload the base in 16-bit rather than 4-bit and fold the adapter in.

from peft import AutoPeftModelForCausalLM

model = AutoPeftModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("TinyLlama-1.1B-qlora", device_map="auto")
merged_model = model.merge_and_unload()

This matters for the reason adapters do not share: because ΔW has the same shape as W, the two can be added into a single matrix. The merged model has exactly the original architecture and exactly the original inference cost. Nothing has been inserted, so there is no latency penalty at all, which is the practical advantage of LoRA over adapters. Merge in 16-bit and not 4-bit, because merging into quantised weights would round away much of what you just trained.

Keeping the adapter unmerged is also a legitimate choice: it lets one loaded base serve several tasks, at a small runtime cost. Merge for a single-purpose deployment, keep separate for a multi-tenant one.

The same technique, pointed elsewhere This lecture uses QLoRA for its headline purpose, turning a base model into an instruction follower. The mechanism does not care. Point it at question-and-SQL pairs and you get a model that writes your schema's SQL; point it at documents and correctly formatted JSON and you get reliable structured output, which is the third of the output controls lecture 8 named and deferred. Anywhere you have query-and-response pairs and want changed behaviour rather than new facts, this is the tool.
Step 5

Synthesis and what's next

Three stages make a usable generative model, and this lecture is the second: supervised fine-tuning keeps next-token prediction as the objective and changes the data to instruction-and-response pairs formatted in the model's own chat template, converting a completer into something that answers and stops. Full fine-tuning does this expensively, mostly because gradients and optimiser states multiply the memory cost of every trainable parameter. LoRA removes almost all of them by representing the weight update as a product of two thin matrices, taking a 150,994,944-parameter update down to 196,608 at rank 8, a factor of 768, and it works because the adaptation a language model needs has very low intrinsic dimension. QLoRA stacks quantisation on top, storing the frozen base in 4-bit normalised floats with blockwise and double quantisation so a 1.1-billion-parameter model loads in about 1 GB, while the adapters keep the precision that gradients require. Merging the adapter back afterwards restores the original architecture, so inference is not slowed at all.

The instinct, one last time Freeze the large thing and train something small beside it. BLIP-2's bridge in lecture 13 was 2.3 per cent of its parameters. Freezing all but the top BERT block in lecture 16 recovered most of a full fine-tune. LoRA is 0.130 per cent. The recurring finding is that large pretrained models are already nearly right, and adapting them is a small correction rather than a rebuild. That is the single most useful idea in Part V.

Lecture 18 closes the course with the third stage. Supervised fine-tuning teaches a model to imitate correct answers, which works only where a single correct answer exists; most of what we want from a generative model is a matter of better and worse rather than right and wrong. Preference tuning learns from comparisons instead, first through a reward model and then, with direct preference optimisation, without one. And it ends where any responsible treatment must, on how to tell whether any of this actually worked.
