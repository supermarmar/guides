Semantic search and RAG

Lecture 10 ended by saying that most agents are reached for because the model lacks a fact, and that a lookup does not need a loop. This lecture is that lookup. Embed the corpus once, embed the question, fetch the nearest passages, and put them in the prompt. It is the fourth memory strategy from lecture 9, and on the worked figures it does the same job as a ten-step agent for a fourteenth of the tokens.

Lecture 11 of 18 Reading time, 45 minutes Part III: building systems
Step 1

The 30-second version

Retrieval-augmented generation is two systems joined by a prompt. A retriever finds passages related to a question, using the embeddings from lecture 3 rather than keyword matching, and a generator answers using those passages as its evidence. The model gains no knowledge; it gains a place to read from.

The corpus has to be cut into pieces first, because a whole document will not fit in a prompt and a whole document is not what the question is about. On a 100,000-token corpus, 512-token chunks with a 64-token overlap give 224 chunks and cost 14.7 per cent extra storage for the overlap, which is cheap insurance against a passage being cut in half. Storage never becomes the constraint: even a billion tokens embeds to under seven gigabytes.

The context window is the constraint. With a 4,096-token window, a 400-token system prompt and a 500-token answer reserved, there is room for six chunks. So the retriever must place the answer in the top six, and recall at six is the metric that matters, not recall at a hundred.

Dense retrieval fails in a specific and predictable way: it matches meaning, so it misses exact identifiers, rare tokens, negation and numerical constraints. Lexical retrieval fails on paraphrase. Because those are different queries, combining the two is worth much more than it looks. On 80 and 70 per cent recall with independent failures the union reaches 94 per cent; if the failures were identical it would reach 80.

Feynman test Chop the documents into paragraphs, turn each into a list of numbers, and do the same to the question. Find the paragraphs whose numbers are closest, paste them above the question, and ask. The model is not remembering anything; it is reading what you handed it.
Step 2

Why this matters

The first reason is that this addresses the failure lecture 8 could not. Output control makes a reply well-formed; it cannot make it true. Retrieval does not make it true either, but it does something almost as useful: it makes the answer checkable, because a claim accompanied by the passage it came from can be verified by a person or a program. Step 5 argues that auditability, rather than accuracy, is what RAG actually buys.

The second reason is cost, and it is the direct payoff of lecture 10. A six-chunk retrieval prompt is 3,532 tokens against 48,750 for a ten-step agent doing the same lookup, which is 13.8 times cheaper, with one generation instead of ten and a bounded step count instead of an unbounded one.

The third reason is that the failure modes of dense retrieval are unintuitive if you have come from keyword search. A system that answers a paraphrased question perfectly may fail to find a document by its own reference number, and a user who does not know that will conclude the system is broken at random. Knowing which query types fail is what makes the hybrid design in step 4 obvious rather than clever.

And a fourth reason about where the effort goes. Almost every disappointing RAG system is disappointing at the retrieval step rather than the generation step: the answer was never in the prompt. That is a search problem with fifty years of literature behind it, and lecture 12 measures it properly.

Step 3

The mental model

One pipeline that runs once, one that runs per question, and a prompt where they meet.

Chunk
The unit of retrieval. Not a document, because a document is too big to place in a prompt and too broad to be relevant as a whole; not a sentence, because a sentence usually lacks the context to be understood alone. A few hundred tokens with a small overlap is the working compromise.
Dense retrieval
Search by vector similarity: embed the question, embed the chunks, return the nearest. Matches meaning, so it finds a passage that answers the question in different words. Its failures follow directly from that strength.
Grounding
Instructing the model to answer only from the supplied passages and to say so when they do not contain the answer. It does not compel obedience; it makes disobedience detectable, which is a different and more useful property.

THE SIZE JUDGEMENT SMALL chunks are precisely relevant and often lack the context to be understood, so the model sees a sentence that assumes a definition three paragraphs earlier. LARGE chunks carry their context and dilute the signal, both in the embedding, which averages over more content, and in the prompt, where the relevant line is buried. Structure beats arithmetic: split on headings, clauses or turns where the document has them, and fall back to fixed sizes only where it does not.

Where dense retrieval fails, and why the fix is a different retriever

Dense retrieval matches meaning. Four query types therefore defeat it, and all four are ordinary rather than exotic.

Exact identifiers
A product code, an error number, a policy reference, a person's surname. The embedding of POL-4417 is close to the embedding of POL-4418, because they look alike, and neither is close to the passage that actually defines it.
Negation
"Which policies do not cover contract staff." The embedding of a negated sentence sits near the embedding of its affirmative form, so the retriever returns the passages saying exactly the opposite of what was asked.
Numerical and temporal constraints
"Claims above £50,000 settled after March." Embeddings represent that these are numbers and dates; they do not evaluate the comparison. Filtering on structured metadata is the answer, not a better embedding.
Rare or in-house vocabulary
Terms the embedding model never saw during training receive uninformative representations. This is the case that domain fine-tuning in Part V addresses, and the only one of the four that a better model fixes.
WHY HYBRID RETRIEVAL IS WORTH MORE THAN IT LOOKS suppose dense retrieval succeeds on 80 per cent of queries and lexical BM25 on 70 per cent. Taking both and merging gives a union whose value depends entirely on whether the two fail on the SAME queries. correlation of failures union recall gain over dense 0.0 independent 94.0% +14.0 pp 0.3 mildly correlated 88.5% +8.5 pp 0.6 strongly correlated 83.0% +3.0 pp 1.0 identical failures 80.0% 0.0 pp AND THE EMPIRICAL POINT IS THAT THE FAILURES ARE DISJOINT dense fails on identifiers, rare tokens, negation, numerals lexical fails on paraphrase, synonymy, question-to-statement mismatch those are different queries. So a hybrid system sits near the independent row rather than the identical one, and the fourteen-point gain is roughly what practitioners report. WHICH MAKES THE DESIGN RULE SIMPLE do not choose between semantic and keyword search. Run both, merge the rankings, and let each cover the other's blind spot. The merge itself is usually reciprocal rank fusion, which needs no score calibration between the two systems and is three lines of code.

Grounded generation

The prompt is where the two halves meet, and its instructions are doing more work than they appear to. Three elements matter: the passages must be delimited and identified, the model must be told to use only them, and it must be told what to do when they are insufficient.

You answer questions using ONLY the passages below.
If the passages do not contain the answer, say
"The provided material does not answer this" and stop.
Cite the passage id in square brackets after each claim.

[1] {chunk_1}
[2] {chunk_2}
...
[6] {chunk_6}

Question: {question}

The escape clause in the second line is the part most often omitted and the most valuable. Without it the model has no permitted way to fail, so when the retrieval misses it will answer from its parameters instead, producing exactly the unsourced claim the system was built to avoid, and producing it in the same confident register as a grounded one.

Step 5

Synthesis and what's next

Retrieval-augmented generation indexes a corpus once and consults it per question. Chunking is the first design decision and the arithmetic is undramatic: 512-token chunks with a 64-token overlap turn 100,000 tokens into 224 pieces at 14.7 per cent duplication, and even a billion tokens embeds into under seven gigabytes. The context window, not storage, is what binds: six chunks at a 4,096-token window, which makes recall at six the target. Dense retrieval fails on identifiers, negation, numerical constraints and in-house vocabulary; lexical retrieval fails on paraphrase; and because those are different queries, a hybrid reaches 94 per cent where dense alone reaches 80.

What RAG actually buys It is tempting to say that retrieval makes a model accurate. It does not. A model handed the right passage can still misread it, and a model handed the wrong passage will answer from it confidently. What retrieval changes is that the answer now comes with the evidence it was drawn from, so a claim can be checked, by a person, a reviewer, or an automated test that asks whether the cited passage supports the sentence. An ungrounded answer cannot be checked at all without independently knowing the answer. Auditability is the deliverable; accuracy is a consequence of it only when somebody audits.

And it is dramatically cheaper than the alternative it replaces. A six-chunk retrieval prompt is 3,532 tokens with one generation. An agent doing the same lookup takes 12,500 tokens over five steps or 48,750 over ten, so retrieval is between 3.5 and 13.8 times cheaper, with a step count of one rather than a step count the model chooses. That is lecture 10's closing claim made concrete: if the task is really a lookup, do not put it in a loop.

Lecture 12 asks the question this lecture has deferred at three separate points: how good is the retrieval, actually? It covers cross-encoder reranking, which is how you retrieve widely and still send few; the rank-sensitive measures nDCG and mean average precision, which is how recall at six gets measured properly; and the four axes on which a whole RAG system is evaluated, since a system can retrieve well and answer badly, or answer well from the wrong passage.
