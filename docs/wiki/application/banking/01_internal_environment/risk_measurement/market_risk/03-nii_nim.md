# Net Interest Income (NII), NIM and NII over the Economic Cycle

The banking book of a bank produces two main sources of revenue: **net interest income (NII)** and **non-interest revenue (NIR)**. NIR consists of service-related revenue streams (account fees, bank charges, penalties, etc.). As a rule of thumb, banks in developed countries derive the majority of revenue from NII, while banks in developing countries (such as South Africa) rely more on NIR due to the riskier operating environment.

## Net Interest Income (NII)

NII is the total interest income earned on a bank's loans, investment securities, and money market deposits, less the cost of interest expenses related to the funds used to make those loans and investments.

```math
\text{NII} = \text{Interest income} - \text{Interest expense}
```

- **Interest income** is earned on interest-bearing assets (e.g. retail: mortgage bonds, vehicle asset finance, credit cards; commercial: commercial real estate lending, revolving facilities).
- **Interest expense** is the interest paid on liabilities used to support those assets (e.g. retail: term deposits, savings accounts, lazy balances in cheque accounts; wholesale: NCDs, bonds, repos, securitisations).

### Credit Loss Adjusted NII

Adjusting for [[02-credit_losses|credit losses]] gives a clearer view of revenue generated by the lending business:

```math
\text{Credit loss adjusted NII} = \text{Interest income} - \text{Interest expense} - \text{Impairment charge}
```

where the impairment charge = change in [[ifrs9_standard|IFRS 9]] provisions + bad debts written off for the period.

### Risk-Adjusted NII

A "risk-adjusted" view accounts for the imputed cost of equity capital held against the interest-bearing asset portfolio:

```math
\text{Risk-adjusted NII} = \text{Credit loss adjusted NII} - \text{Cost of capital}
```

These risk-adjusted views demonstrate the actual value added or destroyed by the bank's lending business.

**Example** — R100m vehicle asset finance (VAF) portfolio, customer rate 10%, write-off rate 1%, funded by R10.5m equity at 15% and R89.5m debt at 6%:

| Line | Calculation | Rm |
|---|---|---|
| Interest income | Asset balance × (1 – Write-offs) × Customer rate | 9.90 |
| Interest expense | Debt balance × Cost of debt | (5.37) |
| NII | | 4.53 |
| Impairment charge | Asset balance × Write-offs | (1.00) |
| Credit loss adjusted NII | | 3.53 |
| Cost of capital | Regulatory capital × Imputed cost of capital | (1.58) |
| Risk-adjusted NII | | 1.96 |

## Net Interest Margin (NIM)

The NIM calculates the margin earned on interest-bearing assets, expressed as a percentage (usually annualised):

$$
\text{NIM} = \frac{\text{NII}}{\text{Average interest-earning assets}}
$$

The asset rate can be decomposed as:

```math
\text{Asset rate} = (\text{Base rate} + \text{Liquidity risk premium}) + \text{Credit risk premium} + \text{Profit margin}
```

The base rate plus liquidity risk premium represents the bank's cost of funding; thus NIM implicitly measures the gross profit margin (before [[02-credit_losses|credit losses]]) earned on assets.

**Average interest-earning assets** excludes non-interest-bearing assets (e.g. certain central bank deposits) and is typically calculated as the average of opening and closing balances.

### Usefulness of NIM

- Tracks profitability of the bank's lending and deposit-taking business in totality.
- Can be adjusted to a risk-adjusted view.
- NIM is a profitability indicator predicting long-run bank performance.
- NIM values are generally comparable for banks within the same jurisdiction but not across jurisdictions.

### Limitations of NIM

- Cannot split out the margin attributable to the deposit-taking vs lending business separately.
- The NIM per asset type cannot be objectively isolated (e.g. home loans vs VAF).
- Banks cannot be objectively compared because differences in balance sheet composition (asset mix, funding mix) drive different NIMs.

**Margin analysis** — a more granular view leveraging the bank's Funds Transfer Pricing (FTP) process, showing the contribution to NII from each business unit as the difference between the customer rate and the transfer pricing rate.

### NIM Management

Banks track [[03-nii_nim|NIM]] by "front book" (new business) and "back book" (existing book) to identify trends, since margins may erode on the front book during competitive growth phases.

Several forces compress [[03-nii_nim|NIM]] over time: competitive pressure reduces loan spreads during economic expansions; [[02-credit_losses|credit losses]] increase during recessions; and rate mismatches between fixed-rate loans and variable-rate deposits create margin exposure when interest rates move. [[03-nii_nim|NIM]] management therefore requires active product design and repricing capability.

Banks must also match the [[03-nii_nim|NIM]] concept to the funding structure. For corporate and investment banking, the **term liquidity premium (TLP)** methodology is essential — treasury charges lending products the true cost of term-matched funding, discouraging cheap short-term funding of long-term assets. For retail banking, the same concept applies but TLP rates must reflect the actual retail deposit mix, not just the wholesale curve, to avoid mispricing loans relative to competitors.

## Net Interest Spread (NIS)

The net interest spread is the difference between the average interest rate on all of the bank's interest-earning assets and the average interest rate on all of its sources of funds. Unlike NIM, NIS does **not** consider the size of the interest-earning asset base. NIM can change when the asset base expands or contracts even if the spread is unchanged.

## NII over the Economic Cycle

NII varies over the economic cycle due to both idiosyncratic factors (bank's origination, pricing and funding strategies) and systemic factors (the macroeconomic environment).

### Economic Growth Phase

During growth, inflation is expected to rise, and the Reserve Bank increases the repo rate. Effects on NII:

- Increased consumer spending → increased credit origination → larger credit portfolio → higher absolute NII.
- Growing consumer real income expands the addressable market and increases credit demand.
- Higher retained earnings → increased CET1 capital supply.

**Floating rate assets:** For banks mainly funded with floating rate instruments, an increase in rates has a minimal net effect on NII from floating rate assets, apart from repricing timing differences. Banks typically exhibit **asymmetric repricing behaviour** — they are slow to reprice deposit rates upward when rates increase, so NII increases over the short term before deposit rates adjust. This behaviour is subject to the bank's pricing strategy and competitive pressures.

**Fixed rate assets:** Assuming successful hedging of fixed rate exposure, NII from the fixed book remains relatively stable as rates increase (barring basis mismatch between the funding base rate and the floating leg of the hedge). The front book is priced at market rates.

**Credit risk:** [[02-credit_losses|Credit losses]] are subdued in growth, increasing risk-adjusted NII.

### Economic Downturn Phase

During a downturn, the Reserve Bank reduces the repo rate (subject to exchange rate stability considerations). Effects on NII:

- Decreased consumer spending → reduced origination → shrinking credit portfolio → lower absolute NII.
- Reduced real income and higher unemployment reduce the target market and credit demand.

**Floating rate assets:** A decrease in rates has a minimal net effect on floating rate NII, but banks typically **reprice deposit rates down faster** in a falling rate environment than they raise them in a rising environment, so NII decreases slightly over the short term (**margin compression**).

**Fixed rate assets:** Assumed immunised to small rate changes; front book priced at prevailing market rates.

**Credit risk:** [[02-credit_losses|Credit losses]] are expected to increase, reducing risk-adjusted NII.

**Funding mix:** In a falling rate environment, depositor funding may increase as customers become more risk-averse. Since retail deposit funding is generally cheaper than wholesale funding, this improves NII.

## NIM Analytics: Finance vs Treasury

Banks can be prone to poor NIM analytics because finance and treasury departments approach NIM differently.

**Finance focus** — focuses on headline NIM per business line based on observed trends and contractual balance sheet run-off. May not highlight changes in maturity, repricing, or rate sensitivity. Reporting involves business lines submitting high-level product views.

**Treasury focus** — focuses on risk limits, liquidity, and capital buffers. The group treasury considers the mismatch between assets and liabilities and their repricing behaviour, measuring risk via VaR and PV01. Analysis is conducted on both contractual and behavioural bases (modelling customer prepayment and withdrawal tendencies). Re-pricing gaps also ignore basis risks (e.g. assets at prime vs liabilities at JIBAR 3-months), so more sophisticated EAR and EVE measures are used.

South African banks have invested in sophisticated interest rate modelling and balance sheet management systems that can model [[04-irrbb_measurement|IRRBB]] on both a contractual and behavioural basis, including scenario modelling capabilities for what-if analysis.
