# Underwritting

Extending credit is one of the core business activities of a bank. This activity incurs credit risk, which should be measured and managed appropriately. There are many considerations when measuring credit risk, such as the factors to assess, prevailing regulation and guidance, modelling techniques, and product characteristics.

## Books

### Front Book

Refers to newly originated or recently booked exposures — accounts, loans, or facilities that have been approved under current credit policies and pricing standards.

Used to assess current underwriting quality and the forward-looking risk profile of new lending. The performance of the front book shows how recent strategy and model changes are working.

Front book exposures are important for ongoing monitoring and model application — ensuring that new lending is consistent with current [[02-risk_appetite|risk appetite]] and that models remain predictive under current origination practices.

### Back Book

Refers to existing exposures that were originated under past policies, before current standards or models were applied.

Used to assess legacy risk still on the balance sheet. The back book often reflects older vintages with potentially different risk characteristics, pricing, or credit criteria.

Back book exposures are key for model validation, backtesting, and calibration — they provide historical performance evidence and show how well models would have predicted defaults or losses over time.

## Qualitative Factors

Part of the decision to lend should be based on not just a borrower’s ability to pay, but also their sense of obligation to pay (willingness). Qualitative factors allow for banks to consider factors that cannot be easily measured quantitatively or numerically. Qualitative factors are less tangible and harder to quantify, but crucial.

This system is particularly susceptible to subjectivity and bias, especially when personal success and incentives of bankers are linked to those who “land the best deal”. However, this is not to say that qualitative factors should not be considered at all.

Wholesale borrowers generally have more readily available data that can be more easily verified. For example, listed companies are required by regulatory bodies to disclose certain information to shareholders and the public, and valuable qualitative information such as strategy is made available. Primarily, management and business characteristics and structure are broad areas of “soft factors” which should be taken into account when considering whether credit should be granted. Essentially, factors that are relevant in assessing credit risk but cannot be captured quantitatively or lack viable data sources can be assessed qualitatively.

For many years, retail credit decisions were made by local bank relationship managers based on qualitative factors. Models used to analyse wholesale loans could not be applied to small, unrated borrowers. Data was expensive to collect and verify, with the cost amplified given the small potential profit. Sound retail credit decision frameworks were developed by smaller banks with dedicated resources, local knowledge, and relationships – when it was necessary to collect data manually and thus frameworks needed to be thorough. However, data collection practices have improved and have allowed for more precise frameworks to be developed by larger banks, although verification of this data is often still a cause for concern.

However, strictly subjective retail credit decisions allow for inconsistency, and sometimes bias occurs on the basis of race, religion, nationality, gender, or marital status. Perceptions as to the most favourable types of employment could also lead to less rational decisions. This leads to many banks following a best practice route of introducing as much objectivity into assessing qualitative factors as possible. Banks may list qualitative factors that may be considered, and the levels to which these factors can be considered, in the form of lending criteria or scorecards. This allows qualitative factors to be considered on a larger scale, and more objectively.

Specialised lending possesses unique loss distribution and risk characteristics. Given the source of repayment, the exposures exhibit greater risk volatility, with both high default rates and high loss rates in times of distress. Historical data is often not as readily available or comparable and relevant to the current specialised financing exposures being assessed. Qualitative factors for specialised lending include financial strength and flexibility, collateral control, project phase, and marketability; these are also unique to this type of lending.

### 5 C’s of Credit

The 5 Cs of Credit is a framework used by financial institutions and other non-bank lenders to evaluate the creditworthiness of a borrower, as well as the strength of an overall borrowing request.

The 5 Cs are Character, Capacity, Capital, Collateral, and Conditions. The 5 Cs must be taken collectively; no single C in isolation can provide sufficient insight to approve or decline a transaction. Strength in one C can help to offset weakness in another.

#### Character (Will they pay)

In credit terms, this is the "willingness" to pay. Character tends to be a very comprehensive, though sometimes subjective, aspect of the evaluation of creditworthiness. The premise is that a borrower’s historical track record of managing credit and making payments should serve as a proxy for future creditworthiness, too.

For individual borrowers, the assessment seeks to assess what kind of “person” they are by understanding their credit history, often using a credit score (such as FICO).

A corporate borrower is a little more complicated, particularly if it’s a private company that’s new to a lending institution. Loan officers will want to try and understand the character of the business by unpacking the management’s (and ownership’s) reputation and credibility.

- Experience & Track Record: Assessing technical skills, organizational ability, and how leadership performed during past "periods of stress."
- Strategic Vision: Checking the consistency of management’s public statements against their actual progress and actions.
- Succession Planning: Vital for SMEs; ensuring the business doesn't collapse if a key founder or CEO leaves.
- Corporate Governance: Evaluating the "checks and balances," such as the independence of the board, internal audit functions, and adherence to codes (e.g., King IV, UK Corporate Governance Code, or Sarbanes-Oxley).
- Ownership Structure: Analyzing the stability of owners and their "willingness and capacity" to provide support during a crisis. The larger a business’s operations and the larger its lending requirement from banks, the more corporate a company’s structure should be.
- Remuneration & Incentives: Monitoring stock options and insider selling to see if management's interests align with the business's health.
- Transparency: The openness of management and their willingness to disclose problems promptly.
- Reporting Integrity: The "timeliness and thoroughness" of audited financial statement

However, this is not a substitute for [[05-aml_kyc|KYC]] procedures.

#### Capacity (Can they pay during good times)

Capacity really speaks to a borrower’s ability to service debt obligations into the future. A borrower’s capacity, whether personal or corporate, is typically measured using a variety of financial ratios like total debt service (TDS) or debt service coverage (DSC).

- [[01-business_model|Business Model]] & Sector: Understanding how the company actually makes money and the specific risks inherent to its industry (e.g., Mining vs. Airlines).
- Competitive Advantage (Franchise Value): Evaluating "moats" like brand strength, innovation, efficiency, and barriers to entry for new competitors.
- Market Share: Assessing the company’s dominance and its ability to withstand competitive pressure.
- Viability & Sustainability: Determining if the business is built for the long term or if it is vulnerable to being disrupted.

#### Capital (Can they pay during bad times)

Capital can be thought of as a borrower’s overall financial strength, but in particular, what other unencumbered assets (or sources of cash) may be available to support debt repayment if cash flows were to dry up?

For a personal borrower, are there marketable securities or real estate assets that could be sold to free up cash in the event the borrower needed it?

For business and commercial borrowers, an important thing to understand is the company’s capital structure – meaning what proportion of funding comes from debt vs. equity. If a company is generally under-leveraged, then a lender is likely more willing to extend credit than if that company were already over-leveraged.

#### Collateral

Collateral is when an asset is pledged to a lender as security against credit exposure. Understanding what (if any) collateral is available, particularly for senior secured lenders, is absolutely essential.

When structuring credit, collateral security plays a really important role in mitigating credit risk. After all, if a borrower triggered an event of default and the lender were required to take enforcement action against their security, the quality of the collateral would dictate the likelihood of full repayment.

The nature, condition, and overall desirability of an asset will influence the loan-to-value (LTV) that a lender is willing to extend, as well as the terms under which the loan will be structured.

#### Conditions

Conditions are a broad umbrella, but an important one. They, at least in part, refer to the purpose of the credit that’s being requested. It also includes forces in the external environment (such as macroeconomic factors) as well as industry-specific risks and opportunities.

- Macroeconomics: The state of the economy in the specific regions where the company operates.
- Industry Volatility: Identifying if the sector is prone to boom-and-bust cycles.
- Buyer Diversification: Assessing risk concentration—if a business relies on only one or two major customers, the credit risk increases significantly.

### CAMPARI Framework

While the 5 C’s of Credit is the "Old Guard" framework, modern banking and top-tier consulting firms (like McKinsey or BCG) often use more granular models to drill into qualitative risk. The CAMPARI framework is the most popular alternative to the 5 C’s, especially in UK and European banking. It is often preferred because it includes the profitability of the deal for the bank, not just the risk of the borrower.

- Character: Integrity, reputation, and honesty of the management.
- Ability: The borrower's competence and experience in their specific field.
- Margin: The interest rate and fees; does the return justify the risk?
- Purpose: What is the money being used for? (Working capital vs. speculative expansion).
- Amount: Is the request sufficient, or is it so small the business will still struggle?
- Repayment: The primary source of cash (e.g., trading profits) to pay the loan back.
- Insurance (or Security): The "Plan B" if the business fails (collateral or guarantees).

## Quantitative Factors (Ratings)

Ratings are intended to capture quantitative information to be considered in line with qualitative factors. However, in many instances, ratings do take qualitative factors into account when there is little quantitative information. The assessment of these qualitative factors will tend to be as quantitative as possible, for example, the use of qualitative scorecards or overlays.

### Internal Ratings

Internal ratings are produced by banks to rate the credit risk of their internal clients, both prospective or current clients. These can be produced using internal or external models.

Whereas external [[04-ratings_agencies|ratings agencies]] were founded in the nineteenth century (mostly to analyse US railroads), bank internal ratings only took off in the 1990s with [[basel_2|Basel II]].

### External Ratings

External ratings are produced by credit rating agencies using in-house models, that can provide an arguably unbiased view of an entity that the bank has no private information on.

External ratings can be seen as having resulted from a “black box” methodology, where banks do not have clear insight into the manner in which ratings were assigned. Many rating agencies do, however, release detailed reports on their methodologies – these do not reveal proprietary information but do provide insight into assumptions, underlying data, and performance measurements.

Some are of the opinion that external ratings are biased to the upside given that borrowerspay for their ratings. Credit rating agencies have increased their disclosure over the years, butit is still important to consider the benefits and shortfalls of using external ratings.
