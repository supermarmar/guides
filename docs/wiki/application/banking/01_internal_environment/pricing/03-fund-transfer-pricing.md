# <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">Fund Transfer Pricing</mark>

Fund Transfer Pricing (FTP) is the internal market through which a bank's treasury function acts as a central counterparty to every business line, buying deposits at an internal bid and selling funding at an internal offer along a tenor-specific curve. Its purpose is not accounting but economic: to strip interest-rate and liquidity risk out of commercial P&L, concentrate those risks in a function that can hedge them, and leave business units accountable only for what they actually control — origination, credit, service and operations. Without FTP, deposit franchises look unprofitable, long-dated lenders look like geniuses, and treasury is a residual P&L of whatever the macro environment delivered. The 2007–08 crisis demonstrated that this is not a harmless book-keeping choice: banks which priced funding but not liquidity systematically over-accumulated illiquid assets, under-rewarded stable deposit gatherers, and wrote off-balance-sheet commitments whose true cost only appeared when wholesale markets closed. Every post-crisis supervisory framework — BCBS 144 (2008), CEBS/EBA (2010 and 2022), US SR 16-3 (2016), PRA SS24/15 — has since rested on the same principle: that **liquidity costs, benefits and risks must be incorporated into internal pricing, performance measurement, and new-product approval for all significant on- and off-balance-sheet activities**. The March 2023 failures of Silicon Valley Bank, Signature and First Republic added a new chapter: that the behavioural assumptions embedded in FTP — especially for non-maturing deposits — must hold up in a digital, socially networked, rising-rate world, not only the world of 2010–2021 regression samples.

This report builds FTP from first principles: why it exists, how its curves and spreads are constructed, how its hardest conceptual challenge — behavioural modelling — is handled, what risks it actually transfers, and where its current controversies lie.

## Purpose

A bank's net interest margin is the joint output of three distinct activities: credit origination by lending units, deposit gathering by liability units, and maturity/liquidity transformation performed centrally by treasury/ALM. Without an allocation mechanism, the entire spread defaults by accident to whichever unit happens to own the customer. A 10-year fixed-rate mortgage funded implicitly from a pool of overnight retail balances looks spectacularly profitable — until rates move or wholesale markets shut. A sticky 5-year-behavioural-life current account earning the overnight policy rate looks like dead weight. FTP attaches an **internal transfer rate** to every balance-sheet item: assets are charged a funding cost, liabilities are credited a funding benefit, and the residual transformation margin — whatever treasury earns or loses by absorbing the maturity, liquidity and basis mismatches — is captured where hedging decisions are actually made.

This surgical decomposition is the prerequisite for every modern risk-adjusted performance metric. RAROC, EVA, economic-capital-based capital allocation and variable compensation formulae all require a business-unit NII that has been stripped of funding and rate mismatch — a number only producible through FTP. It also underpins product pricing (the "wholesale input cost" on top of which credit spread, capital charges, opex and target margin are stacked), new-product approval (a product that cannot bear its fully-loaded FTP charge should not be approved), and the behavioural incentive structure that flows from all of the above.

**One pedagogical point deserves bold emphasis: FTP is a management-information construct, not accounting.** Every FTP charge to a business line is matched by an equal-and-opposite credit to treasury; internal transfers net to zero at the group level and never touch consolidated IFRS or GAAP net interest income. Pillar 3 disclosures, IFRS 9 EIR, hedge accounting and external auditor attestations interact with treasury's *external* hedges, not with the internal transfer prices. Confusion between the two is perhaps the most common pedagogical pitfall among readers coming from a financial-reporting background.

## History

FTP emerged in US money-centre banks (Citicorp, Bank of America, Bankers Trust) during the 1970s–80s interest-rate volatility of the Volcker disinflation and the S&L crisis, originally as a crude **single-pool method** applying one weighted-average cost of funds to all assets and liabilities. As ALM matured through the 1990s and 2000s, practice moved through **multi-pool methods** (maturity buckets) toward **matched-maturity marginal cost of funding (MMMCOF)**, pricing off wholesale swap/LIBOR curves. But **liquidity was treated as essentially free**: in June 2005, UK major-bank 5-year senior unsecured funding traded only around 0.5 basis points over the risk-free rate (Grant 2011). Contingent liquidity — undrawn commitments, SPV backstops, conduit support — was rarely charged at all.

The Senior Supervisors Group diagnostic (October 2009) put the failure bluntly: banks with weak or absent FTP "would not have carried the significant levels of illiquid assets, and the significant risks that were held off-balance sheet, that ultimately led to sizeable losses." Post-Lehman, bank-funding spreads jumped by hundreds of basis points and never returned to pre-crisis compressions. BCBS 144 (September 2008), the CEBS Liquidity Cost Benefit Allocation Guidelines (October 2010), the BIS FSI's Grant paper (December 2011), and US SR 16-3 (March 2016) institutionalised a new orthodoxy: FTP must price **term liquidity** and **contingent liquidity** explicitly, not merely interest-rate risk.

The March 2023 regional-bank episode added a second layer of lessons. SVB held 94% uninsured deposits concentrated in a VC/tech network; roughly $42 billion — about a quarter of liabilities — left in a single day via digital channels, with around $100 billion more queued. Its ALCO had, in May 2022, extended the deposit curtailment assumption from 5.5 years to 12 years "without any valid justification" (per the subsequent FDIC suit), producing a large IRRBB adjustment that masked true economic exposure. Signature and First Republic followed through confidence contagion; Credit Suisse's 100% write-down of ~CHF 16 billion of AT1 notes re-priced the cost of loss-absorbing capital across the industry. The supervisory conclusion was that **behavioural assumptions estimated on a decade of stable low-rate data systematically over-state deposit stickiness in a rising-rate, digitally-run, uninsured-concentrated world**, and that FTP frameworks must differentiate deposit cohorts and align with stress-test assumptions rather than mark their own homework.

## Regulation

Notwithstanding jurisdictional differences in legal form, regulators converge on a strikingly consistent principles-based framework.

**BCBS 144, "Principles for Sound Liquidity Risk Management and Supervision" (September 2008)** is the foundational global text. Its **Principle 4** is the FTP anchor: "A bank should incorporate liquidity costs, benefits and risks in the internal pricing, performance measurement and new product approval process for all significant business activities (both on- and off-balance sheet), thereby aligning the risk-taking incentives of individual business lines with the liquidity risk exposures their activities create for the bank as a whole." Supporting paragraphs require pricing to reflect costs associated with the length of time funds are committed and with contingent liquidity risk — undrawn commitments, collateral calls, deposit run-off. BCBS 238 (LCR, 2013) and the NSFR standard (2014) are not FTP documents, but establish the quantitative liquidity constraints whose buffer costs must be allocated *through* FTP.

**US SR 16-3 / OCC Bulletin 2016-7 / FDIC FIL-12-2016 (1 March 2016)** is the most operationally detailed regulatory text. Applicable to firms with ≥$250 billion in consolidated assets, it sets out four explicit FTP principles: (1) allocation of both funding-risk and contingent-liquidity costs/benefits to the business lines creating them; (2) a consistent, transparent, granular framework with transaction-inception measurement; (3) robust governance with senior management oversight, a quarterly FTP report, and SR 11-7 model validation; (4) integration with product pricing, performance metrics and new-product approval. Its attachment explicitly endorses **matched-maturity marginal cost of funding** for non-trading books and weighted-average cost of debt / marginal cost for trading exposures.

**European framework.** The **CEBS 2010 Guidelines on Liquidity Cost Benefit Allocation** set five high-level requirements — comprehensive scope, adequate governance, integration with product pricing, behavioural calibration, and linkage to the risk appetite framework and ICAAP/ILAAP. These were embedded into law via CRD IV Article 86, requiring "adequate allocation mechanisms of liquidity cost, benefits and risks." The **EBA Guidelines on IRRBB and CSRBB (EBA/GL/2022/14, effective 30 June 2023 for IRRBB and 31 December 2023 for CSRBB)** bind FTP more tightly to measured risk: a 5-year cap on the behavioural weighted-average repricing maturity of retail and non-financial NMDs in the internal model (not only the standardised approach), a 5% Tier 1 threshold for the NII Supervisory Outlier Test, and a 15% Tier 1 threshold for the EVE SOT. The EBA requires at least 10 years of historical data behind pass-through assumptions.

**UK PRA SS24/15** expects firms to fully incorporate liquidity and funding costs, benefits and risks into product pricing, performance measurement, incentives and new-product/transaction approval, covering both stressed and business-as-usual costs, across all significant business lines on- and off-balance-sheet. The Bank of England's semi-official pedagogical treatment, Cadamagnani, Harimohan and Tangri's "A bank within a bank" (Quarterly Bulletin 2015 Q2), remains the clearest central-bank exposition of how treasury operates as an internal intermediary. OSFI (Canada) Principle 10 and APRA APS 210 mirror BCBS 144 Principle 4. The FSI's Grant paper (2011) — based on a survey of 38 banks across 9 countries — is the definitive international practitioner benchmark.

The **convergence** is near-complete: comprehensive scope; three-component cost stack (interest-rate, term liquidity, contingent liquidity); ALCO/senior-management ownership; transparency to business lines; integration with pricing, performance and NPA; regular review; stress-test-consistent behavioural assumptions; independent validation; and proportionality.

## Methodologies

Four methodological families span the history of FTP, and the modern consensus has unambiguously selected one.

### Single-pool Method

The **single-pool method** — one weighted-average cost of funds applied to everything — is operationally simple but pathologically cross-subsidising. A 1-year and a 5-year loan receive the same charge; long-dated assets are systematically under-priced (Grant's worked examples put the under-charge on a 5-year bullet at 8–32 bps in a normal post-crisis curve) and short-dated deposits systematically over-rewarded. The mechanism **mechanically encourages maturity transformation** — precisely the pathology BCBS 144 was written to curtail. 

### Multi-pool Method

**Multi-pool methods** (maturity buckets, each bucketed average) address first-order term structure but retain intra-bucket smearing and remain historical rather than marginal.

### Matched-maturity Marginal Cost of Funds (MMMCF)

**Matched-maturity marginal cost of funds** — each transaction priced at the point on a curve corresponding to its contractual or behavioural maturity and repricing profile — is the approach explicitly endorsed as best practice by BCBS 144, SR 16-3, CEBS 2010, the PRA's cross-firm work, and Grant (2011). Its conceptual payoff is the **certainty-equivalent margin**: by locking in FTP at origination, each business's P&L becomes insensitive to subsequent curve and spread moves on the existing book. Treasury absorbs the mismatch and hedges it externally, netting across business lines. This is the "bank within a bank" formulation — treasury is the internal intermediary that borrows from deposit units at the FTP bid and lends to asset units at the FTP offer.

The **average-versus-marginal** debate sits one level deeper. For **new business**, marginal cost is theoretically correct: the decision is whether the incremental loan earns a return above the incremental funding cost. Average cost implicitly cross-subsidises new origination with legacy low-cost funding, producing adverse selection — business lines arbitrage treasury. A Modigliani-Miller-style argument reinforces the point: the composition of the funding stack is irrelevant; what matters is the opportunity cost of the marginal unit. For **back-book performance attribution**, however, average cost retains legitimacy because the funding has been contractually raised and is a sunk economic fact. Dermine (INSEAD 2011–2016) develops the rigorous microeconomic case for marginal pricing plus explicit adjustments for buffer costs, asset-specific riskiness, and the bank's own credit spread.

## Curve Choice

The curve choice determines what FTP actually measures. Four families have dominated.

### Interbank Curves

**Interbank / IBOR curves** (LIBOR, EURIBOR, historical swap curves) dominated pre-2008: observable, liquid, hedgeable. But they embed panel-bank credit risk — so they are not a clean "risk-free" base — and they proved manipulable (the 2012 LIBOR scandal) and unreliable under stress (LIBOR barely moved during March 2020 while secured overnight rates collapsed, a diagnostic failure of the benchmark). IBORs have now been discontinued or transformed in all major currencies; USD LIBOR ceased on 30 June 2023.

### Risk-free Rates

**Risk-free rates** (SOFR, SONIA, €STR, TONA, SARON) are the post-reform consensus: overnight, transaction-based, nearly risk-free (SOFR alone transacts ~$1 trillion daily in Treasury repo). Their appeal is that they cleanly isolate the time-value-of-money / interest-rate-risk component and behave robustly in stress. Their structural limitation is that, by construction, **RFRs do not move with bank funding costs in stress** — so pure RFR pricing would leave long-tenor RFR-indexed assets funded by credit-sensitive liabilities. The New York Fed's analysis of revolving-credit transition quantified this: pure SOFR pricing mechanically raises contingent funding risk by roughly 15 bps and compresses commitments by about 5%. An **explicit term liquidity premium add-on is therefore not optional but structural**.

### Internal Curve

**The bank's own funding curve** — derived from secondary bond spreads, primary issuance, or CDS — is theoretically the most coherent representation of the bank's true marginal cost and thus the economically correct hurdle. Its weaknesses are procyclicality (the curve widens sharply in stress, curtailing origination when the macro-environment may most need credit), observability gaps at some tenors especially for smaller banks, and circularity when CDS markets are thin.

### Synthetic Curves

**Blended / synthetic curves** — tenor-by-tenor weighted averages of the bank's actual funding mix (retail deposits, secured wholesale, senior unsecured, covered bonds, FHLB advances) — are conceptually optimal because they reflect how the bank *actually* raises a marginal unit. The PRA's cross-firm review documents a trend toward these blended curves. The challenges are endogeneity (the marginal cost of deposits is partly a function of the FTP output itself), ambiguity in the weighting scheme (flow, target-mix, or stock?), and opacity to business lines.

## Components

The FTP curve is built as:

$$r_{\text{FTP}}(T) = r_{\text{swap}}(T) + \text{LP}(T) + \text{CC}(T) + \text{Optionality}(T)$$

The **base rate** is the risk-free rate at the matching tenor — typically the SOFR swap curve (US) or SONIA swap curve (UK) post-LIBOR transition. Since SOFR is a secured rate, a compensating **credit/liquidity spread** must be added to approximate the bank's actual unsecured funding cost. The **liquidity premium (LP)** — also called the term liquidity premium (TLP) — represents the spread above the risk-free rate that the bank pays for term wholesale funding, derived by stripping the bank's own senior unsecured issuances into their floating-rate equivalent and observing the spread over the reference rate. Pre-2007, bank 5-year unsecured spreads were negligible; post-crisis they widened to **100+ bps** and have remained structurally elevated. The **contingency cost (CC)** allocates the carry cost of LCR/NSFR-compliant HQLA buffers to the instruments creating the liquidity requirement.

### Base rate

The base is the tenor-matched RFR — SOFR, €STR, SONIA, TONA, SARON — constructed by bootstrapping an OIS zero curve from overnight-index swaps, RFR futures (with convexity adjustment) and basis swaps. Post-October 2020, LCH and CME use SOFR as the PAI discount rate for cleared USD derivatives, making SOFR the single discounting curve for collateralised positions. The multi-curve world requires banks to maintain at least the RFR, any legacy IBOR in runoff, and the OIS, plus pairwise bases.

**Negative rates** force a choice: pass-through fully (analytically clean, CEBS-preferred), apply a zero floor (creating a basis that treasury absorbs), or tier by customer segment. EBA IRRBB requires post-shock negative scenarios to be tested, so any floor must be disclosed and its residual cost retained centrally.

### Term liquidity premium

The TLP is the spread over the RFR compensating treasury for committing funds for a term. Economically it bundles three frictions: the yield premium wholesale investors demand to lend long to *this* bank net of the credit component, the cost of maturity-transformation insurance against interbank freezes, and the franchise cost of term funding. Pre-2007 the TLP was negligible; post-crisis it is structurally 50–100 bps or more at the 5-year point for major banks.

Five derivation methods compete:

- The **secondary-market funding-spread approach** uses the observed spread of the bank's own senior unsecured bonds over RFR-OIS at each tenor. It is granular and market-observable, but contaminated by bond-specific liquidity, coupon effects and infrequent issuance at some tenors.
- The **primary-issuance approach** uses recent new-issue spreads. It represents actual marginal cost — a cleaner economic signal — but is episodic and concentrated at tenors the bank chose to issue at.
- The **CDS-based approach** subtracts the CDS spread (pure credit) from the bond spread to isolate the liquidity component: TLP_liquidity ≈ bond spread − CDS. The CDS-bond basis is persistently non-zero (BIS WP 631, 2017) and CDS markets are often thin (the ESRB 2025 report notes that Italian sovereign single-name CDS averages only $571 million daily volume across 13 trades), so the decomposition is contaminated by arbitrage limits and CDS-market liquidity premia themselves.
- **Peer-average / industry-benchmark** approaches are used by regional banks without traded debt — smoothed and defensible, but prone to systematically under-pricing TLP at outlier institutions.
- The **internal replicating / marginal-cost approach** (MMMCOF) defines the actual funding mix treasury would realistically raise per tenor (wholesale senior unsecured, covered bonds, FHLB advances, retail term deposits) and builds a weighted-average marginal cost. Some banks add a "gearing factor" keyed off the loan-to-deposit ratio: banks with excess deposits use less than 1×, banks hungry for liquidity use more — a deliberate deviation from pure FTP to steer balance sheet direction.

The TLP curve is typically **upward sloping and steeper than the OIS curve**; charging a flat rate reproduces the pre-crisis cross-subsidy.

The **credit-liquidity decomposition** remains irreducibly imperfect. Whether a bond spread is "credit" or "liquidity" matters because only the liquidity portion is conceptually allocable via FTP — the credit portion reflects the bank's own default risk, a capital/structural issue. Schwarz (2014), Filipović–Trolle (2013) and Christensen et al. (2014) impose term-structure restrictions to identify liquidity distinctly from residual credit. Most banks finesse this by taking the full observed spread as the TLP input, accepting that some credit leakage exists, and disclosing the choice.

### Contingent liquidity charge

SR 16-3 elevates contingent liquidity to co-equal status with funding risk. Even when no funds are actually out the door — on undrawn commitments, committed liquidity lines, standby LCs, derivative collateral triggers, non-contractual deposit withdrawals, secured-funding haircut widening — the bank must pre-fund an HQLA buffer whose carry (HQLA yield minus marginal funding cost) is negative. Four calibration methods coexist: expected-drawdown × funding cost; stress-drawdown × stress funding cost; LCR-runoff-based (applying Basel LCR outflow rates — 10% non-operational corporate lines, 30% retail committed lines, 100% interbank, 5–40% unstable deposits — to the facility and charging the carrying cost of the HQLA offset); and trading-book haircut-widening. Moody's 2024 survey identifies the LCR as the most binding liquidity constraint at surveyed banks, making this overlay often larger than the term-liquidity component itself.

### Optionality charges

Prepayment options on fixed-rate mortgages are economically short calls on rates, producing negative convexity. The Schwartz–Torous (1989) and Richard–Roll (1989) OAS framework prices the option via Monte Carlo under a multi-factor short-rate model (Hull-White, LMM) combined with a dynamic CPR function of refi incentive, burnout, seasonality and demographics. The market-implied option cost is cleanly expressed as **Z-spread minus OAS**. In US conforming mortgages (no-penalty refinance), the option is worth 20–60 bps of annualised FTP spread in normal-volatility regimes, sometimes reaching 50–150 bps. In break-fee jurisdictions (Canada's IRD formula, European statutory caps, German Bauspar lock-ins), the net option value is materially smaller. Caps, floors and collars are valued via Black-76 on caplets/floorlets; convertibility and product-switch options via Bermudan-swaption techniques adjusted for non-rational retail exercise. After charging, option risk transfers to treasury, which hedges externally — centralising, as SR 16-3 puts it, to "take advantage of natural offsets, centralised hedging activities, and a broader view of the firm."

### Basis risk

Wherever the asset's index does not match treasury's actual funding index, basis charges arise: index basis (Prime vs SOFR), tenor basis (1M vs 3M SOFR; 3M vs 6M LIBOR during transition) and currency basis (EUR-funded USD asset via CCBS). Tenor- and cross-currency-basis swaps provide market quotes where the bank retains the basis centrally. SR 16-3 specifies that for a 5-year loan resetting quarterly, the interest-rate component is priced at 3M while the liquidity component is priced at 5Y — a dual-tenor principle many mid-tier banks implement incorrectly.

### Credit spread: the exclusion by design

The borrower's credit spread is **not** in FTP. The customer rate stack is:

> Customer rate = FTP (RFR + TLP + contingent liquidity + optionality + basis + regulatory) + expected loss + cost-of-capital charge + operating cost + commercial margin.

The rationales for excluding credit are structural: clean attribution between treasury (ALM) and business (origination and credit); preventing treasury from taking credit risk; placing credit judgment where the information resides; and aligning with the Basel IRB framework in which EL and RWA attach to the originating business. **Dermine's dissent** (INSEAD 2012, 2013) is the most rigorous theoretical challenge: in a leveraged bank with non-trivial default probability, the marginal cost of debt to fund a *risky* asset is higher than the cost to fund a *safe* asset, because asset risk feeds back into the bank's own credit spread. A fully coherent FTP would adjust for asset-specific credit riskiness. Almost no bank implements this because it violates the clean-attribution principle; the compromise is funding-value-adjustment (FVA) overlays for derivatives and IFRS 9 / CECL expected-loss charges at the business level. Dermine's critique remains the clearest articulation that the orthodox rule is an approximation which breaks down for highly leveraged or high-PD banks.

### Equity and capital

Equity is the bank's paradox: zero cash cost but highest opportunity cost (Ke of 8–12%). If equity were ignored in FTP, deposit gatherers would appear unprofitable and asset generators would receive an implicit subsidy. The industry solution is the **Equity Investment Portfolio (EIP) / replicating portfolio**: equity is notionally invested in a rolling bond ladder (typically 5 years), whose yield becomes the equity benefit credited to business lines in proportion to allocated capital (RWA, economic capital, or leverage exposure). A 5-year ladder produces a roughly 2.5-year duration, smoothing NII contribution when rates move and matching the long-dated nature of shareholders' economic claim. Two variants — Zanders' **marginal investment strategy** (new tranches at current long rates) and **portfolio investment strategy** (full ladder rebalanced to target) — trade margin volatility against reactivity; most banks use the portfolio variant.

The **duration-of-equity decision** is a strategic ALCO choice: a short ladder minimises EVE risk but yields a low, volatile income; a long ladder stabilises NII but exposes EVE. BCBS 368 explicitly flags the trade-off; EBA IRRBB permits modelled equity duration up to 5 years, aligning with the NMD cap. An alternative is the hurdle-rate approach — charging each business at Ke × allocated equity outside FTP — which is cleaner for pure RAROC but neglects the duration benefit of equity on NIM.

### Regulatory cost allocations

LCR carry (HQLA yield minus marginal funding cost) is allocated to products generating outflows — unstable retail deposits at 10%, non-operational corporate at 25–40%, undrawn corporate lines at 10%, interbank at 100% — with the best frameworks also crediting inflow-generating products for symmetry. NSFR surcharges are charged to assets with high RSF factors (unencumbered loans >1yr at 85%, illiquid/encumbered at 100%) at the spread between 1yr+ stable funding and short-term funding; this binds most heavily on wholesale-funded banks. Reserve-requirement costs are allocated to reservable deposits; post-2022 ECB remuneration of minimum reserves at 0% has re-emerged as a meaningful drag. Deposit-insurance premia (FDIC, FSCS, DGS, CDIC — typically 5–30 bps) are allocated to insured deposit books, often tiered by product. **MREL/TLAC premia** — the 50–150 bp cost of senior-non-preferred and subordinated debt used to satisfy the FSB's 18%-RWA TLAC standard for G-SIBs or the SRB's 22–27%-RWA MREL — are allocated either by RWA or by leverage exposure, materially raising the marginal cost of RWA-heavy businesses relative to low-RWA ones. Post-Credit-Suisse, the write-down of AT1 instruments re-priced this cost sharply.

## Behavioural Modelling

FTP's hardest conceptual challenge is converting positions with no contractual maturity or with embedded optionality into deterministic repricing and maturity profiles. Four categories dominate.

### Non-maturing deposits

Demand deposits, savings, MMDAs and operational corporate deposits contractually reprice overnight and can be withdrawn on demand (zero legal maturity, zero legal duration). Behaviourally, a large fraction is sticky; the deposit rate adjusts partially and with a lag to market rates. This embeds both a liquidity option sold by the bank (the depositor's withdrawal right) and an interest-rate option (the bank's right to reset rate only partially). Jarrow and van Deventer (1998) showed that an NMD is economically equivalent to an exotic interest-rate swap receiving the short market rate and paying the deposit rate on a path-dependent principal.

Decomposition is the first step: **core versus non-core**, typically via volatility/percentile analysis (the 1st–5th percentile of rolling balances is the stable floor), trend analysis (trend + unexpected), and regression of deposit rate on market rate to estimate pass-through β and repricing lag. BCBS 368 caps core proportions at 90% retail transactional, 70% retail non-transactional and 50% wholesale non-financial.

The **replicating-portfolio method** is the gold standard (Kalkbrener–Willing 2004; Nyström 2008; ABN AMRO 2018): construct a bond ladder whose aggregate cash-flow profile matches expected deposit run-off, whose yield tracks the passthrough-adjusted client rate, and whose tranching reflects the core/non-core split. The portfolio weights are chosen by optimisation — typically minimising the variance of (replicating-portfolio yield minus deposit rate paid) subject to weighted-average-maturity, non-negativity and stationarity constraints. The portfolio yield is the FTP credit paid by treasury to the deposit business. Two strategy variants exist as they did for equity: marginal investment (volatile margin) versus portfolio investment (stable — Zanders finds margin volatility ~2.3× lower). Empirically, retail NMD behavioural durations fall in the 2–5 year range, with weighted-average repricing maturities of 3–7 years commonly disclosed in Pillar 3; corporate/operational deposits run 0.5–2 years.

**Deposit beta is the master parameter**: the ratio of deposit-rate change to policy-rate change. Lower β implies greater franchise value, longer behavioural maturity, longer replicating duration, higher FTP credit. Stylised post-2022 US ranges: 30–70% for retail savings/checking (NY Fed Liberty Street reports a cumulative US interest-bearing β of ~0.4 at end-2022 rising to ~0.6 by late 2023), 50–90%+ for corporate/wholesale operational deposits, close to 1.0 for uninsured wholesale (Drechsler–Savov 2023). Critically, β is **not static**: it is convex in the rate level (Dallas Fed WP 2315 "Deposit Convexity"), rising sharply once rates cross a threshold.

**Regulatory caps** on behavioural maturity are now the binding constraint. BCBS 368's standardised approach caps weighted-average repricing maturity at 5.0y / 4.5y / 4.0y for retail-transactional / retail-non-transactional / wholesale non-financial; financial-institution NMDs are treated as overnight. EBA/GL/2022/14 extends the 5-year cap to the **internal model**, not just the SA — a genuine tightening — and requires at least 10 years of historical data behind pass-through assumptions. The EBA 2026 Heatmap reaffirms the cap despite industry pushback arguing for symmetry of interest-rate risk.

**The 2023 lessons are profound**. Drechsler, Savov and Schnabl's "deposit franchise run" theory shows that after 2012, uninsured funds migrated from high-β large CDs into low-β checking/savings accounts, creating a regime in which low-β deposits embody *both* maximum franchise value *and* maximum run risk. SVB's 94% uninsured concentration combined with VC-network coordination and mobile banking produced an outflow of roughly 24% of liabilities in hours — an order of magnitude faster than any Basel LCR assumption (which contemplates 25%/40%/100% for operational/non-operational wholesale over 30 days, not a single day). Koont et al. (2023) and ECB WP3140 show digitalisation materially reduces stickiness and raises β, especially in rising-rate environments. The supervisory implications for FTP are (i) granular cohort segmentation — insured/uninsured, retail/SME/corporate, digital-heavy/branch-heavy, concentration-adjusted — (ii) reduced assumed behavioural maturities on uninsured/digital cohorts, (iii) dynamic β that rises in stressed-rate scenarios and (iv) a liquidity-risk-premium haircut applied to FTP credit when behavioural assumptions rest on thin data.

### Prepayable assets

The prepayment option is a short call on rates written by the lender, producing negative convexity. Standard drivers — refinance incentive (often modelled as a saturating arctangent of current-coupon minus current-rate), turnover (5–7% CPR floor from home sales, divorce, death), burnout (heterogeneous pools exit the responsive borrowers first, reducing future responsiveness), seasonality (May–August peak) and PSA ramp — feed a dynamic CPR function. The industry workhorse is an OAS Monte Carlo: simulate hundreds of rate paths from a calibrated short-rate model, apply the CPR function path-by-path, and solve for the spread that equates model-weighted mean PV to market or par. The option cost is **Z-spread minus OAS**.

FTP integration treats the asset charge as the **behavioural** weighted-average-life matched-maturity rate plus an option-cost add-on compensating treasury for the convexity it inherits. Behavioural WAL on a US 30-year conforming mortgage is typically 5–9 years versus the 15–18 years of contractual WAL, reflecting the deep and liquid TBA-market freedom of US borrowers to refinance without penalty. In break-fee jurisdictions — Canada's greater-of-3m-interest-or-IRD, French statutory caps, German Bauspar lock-ins — behavioural WAL sits much closer to contract and option-cost add-ons are correspondingly smaller, sometimes ignored below materiality.

### Committed undrawn facilities

Revolvers, committed liquidity lines, standby LCs and retail overdrafts create a two-dimensional FTP problem: probability *and* timing of drawdown on a currently off-balance-sheet notional. Best practice (Choudhry; Grant 2011; SR 16-3) charges the business on the undrawn amount for the facility's full contractual tenor, not merely at drawdown, because resetting at draw leaves the business exposed to the liquidity-spread widening that typically coincides with drawdowns — precisely what happened in March 2020, when ING reported a single quarter's RCF-utilisation increase of €9.4 billion. Drawdown modelling uses customer-history logistic or hazard-rate models: investment-grade corporate expected drawdown around 10–15%, sub-investment-grade 40–70%; retail overdraft utilisation 20–40%; liquidity lines to conduits/SPVs binary (low baseline, 100% in crisis, as 2007–08 ABCP demonstrated).

**LCR alignment** uses Basel outflow weights as a floor to the stress add-on, keeping FTP internally consistent with the regulatory buffer cost. **Wrong-way risk** — the correlation of drawdowns with the bank's own stress — is the pervasive but often-unpriced danger: Pennacchi (2006) documented partially offsetting flight-to-quality effects in insured banks, but BCBS WP24 shows this blunting disappears in systemic crises, leaving leveraged-loan pipelines and ABCP backstops with strong positive correlation between drawdown and funding-spread widening. Sound FTP includes an explicit wrong-way premium calibrated to this covariance.

### Equity

Equity behavioural modelling, treated earlier as a rate component, is mechanically the same problem as NMDs: convert a permanent, zero-contractual-interest liability into a synthetic term for FTP via a replicating ladder. The same Jarrow-style decomposition applies; the same marginal-versus-portfolio-strategy choice exists; the same regulatory cap (5 years in EBA IRRBB) constrains modelled duration.

## What FTP actually transfers, and what it leaves behind

Matched-maturity FTP transfers interest-rate risk by construction: once the business is charged or credited at the tenor-matched rate, its P&L is locked into a certainty-equivalent margin, insensitive to subsequent curve moves. Treasury inherits the mismatch and hedges externally, netting across business lines via internal swaps. Liquidity risk transfers similarly via the TLP and contingent-liquidity charge. Optionality and basis transfer via their specific charges; each risk maps to a specific component.

**What remains with the business**: credit risk (owned jointly with the credit function and covered by commercial margin); operational risk; fee and commission volumes; reputational and conduct risk; and residual basis risk between modelled behavioural assumptions and actual customer behaviour. Treasury becomes the internal market-maker, standing ready to fund any new asset at the FTP offer and accept any new liability at the FTP bid. A bid–ask spread is uncommon but appears where treasury wants to signal a desired balance-sheet direction.

## Governance, implementation and pitfalls

The canonical governance model is **treasury proposes, ALCO approves, independent risk/finance validates** under SR 11-7 model-risk standards. ALCO is the forum in which NIM attribution, behavioural assumptions and the TLP curve are contested between treasury and business heads — a process that is inherently political because FTP sits on the fault-line between risk management and reported business revenue. Updates follow a tiered cadence: market-linked legs intraday; full curve daily to weekly; liquidity premia monthly; behavioural parameters (NMD duration, prepayment) quarterly; deep methodology (replicating portfolio structure, contingent-liquidity framework) annually with ad-hoc reviews triggered by market breaks.

**The transparency–simplicity trade-off** is genuine. Businesses need rates they can quote to customers in real time; over-engineered FTP is opaque, reducing accountability and encouraging gaming, while under-engineered FTP hides cross-subsidies. Oliver Wyman's survey found under-engineering is still common among smaller US banks; Cadamagnani et al. found opacity in management overlays is the single most corrosive governance weakness.

The **canonical pitfalls** recur across BIS, Fed and consultancy post-mortems. Stale curves that fail to update when wholesale spreads widen — the GFC archetype. Mispricing NMDs in rising-rate environments by assuming long durations that digital-era behaviour does not support. Under-charging contingent liquidity on committed lines, prime-brokerage, derivative margin and rating triggers. Ignoring LCR/NSFR costs by pooling them centrally. Running pre-crisis methodology (flat or zero TLP) in a post-crisis world. Trading-book "ageing" — positions held longer than originally intended retain the original cheap FTP charge. Circularity, where FTP models use the bank's own stale NMD duration estimates to drive further origination that reinforces the error.

## How FTP behaves in stress

The historical walk-through is instructive. Pre-2007, banks charged funding but not liquidity; long-dated CRE, leveraged loans and structured credit were funded at near-overnight LIBOR. When wholesale markets closed in August 2007 and LIBOR-OIS blew out to ~365 bps post-Lehman, the true cost of term funding was revealed, business-line economics inverted, and the over-origination of illiquid assets was laid bare. BCBS 144 was the direct supervisory response. The 2011–12 European sovereign/bank crisis added funding fragmentation across currencies and jurisdictional ring-fences, requiring multi-currency, multi-entity FTP. March 2020 revealed inadequate contingent-liquidity pricing on committed facilities, as corporates drew revolvers en masse at precisely the moment bank funding spreads were widest — a textbook wrong-way-risk manifestation.

**March 2023 is the defining FTP post-mortem**. Federal Reserve, FDIC and OIG reports, supplemented by the FDIC civil suit against former SVB executives, document: (i) NMD stickiness assumptions catastrophically wrong — SVB's ALCO extended curtailment from 5.5 to 12 years in May 2022 "without any valid justification," producing an IRRBB adjustment that masked true exposure; (ii) 94% uninsured, VC-concentrated deposits running at ~$42 billion in a day through digital channels; (iii) HTM accounting disguising economic losses the FTP framework treated as stable; (iv) deposit gatherers over-credited for concentrated, digital-era balances; (v) interest-rate hedges removed rather than recalibrated — the bank adjusted the measurement rather than the risk; (vi) First Republic collapsing via confidence contagion after its examination hours had fallen 11% while assets doubled; (vii) Credit Suisse's 100% AT1 write-down repricing loss-absorbing-capital costs industry-wide.

The distilled 2023 lessons are that NMD behavioural maturities estimated on 2010–2021 data systematically overstate stickiness in a rising-rate, social-media, digital-banking era; that uninsured-deposit concentration requires differential FTP pricing; that HTM classification cannot be allowed to insulate the FTP framework from economic-value changes; and that contingent-liquidity charges must be calibrated to hours, not days. The EBA's 5-year behavioural-repricing cap on NMDs under EBA/GL/2022/14 is a direct supervisory response.

## Interactions with the wider risk framework

FTP is not an island. Its behavioural assumptions must equal those in the IRRBB replicating portfolios, otherwise economic risk is mis-allocated; EBA/GL/2022/14's NMD caps, 5% Tier 1 NII SOT and 15% Tier 1 EVE SOT all bind on the FTP curve's treatment of sight deposits. Delegated Regulations 2024/856 and 2024/857 further specify the SOT methodology. The outflow assumptions in LCR and Available Stable Funding requirements in NSFR must match the contingent-liquidity charge and term-liquidity surcharge respectively, or the bank is running two inconsistent views of its own liquidity; post-2020 banks now also price in stressed initial-margin-call exposure.

**Accounting interacts only through treasury's external hedges**, not through the internal transfer prices. This distinction is pedagogical bedrock: every FTP charge is matched by an equal credit; internal transfers net to zero and never touch consolidated P&L. But the GFC and 2023 also showed that HTM/AFS accounting can blind a crude FTP framework to real economic losses — a subtler interaction that requires FTP to look beyond accounting book values.

**Product pricing** consumes FTP as its wholesale input; any distortion in FTP flows directly into mispriced customer products and biased portfolio composition. A single-percent FTP error is structural. **Recovery and resolution planning** requires FTP that functions in gone-concern: intragroup funding must be transparent and severable; MREL/TLAC costs must be embedded at the resolution-entity level; multi-point-of-entry groups need separate FTP curves per resolution group; single-point-of-entry groups down-stream internal MREL whose cost must be attributed via FTP.

## Open controversies

**Should credit risk be inside FTP?** Mainstream practice — Oliver Wyman, Moody's, SR 16-3, CEBS 2010 — keeps credit outside, owned by the credit function through the risk-adjusted commercial margin. Dermine's academic dissent argues that for leveraged or high-PD banks the marginal debt cost depends on asset riskiness, implying some asset-specific credit feedback should enter FTP. Almost no bank implements this; convergence is unlikely and the framework choice should be explicit.

**Negative rates** exposed a pricing asymmetry: banks could or would not pass negative rates through to retail depositors, so deposit FTP credits had to be floored at zero while asset-side charges could go negative. Variants exist (zero FTP up to a threshold, negative thereafter) to avoid over-incentivising deposit hoarding. The issue re-emerges every time a central bank approaches the ZLB.

**IBOR transition** required wholesale curve reconstruction onto RFRs with ISDA spread adjustments (5-year median historical lookback), forced explicit decoupling of the term/credit premium that LIBOR implicitly embedded, and created forward-versus-backward-looking term-rate discontinuities. The UK "synthetic LIBOR" patch avoided value transfer but has been unwound. The legal and operational transition is substantially complete in G4 currencies but still live for some emerging-market benchmarks.

**Procyclicality** is an unresolved tension: FTP tightens in good times and widens sharply in stress, amplifying credit contraction when the economy most needs credit. Through-the-cycle smoothed TLPs have been proposed in central-bank speeches, but they conflict with marginal-cost purity and risk rebuilding pre-2008 complacency. There is no regulatory consensus.

**Incentive distortions** from poorly designed FTP are real and recurrent: over-generous deposit credits in rising-rate environments cause hoarding; under-charged prepayment optionality over-originates mortgages; flat uninsured-deposit credits incentivise concentration (the SVB story); pooled HQLA-carry subsidises originators of draw-prone committed lines (the March 2020 story).

**Academic–practitioner divergence** reflects different objective functions rather than error: academics (Dermine; Lindblom and Elliot) tend toward marginal-cost purity, credit integration and market-structure-aware pricing; practitioners prioritise operational stability, transparent rules and defensibility to ALCO and audit, pushing toward average curves, explicit TLP add-ons and decoupled credit. Neither side is wrong.

**Open questions still being debated**: how to price social-media-induced run risk for which no stable historical regression exists; how to incorporate climate-transition funding differentiation; how to treat central-bank facilities (BTFP, discount window) — free option or stigma cost; whether FTP should internalise the cost of being a G-SIB (capital surcharge, TLAC, resolvability); and how to handle tokenised deposits and stablecoin-type liabilities if they reach scale.

## How understanding has changed

The crude picture of FTP as a back-office reallocation that tidies up business-unit P&L has been displaced, across two crises, by something more fundamental: FTP is the price system of the bank's internal economy, and when that price system is wrong, real economic decisions — how much to lend, at what tenor, against what deposits — go systemically wrong with it. 2008 demonstrated that ignoring term and contingent liquidity is not a footnote but a first-order pathology. 2023 demonstrated that the behavioural assumptions on which every FTP credit for non-maturing deposits rests are as fragile as the historical regime on which they were estimated — and that in a rising-rate, digitally-networked, uninsured-concentrated world, the stickiness of deposits can collapse faster than any supervisory stress scenario envisaged.

Three deeper takeaways emerge. First, **FTP is governance first and quantitative second**: the 2008 lesson was not a missing formula but a missing ALCO-level conversation. Methodological sophistication without a senior body that reviews assumptions, overlays and incentives is wasted. Second, **the certainty-equivalent insight** is the core economic payoff of matched-maturity FTP: business-line margins become insensitive to curve moves on existing books, enabling clean separation of customer-franchise economics from balance-sheet-transformation economics. Third, **behavioural modelling is the binding constraint**: replicating-portfolio methods for NMDs, OAS engines for mortgages, drawdown hazard models for commitments and duration-of-equity ladders for capital are all variations on one problem — converting indeterminate cash flows into a deterministic term for transfer pricing. Every post-crisis supervisory tightening has operated on these assumptions, not on the arithmetic that uses them.

FTP therefore occupies a strange position in modern bank management: operationally a set of daily quoted rates on screens; conceptually an internal market whose design decisions encode the bank's views on liquidity, time, depositor behaviour and capital structure. Done well, it is invisible — business lines take the rates as given and compete on what they control. Done badly, it silently misdirects the entire bank, and the consequences appear only in crises.
