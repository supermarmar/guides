## 1. Risk Management Governance



## 2. Main Risks in a Banking Operation

Banks face many risks that may change based on their operating model and the nature of the banking institution. These high-level risks are further expanded on in later chapters.

### 2.1 Credit Risk

This is typically the largest risk exposure that the bank is faced with. Credit risk is the risk that the bank's customer is unable to repay any loans provided. This is a Pillar 1 risk that the bank is exposed to. While lending is generally where most of the source of credit risk exists, it can also exist in the trading book. Additionally, credit risk can be both on and off-balance-sheet. Detailed treatment: Chapter 5.

### 2.2 Market Risk / Interest Rate Risk in the Banking Book (IRRBB)

Market risk is the inherent risk that the market in which the bank operates changes adversely, therefore resulting in potential losses for the bank. Interest Rate Risk in the Banking Book (IRRBB) refers to the risk that prevailing interest rates change significantly, therefore resulting in the bank suffering a change in profit or potentially ending up losing money. This adverse movement in interest rates would result in losses incurred by the bank. Detailed treatment: Chapter 6.

### 2.3 Operational Risk

Operational risk is the risk of potential losses that arise from failures in systems and processes; people; and external events. Detailed treatment: Chapter 7.

### 2.4 Capital Risk

This is the risk that the bank does not have adequate capital available to meet regulatory capital requirements. There is a risk that, due to the risks faced by the bank in its day-to-day operations, the bank does not have adequate internal capital needed to absorb losses that the bank may incur. A common measure of this is the capital adequacy ratio. A bank that has adequate capital on hand in relation to its risk-weighted assets is more likely to be able to absorb unexpected losses and is less likely to go insolvent due to these losses. Detailed treatment: Chapter 8.

### 2.5 Liquidity Risk

This is one of the major risks faced by the bank. It can be split into two:

- **Funding liquidity risk**: The risk that the bank does not have adequate liquid funding to meet the outflows that fall due
- **Market liquidity risk**: This refers to the ability of the bank to convert the assets held into cash

Detailed treatment: Chapter 9.

## 3. Aggregation of Risk

Banks must place sufficient emphasis on their processes to identify, monitor, and aggregate risk. Action cannot be taken to dispose of or mitigate risk and limit losses without a complete picture. It is inefficient to take aggressive steps to deal with risk in individual parts of the bank when these risks may actually have some offset in others, so that the need for selling or hedging is limited. This includes lending, trading, funding, interest-rate risk exposure, and off-balance-sheet businesses.

The Financial Stability Board issued a report in 2011 noting that aggregate data reporting at global systemically important banks was inadequate and set a deadline of 2016 to meet supervisory expectations. These "Principles for effective risk data aggregation and risk reporting" are set out in [BCBS 239](international/bis/bcbs_239.md), which lays out what risk data aggregation capability G-SIBs were required to have in place by January 2016. Complex group structures and businesses ranging across legal entities and regions must not hinder risk data aggregation.

Risk measurement should not consider merely position size, but also potential loss and effect on capital.

### Risk Aggregation Models

| Model | Approach |
|---|---|
| Summation | Add together individual capital components |
| Constant diversification | Subtract a fixed risk percentage from summation to reflect diversification |
| Variance-covariance | Components are weighted based on bi-lateral correlation |
| Copulas | Use multivariate probability theory to evaluate marginal risks |
| Full modelling | Simulate the impact of multiple risk factors on all components |

Banks must consider clarity and simplicity, IT cost, data availability, non-linearity of risks, and over-reliance on assumptions when assessing the most appropriate model.

Risk management models must be effective not only in normal conditions, but also in extreme situations. Variance-covariance Value at Risk (VaR) was commonly calibrated to two standard deviations (95%), which was not effective in the extreme markets of the late 1990s. Tail risk (three standard deviations beyond the mean) must also be captured and monitored. Risks with disproportionately high probabilities of extreme outcomes are referred to as having "fat tails."

Any model is dependent on assumptions that are generally based on historical experience. The key assumption in using historical experience is that this is representative of future experience. Numbers generated by a model therefore cannot be taken as the final assessment of risk. Management judgement is also an important element of risk management, as not all risks can be quantified using mathematical and statistical techniques. Some risks, such as reputational risk, are largely unquantifiable.

Banks may choose to compare and benchmark their risk management against peers and competitors to validate models. While helpful, adherence to the same methodologies and assumptions can lead to systemic (as opposed to idiosyncratic) risk.

### 3.1 Diversification

The concept of diversification is intuitive and well known — "don't put all your eggs in one basket." Diversification helps protect against losses arising from idiosyncratic risk. It does not help with systemic risk, which is what would lead to catastrophic losses or total failure of the financial system. A well-diversified portfolio will perform in line with overall markets and the economy, and have more predictable volatility and manageability.

Important limitations of diversification:

- Diversification can be a problem for specialised and regional banks (e.g. US banks forced to lend only within their states prior to relaxation of interstate banking laws, which were dominated by a few industries)
- It is not a solution to all risks faced by banks — banks that diversify by lending to sectors and regions where they have limited expertise may find other risks arising as a result
- It is not a substitute for quality lending — credit provisions and risk capital only allow banks to absorb losses up to a certain point
- Default correlation increases significantly in deteriorating market conditions, while asset return correlation is less sensitive
- Diversification for investment returns must be viewed very differently from diversification for credit risk management: investment returns focus on the "upside", while credit risk management is concerned with the "downside" risk

Diversification analysis will review industry sectors, size, products, and regions.

### 3.2 Concentration

Excessive concentration of credit risk has been identified by global regulators as one of the key causes of bank crises and failures. Traditionally, concentration risk was viewed as the risk of over-exposure to single borrowers, but the emphasis is now on excessive correlation across the entire portfolio. BIS studies have concluded that sector concentrations have had a bigger impact on bank credit portfolios than single-name exposures.

Historically, many regulatory regimes across the world set a large exposure limit to a single entity or related entities of 25% of a bank's regulatory capital. In April 2014, the BIS issued a new standard addressing concentration risk, considering exposure to both instrument-based asset classes (e.g. asset-backed securities and collateralised debt obligations) and systemically important financial institutions (SIFIs) and individual companies. The revised framework will help ensure a common minimum standard for measuring, aggregating, and controlling concentration risk across jurisdictions.

### 3.3 Correlation

Even if single-name exposures are limited, and transactions are evaluated and structured on a sound basis, management must make correlation assessments on the aggregate portfolio. If different borrowers are sensitive to the same changes in economic, business, regulatory, financial market, and political conditions, a much larger exposure akin to a single large borrower is created, which can result in heavy losses.

The US Comptroller of the Currency has identified as highly correlated credit exposures to borrowers those that are:

- Related through group structure
- Dependent on the same guarantor
- Dependent on the selling of the same manufacturer's product
- In the same industry or economic sector
- In the financial sector
- Within a geographic area dominated by few business enterprises
- Owned by a foreign government
- Secured by a common debt or equity instrument

Product areas where correlation risk has been identified by global regulators as having created large concentration risk include retail products (e.g. credit cards, home equity), leveraged loans, collateralised debt obligations (CDOs), and commercial real estate.

For maximum diversification, banks would construct a loan portfolio with the lowest possible correlations. Correlation is difficult to measure as a result of data constraints — calibrating correlation accurately requires data points for all possible scenarios including periods of low, medium, and high economic stress, as well as corresponding default rate data.

In mitigating risks, banks must consider further correlation between exposures and hedging instruments. For example, a credit derivative purchased to hedge exposure to a certain bank introduces correlation risk between the hedged bank defaulting and the selling bank not being able to meet its obligations.

## 4. Controlling Risks

### 4.1 Risk Appetite

The risk appetite of a bank is driven to a large extent by the size of its free or available capital, and to a material extent by its funding model and its ability to raise sufficient levels of long-term (or stable) liquidity. Banks must maintain minimum regulatory capital ratios but should also perform a thorough analysis of their business model and capital needs under various scenarios. The amount of capital required as assessed by a bank in order to remain solvent at a given confidence level and time horizon is known as **risk capital** or **economic capital**.

A risk appetite statement must be coordinated by the risk management team and include senior management before being approved by the board. The requirements of stakeholders (including shareholders, regulators, rating agencies, and customers) must be considered when creating the risk appetite statement.

**Quantitative metrics** can be used to set concentration thresholds and performance triggers. These can be actively managed and monitored. Quantitative metrics include:

- Minimum external risk rating
- Minimum risk-adjusted return on capital (RAROC) hurdle rate for new business
- Minimum capital adequacy ratio
- Maximum earnings volatility
- Minimum weighted average portfolio rating
- Maximum thresholds for relative internal capital allocation to credit, interest rate, market and operational risk
- Maximum concentration thresholds (obligor, sector, country)
- Maximum asset-liability management mismatch
- Liquidity thresholds and metrics
- Minimisation of controlled or undesirable exposures
- Targeted levels of risk asset ratios or leverage

**Qualitative metrics** speak to the risk culture ambitions of the bank and could include:

- Ensuring that there is a connection to business strategy
- Zero tolerance for internal fraud
- Customer conduct considerations / treating customers fairly
- Minimise potential adverse impacts on climate and society

The statement will anticipate scenarios of market stress and plan possible responses. Tolerances levels for the above parameters are likely to be determined at an overall corporate level as well as a business unit and departmental level. The bank can develop dashboards for various levels of seniority to monitor the desired parameters relative to their tolerance levels.

The bank's risk appetite is aligned to the [ICAAP](01-regulatory_environment.md) (internal capital assessment and adequacy plan) and ILAA (internal liquidity adequacy assessment). The risk management framework and appetite is subject to strict scrutiny by the regulator in the SREP (supervisory review and evaluation process) reviews.

**Formulating the Risk Appetite Framework (RAF)** requires four main steps:

1. Identify the key risks facing the bank
2. Liquidity evaluation
3. Capital adequacy and regulatory requirements
4. Funding requirements

### 4.2 Key Risk Indicators (KRIs) and Limits

An effective risk management framework will lay out the overall governance framework of the bank. This ensures adequate delegation of authority from the board down through various sub-committees, then onto management and the business at large. The KRIs that the bank sets out in the risk appetite statement monitor the bank's compliance with the board's stated risk appetite. The risks and risk measures identified should align with the strategic plan of the bank.

Bank-specific KRIs include:

- External credit rating
- Risk adjusted return on capital
- Capital adequacy ratio
- Credit loss ratio
- Provisions coverage
- Concentration limits
- Net stable funding ratio
- Liquidity coverage ratio
- Encumbrance ratio

Banks are most likely to be more conservative in setting their breach levels for the indicators set above — the risk appetite boundaries are likely to be set above the regulatory minimums.

The risk appetite statement is likely to consist of a table that summarises the various KRIs calibrated to various levels of severity. These levels indicate the breach levels of the bank with each KRI. If a bank is to breach its business as usual (BAU) status for a given KRI, there is a defined trigger plan that is to follow.

In determining and monitoring potential breaches against these thresholds, data analysis and reporting should be performed. There should be methods in place to quantify these KRIs so that effective monitoring can be performed.

### 4.3 Loan Underwriting Criteria

New loans must be sanctioned only within the context of the risk appetite statement and risk-weighted capital criteria:

- **Corporate and wholesale banking**: A request to provide credit should involve the completion of a form to be submitted to a separate entity from the relationship bankers, whether it be a credit committee or (in the case of smaller banks) executive management and ALCO. The request will contain all the information needed for the committee to discuss the risk and return of the loan, terms, and the customer, so a determination can be made as to whether it fits into the broader strategy.
- **Retail banking**: New loans can be granted via automated applications, which will be approved or referred to by loan origination officers.

"Know Your Customer" (KYC) is a key element of the loan origination process. For corporate and wholesale banking, relationship bankers should have an extensive dialogue with customers to have a thorough understanding of their business, strategy, and risks. For retail banking, the bank will maintain behavioural scorecards and delinquency information to monitor loan performance and take action should the loan be at risk of defaulting.

Beyond the credit risk of the borrower and the rate charged, the bank must also evaluate the specifics of the loan:

- Legal framework and jurisdiction risks
- Collateral: acceptable forms and enforceability
- Disclosure requirements
- Ability to terminate: material adverse change clauses and covenants
- Transferability: can the loan be sold?
- Currencies
- Fixed or floating rate
- Prepayment options

Within the bank, many of the items should align with credit risk policies and procedures as laid out in the bank's risk appetite framework. Some specifics will require legal consultation and others may require input from various committees and levels of staff.

### 4.4 Allocating Risk-Based Capital

Allocation of risk-based capital is not only about risk but must also be linked to potential rewards. In order to increase returns, the bank will need to take on additional risk, and it is necessary to optimise risk capital allocation to the portfolio in seeking higher profitability. Banker and trader pay must be linked not only to returns, but also to the amount of risk-based capital used in its generation, as higher risk positions attract higher regulatory and economic capital.

Capital must be allocated with a view towards sustainability. Business plans must anticipate ongoing capital needs, costs, and availability, without which growth and continued competitiveness will not be attainable. Consideration must be given to the time value of money, tax consequences, and varying rates when assessing returns.

In allocating capital, banks can perform a comparison between the economic capital and regulatory capital charges applicable to different areas of their business. In doing so, they can identify those areas of their business which attract high amounts of regulatory capital, but low amounts of economic capital — and those which attract low amounts of regulatory capital, but high amounts of economic capital. This type of analysis will help the bank with strategic business planning and in setting growth and performance targets for the future.

## 5. Risk Modelling

Risk modelling is one technique banks use to manage their risks. The financial crisis has shown that intuition and experience are very necessary "soft skills" that can assist judgement when used in conjunction with quantitative models. Models are only as good as the underlying assumptions and data, but are a key tool in measuring and clarifying the overall portfolio. It is therefore important for management to apply judgement to the results produced by risk models in assessing their reasonableness, especially their appropriateness for predicting risk going forward.

### 5.1 Frequency and Severity Modelling (Credit and Operational Risk)

Models can simulate stochastic default / delinquency numbers and volumes, loss severity, and timing. The models will use simulation, address correlation, and be tailored bank-by-bank to unique portfolios of risk.

**Corporate credit**: Due to the low number of defaults on corporate portfolios, simulation techniques such as Monte Carlo may be used to estimate default probabilities, delinquency numbers, and losses. Banks may also rely on external credit ratings performed by agencies such as Moody's and Standard & Poor's to provide an assessment of default experience on corporate loans.

**Operational risk**: Banks will usually not have sufficient credible data relating to operational risk events to calibrate a model using internal data only. As a result, a combination of simulation, expert judgement, and internal / external data is used.

**Retail banking**: Most banks can rely on historical data to calibrate their models. As a result, these models (both frequency and severity) are usually deterministic in nature. The depth of historical data and constant new data allow banks to create more homogeneous risk segments of retail exposure, using factors and sectors such as loan type, length of loan, loan-to-value ratios, and region.

### 5.2 Statistical Modelling for Portfolio Management

Statistical modelling will analyse as many variables as possible in assessing correlations in the risk portfolio. Key parameters include the performance of the economy (analysed by sector and region) and interest rate environment. Portfolio management will also consider the exposure of the bank to single large counterparties, as well as concentration risk by geography or industry.

### 5.3 Survival Models for Credit Risk Management

Just as survival analysis is used in insurance to predict mortality rates, duration analysis can be used to predict frequencies of default throughout the economic cycle. The key parameters:

- **P(W|D)**: The probability of an account being written off, given that it has "survived" to a certain duration in default. This is calculated as the exposure which is written off at the end of the period divided by the exposure at risk at the start of the period.
- **LGW**: The loss given write-off at that duration in default. This is calculated as the loss at write-off (the difference between the outstanding balance and any recoveries made) divided by the outstanding balance at write-off.

These two parameters can be combined to provide an estimate of loss given default that is dependent on the duration spent in default. This curve is usually upward sloping, with loans suffering a higher loss the longer they have remained in default. Unlike with mortality rates, recurring or repeated event models are required in banking — "default" is a non-absorbing state from which cure is possible.

### 5.4 Market Implied Probability of Default and Survival Curves

In the absence of sufficient data to create internal PD and survival curves, benchmarked external data for credit rating agencies can be used for corporate counterparties. Alternatively, the credit spread difference between a default-risk-free bond and a corporate bond represents the market implied probability of default of a corporate.

### 5.5 Asset-Liability Modelling (ALM) for Balance Sheet Management

Actuaries have traditionally been involved in performing asset-liability modelling for life insurers and pension funds. Actuaries determine funding status, cash requirements, and balance sheet positions, and make projections under varying future economic and capital market environments. Projections are deterministic (scenario based) or stochastic (using Monte Carlo techniques). These same skills and techniques are extremely valuable to banks in their asset-liability management.

### 5.6 Cashflow Models for Budgeting and Balance Sheet Management

Cashflow is an important measure of sustainability of a bank or business. Banks must be able to estimate cashflows (timing and severity) in the most extreme scenarios so as to be able to devise a strategy to ensure adequate funding. Understanding cashflows under stressed economic conditions also forms part of a bank's regulatory reporting requirements.

### 5.7 Discounted Cashflow Models for Product Pricing

When pricing a product in a bank, by decomposing the product into all expected future cashflows, the bank's expected inflows and outflows of the potential product can be considered. By using a risk-adjusted internal rate of return as a benchmark, the bank can then determine if the considered product would be profitable on a risk-adjusted basis. Pricing in this way allows a bank to consider potential areas of risk, be it in the variability, severity, or timing of cashflows.

### 5.8 Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning (AI/ML)

The use of AI/ML algorithms in banking risk management has been on the rise due to the ability to make use of high volumes of data, including unstructured data, for deep predictive analysis and superior predictive capabilities. Examples of where AI/ML may be used in risk management include fraud detection and the use of algorithms to help identify fraudulent transactions, and credit risk modelling where algorithms could be used to better identify higher risk accounts.

AI/ML comes with a large drawback in increased model risk due to increased model complexity and lack of explanatory insight (the "black box" effect). Banks must also consider the operational and financial risks that may arise if an AI/ML model breaks or produces incorrect results, as well as the regulatory and reputational risk if an AI/ML model produces results which are unduly biased and therefore violate anti-discrimination laws.

Key considerations for appropriate adoption of AI/ML algorithms:

- **Explainability and interpretability**: AI/ML algorithms should be sense-checked and the result should not be blindly accepted by the users of the model
- **Bias**: Bias in the underlying data over which the model is calibrated may result in biased results
- **Overfitting and feature engineering**: AI/ML models may make use of overly complex feature engineering, which can result in overfitting and ultimately result in poor or incorrect performance of the model. Hyperparameters must be chosen as soundly as possible.
- **IT infrastructure integration**: While an AI/ML model may perform very well, it may be highly complex and require time and computing resources that do not make it feasible for business use
- **Model supervision and recalibration**: Banks need to decide whether an AI/ML model should dynamically update as new information is presented to it. The upside is that models can then adapt to current information and provide useful and timely insights. The downside is that new information presented may be outside the calibration norm of the model and produce counter-intuitive and incorrect results.
- **Regulation**: There may be local or international regulations in place (e.g. GDPR) that hinder the ability of a bank to build or adopt AI/ML models

Banks have been slow to adopt AI/ML models and approaches to core functions, instead adopting these models first in lower risk areas such as marketing and sales.

### 5.9 Control Cycle for All Models (Model Risk Committee)

The actuarial control cycle is useful to the risk management area of any bank. Bank risk management models should be evaluated against all aspects of the actuarial control cycle: monitoring and assessing experience, determining assumptions, applying relevant approaches and techniques to valuation and pricing, and measuring, reporting, and managing results.

A **Model Risk Committee (MRC)** is set up to have oversight of the various models used by the bank. The MRC will:

- Independently validate and review new models and any new calibrations that are required
- Monitor the ongoing performance of models against pre-defined risk thresholds to ensure that the model is working as intended
- Determine when intervention is needed — model recalibration, re-parameterisation, or rebuild — when the model breaches defined thresholds

## 6. Additional Risks Faced by Banks

Beyond the five primary risk types detailed in section 2, banks face several further risks that warrant separate management focus.

### 6.1 Margin Risk

Margin risk is the risk of a significant fall in a bank's net interest margin. Margin compression may occur as a result of three reasons:

- **Competitive pressure** — leading to lower interest rates on loans without corresponding reductions in deposit rates. Loan prices are not unusual to be reduced in periods of economic growth (with higher lending volumes and low credit losses) and to be increased in periods of recession (with lower lending volumes and high credit losses).
- **Interest rate changes on mismatched assets and liabilities** — for example, the negative impact of higher interest rates on a bank with deposits of shorter maturities than its loans, or with fixed-rate loans financed by variable-rate deposits.
- **Sustained low interest rates** — banks offering loans at historically low rates but being understandably reluctant to impose negative interest rates on retail deposits. Some European banks have charged negative interest rates on large deposits.

### 6.2 Systemic Risk

In addition to the above risks, banks face systemic risk. Banks are connected to each other, for example, through inter-bank deposits and derivative transactions. The failure of one bank could lead to "domino effect" failures of other banks. Following the failure of Lehman Brothers in October 2008, governments in various countries, including the US and the UK, injected substantial funds to protect their banking systems.

Banks may also be connected to other financial institutions, for example, asset managers and pension funds that invest in a bank's shares or bonds. A large bank's failure could also have a systemic risk on the broader financial services sector.

One of the most important lessons from the banking crisis of 2007–2008 has been the need for banks and regulators to consider systemic risk. Because of the vital role that large banks play in their economies, and the economic hardship that would arise from savers losing their deposits, large banks may be regarded as "too big to fail." However, to reduce the risk that taxpayers might have to inject funds to support their banking system, large systemically important banks must hold an additional capital buffer known as a systemic risk buffer.

### 6.3 Conduct Risk

Conduct Risk is the risk associated with negative or unfair outcomes for a bank's customers. Technically, Conduct Risk forms part of Operational Risk in that it relates to failed people and systems that ultimately impact a bank's customers. However, due to the potential impact of Conduct Risk, it is often managed as a separate process to Operational Risk so that it receives the focus that it deserves. An added benefit of this approach is that other Operational sub-risk types, such as fraud, can receive additional focus.

Conduct Risk events have the potential for regulatory and reputational backlash — regulators don't appreciate poor business practice and may scrutinise the bank in future, which would result in increased burden on the bank to prove that they are acting appropriately. Examples of conduct risk events include PPI mis-selling in the UK (selling personal protection insurance at high prices) and, internationally, LIBOR manipulation and money laundering.

Conduct Risk cuts across both the retail and wholesale arm of the bank, and generally relates to how the bank interacts with its current and potential clients. The difficulty in managing this risk is that it relates to how salespeople at a firm interact with the clients of the bank — these individuals are generally not risk practitioners themselves and are likely to be incentivised to sell as many of the bank's products as possible, creating an implicit incentive towards activities that can result in Conduct Risk events.

Conduct Risk is predominately managed qualitatively through a strong internal operating and control environment.

### 6.4 Reputational Risk

Reputational Risk is defined as the risk of reputational damage to a firm's good standing. As with Conduct Risk, Reputational Risk is technically an Operational Risk but, due to its potential impact on the bank, is often managed separately. Banks' reputations are highly important intangible assets and can have a material impact on their ability to maintain and write new business.

A common method for measuring Reputational Risk is first to estimate the revenue-generating ability of a bank's brand value, and then to determine current and future factors that may affect brand quality. Conduct Risk considerations, for example, may form part of this process as a common consideration is the quality of the bank's engagement with its client base.

### 6.5 Equity Risk

Equity Risk is defined as the risk associated with changes in price of equities held by the bank. An important distinction exists between Market Risk and Equity Risk: Market Risk relates to assets that are generally held with the intent to trade and make a profit. Equity Risk tends to relate to equity holdings that are held for the purpose of ownership in the underlying firm, and the impact of the fluctuations in these equity valuations on the bank's balance sheet and income statement. These equity holdings are asset holdings on a bank's balance sheet — if they reduce in value this would be captured as a loss on the income statement.

With the rise of fintech, many banks have adopted an acquisition approach to managing changing markets and consumer needs. As a result, they have increasing equity for non-trading purposes holdings, thus becoming increasingly exposed to Equity Risk.

### 6.6 Business Risk (Strategic Risk)

Business Risk stems from any factors that may impact the profitability of the bank in the future (not explicitly covered by other risks). This is something of a catch-all risk and covers many of the balance sheet and income statement line items that don't explicitly get covered by other risk types. As a result, these tend to refer to expense line items on the income statement, where the risk lies in these cost items fluctuating more than expected.

Business Risk is also known as Strategic Risk, which can also apply to inappropriate planning due to inappropriate assumptions or information being used in the planning process, or a lack of skills with respect to planning. To minimise this risk, it's important for banks to hire effective managers and board members.

### 6.7 Cyber Risk

Cyber Risk is defined as the risk of loss due to digital incidents. Due to the increasing importance of Cyber Risk as a result of widespread digitalisation of banks, Cyber Risk is fast becoming the most material Operational Risk that a bank faces. As with many other Operational Risk sub-risk types, Cyber Risk is not independent of other risks — for example, poorly built systems can result in the bank being hacked and losing customer information. This in turn can result in customers being defrauded, and the bank ultimately needing to cover this cost. Moreover, this type of event can result in reputational damage, as customers of the bank feel that their information and money isn't safe.

### 6.8 Model Risk

Model Risk is defined as the negative outcomes associated with the misuse of models. In the context of a bank, it covers two distinct areas:
- **Inappropriate use of a model** — for example, if a model used to forecast revenue in one business line is used without any adjustment in another business line, this can lead to inappropriately planning and ultimately lost revenue. Ensuring a strong internal audit environment where models that can have a material effect on business decision making or regulatory reporting are vetted and tracked is essential.
- **An inherently inaccurate model** — the model itself being inappropriate for the action, or the model parameters being inaccurate. This is largely managed through having a strong internal validation unit who checks that models are built appropriately and scientifically.

### 6.9 Pension Risk

Pension Risk refers to the risk that the bank would need to cover underperformance of a pension scheme for its employees. This risk is becoming smaller in the South African context due to the decreasing use of defined benefit pension schemes. It is included here for noting, as some banks still track their Pension Risk.

## 7. Risk Mitigation

Before focusing on technical and complicated risk management matters, management should always know and be thinking about a few basic questions: What are the bank's largest individual, sector, product, and regional exposures? In what circumstances should these exposures be reduced or grown? And, very importantly, what are the means and options for reducing these exposures under a range of circumstances? The bank would also want to understand how to grow certain exposures to meet their strategic and business objectives, without taking on excessive risk.

### 6.1 Future New Business and Sales of Assets

The risk portfolio of a bank can be adjusted by monitoring maturing business and adjusting policy for assets accordingly. New business for sectors can be frozen, reduced, or subjected to stricter standards (e.g. increased spread and fees, more collateral, tighter covenants). Emphasis can be placed on booking business in sectors that can be expected to perform counter to the concentration risk — sectors that outperform in different phases of the economic cycle (e.g. consumer staples are usually relatively strong in a downturn).

A more direct approach is the outright sale of loan and bond positions. Selling has the advantage of immediacy and avoids the risk that a hedging instrument does not perform as expected (basis risk). Liquidity varies based on factors including market size and standardisation, complexity and terms of the debt instrument, and borrower credit rating. In stressed markets, liquidity can be expected to diminish when it is needed most.

### 6.2 Hedging

Best-practice credit risk management discipline dictates that banks apply a sound credit policy at origination — prevention is better than cure. Credit risk is, for practical purposes, difficult to hedge. The optimum approach to credit risk for most banks involves diversification of the loan book, avoiding concentration with one borrower or sector, and the operation of conservative origination principles.

For a more proactive approach, historically the means of hedging credit risk was to short the bonds of the borrower or similar borrowers. However, it is not always possible to borrow the bonds in sufficient size, and the repo rate can be prohibitively expensive. Basis risk can again be a challenge.

Since the early 1990s, the management and transfer of credit risk has been transformed by the emergence of **credit derivatives**, which are contracts and instruments that separate and transfer credit or default risk from the lender / noteholder to another party.

**Credit Default Swaps (CDS)** — CDS spreads are watched closely by issuers, investors, and banks, and are key to issuance, investment, and pricing decisions. CDSs allow credit protection to be bought and sold between bilateral counterparties, with premiums paid as in traditional insurance. Key characteristics:

- Specify the nominal amount of reference asset (e.g. bond, entity, index, or tailored basket) against which a payment amount will be made if a specified credit event is triggered
- Credit events include: default on a payment, rating downgrade, or bankruptcy
- Are considered "unfunded" — the nominal amount is never transferred
- Under the International Swaps and Derivatives Association (ISDA) master agreement provisions, margining and collateral are made

In the early years of credit derivatives, there were controversies in agreeing exactly when credit events were triggered (e.g. no payout on Greece because default was accepted "voluntarily"). Many call for greater market transparency and better statistics to build confidence and liquidity to maximise opportunities for active credit portfolio management.

Hedging of various other risks via financial derivatives can be used to protect against interest rate and exchange rate risk.

### 6.3 Securitisation

At its simplest, securitisation involves transforming assets into securities. Assets are sold to a bankruptcy-remote special purpose vehicle (SPV), which issues securities backed by the assets. Benefits include enhanced liquidity, balance sheet management, diversification, and risk management.

Banks have securitised — with varying success — most types of assets. These range from straightforward government bonds to trade, auto, and credit card receivables; mortgages; auto leases; and loans to the more exotic future streams of rock star royalties, football gate receipts, pharmaceutical patents, and funeral home burials.

**Common forms of securitisation:**

- Asset backed securities (ABS)
- Mortgage backed securities (MBS)
- Collateralised debt obligations (CDOs) — generally bonds, which can take synthetic exposure through derivatives including credit default swaps
- Collateralised loan obligations (CLOs) — consist of loans
- Asset backed commercial paper (ABCP)

**Credit enhancement** is required depending on the perceived risk and liquidity of the underlying assets. Enhancement can take forms including overcollateralisation, reserves (established upfront or through retained earnings), guarantees, letters of credit, credit default swaps, and first loss provisions. Securities can be "tranched," with junior, mezzanine, and senior issuance having different risk and return profiles. Regardless, investors must be largely confident that the assets can service the repayment of interest and principal, without looking to the bank / originator for payment.

**Issues with securitisation — the "originate to distribute" model**: Securitisation ran into trouble in the financial crisis. Because each participant in the "chain" of creating a securitisation is responsible for only their function (asset generation, securitisation structuring, sales), none had the responsibility for knowing the borrower fully and verifying and understanding that the entire deal makes sense. Each earned a fee upfront and none had a direct ongoing economic stake in the performance of the borrower.

**Post-crisis regulation (EU)** — Changes have been introduced to revive the securitisation market on a sound basis:

- Enforce and standardise risk retention requirements on banks offering securitisations
- Enforce due diligence requirements on investors both before investing and while holding a securitisation position
- Increase transparency requirements for originators and sponsors to ensure investors and supervisors have access to all relevant information in a standardised format
- Capital requirements on securitised assets can be calculated using: the internal ratings-based approach (SEC-IRBA), external ratings-based approach (SEC-ERBA), or the Standardised Approach (SEC-SA)

**STS / STC securitisation** (Simple, Transparent, Standardised / Comparable) — A new kind of securitisation introduced in Europe with specific requirements:

- Restrictions on the types of underlying exposures that may be incorporated into the securitisation
- Limits on the risk-weighting of underlying exposure to ensure high-risk assets are not bundled into these securitisations
- Rules regarding granularity of the underlying pool of assets — no exposure can account for more than 1% of the total exposure
- At origination, the underlying exposures should not be in default nor represent loans to credit-impaired borrowers
