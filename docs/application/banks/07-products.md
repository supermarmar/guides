## Retail Banking Products

There are four tests for characterisation as retail exposure, termed “regulatory retail”, according to Basel:

- Orientation – individual or small business (SME) exposures
- Product type:
  - Revolving credits and lines of credit
  - Personal term loans and leases
  - Small business facilities and commitments (see below exposure limits)
  - Residential mortgage loans (subject to limits on the number of housing units per exposure)
- Granularity – the segments (as mentioned above) should exhibit homogeneity but also should be sufficiently diversified so that a view of the credit risk is applicable to the entire portfolio equally (e.g. if a loan is substantially riskier and larger than the rest of the segment, it is not reasonable to weigh the risk equally)
- Maximum individual exposure – the aggregate exposure to one client cannot surpass the threshold (mandated by Basel to be €1 million).

### Current Accounts

Current accounts are usually “bundled” products which facilitate the three main services offered by retail banks: payments, saving and borrowing, as discussed below. Current accounts enable banks to establish and maintain long-term relationships with their customers, and can provide opportunities for cross-selling other financial products.

#### Payments

Banks allow customers to make payments in a variety of ways.

- **Cash**: Customers can withdraw cash at branches, from Automated Teller Machines (ATMs) and at supermarkets that offer these services to customers.
- **Debit Cards**: Debit card payments can be made in stores, on the phone or over the internet using a computer or mobile phone. Some countries have introduced “tap and go” facilities that enable customers to make small payments by tapping their card against a specially designed payment terminal. Fraud is reduced by periodically requiring the customer to input their PIN number to prove the card is not lost or stolen.
- **Cheques**: Most banks still allow customers to make payments using cheques. Paying in cash or cheques can be more difficult: some ATMs can take these, but many customers will have to visit their local branch.
- **Debit Orders**: Regular payments can be set up using direct debits (where the request comes from the recipient’s bank) or standing orders (where the payments are initiated by the customer’s bank).

#### Overdrafts

Current accounts enable borrowing through overdrafts. Overdrafts are short term lending facilities which allow customers to draw more money than their account hold. Overdrafts are unsecured and banks may be able to demand repayment at any time. There are two types of overdraft:

- **Authorised overdrafts**: This is an overdraft where there is a limit agreed in advance. For example, a customer with an authorised overdraft limit of R10 000 can withdraw up to R10 000 once they have spent all the money in their account. Banks typically charge relatively high rates of interest on overdraft borrowings. They may also charge fees for setting up or maintaining overdraft limits. Banks need to allow for the additional credit risk, and for the impact on their liquidity risk, that might result from customers with authorised overdrafts increasing the use of their overdraft facilities up to the agreed credit limits.
- **Unauthorised overdraft**: This is an overdraft facility where a customer can spend more money than they have available in their account but where there is no pre-agreed limit to this spending. This may also include spending amounts over an authorised overdraft. Banks allowing an unauthorised overdraft will usually have a “shadow limit” for each customer – that is, a pre-determined level up to which they are prepared to lend. If the payment is within this limit, the bank may honour the customer’s payment request. Banks can charge for unauthorised overdrafts in a variety of ways. There might be a fee associated with each payment honoured with an unauthorised overdraft and/ or a monthly fee for activating the unauthorised facility, as well as interest costs on the amount borrowed. This practice has come under scrutiny from regulators due to concerns around treating customers fairly. For example, the UK regulators constrained bank charges for unauthorised overdrafts, after these became an important income stream for banks, despite being generally paid by more vulnerable customers.

### Savings Accounts

Some banks pay interest on current account savings balances, to attract new customers, but most banks in many parts of the world do not pay interest, or only pay interest at a low rate. So, most customers hold only enough cash for expected outgoings in their current accounts and hold larger amounts of cash savings in deposit accounts.

#### Instant Access

Instant access savings accounts allow savers to deposit money and to withdraw it at any time they wish. In many countries, savings are guaranteed, up to a certain amount, by an [deposit insurance scheme](), the costs of which are borne by the banks.

### Credit Cards

A credit card provides payment services and short-term borrowing up to an agreed credit
limit. Credit card borrowing is known as revolving credit as the line of credit to a customer is
automatically renewed as prior debts are paid off. The credit card holder can spend up to the
agreed borrowing limit and must pay back at least a minimum amount each month.
