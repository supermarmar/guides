# Products

Banking products provide three main services:

- **Saving**: Bank deposits help customers to save and to keep their deposits safe.
- **Borrowing**: Bank loans help customers to make purchases, invest in assets and manage cashflows.
- **Transactional**: Banks enable customers to make and receive payments, in their domestic currency and in foreign currencies.

Many banks also offer insurance-related products (both life and non-life). The combination of insurance and banking is known as **bancassurance**, which benefits the bank through the ability to cross-sell, boosting non-interest income in times of decreasing interest margins, and achieving risk diversification.

Investment banks offer products and services in two categories:

- **Transactional services**: buying/selling financial assets (bonds, shares, derivatives such as swaps and options)
- **Advisory services**: helping large corporate customers raise capital or engage in corporate transactions

The purpose of studying banking products is not to make students experts in a single product area, but rather to ensure that those who work in central functions — such as strategy, risk, finance or IT — understand the opportunities and risks associated with a broad range of banking products.

## Retail Banking Products

There are four tests for characterisation as retail exposure ("regulatory retail") according to Basel:

- **Orientation** – individual or small business (SME) exposures
- **Product type**:
  - Revolving credits and lines of credit
  - Personal term loans and leases
  - Small business facilities and commitments
  - Residential mortgage loans (subject to limits on the number of housing units per exposure)
- **Granularity** – the segments should exhibit homogeneity but also be sufficiently diversified so that a view of credit risk is applicable to the entire portfolio equally (e.g. if a loan is substantially riskier and larger than the rest of the segment, it is not reasonable to weigh the risk equally)
- **Maximum individual exposure** – the aggregate exposure to one client cannot surpass €1 million (mandated by Basel)

Retail banking products have become increasingly commoditised, leading competition to focus mainly on price. Banks differentiate through reward programmes, ratings, and innovation — though product innovation can be replicated quickly and may not provide sustainable competitive advantage.

FirstRand's retail banking business is dominated by FNB, serving individuals and SMEs across South Africa and the wider SADC region. WesBank complements this with specialist vehicle and asset finance solutions.

### Current Accounts

Current accounts are usually "bundled" products which facilitate the three main services offered by retail banks: payments, saving and borrowing. They enable banks to establish and maintain long-term relationships with their customers, and provide opportunities for cross-selling other financial products.

#### Payments

Banks allow customers to make payments in a variety of ways.

- **Cash**: Customers can withdraw cash at branches, from Automated Teller Machines (ATMs) and at supermarkets that offer these services to customers.
- **Debit Cards**: Debit card payments can be made in stores, on the phone or over the internet using a computer or mobile phone. Some countries have introduced "tap and go" facilities that enable customers to make small payments by tapping their card against a specially designed payment terminal. Fraud is reduced by periodically requiring the customer to input their PIN number.
- **Cheques**: Most banks still allow customers to make payments using cheques.
- **Debit Orders**: Regular payments can be set up using direct debits (where the request comes from the recipient's bank) or standing orders (where the payments are initiated by the customer's bank).

#### Overdrafts

Current accounts enable borrowing through overdrafts. Overdrafts are short-term lending facilities which allow customers to draw more money than their account holds. Overdrafts are unsecured and banks may be able to demand repayment at any time. There are two types of overdraft:

- **Authorised overdrafts**: An overdraft where there is a limit agreed in advance. For example, a customer with an authorised overdraft limit of R10 000 can withdraw up to R10 000 once they have spent all the money in their account. Banks typically charge relatively high rates of interest on overdraft borrowings. They may also charge fees for setting up or maintaining overdraft limits. Banks need to allow for the additional credit risk, and for the impact on their liquidity risk, that might result from customers with authorised overdrafts increasing the use of their overdraft facilities up to the agreed credit limits.
- **Unauthorised overdraft**: An overdraft facility where a customer can spend more money than they have available in their account but where there is no pre-agreed limit. Banks allowing an unauthorised overdraft will usually have a "shadow limit" for each customer — a pre-determined level up to which they are prepared to lend. Banks can charge for unauthorised overdrafts in a variety of ways. This practice has come under scrutiny from regulators due to concerns around treating customers fairly (e.g., the UK regulators constrained bank charges for unauthorised overdrafts after these became an important income stream, despite being generally paid by more vulnerable customers).

#### Current Account Charges and Barriers to Entry

Current account charges vary by country. In SA and the USA, many customers pay monthly fees and may also pay transaction fees for certain activities. The UK is dominated by "free if in credit" banking (introduced by Midland Bank/HSBC in the 1980s), where no fees are charged on accounts with positive balances. UK banks make their income from interest earned on overdraft balances and from charges for authorised and unauthorised overdrafts.

Complexity and cash handling costs make current accounts expensive to operate. They can act as a barrier to entry to retail banking as a whole, given how important current accounts can be for long-term customer relationships. However, barriers are being reduced by Open Banking and Payment Services Directive 2 (PSD2), which allow customers to authorise the transfer of their current account information to trusted third parties through Open APIs. This means new entrants can gather customers' personal data without having to offer current accounts themselves.

#### Operational Risks of Current Accounts

The provision of current accounts creates various operational risks, in addition to the funding and credit risk associated with overdraft lending:

- **KYC/AML failures**: Fines for failing to detect money laundering or payments to sanctioned individuals or countries can be very large (e.g., HSBC was fined $1.9 billion in 2017; NatWest was fined £265 million in 2021).
- **Legacy systems and IT failures**: Banks relying on older IT programs face operational risk when new services are added to current accounts. New entrants using new technologies have an advantage over incumbents.
- **Treating customers fairly**: Regulators may intervene if banks are deemed to have been unfair to vulnerable customers, such as charging disproportionately for unauthorised overdrafts.
- **Fraud**:
  - *First party fraud*: An account is opened under false pretences (e.g., to create a student overdraft with no intention of repaying).
  - *Second party fraud*: An individual allows somebody else to use their account for fraudulent purposes (e.g., money laundering — they accept cash and then make an electronic payment to the criminal's real account).
  - *Third party fraud*: A customer's account is taken over or used without their consent. Banks may need to use two-factor authentication (knowledge, possession, and/or inherent biometrics).

### Credit Cards

A credit card provides payment services and short-term borrowing up to an agreed credit limit. Credit card borrowing is known as **revolving credit** as the line of credit to a customer is automatically renewed as prior debts are paid off. The credit card holder can spend up to the agreed borrowing limit and must pay back at least a minimum amount each month.

The two main credit card schemes, VISA and MasterCard, are **four-party schemes**:

1. **The customer**: Holds credit cards to spend without demonstrating creditworthiness at point of purchase, and to borrow (at least until the next monthly payment date).
2. **The card issuer** (normally a bank): Decides how much credit to give each customer, tracks and records transactions, sends monthly statements. Makes money from interest on borrowing (if customers do not repay in full), service fees, and interchange fees.
3. **The retailer**: Signs up to an "honour all cards" rule — any store displaying a VISA or MasterCard sign must accept payments made with a valid scheme credit card, regardless of credit, location or currency, provided they follow the required security checks.
4. **The merchant acquirer**: Provides the necessary payments technology and support to retailers; agrees terms and signs up retailers to accept payments through the card scheme.

**American Express (Amex)** is a three-party scheme, where the bank issues cards to customers and also acts as the merchant acquirer for retailers.

**Interchange fees** are amounts deducted from payments made to retailers. They are split between the card issuer, merchant acquirer and scheme itself. The interchange fee charged to the retailer depends on the credit card network, type of card used, and type of transaction. Regulators in the UK and EU have challenged the level of interchange payments, claiming that merchants pay too much.

Credit cards come with significant fraud risks (especially for online payments) and regulatory risk.

Banks separate customers into behavioural groups:

- **Transactors**: Customers that pay off their balances in full every month. The card issuer receives interchange fees on transactions, but for transactors with only limited spending, interchange fees do not cover the operating costs of their credit cards.
- **Revolvers**: Customers that carry credit balances forward from one month to the next. Revolvers can be high risk if they only pay the minimum amount required each month — credit card borrowings are unsecured, and credit losses are likely to be high in periods of pressure on household incomes. Banks often increase the interest rate if a customer misses a monthly minimum payment.
- **Spend revolvers**: The most profitable customers — they spend on their cards each month and pay off their credit card borrowings over time. Banks earn both interchange fees on spending and interest on borrowing, but generally do not suffer credit losses.

### Savings Accounts

Some banks pay interest on current account savings balances to attract new customers, but most banks in many parts of the world do not pay interest, or only pay interest at a low rate. So, most customers hold only enough cash for expected outgoings in their current accounts and hold larger amounts of cash savings in deposit accounts.

#### Instant Access Savings Accounts

Instant access savings accounts allow savers to deposit money and to withdraw it at any time they wish. In many countries, savings are guaranteed up to a certain amount by a deposit insurance scheme, the costs of which are borne by the banks.

Access channels include: branch only, online only, telephone banking, passbook (common with mutual institutions like building societies), or all channels.

Instant access accounts in some regions seek to attract new deposits by offering a higher rate of interest on new deposits, creating a system of **front book and back book pricing**. A new account might offer a higher rate, possibly with a temporary bonus rate for the first six months or year. In many regions, this practice is not allowed by regulation.

The interest rate paid can be tiered by balance size. However, because there is a greater risk of large deposits (above the guarantee limit) being withdrawn in times of stress, the value of larger deposits to banks is limited by their need to hold greater amounts of HQLA against deposits above the amount that is guaranteed.

#### Notice Accounts

Savers in notice accounts must give their bank notice of withdrawals an agreed period before the withdrawals are made. The notice period is often 30, 60 or 90 days. Interest rates on notice accounts are usually variable rate.

Notice accounts often pay a higher rate of interest than instant access accounts because these deposits are more valuable to the bank — it does not need to hold HQLA against them because they cannot be withdrawn during a 30-day period of liquidity stress.

#### Term Deposits

Term deposits are a form of savings where customers deposit money for fixed periods of time, often one, three or five years. These generally are fixed rate. Term deposits are more valuable to the bank because they cannot be withdrawn in a period of liquidity stress and therefore do not require banks to hold HQLA as liquidity buffers against them. Banks usually offer higher rates of interest on these than on instant access savings accounts.

However, term deposits are less flexible and more risky for savers (e.g., holders cannot switch to new bonds with higher interest rates, and they cannot access their savings except possibly in limited circumstances which may garner high early-withdrawal fees).

Interest rates paid on term deposits vary according to the yield curve, the bank's need for term deposits and to an extent the perceived credit risk of the bank. In line with the normally upward-sloping yield curve, interest rates are generally higher on longer-term deposits. Banks can enhance profitability by auto-renewing fixed deposits when they mature (provided local regulations permit this).

#### Money Market Mutual Funds

Money market accounts are an American product introduced in 1969 to circumvent 1930s rules designed to prevent bank runs. Money market mutual funds are a form of unit-linked account that invests units in short-term debt (less than three months), whose units are cashed in at a nominal value of $1.

Money market funds were seen as an alternative to savings accounts, and were trusted before the 2008 financial crisis because no money market fund had ever "broken the buck" (i.e., the value of units had never fallen below $1). When Lehman Brothers failed in 2008, the Reserve Primary Fund — the oldest US money market mutual fund — had 1.2% of its funds invested in Lehman Brothers' commercial paper. The Fund was forced to "break the buck" and a panic ensued, with the funds holding about $3.6 trillion (roughly half the value of US bank deposits guaranteed by the FDIC). The US Treasury put $50 billion behind a new insurance fund for money market funds to contain the crisis.

### Personal Loans

Personal loans are unsecured loans, taken out by individuals and paid back over fixed periods, typically between one and seven years. Interest rates on personal loans are usually fixed for the duration of the loan. There may also be early repayment charges when loans are repaid early.

The interest rate on a personal loan normally reflects the size of the loan and the creditworthiness of the borrower. Personal loans use **risk-based pricing** to reflect the expected credit risk of individual borrowers. This enables banks to offer loans to higher-risk customers (e.g., a bank might decide that a customer is too risky for a personal loan at 7%, but that a loan at 15% would be profitable, allowing for expected credit losses).

Regulators may set rules to avoid higher-risk borrowers being misled by low headline rates. For example, in the UK, banks can only advertise a headline rate if over 50% of borrowers receive that rate or lower. In SA, the **National Credit Act** specifies the maximum lending rate that could be charged to retail customers.

Personal loans are often used to consolidate more expensive debts and provide a structured repayment solution — for example, to help people who have become over-indebted with credit card borrowing.

### Vehicle Finance Loans

Most personal loans are unsecured. However, car loans are **secured on the cars purchased**. Vehicle finance enables a customer to pay for the vehicle in monthly instalments over a period of time (typically 12–84 months). Some payment options allow for a large balloon payment at the end of the contract to reduce monthly instalments.

**Personal Contract Purchase (PCP) / Guaranteed Future Value (GFV) agreements** have become common for new car purchases. Under these agreements, customers do not purchase their car outright — they lease the car for the period of their agreement. Monthly costs will be lower than under equivalent normal vehicle finance. At the end of the agreement, the customer can either purchase the car at the agreed price upon the outset by refinancing or other means, or hand back the vehicle.

A key issue in PCP agreements from the bank's point of view is the **residual value of the car** at the end of the agreement. Banks are exposed to **residual value risk** — that cars will be worth less than their expected values at the end of PCP agreements. Banks seek to mitigate this by setting annual limits on cars' mileage, with higher PCP payments for greater annual mileage limits to allow for lower expected residual values.

**Hire purchase loans** are also used to spread the cost of buying cars. Customers do not own their car until the hire purchase loan has been repaid in full. Customers get the use of their car, but they cannot sell it on to anybody else without the lender's permission. In the event of early termination, the car reverts to the lender and the borrower may need to make an additional payment.

### Mortgages

A mortgage is a loan taken out to purchase a property and the loan is **secured against the property**. If the borrower defaults, the lender can repossess the property and sell it to recover its debt. Mortgages may be residential (where the borrower lives in the property), buy-to-let (where the property is rented to a third party), or commercial real estate mortgages. Interest rates on mortgages can be fixed or variable. Capital is normally repaid over the term of the mortgage, though interest-only mortgages may also be available.

**Key risk considerations:**

- Banks assess the **probability of default** — not only under current circumstances, but also if mortgage rates are variable and interest rates increase.
- Banks assess the **loss given default** — the key method is based on **loan-to-value (LTV) ratios** (the amount of the mortgage divided by the value of the property). With a 70% LTV, the house price must fall by 30% before the bank is exposed to a loss.
- Rising unemployment in a period of recession can lead to higher levels of defaults on mortgages — at a time when property prices may well be depressed by higher interest rates.
- Mortgage sales are highly regulated, particularly in regard to the provision of advice. Banks may be reluctant to provide too much information at the outset in case they are deemed to be giving advice before entering into the formal advice process.

**Interest rate structure**: In full-service banks, mortgages are funded mainly by short-term variable-rate deposits. Banks are willing to accept the mismatch between the behavioural maturities of their mortgages and deposits, but wish to be able to reprice their mortgage books if deposit interest rates increase. This influences the design of mortgages in some countries, where many mortgages offer a fixed rate for an initial period of two years or longer, with higher rates for longer periods. After the initial period, the interest rate reverts to the lender's standard variable rate.

**US mortgage market**: Dominated by Government Sponsored Entities (GSEs) such as Freddie Mac and Fannie Mae. The design of US mortgages is influenced by requirements of the Federal Housing Administration. US banks arrange mortgages and then sell them to the GSEs, which may package mortgages as **mortgage-backed securities (MBS)** and sell them to investors. This was a fundamental driver of the Banking Crisis of 2007/08, where much of the bubble centred on sub-prime mortgages packaged as CDOs.

**Mortgage-related systemic risks**: Mortgage lending can contribute to periodic booms and busts. The availability of larger mortgages at higher LTV ratios can push up house prices. A credit-fuelled housing bubble can start when house prices are rising, credit losses are low, and banks are willing to offer mortgages at higher LTV ratios and lower interest rates. This pattern also contributed to the "lost decade" in Japan after property and stock market bubbles burst in the late 1980s.

### Credit Insurance

Credit insurance, known as **Personal Protection Insurance (PPI)** in the UK, is insurance that continues to repay a loan if the borrower is unable to make repayments because of events such as illness, death, accident or involuntary redundancy. The type of cover varies across providers.

In SA, it is mandatory for credit insurance to cover repayments for up to 12 months if a customer is unemployed or unable to earn an income (and not necessarily due to illness).

Historically in the UK, PPI was offered and even bundled with mortgages, credit cards and personal loans. It became widespread practice for competitively priced loans to be cross-subsidised by sales of profitable PPI. Regulators required banks to repay the full PPI premium plus a high rate of simple interest — costs to UK banks have been in excess of £50 billion.

As for retail banking, corporate banking services include payments, deposits and loans, but corporate banks may also offer services such as foreign exchange and risk management.

### Payments

Given the scale of payments made by large companies, their bank will normally allow them to access domestic and international payment systems through terminals in their offices. Banks may charge corporate customers for payment services or offer them at a discounted rate or even "for free" as part of an overall customer relationship.

Companies may request corporate credit cards for their employees to pay for and keep records of business expenses, and to enable employees to separate their personal and business expenses.

### Foreign Exchange

International trade can require payments to be made in several different currencies. Corporate banks offer businesses bank accounts in a range of currencies, simplifying making and receiving payments in different countries. Corporate banks provide foreign currency conversions for companies, as they do for individuals. However, for large corporate transactions, the bid-offer spread is likely to be much narrower.

### Corporate Savings

Corporate customers may place their cash in deposit accounts with banks, in their domestic currency and in various foreign currencies. However, banks are likely to prefer retail deposits, because of their greater stability, given that retail deposits are protected (up to some amount) in many countries by deposit guarantee schemes.

### Corporate Loans

A corporate loan is a loan made to a corporate banking customer. Loans made to smaller companies supported by retail banking are generally known as business loans.

Corporate loans are normally **variable rate** (floating rate), with the rate of interest being set relative to a defined benchmark:

- Before the financial crisis of 2007-08, the commonly used benchmark was **LIBOR** (London Inter-Bank Offered Rate), calculated daily using estimates submitted by individual banks of their cost of borrowing. After the banking crisis, some banks were found to have submitted low estimates, to reassure markets about their financial position and ability to attract funding. LIBOR was discontinued at the end of 2021.
- Variable rate loans are now priced relative to a risk-free interest rate such as **SONIA** (Sterling Overnight Interest Average, administered by the Bank of England). The equivalent benchmark for SA is **ZARONIA** (South African Rand Overnight Index Average, published by the SARB).

A corporate loan can be secured or unsecured, with lower interest rates on secured loans than on unsecured loans because of lower expected losses given default. In addition to seeking security, banks reduce credit risk by setting out **loan covenants**, which the borrower must satisfy at all times:

- **Negative covenants**: Things the borrower must not do (e.g., not exceeding a stated debt to income ratio, not taking on additional debt without the bank's permission).
- **Positive covenants**: Things the borrower must do (e.g., providing the bank with fully audited accounts every quarter).

### Project Finance

A company undertaking a large, long-term project might fund it by borrowing against its own balance sheet. However, it is often cheaper and safer for the company to limit the recourse of lenders (the ability of lenders to seek loan repayment) by borrowing against the infrastructure or industrial project on which the company is working. In this case, the loan repayments will be met by the cashflows generated by the project.

A project finance loan will normally be made to a **Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV)** — a limited liability company whose sole purpose is to undertake the project. It generally sub-contracts the work to be done. Contractors may offer guarantees over timescales and performance that the lender can claim against if the SPV defaults on the loan.

### Syndicated Loans

A loan to a large company, or for a large project, might be too large for a single bank to extend without taking on excessive credit concentration risk. The lending risk can be reduced by spreading the loan between a number of different lenders who form a **syndicate** to collectively provide the loan.

A syndicated loan might be set up as a fixed amount, as a flexible credit facility, or both. One or more banks will become the **lead arranger(s)** — typically the largest lenders, or those with the closest relationship with the borrower or the greatest experience in managing credit risk and putting together a syndicate.

Syndicated loans can be **"underwritten"**, meaning the lead arrangers guarantee to meet the obligations of the agreed loan regardless of finding other banks to join the syndicate. Alternatively, **"best-efforts" syndication** means the amount the borrower receives depends on how successful those best-efforts are. Small, syndicated loans can be set up between an agreed set of banks as a "club" deal.

Each bank on the syndicate undertakes a bilateral agreement with the borrower. A trustee might accept security on behalf of all banks to simplify the process, and an agent is appointed with an administrative role between the lending banks and the borrower.

### Commercial Real Estate Lending

Commercial real estate loans can be used to purchase properties such as offices, shops or warehouses that are used by businesses, or to purchase properties such as shopping centres that are let out. Property-related loans can also be made to property developers, to redevelop existing properties or to build new properties.

Almost all real estate lending is secured against the value of properties. Large projects might be financed using project finance and SPVs or syndicated loans. A maximum LTV ratio may be applied to a property loan.

Loan covenants include normal financial requirements (loan to income ratios and debt servicing ratios), operational requirements (financial reporting), as well as property-specific requirements (e.g., that the borrower must maintain adequate insurance on the property, or that there must be no material detriment to the condition of the property).

Types of loans in this space include:

- **Bridging finance**: Short-term finance to pay for building or development work before properties are sold or before income can be generated by leasing the property. Commonly used for refurbishment taking between three months and two years.
- **Development finance loans**: Relatively short terms (often less than three years). Interest-only, with capital repaid after the properties are completed. Often variable rate, drawn down in tranches so that money is only borrowed when needed for the next phase of development.
- **Investment finance**: More like traditional mortgages. Loans secured on properties for which professional valuations are required. Loan terms might be 20 years for residential property but only 15 years for commercial real estate, which is more prone to deterioration. Can be fixed or variable rate, and interest-only or capital and interest. These loans can sometimes allow payment holidays, which are helpful if there is a void period in the leasing of a property.

### Equipment Leasing

Leasing takes three main forms. Throughout the period of a lease, the borrower has responsibility for maintaining and insuring the equipment or asset being leased.

- **Finance leases**: A loan that allows a firm to use, and ultimately own, a piece of equipment. While the loan is being repaid, the borrower can use the equipment, which is legally owned by the bank or provider of the financial lease. Finance leases are often for more than three years.
- **Operational leases**: Enable companies to use pieces of equipment without ever owning them. With an operational lease, the bank takes on **residual value risk** — the monthly charge on the lease must cover the interest cost and the expected depreciation of the equipment over the lease period. If depreciation is greater than expected, the bank will make a loss from selling the equipment at the end of the lease. Common with trains, planes and ships (airlines often lease planes for a certain period; banks often re-lease planes because their operational life can be twenty years or more).
- **Contract hire / contract leases**: Similar to operational leases, common when a company leases a fleet of company cars. The main difference is that the leasing company takes some responsibility for maintaining the cars (repairs and servicing).

### Trade Finance

Trade finance is a collection of short-term financial products that help businesses manage their cashflows and make international payments. These services emerged in the Middle Ages when European trade expanded beyond national boundaries.

#### Letters of Credit

A letter of credit is a document issued by a bank that guarantees a payment will be made for a given amount on a given date. It simplifies trade when buyers and sellers do not know each other well — the issuing bank is taking on the counterparty credit risk that the buyer will pay the seller.

The typical four-step process:

1. A deal is agreed between buyer and seller. The buyer's bank issues the seller with a letter of credit.
2. An intermediary transports the goods and inspects them for quantity and quality. The seller receives a "bill of lading" as proof of the transaction.
3. The seller sends the bill of lading to its own bank as proof of sale and receives the agreed payment. The bill of lading goes to the issuing bank and then to the buyer.
4. The buyer exchanges the bill of lading for the goods purchased.

Variants include:

- **Commercial letter of credit**: The issuing bank makes the payment directly.
- **Standby letter of credit**: A form of insurance — the issuing bank only pays the stated amount if the seller cannot do so.
- **Revolving letter of credit**: The holder can make multiple uses of the facility up to a certain limit within an agreed period (often a year).
- **Confirmed letter of credit**: Has a second confirming bank (usually the seller's bank) guaranteeing payment if both the holder and the issuing bank default.

#### Bills of Exchange

A bill of exchange serves a function similar to a letter of credit, by de-risking the payment to the seller of goods. It is a legally binding agreement made by one party to make a specified payment on a specific date (a time draft), or on demand (a sight draft). It can be issued by banks or by companies.

- **Drawee**: The party committed to making the payment (who signs and accepts it).
- **Drawer**: The party who created the bill of exchange (usually the seller of the goods).
- **Payee**: The party to be paid (often the seller, or a bank that has extended credit to the seller).

A bill of exchange can be **transferred** (endorsed) to a new party. Transferability allows a bill of exchange to be **discounted** — the buyer of the bill pays a discounted amount of the face value to the original payee, which gets the money earlier and no longer has any counterparty risk.

#### Trade Loans

A trade loan is a short-term revolving credit facility that funds the period between a business paying for goods and receiving money from selling them. Documentation (invoices and transportation documents) must be specified before any drawdown of credit can occur, as a step to protect the issuing bank. The interest rate charged reflects the credit risk of the borrower and is usually fixed in advance. Facilities are generally set up to last from one month to up to one year. This form of lending can be used to separate trade requirements from more general business overdrafts.

### Transactional Services

Investment banks are active in sales and trading of bonds (government bonds, corporate bonds and asset-backed securities) and equities. They are also active in derivatives, including swaps (interest rate swaps, currency swaps and credit default swaps) and options. Derivatives may be exchange-traded or over-the-counter (OTC). In addition, many investment banks are active in currencies and some are active in commodities (including oil and gas, metals and agricultural goods).

#### Derivatives

A derivative is a contract based on an underlying asset or index. Financial derivatives may be based on bonds, equities, interest rates, currencies, commodities or other types of assets. Investors, businesses and financial institutions can use derivatives to reduce risk, take financial positions and offer risk management services to their customers.

##### Swaps

Swaps are a form of derivative contract under which two parties agree to swap payments for an agreed period. Swaps are OTC contracts agreed directly between two parties and are not traded on an exchange. A swap agreement defines a notional capital amount on which cashflows are exchanged at agreed dates for an agreed period of time.

- **Interest rate swaps**: Most bank loans and deposits are variable rate. However, banks may offer fixed rate loans such as mortgages, not fully funded by fixed rate deposits. Banks may choose to manage the interest rate risk in their banking book (IRRBB) through interest rate swaps.
- **Currency swaps**: The two parties agree to exchange interest and/or principal payments on a notional or actual amount in different currencies.
- **Credit Default Swaps (CDS)**: A CDS provides a form of credit insurance. It is an OTC derivative agreed between two parties and is the most common form of credit derivative. The **buyer of a CDS** (protection buyer) pays the seller (protection seller) an agreed premium for an agreed period. If the reference entity defaults during this period, the buyer of the CDS is compensated. This allows credit risk to be transferred without the need to sell the underlying financial asset. The credit spread, stated in basis points, indicates how much the seller of a CDS needs to receive to justify taking on the credit risk. Banks use CDSs to allow them to offer large loans to key corporate clients while avoiding excessive credit concentration risk. CDSs can also be used for trading by speculative investors who think the creditworthiness of underlying assets is likely to decline.

##### Options

An option is a form of derivative contract that gives the buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy or to sell an asset at an agreed price at or up to an agreed date in the future.

- A **call option** gives the buyer the right to buy an underlying asset (often a share) at an agreed strike price at an agreed date. A call option is "in the money" if the share price is above the strike price, indicating a positive payoff. The seller of a **naked call option** (i.e., not owning the underlying share) has theoretically unlimited risk.
- A **put option** gives the buyer the right to sell an underlying asset at an agreed strike price at an agreed date. A put option is "in the money" if the share price is below the strike price. Unlike the unlimited risk of selling a naked call option, the downside risk of selling a put option is capped by the strike price.
- **European options** can only be exercised on the agreed date; **American options** can be exercised at any time up to the agreed date (though it generally does not make financial sense to exercise early if the option is priced correctly).
- **Exchange-traded ("vanilla") options**: Based on defined contracts issued by an exchange (e.g., the Chicago Options Exchange Board). Firms selling options on an exchange post collateral margin to demonstrate they are able to meet their obligations as the option value changes.
- **OTC ("exotic") options**: Not traded on an exchange — bilateral contracts offering more flexibility to design bespoke derivatives with payout structures that meet the needs of individual companies. Can include features such as path dependency (where the payoff of an option depends on the price history of an underlying asset), or dependency on two assets simultaneously.

#### Asset-Backed Securities (ABS)

An asset-backed security (ABS) is an interest-bearing security backed by a pool of financial assets that are ring-fenced. Investors are repaid from interest and capital payments made from the pool of assets. Asset-backed securities may be backed by mortgages, credit card receivables, car loans, unsecured personal loans or other types of loans and receivables. ABSs are normally issued in **tranches** (often called A, B and C), with different yields and different levels of risk.

The risk associated with ABSs is reduced in two ways: firstly, by **diversification** of assets in the underlying pool; and secondly by creating **tranches** with different levels of risk.

**Tranche structure** (from most junior/risky to most senior/safe):

- **Equity tranche**: The highest-risk tranche — if anything goes wrong with the pool of assets, investors in this tranche will be the first to lose out. It is not unusual for the bank issuing the ABS to retain part or all of the equity tranche, demonstrating its confidence in the ABS (typically ~2%).
- **Mezzanine tranche**: Will suffer losses next, if the equity tranche is wiped out. Marketed as having a risk profile similar to higher-risk junk bonds — higher yield but higher risk (~3%).
- **Graded tranche**: A middle tier (~10%).
- **Investment grade (senior) tranche**: The highest quality tranches, generally regarded as being similar to relatively safe corporate bonds (typically ~85% of the structure, rated AAA).

It is normal for the senior tranches of an ABS to be rated by credit rating agencies such as Fitch, Moody's and Standard & Poor's. Investment banks estimate the risks associated with each tranche using a combination of past experience, assumptions and models.

In the case of bank loans in pools of assets, there is likely to be a degree of positive correlation, because credit risks are lower in periods of growth and higher in periods of recession. Reliance on past measures of correlation can be dangerous because, when a market dislocation occurs, credit risks associated with bank loans can become more correlated.

##### Mortgage-Backed Securities (MBS)

Mortgage-backed securities (MBSs) form a category of ABSs important for retail banks who wish to realise capital to support further lending. In MBSs, the financial assets used to create ABSs are ring-fenced pools of mortgages.

The **securitisation process** for MBSs:

1. The originating bank pools the mortgages it wishes to remove from its balance sheet and transfers them to a **Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV)** — a legally defined company that is separate from the issuing bank. This separation is a key feature of a securitisation. Investors need to assess the credit risk associated with the assets held in the SPV, but do not need to worry about the creditworthiness of the issuing bank. Even if the issuing bank fails, investors will still continue to receive capital and interest payments from the mortgages in the SPV.
2. The SPV issues bonds (potentially in tranches with different levels of risk), which may be rated by credit rating agencies.
3. An underwriter (typically an investment bank) analyses the risk of the assets held in the SPV and advises on the best way to structure the tranches.
4. The originating bank often retains an operational link with the mortgages, collecting interest payments and capital repayments, and undertaking debt recovery action against mortgages that have defaulted. It is paid fees for this work.

The excess spread (interest paid by mortgage borrowers greater than total interest paid to investors in the SPV tranches plus any servicing costs) represents profit distributed to the equity investors in the SPV.

##### Collateralised Debt Obligations (CDOs)

CDOs represent the most general form of ABS. As well as financial assets such as mortgages and other types of loans, CDOs can hold bonds or debt instruments issued by other asset-backed securities. This enables them to create more diversified pools of assets. As for securitisations, assets are ring-fenced in an SPV and different tranches with different risk profiles are sold to investors.

A **CDO² (CDO-squared)** is an instrument that provides investor security through a pool of CDO tranches. The CDOs held by the CDO² are referred to as "inner CDOs" and the investment tranches issued by the CDO² are referred to as the "outer tranches." The CDO² is sometimes called the "master" CDO.

Modelling the risks of a CDO² is a complex mathematical process requiring Monte Carlo simulation approaches. An important difficulty is the challenge of assessing tail risk — the models and correlations used in the simulations can break down under stress. This occurred during the Banking Crisis of 2007-08, when mortgage-backed CDOs (particularly those including sub-prime mortgages) were used to create CDO²s. A distressed credit environment led to significantly greater losses in CDO² senior tranches than had been predicted by the models used.

Before the Banking Crisis of 2007-08, **CDO³ (CDO-cubed)** structures were also created, where the assets in the outer CDO were tranches of CDO²s. These are probably too complex for their risks to be modelled accurately.

### Advisory Services

#### Mergers & Acquisitions (M&A)

Investment banks offer advisory services on mergers and acquisitions. M&A is often seen as the most glamorous and potentially the most profitable area within investment banking, though M&A activities experience peaks and troughs that are reflected in expansion and contraction in the number of employees working in M&A.

In acquisitions, it is normal for the buyer to pay a premium to the seller's market value. This premium may be justified by transaction benefits in the form of cost savings and income benefits. Investment banks help clients quantify expected transaction benefits, determine the price to be paid, and help with the negotiation of "social issues" such as the name of the ongoing entity, the location of the head office, and whether key appointments are to be from the buyer or from both sides.

Modern Portfolio Theory (Markowitz, 1952) suggested that investors could maximise returns on their investments for a given risk through a properly diversified share portfolio — meaning investors no longer needed to invest in conglomerates. This has led to many conglomerates selling non-core businesses and companies focusing on their core competences.

In addition to mergers and acquisitions, advisory teams offer services on disposals, **Initial Public Offerings (IPOs)** when companies come to the market, and **Leveraged Buy Outs (LBOs)** when public companies are taken private.

#### Bond Issuance

Investment banks can help governments, local authorities and companies raise money by issuing bonds.

Issuing government bonds is relatively straightforward — pricing can be determined by comparison with existing bonds being actively traded in the secondary market.

Issuing corporate bonds is less straightforward. The investment bank advising the borrower has to consider questions such as:

- Is new debt allowed by the company's memorandum and articles of association?
- Is new debt permitted by other debt holders?
- Will the new bonds be rated by a credit rating agency?
- What duration will be attractive to the borrowing company and to investors?
- What interest rate is appropriate?
- Does the investment bank want to underwrite the entire bond issue, or create a syndicate with other financial institutions?

The investment bank needs to distil this information into a detailed **prospectus** for possible investors. They need to set the interest rate on the bond high enough to attract sufficient investors and low enough to be acceptable to the bond-issuing company. The investment bank may help reduce the interest rate on the bond by offering **stock warrants** that can be converted into shares at an agreed price at an agreed future date. Warrants act like call options and have value to investors, reducing the interest rate they require on the bond. However, warrants will only be exercised if the company borrowing money is doing well.

#### Equity Issuance

When a company wishes to sell shares to the public on an exchange, investment banks play a vital role in **price discovery**. Investment banks are trusted by all parties to manage potential conflicts of interest as they bring together companies looking to sell shares with groups of investors.

For an **IPO (Initial Public Offering)**, little or no pricing information exists when private companies want to sell shares on an exchange for the first time. Investors may only be willing to buy shares at a low price allowing for possible risks, but the company issuing shares will not be willing to accept such a low price. Price discovery by investment banks makes an important contribution to the effectiveness of markets for companies selling shares and for investors buying shares.

In addition to price discovery, the investment bank will need to ensure that the company satisfies the requirements of the exchange through which it wishes to sell shares. This will involve corporate governance and public disclosures. The investment bank will prepare a **prospectus** for potential investors.

The investment bank may **underwrite** the IPO, agreeing to buy any shares that are not sold to investors (with fees reflecting its underwriting risk as well as its advisory services). For a large IPO, it is likely that a syndicate of investment banks will spread the underwriting risk and ensure a wide distribution of shares.

The fees charged for an IPO are generally expressed as a percentage of the amount raised, and can vary from 3% for large IPOs to 7% for smaller IPOs. The price discovery role of investment banks has had the effect of limiting competition in equity issuance.
