# Anti-Money Laundering (AML) and Know Your Customer (KYC)

AML and KYC represent a critical subset of operational [[01-risk_management|risk management]]. While the legal definition of money laundering differs across jurisdictions, the [[bis|Basel Committee on Banking Supervision]] ([[bis|BCBS]]) defines it as "acts that acquire, transfer, or conceal proceeds of a certain criminal act." The underlying criminal act is known as the **predicate crime** (e.g. tax evasion, human and drug trafficking, bribery).

## Anti-Money Laundering (AML)

### The Three Phases of Money Laundering

According to the [[bis|BIS]] framework, money laundering typically consists of three phases:

1. **Placement** — illegally obtained funds are brought into the financial system (e.g. depositing cash received from drug trafficking into a bank account).
2. **Layering** — the true origin of funds is concealed (e.g. moving bank account balances, often across national borders).
3. **Integration** — the disguised funds are used for other legal purposes of investment or consumption.

### International AML Bodies

Two key international bodies set standards and guidance:

**[[bis|BCBS]]** — issues AML/CFT guidance and incorporates recommendations from the FATF.

**Financial Action Task Force (FATF)** — an independent inter-governmental body set up to combat money laundering, terrorist financing, and the financing of weapons of mass destruction proliferation. FATF focuses on three main areas:

- Setting AML and countering the financing of terrorism (CFT) standards through regularly updated **FATF Recommendations** (40 recommendations).
- Assessing progress as member countries mutually evaluate one another's jurisdictions against compliance with the FATF Recommendations.
- Identifying trends and engaging with high-risk, non-cooperative jurisdictions.

All member countries should implement the FATF Recommendations. A member country that fails to implement these controls risks being subjected to enhanced due diligence or, worse, **grey-listed** for non-compliance.

### Managing and Mitigating Money Laundering Risks

Banks should follow a **risk-based approach**, which involves:

1. Identifying and assessing inherent risks individually.
2. Applying specific mitigants.
3. Regularly monitoring measures.
4. Documenting strategies.

Potential high-risk categories requiring enhanced due diligence include politically exposed persons (PEPs), business areas with high cash usage (e.g. casinos), and countries flagged as non-compliant by the FATF.

### Three Lines of Defence

The board of directors bears principal responsibility for managing money laundering and financing risks, and implements this via a three lines of defence model:

| Line | Parties | Role |
|---|---|---|
| First | Business units (e.g. retail banking) | Identify, assess, and control risks using customer due diligence controls |
| Second | Chief AML/CFT officer, compliance function, HR, technology | Independently assess AML/CFT controls and report effectiveness to management |
| Third | Internal or external auditors | Compile an independent view of the bank's AML/CFT practices |

### Role of Supervisors

Supervisors interpret, tailor, and implement international AML/CFT standards at the national level. In South Africa, the [[pa|Prudential Authority]] (PA) has issued local regulations under the **Financial Intelligence Centre Act, 2001 (FICA)**. Financial institutions including banks are regularly supervised for FICA compliance. The National Treasury performs periodic evaluations of South Africa's compliance with the FATF 40 recommendations and assesses the effectiveness of AML/CFT controls.

## Know Your Customer (KYC)

The KYC concept is closely interconnected with AML/CFT and plays an integral part in protecting against it. Before accepting a client, a bank should spend enough time collecting and verifying the customer's information and regularly monitor their transactional activity. KYC encompasses five areas:

### 1. Customer Acceptance Policy

Banks must set clear customer acceptance policies and procedures to identify higher-risk customers. Risk factors to assess include the customer's background, occupation (including public or high-profile positions), source of income, country of birth and residence, product usage, nature and purpose of accounts, linked accounts, and business activities.

The amount of due diligence should be commensurate with the perceived risk level. Simple salaried customers require standard customer due diligence (CDD), while high-ranking public officials (e.g. PEPs) warrant **enhanced due diligence**. Acceptance policies should also specify circumstances where the bank may deem the customer risk too high and decide against opening the account or terminate an existing relationship.

### 2. Customer and Beneficial Owner Identification, Verification, and Risk-Profiling

As per FATF Recommendation 10, a customer refers to "any person who enters into a business relationship or carries out an occasional financial transaction with the bank." Banks must apply CDD to customers, persons acting on their behalf, and beneficial owners.

Where a bank cannot adequately identify and verify a customer, it should not establish a relationship or perform transactions. If verification cannot be completed at account opening, the bank must have appropriate policies and procedures in place. If CDD cannot be satisfactorily completed, the bank should block access to the account and raise a **suspicious transaction report (STR)** with the regulator.

Customer information and subsequent transaction data should be used to profile customers' risk — these profiles can flag customers who develop a higher risk profile even after the relationship has been established.

### 3. Ongoing Monitoring

Post account opening, a bank should satisfy itself that customers continue to meet CDD requirements. The bank should continually monitor its banking relationships to identify unusual transactional activity and report suspicious transactions to relevant authorities. Monitoring should be commensurate with the customer's risk profile — higher-risk customers warrant enhanced due diligence.

Jurisdictions consider the following patterns to identify suspicious transactions:

- Transactions that do not make economic sense (e.g. unrealistically high profits or returns on a business venture).
- Staggered/structured transactions (e.g. multiple small transactions when a single large transaction could have been made, and the latter would have breached AML thresholds).
- Unusual transaction withdrawals, either in cash or post account closure.

### 4. Management of Information

For a proper audit trail and to fulfil information requests from supervisors, a bank should keep accurate records of customer information and documents collected during CDD, as well as transaction history. IT systems must be able to cater to the types of information and documents required. According to [[bis|BCBS]] guidelines and **FATF Recommendation 11**, banks should retain customer records, account files, and business correspondence for at least **five years** after the relationship is ended or after the latest transaction.

### 5. Reporting of Suspicious Transactions

A bank should be able to identify suspicious and unusual transaction activity through ongoing monitoring and report these to relevant authorities. A transparent process for identifying, investigating, and reporting suspicious transactions should be established, communicated to all employees, and supported by appropriate staff training.
