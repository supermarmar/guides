# Central Banks

Central banks are banks to governments. They do not have personal or corporate customers but do interact with banks which have reserve accounts at their central bank. Reserves held in these accounts count as high-quality liquid assets and, if needed, can be used to finance an outflow of deposits. Central banks may also engage in money market operations with these banks.

Central banks are responsible for issuing notes and coins, for managing the country's foreign reserves, for managing monetary policy including by setting interest rates and by money market operations, and for ensuring the effectiveness of national payment systems. Central banks are also often responsible for the regulation and supervision of banks in their jurisdiction.

In the UK and South Africa, the central bank ([[bank_of_england|Bank of England]] or [[sarb|SARB]]) is responsible for financial stability, while a subsidiary of the central bank ([[pra|PRA]] or PA) is responsible for the regulation and supervision of banks. In the US, the Federal Reserve Bank (FRB) regulates bank holding companies and certain other categories of bank, while the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC), an independent entity within the US Treasury, regulates national and other types of bank.

## Monetary Policy

A principal responsibility of central banks in many countries is to control inflation. 

Central banks reduce inflation by raising interest rates to reduce demand for borrowing by individuals and companies. At other times, when inflation is low, central banks stimulate demand by lowering interest rates. In the exceptional period after the banking crisis of 2007–2008, when interest rates had been reduced significantly, central banks stimulated demand by **quantitative easing (QE)** — buying large amounts of government bonds and other securities from banks and other financial institutions. QE increases the money supply and reduces the yield on government bonds.

## Money Market Operations

In their regular money market operations with banks, central banks purchase or sell securities, or make temporary loans against securities as collateral, to maintain an appropriate level of liquidity in the banking system. Temporary loans against securities, with the intention of repurchasing the securities, are known as **repurchase agreements** or **repos**. The opposite arrangements are known as **reverse repurchase agreements** or **reverse repos**.

## Financial Stability

Central banks are responsible for financial stability, the importance of which was underlined by the banking crisis of 2007–2008. The **countercyclical capital buffer (CCyB)** — added to the [[bis|Basel]] regulations after the banking crisis — is set by central banks, within bounds specified in the [[bis|Basel]] regulations. It is expected to increase in periods of economic growth, limiting the risk of a credit bubble, and means banks have capital available in a subsequent period of recession. Large systemically important banks must hold an additional capital buffer known as a **systemic risk buffer**. Each bank must also prepare and submit to its supervisor a recovery plan and a resolution plan.

## Supervisory Role

Central banks issue regulations and guidance on matters such as risks not covered in the [[bis|Basel]] regulations and [[02-stress_testing|stress testing]]. A principal element of the annual supervisory review process for individual banks is related to [ICAAP and ILAAP](01-regulatory_environment.md) submissions made by each bank. Central banks also issue statistics on a broad range of economic and banking topics. For example, in the UK, the [[bank_of_england|Bank of England]] issues a Quarterly Bulletin, a Monetary Policy Report (quarterly), and a Financial Stability Report (half yearly).
