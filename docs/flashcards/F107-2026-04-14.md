## Card 2 — IFRS 9 · Easy

Define Significant Increase in Credit Risk (SICR) and state the primary quantitative trigger for Stage 2 migration under IFRS 9.::SICR is the threshold concept in IFRS 9 that determines when a financial asset migrates from Stage 1 (12-month ECL) to Stage 2 (lifetime ECL). ## Card 3 — Operational Risk · Easy

What is the Basel IV Standardised Measurement Approach (SMA) and what did it replace?::The SMA is the single Basel IV method for calculating operational risk capital, introduced in the Basel III finalisation (December 2017) and effective from 2023. It replaced the three Basel II approaches: Basic Indicator Approach (BIA), Standardised Approach (TSA), and Advanced Measurement Approach (AMA). Under SMA, capital = Business Indicator Component (BIC) × multiplier, adjusted by a Loss Component (LC) based on the bank's own historical internal loss data. Banks with higher historical operational losses face higher capital requirements than those with the same BIC but lower loss history, incentivising loss reduction. #flashcard #topic/operational-risk #topic/basel #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-14

## Card 5 — Credit Risk · Easy

What is IRB model backtesting and which two PD calibration tests are most commonly used?::IRB backtesting is the process of comparing a model's predicted outcomes (PD, LGD, EAD estimates) against observed actual outcomes over a validation period, to verify calibration accuracy. For PD, the two most common tests are: (1) Binomial test — tests whether the observed default rate per grade is statistically consistent with the predicted PD, using a one-tailed binomial distribution. Flags grades where PD is systematically underestimated. (2) Traffic light approach (Spiegelhalter test / Normal approximation) — assigns a red/amber/green signal to each grade based on how far observed defaults deviate from predicted PD, accounting for portfolio size. Both must be run at grade level and at portfolio level under CRR Art. 185. #flashcard #topic/credit-risk #topic/risk-management #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-14

## Card 6 — Market Risk · Easy

Define the three primary option Greeks — delta, gamma, and vega — and state what each measures.::Delta (Δ): sensitivity of the option price to a unit change in the underlying asset price. A delta of 0.6 means the option price moves R0.60 for every R1 move in the underlying. Delta-hedging neutralises first-order price risk. Gamma (Γ): rate of change of delta with respect to the underlying price — the second-order (convexity) effect. High gamma means delta changes rapidly; a delta-hedged position must be rebalanced frequently. Vega (ν): sensitivity of the option price to a 1% change in implied volatility. A long option position is long vega — it benefits from rising volatility. Vega is not a Greek letter but is market convention. #flashcard #topic/market-risk #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-14

## Card 7 — Basel IV / Regulation · Easy

What is the Basel IV output floor and what problem does it address?::The output floor (introduced in Basel III finalisation, effective 2025–2028 phased) sets a floor on IRB-calculated RWA at 72.5% of the RWA that would have been produced by the Standardised Approach. I.e., a bank cannot hold less than 72.5% of the SA-equivalent capital even if its internal models produce lower estimates. Problem addressed: wide divergence in RWA across banks using IRB models — studies showed IRB banks held 30–40% less capital than SA peers for comparable portfolios, undermining comparability and competitive neutrality. The floor limits the capital benefit of model optimisation and ensures a minimum level of conservatism. #flashcard #topic/basel #topic/regulation #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-14

## Card 8 — Economic Environment · Easy

Define the SARB repo rate and explain its primary transmission channel to the real economy.::The repo rate (repurchase rate) is the rate at which the South African Reserve Bank lends overnight funds to commercial banks against eligible collateral. It is the SARB's primary monetary policy instrument, set by the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), which meets six times per year. Primary transmission channel: the prime lending rate = repo rate + 3.5% (a spread fixed by convention). When the SARB raises the repo rate, the prime rate rises immediately, increasing the cost of variable-rate credit (mortgages, credit cards, personal loans) — reducing consumer spending and investment, thereby slowing inflation. The SARB targets CPI inflation within the 3–6% target band. #flashcard #topic/risk-management #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-14

## Card 9 — Concentration Risk · Easy

Define single-name concentration risk and state the large exposure limit applicable to SA banks under the Basel large exposures framework.::Single-name concentration risk is the risk of an outsized loss from the default or deterioration of a single counterparty, group of connected counterparties, or sector — where the exposure is disproportionately large relative to the bank's capital. Under the Basel Large Exposures Framework (LEF, incorporated into CRR / SA Banks Act Regulations), the limit for a single exposure to a non-bank counterparty is 25% of Tier 1 capital. For exposures to Global Systemically Important Banks (G-SIBs), the limit is 15% of Tier 1 capital. Breaches must be reported to the PA and cannot be maintained without regulatory approval. Concentration risk not fully captured by Pillar 1 RWA must be assessed and capitalised under Pillar 2 ICAAP. #flashcard #topic/credit-risk #topic/regulation #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-14

## Card 10 — Resolution · Easy

What is TLAC and why was it introduced after the 2008 Global Financial Crisis?::TLAC (Total Loss-Absorbing Capacity) is a minimum requirement for G-SIBs to hold sufficient loss-absorbing instruments that can be bailed-in during resolution — without recourse to taxpayer support. Minimum TLAC = max(16% of RWA from 2019; 18% from 2022; 6% of leverage ratio exposure). Instruments must have maturity > 1 year, cannot be offset by derivatives or be senior deposits. TLAC was introduced because the GFC showed that many G-SIBs were "too big to fail" — their resolution would require government bail-outs since depositor-only bail-ins were politically and operationally infeasible. TLAC ensures that bond investors — not taxpayers — absorb losses first. The MREL (Minimum Requirement for own funds and Eligible Liabilities) is the EU equivalent. SA does not currently have formal TLAC but the SARB's Resolution Framework (2021) moves in this direction. #flashcard #topic/regulation #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-14

## Card 11 — Liquidity Risk · Medium

Explain the NSFR's ASF and RSF factor categories, and describe the dispensation granted to SA banks by the SARB.
?
**Available Stable Funding (ASF)** — reflects how stable and "sticky" a funding source is over a 1-year stress horizon. Higher stability → higher ASF factor:
- Tier 1/Tier 2 capital: ASF = 100%
- Retail and SME deposits (stable, not easily withdrawn): ASF = 90–95%
- Wholesale operational deposits (used for cash management): ASF = 50%
- Short-term wholesale funding (< 6 months from non-financial corporates): ASF = 0%

**Required Stable Funding (RSF)** — reflects how much stable funding an asset type requires, based on its liquidity and encumbrance:
- Level 1 HQLA (sovereign bonds, cash): RSF = 0%
- Residential mortgages (≤ 35% risk weight): RSF = 65%
- Corporate loans (maturity > 1 year): RSF = 65–85%
- Off-balance-sheet items and contingent liabilities: RSF = 5%

**SA dispensation**: The SARB recognised that South Africa has a structural shortage of HQLA — the government bond market is insufficient to supply the HQLA needed for all banks to simultaneously comply with both LCR and NSFR using traditional means. The SARB therefore introduced a Committed Liquidity Facility (CLF) — a backstop whereby banks may count committed lines from the SARB as a HQLA-equivalent for LCR purposes. For NSFR, the PA has allowed modified timelines and phased implementation. This dispensation was formally recognised by BCBS as consistent with Basel III given SA's market structure.

## Card 12 — IFRS 9 · Medium

Explain the mechanics of a 12-month ECL model, covering the role of the PD term structure, marginal PDs, and the incorporation of forward-looking information.
?
**12-month ECL formula**: ECL₁₂ = PD₁₂ × LGD × EAD × Discount factor.

PD₁₂ is the probability of default within 12 months from the reporting date, conditional on no prior default.

**PD term structure**: To calculate lifetime ECL (for Stage 2/3), the model requires a term structure of conditional PDs — the probability of defaulting in each future period given survival to that period. These are called marginal (or conditional) PDs and are derived from the cumulative PD curve:
- Cumulative PD(t) = 1 − (1 − PD₁)^t (simplified geometric approximation for constant PD)
- Marginal PD(t) = Cumulative PD(t) − Cumulative PD(t−1)
- Lifetime ECL = Σ [Marginal PD(t) × LGD(t) × EAD(t) × Discount(t)] over all periods t

**Forward-looking information (FLI)**: IFRS 9 explicitly requires that ECL reflect current and forecast macroeconomic conditions. Banks typically use multiple economic scenarios (e.g. base, upside, downside) weighted by probability:
- ECL_FLI = Σ (Scenario weight × ECL under scenario)
- PDs are adjusted via satellite models linking macroeconomic drivers (GDP growth, unemployment, house price index) to PD.

**Key challenge**: Estimating LGD at the time of default (not at reporting date) and discounting at the EIR (Effective Interest Rate) of the instrument — both of which introduce model complexity.

## Card 13 — Operational Risk · Medium

Explain how the SMA (Basel IV) calculates operational risk capital, distinguishing the Business Indicator Component and the Loss Component, and why the formula is considered superior to the AMA.
?
**Step 1 — Business Indicator (BI)**: A proxy for income-based operational risk exposure, built from three sub-components:
- ILDC (Interest, Lease, Dividend Component) = |Net interest income| + |Net lease income| + Dividends
- SC (Services Component) = max(Fee income, Fee expense) + max(Other operating income, Other operating expense)
- FC (Financial Component) = |Net P&L trading book| + |Net P&L banking book|
BI = ILDC + SC + FC (3-year average)

**Step 2 — Business Indicator Component (BIC)**: BIC = Σ (BI bucket marginal rate × BI increment in each bucket). Three buckets with rates 12%, 15%, 18% for BI < €1bn, €1–30bn, >€30bn respectively.

**Step 3 — Loss Component (LC)**: For banks with ≥ 10 years of loss data: LC = 15 × average annual operational loss over 10 years. The LC is a multiplier applied relative to BIC. Banks with higher historical losses face a higher capital multiplier (Internal Loss Multiplier, ILM = ln[exp(1) − 1 + (LC/BIC)^0.8]).

**Capital requirement** = BIC × ILM (or BIC alone for smaller banks with limited loss data).

**Why superior to AMA**: (1) Comparability — all banks use the same formula; AMA allowed infinite model variation, making Pillar 3 disclosures incomparable. (2) Removes gaming incentive — AMA capital depended heavily on internal model assumptions, which banks could optimise. (3) Loss data incentive preserved — the LC ties capital to actual loss experience, unlike the purely formula-based BIA/TSA.

## Card 14 — Business Model · Medium

Explain Net Interest Margin (NIM) compression: its primary drivers, and the strategic responses available to a retail bank.
?
**NIM = (Interest Income − Interest Expense) / Average Earning Assets.**

**Primary drivers of NIM compression:**

1. **Rate environment**: When the yield curve flattens or inverts, the spread between short-term funding costs and long-term asset yields narrows. Prolonged low rate environments (e.g. post-GFC in Europe) squeezed NIM significantly.

2. **Competitive pressure**: Intensified competition from fintechs and digital banks, particularly for deposits, forces banks to offer higher deposit rates or lower lending rates, compressing both sides of the margin.

3. **Funding mix shift**: If customers migrate from low-cost current/savings accounts to higher-cost term deposits or money market funds (as rates rise), funding costs rise faster than asset repricing.

4. **Asset mix shift**: Regulatory capital requirements (higher RWA for corporate loans vs mortgages) incentivise lending into lower-margin, lower-RWA assets — compressing blended NIM.

**Strategic responses:**

- **Repricing**: Adjusting variable-rate assets quickly when rates rise; locking in fixed-rate funding when rates are high.
- **Product mix**: Shift toward fee-based income (transactional banking, insurance, wealth) to diversify away from NIM dependency.
- **FTP optimisation**: Ensure transfer pricing reflects true marginal cost of funds, preventing internal cross-subsidisation that masks NIM erosion.
- **Asset liability management (ALM)**: Use derivatives (interest rate swaps, floors, caps) to hedge NIM against adverse rate moves, managed via ALCO.
- **Cost efficiency**: Reduce cost-to-income ratio to offset lower revenue per rand of assets.

## Card 15 — Credit Risk · Medium

Describe the IRB model validation requirements under CRR Article 185, covering backtesting frequency, benchmarking, and the distinction between ongoing monitoring and periodic review.
?
**CRR Article 185** sets out the minimum validation requirements for IRB models used for regulatory capital:

**Backtesting (calibration testing)**:
- Must be performed at least **annually** for PD, LGD, and EAD.
- PD backtesting: compare observed default rates per rating grade to predicted PDs over the most recent year and over longer time periods (at least 3–5 years where possible).
- LGD backtesting: compare realised LGDs from workouts completed in the period against modelled LGD estimates.
- Must be documented with statistical test results (binomial test, Chi-square test) and results shared with senior management.

**Benchmarking**:
- Internal estimates must be benchmarked against external data sources (e.g. rating agency default rates, industry surveys, SARB supervisory benchmarks) to verify they are not systematically biased.
- Where a bank's PD estimates are materially lower than external benchmarks without a clear portfolio-specific justification, the PA may impose a floor or add-on.

**Ongoing monitoring vs periodic review**:
- **Ongoing monitoring** (at least quarterly): track model performance indicators — default rates, rating migration, discriminatory power (Gini, AUC). Flag early warning signals.
- **Periodic full review** (at least every 3 years, or sooner after major portfolio/economic changes): comprehensive revalidation including re-estimation of parameters, population stability testing (PSI), and reassessment of model assumptions.

All validation activities must be carried out by a validation function **independent** of the model development function (CRR Art. 189).

## Card 16 — Market Risk · Medium

Explain the delta-gamma approximation for option portfolio risk, state when gamma becomes critical, and describe how a derivatives desk manages gamma exposure.
?
**Delta approximation (first-order):**
ΔV ≈ Δ × ΔS

For small moves in the underlying (ΔS), the change in option portfolio value (ΔV) is approximately delta times the price move. A delta-hedged portfolio has Δ = 0, so small moves produce near-zero P&L.

**Delta-gamma approximation (second-order — Taylor expansion):**
ΔV ≈ Δ × ΔS + ½ × Γ × ΔS²

Gamma captures the curvature (convexity) of the option value vs. underlying price relationship. For a long option position, Γ > 0, so the P&L is convex: large moves in either direction produce gains. For a short option position, Γ < 0 — large moves produce losses (the "short gamma" danger).

**When gamma is critical:**
- Near-the-money options approaching expiry have extremely high gamma — small price moves can swing delta dramatically, requiring rapid delta re-hedging.
- Stressed markets with large intraday moves amplify gamma P&L — which is why VaR (based on normal distributions) systematically underestimates option book losses in stress.

**Managing gamma exposure:**
- Delta-hedging: buy/sell underlying to offset delta. Must be done frequently when gamma is high (costly).
- Gamma-hedging: trade other options to offset gamma directly (e.g. buy options if net gamma is negative). This is more expensive but reduces rebalancing frequency.
- Limits: desks typically operate under gamma limits per underlying, with hard stops triggering risk reduction.

## Card 17 — Resolution · Medium

Explain the MREL/TLAC framework: what instruments qualify, how the requirement is structured, and how it interacts with Basel capital requirements.
?
**Purpose**: TLAC (G-SIBs globally) and MREL (EU/UK banks) ensure that banks enter resolution with sufficient instruments that can absorb losses and recapitalise the bank without taxpayer support — the "bail-in" principle.

**Qualifying instruments** must be:
- Subordinated (rank below senior unsecured operational liabilities)
- Maturity ≥ 1 year remaining
- Not subject to netting or offset
- Issued from the resolution entity (not from operating subsidiaries unless in internal TLAC structures)
Typically: Tier 2 subordinated bonds, senior non-preferred debt (eligible in EU), holdco senior unsecured bonds.

**Requirement structure** (TLAC for G-SIBs):
- Minimum 16% of RWA by 2019, 18% by 2022
- Also minimum 6% of leverage exposure (Tier 1 leverage ratio denominator)
- The higher of the two applies

**Interaction with Basel capital**:
- Capital instruments (CET1, AT1, Tier 2) double-count toward TLAC — they absorb losses first.
- TLAC = Capital requirement (8% minimum) + Additional TLAC-eligible debt
- This means banks must issue external debt beyond their Basel capital stack to meet TLAC.
- The TLAC requirement does NOT replace Basel capital; it sits on top of it.

**SA context**: South Africa does not currently have a formal TLAC/MREL framework, but the SARB Resolution Framework (published 2021) and draft Financial Sector Laws Amendment Bill introduce a bail-in tool, moving SA toward TLAC-alignment for D-SIBs in future.

## Card 18 — IFRS 9 + Capital Management · Hard

(a) Explain the treatment of the difference between IFRS 9 ECL provisions and IRB Expected Loss under the Basel framework, including the CET1 deduction mechanism. (b) A bank reports IFRS 9 Stage 1/2 provisions of R450m. Its IRB Expected Loss (EL) for the non-defaulted portfolio is R600m. Its Stage 3 specific provisions are R200m and IRB EL for defaulted exposures is R180m. Calculate the regulatory capital impact. (c) What is the policy rationale for this asymmetric treatment?
?
**(a) ECL vs IRB EL — regulatory treatment:**

Under Basel III / CRR Article 159, the comparison between accounting provisions and regulatory EL is made separately for defaulted and non-defaulted exposures:

For **non-defaulted exposures**:
- If ECL provisions > IRB EL → Excess provisions: up to 0.6% of credit RWA can be included in Tier 2 capital.
- If ECL provisions < IRB EL → Shortfall: the full shortfall is deducted from CET1 (50/50 deduction was historical; under CRR II, full CET1 deduction).

For **defaulted exposures**:
- Excess of specific provisions over IRB EL for defaulted: can be included in Tier 2 (same 0.6% RWA cap).
- Shortfall of specific provisions vs. IRB EL for defaulted: deducted 100% from CET1.

**(b) Numerical calculation:**

Non-defaulted portfolio:
- IFRS 9 Stage 1/2 provisions: R450m
- IRB EL (non-defaulted): R600m
- Shortfall = R600m − R450m = **R150m → deduct from CET1**

Defaulted portfolio:
- IFRS 9 Stage 3 specific provisions: R200m
- IRB EL (defaulted): R180m
- Excess = R200m − R180m = R20m → eligible for Tier 2 (subject to 0.6% RWA cap, assume cap not binding)

Net CET1 impact: **−R150m** (deduction) + Tier 2 benefit of +R20m (if included).

**(c) Policy rationale:**
- The CET1 deduction for shortfalls prevents regulatory arbitrage: if a bank provisions less than its expected loss (whether by accounting choice or optimism), the shortfall must be covered by the highest-quality capital — not AT1 or T2.
- The Tier 2 inclusion for excess provisions recognises that over-provisioning already absorbs losses that would otherwise reduce capital; allowing T2 credit avoids double-counting of conservatism.
- IFRS 9's forward-looking ECL framework has largely closed historical gaps between accounting provisions and regulatory EL, particularly for Stage 1/2 portfolios — reducing the frequency of shortfalls for banks with well-calibrated models.

## Card 19 — Liquidity + Pricing · Hard

(a) Explain how a bank's FTP framework should incorporate liquidity costs arising from NSFR compliance, giving a worked example for a 5-year fixed-rate mortgage. (b) How does the NSFR structurally influence the shape of a bank's lending spread curve across different maturities? (c) What challenges does the SARB's NSFR dispensation create for FTP calibration in SA?
?
**(a) Incorporating NSFR into FTP — worked example:**

The FTP rate for any asset should reflect: base rate (JIBAR/swap rate) + credit spread + **liquidity premium** (covering both LCR and NSFR costs).

For NSFR, the liquidity premium compensates for the cost of sourcing stable long-term funding to back illiquid assets. The NSFR cost is calculated as:

NSFR liquidity cost = RSF factor × (Long-term funding spread above short-term funding)

For a 5-year fixed-rate mortgage (RSF factor = 65%):
- Assume: 5-year term deposit costs JIBAR + 150 bps; overnight funding costs JIBAR + 30 bps
- Incremental stable funding spread = 150 − 30 = 120 bps
- NSFR cost = 65% × 120 bps = **78 bps** per annum

So the FTP for the mortgage includes a 78 bps NSFR surcharge, which must be priced into the mortgage lending rate.

**(b) Impact on lending spread curve:**
The NSFR creates a structural steepening of the internal liquidity premium curve:
- Short-term assets (< 1 year) have low RSF factors (0–50%), attracting minimal NSFR cost → flatter FTP.
- Long-term illiquid assets (RSF = 65–100%) attract higher NSFR costs → steeper FTP for longer maturities.
- This incentivises banks to prefer shorter maturity lending (or securitisation to reduce RSF) if they face binding NSFR constraints, creating a structural tension with long-term mortgage provision.

**(c) SA NSFR dispensation — FTP challenges:**
Under the SARB's modified implementation (Committed Liquidity Facility for LCR; modified RSF factors for certain assets), SA banks face two FTP calibration challenges:
1. **CLF cost ambiguity**: The CLF charges a fee (currently linked to SARB repo + spread). If banks include CLF cost in FTP but the CLF is only available up to a limit, the marginal cost beyond the CLF limit is much higher — creating a non-linear FTP curve.
2. **NSFR dispensation phase-in**: If SA banks are operating below full NSFR implementation, their FTP rates may understate true long-term NSFR costs — meaning mortgages priced today will be mispriced once full NSFR compliance is required. Banks must forward-price expected NSFR compliance costs to avoid a future NIM shock.

## Card 20 — Operational Risk + Pillar 2 · Hard

(a) A bank's IT systems are compromised in a ransomware attack, resulting in: R50m in direct remediation costs, R30m in regulatory fines, and R20m in compensation to affected customers. Classify this event under the Basel operational risk event type taxonomy and identify the Basel II loss event category. (b) Under the SMA, how does this loss affect the bank's Pillar 1 capital requirement, assuming the bank has a BIC of R800m and average annual losses over 10 years of R40m? (c) How should this event be captured in the ICAAP, and what Pillar 2 assessment is triggered?
?
**(a) Basel ORM event type classification:**

The ransomware attack and its consequences are classified as follows:

- **Primary event type**: External Fraud (ET4) if perpetrated by outsiders; or Systems (people and systems risk) if the primary cause is IT failure enabling the attack.
- More precisely, under Basel's seven event type categories:
  - **ET7 — Execution, Delivery and Process Management**: IT systems failure causing loss
  - **ET5 — Damage to Physical Assets**: if physical infrastructure was damaged
  - **ET3 — Clients, Products and Business Practices**: customer compensation R20m (failure to protect client data — regulatory/compliance failure)
- The regulatory fine (R30m) is categorised under **ET4 — Business Disruption and System Failures** (primary) and ET3 for regulatory sanction.
- Total gross loss = R50m + R30m + R20m = **R100m**. Recovery (insurance proceeds) can reduce net loss but gross loss is used in SMA data collection.

**(b) SMA capital impact:**

BIC = R800m → falls in Bucket 1 (BI ≤ €1bn approximation). BIC contribution = 12% × R800m = **R96m**.

Loss Component (LC) = 15 × average annual loss = 15 × R40m = **R600m**.

ILM = ln[exp(1) − 1 + (LC/BIC)^0.8] = ln[1.718 + (600/96)^0.8] = ln[1.718 + (6.25)^0.8]
(6.25)^0.8 ≈ 4.78 → ILM = ln[1.718 + 4.78] = ln[6.50] ≈ **1.87**

SMA Capital = BIC × ILM = R96m × 1.87 ≈ **R180m**

Without the 10-year average loss (ILM = 1 if LC not used): Capital = R96m. The loss history nearly doubles the capital requirement — illustrating the SMA loss incentive mechanism.

**(c) ICAAP treatment and Pillar 2 triggers:**

1. **Internal loss data update**: The R100m gross loss must be entered into the operational risk loss database immediately, with full metadata (event type, business line, cause, recovery). Under SMA, 10-year loss history feeds the LC.

2. **Scenario analysis revision**: The ransomware event should trigger a review of cyber risk scenarios in the ICAAP scenario library. If this event reveals that historical scenarios underestimated cyber loss frequency or severity, new scenarios with higher loss estimates must be added.

3. **RCSA (Risk & Control Self-Assessment) update**: The control environment for IT security must be reassessed. Identified control gaps should be escalated to the CRO and ALCO/Board Risk Committee.

4. **Pillar 2 capital assessment**: The PA may assess whether the bank's Pillar 2A add-on for operational risk adequately covers cyber risk following this event. If the bank's SMA Pillar 1 capital was insufficient to cover a plausible repeat event, the PA can impose a Pillar 2A surcharge for operational risk. The bank should proactively quantify residual cyber exposure under stress (e.g. a simultaneous multi-system attack) and include this in the ICAAP operational risk capital demand.
