## Card 2 — Value at Risk: Definition and Interpretation · Easy

State the definition of VaR and interpret a statement such as "1-day 99% VaR = R50m".::VaR (Value at Risk) is the maximum expected loss on a portfolio over a given time horizon that will not be exceeded with a given probability (confidence level). Formally: VaR[α] = sup{x ∈ ℝ : P(X < x) ≤ 1 − α}, where X is the return random variable and α is the confidence level. Interpretation of "1-day 99% VaR = R50m": there is an estimated 1% probability that a single trading day's loss will exceed R50 million — roughly one such loss every 100 trading days. A day on which the actual loss exceeds the VaR estimate is called a "VaR break". #flashcard #topic/market-risk #difficulty/easy #date/2026-05-02

## Card 3 — Expected Shortfall: Definition · Easy

Define Expected Shortfall (ES) and state how it differs from VaR in one key respect.::Expected Shortfall (ES) — also called Conditional VaR (CVaR) or Tail VaR — measures the average loss in the worst (1 − α)% of scenarios beyond the VaR threshold. Formally: ES[α] = E[−X | X ≤ VaR[α]]. The key difference from VaR: VaR only identifies the loss threshold at the confidence level; it says nothing about how large losses are *beyond* that threshold. ES captures the expected severity of tail losses, not merely their probability boundary. ES is also a coherent risk measure (satisfying subadditivity), while VaR is not. FRTB (effective 2023) replaced VaR with ES as the primary regulatory market risk measure under the IMA. #flashcard #topic/market-risk #difficulty/easy #date/2026-05-02

## Card 4 — VaR Calculation Methods: Historical, Parametric, Monte Carlo · Medium

Compare the three main methods for calculating VaR — historical simulation, parametric (variance-covariance), and Monte Carlo — across accuracy, assumptions, and practical limitations.
?
All three methods estimate the loss distribution of a portfolio; they differ in how that distribution is constructed.

**1. Historical Simulation**
Uses actual observed P&L over a historical window (typically 250–500 trading days) to build the empirical loss distribution. VaR is the α-th percentile of that historical sample.
- Advantages: No distributional assumption; automatically captures fat tails, skewness, and cross-asset correlations as observed in data.
- Limitations: Entirely backward-looking — the window must contain a stress period to produce conservative estimates. Slow to incorporate new volatility regimes. Equal weighting of observations may over- or under-weight distant data.
- Stressed VaR (sVaR): a variant that uses the worst 1-year window from 2007 to present, mandated under Basel 2.5 to complement current VaR for regulatory capital.

**2. Parametric (Variance-Covariance)**
Assumes portfolio returns are normally (or lognormally) distributed. Computes the mean and covariance matrix of risk factor returns, then derives VaR analytically: VaR = μ − z_α × σ, where z_α is the standard normal quantile at confidence α.
- Advantages: Computationally simple; easy to decompose VaR by risk factor.
- Limitations: Assumes elliptical (normal) return distributions — fails to capture fat tails, skewness, and non-linear payoff profiles (options). Violates the subadditivity condition when used for non-normal instruments.

**3. Monte Carlo Simulation**
Simulates a large number of random scenarios for risk factors (using a chosen distributional model — e.g. GBM, jump-diffusion) and revalues the portfolio under each scenario. VaR is the α-th percentile of the simulated P&L distribution.
- Advantages: Can handle non-linear instruments, complex payoffs, and non-normal distributions including fat tails and jumps.
- Limitations: Computationally intensive; results depend heavily on the chosen simulation model and its calibration. Model risk is significant — the simulation model may itself be mis-specified.

SA practice: South African banks commonly use 1-day and 10-day holding periods. The 10-day VaR is often derived by scaling the 1-day VaR by √10 (the "square-root-of-time" rule), which is valid only under the lognormal assumption.

## Card 5 — VaR: Non-Coherence and the Subadditivity Failure · Medium

Explain why VaR is not a coherent risk measure, with a concrete example of a subadditivity failure. State what this implies for risk management.
?
A coherent risk measure satisfies four axioms: monotonicity, translation invariance, positive homogeneity, and **subadditivity**. Subadditivity requires: ρ(L₁ + L₂) ≤ ρ(L₁) + ρ(L₂) — the combined portfolio's risk cannot exceed the sum of the individual risks. This ensures diversification is always recognised as a risk-reducing strategy.

**VaR fails subadditivity** in the presence of fat-tailed or non-elliptical distributions. Classic example: consider two credit-default options on different bonds, each with a 0.8% probability of a R100m loss (and zero loss otherwise). Individually, 99% VaR = R0 for each (since the 1% loss threshold is not breached at 99% confidence). But held together, the probability that *at least one* defaults ≈ 1.6% > 1%, so the 99% VaR of the combined portfolio = R100m > R0 + R0.

**Management implication**: VaR may actually penalise diversification. A trader adding a new position could see the combined portfolio VaR *exceed* the sum of the individual position VaRs. This creates perverse incentives: risk management based solely on VaR does not guarantee that diversification is rewarded. It also means you cannot simply aggregate VaR limits across desks to compute a reliable firm-wide VaR. Expected Shortfall (ES) is subadditive and avoids this pathology — it is why FRTB mandated the switch from VaR to ES.

## Card 6 — FRTB: Key Changes and IMA Requirements · Hard

(a) Identify the three fundamental changes introduced by the FRTB (effective 1 January 2023) and explain the motivation behind each. (b) State the minimum conditions for supervisory approval of the Internal Models Approach (IMA) under FRTB, including the required testing processes. (c) Explain why the shift from VaR to ES increases capital requirements, and identify one advantage and one disadvantage of ES as the regulatory measure.
?
(a) Three fundamental changes under FRTB (BCBS FRTB standard, now embedded in CRR3 / Basel IV):

1. **Revised trading/banking book boundary**: Instruments are assigned to the trading book or banking book at inception based on explicit intent criteria. This closes the pre-FRTB loophole where banks migrated illiquid, capital-intensive positions into the banking book to avoid the (higher) market risk capital charge, and vice versa for positions benefiting from lower trading-book treatment. Post-FRTB, reclassification requires explicit supervisory approval and must be capital-neutral.

2. **Revised IMA at trading desk level**: Under pre-FRTB rules, IMA approval was bank-wide. FRTB requires desk-by-desk approval — a bank may use IMA for approved desks and the standardised approach (SA) for others. This imposes much stronger governance and ensures capital is set appropriately for each desk's actual risk profile. The model must pass both back-testing and a new P&L Attribution (PLA) test at desk level.

3. **Shift from VaR to Expected Shortfall (ES)**: Replaces VaR as the primary risk measure. ES better captures tail risk — particularly for illiquid, fat-tailed instruments. Additionally, FRTB uses a *liquidity-adjusted* ES (LBES) that applies longer holding periods to illiquid risk factors (ranging from 10 days to 120 days depending on the risk factor's liquidity horizon), rather than a uniform 10-day period.

(b) Minimum conditions for IMA approval under FRTB:
- Supervisory authority must be satisfied that the risk management system is conceptually sound and implemented with integrity.
- The bank must employ a sufficient number of skilled staff in trading, risk control, product control, audit, and back office.
- Models must have a proven track record of reasonable accuracy in measuring risk (demonstrated by back-testing).
- Regular stress testing must be conducted.
- Positions included in internal models must be held in trading desks approved for IMA use.
- A documented set of internal policies, controls, and procedures must exist.
- Models must be independently validated at initial development, after significant changes, and periodically.
- At desk level: **back-testing** (comparing daily P&L against ES/VaR estimates — traffic-light system with escalation at ≥5 exceptions per 250 days) AND **P&L Attribution test** (PLA) — ensuring that the model-attributed P&L explains the actual P&L with acceptable tolerance. Desks failing PLA must switch to the SA.

(c) ES vs VaR for capital purposes:
- **Why ES increases capital**: ES measures the *average* loss in the worst (1 − α)% of scenarios, while VaR measures only the *threshold* loss. For fat-tailed distributions (which are empirically observed in financial markets), the average tail loss is substantially larger than the VaR threshold. Switching from 99% VaR (pre-FRTB) to 97.5% ES (FRTB) was calibrated to broadly increase capital — the 97.5% ES is empirically comparable to 99% VaR for normal distributions, but materially higher for fat-tailed ones.
- **Advantage**: ES is subadditive — it correctly recognises diversification; capital charges can be aggregated across desks without creating perverse incentives to concentrate risk.
- **Disadvantage**: ES has little intuitive meaning compared to VaR — it cannot be easily linked to the current market value of a position, making it harder to set meaningful desk-level limits or explain to non-quantitative stakeholders.

## Card 7 — IRRBB Sources · Easy

Name and define the four sources of interest rate risk in the banking book (IRRBB).::The four IRRBB sources are: (1) **Repricing risk** — mismatches between the repricing dates of assets and liabilities, so that rate changes affect asset yields and liability costs at different times (e.g. a 5-year fixed-rate mortgage funded by 3-month deposits). (2) **Yield curve risk** — risk from non-parallel shifts in the yield curve (e.g. steepening or flattening), which affects positions differently across tenors. (3) **Basis risk** — risk from imperfect correlation between rates on assets and liabilities with the same repricing tenor but referencing different benchmarks (e.g. prime-rate lending funded by JIBAR deposits creates a prime-JIBAR basis). (4) **Optionality risk** — risk from explicit or embedded options in banking book products, including customer prepayment options on mortgages, early redemption of fixed deposits, or rate-linked floors/caps in loan agreements. #flashcard #topic/irrbb #difficulty/easy #date/2026-05-02

## Card 8 — Economic Value of Equity (EVE): Definition · Easy

Define the Economic Value of Equity (EVE) and state the formula, including what the components represent.::EVE is the net present value of all expected cashflows from the banking book's assets minus liabilities (and off-balance sheet items), assuming the current book runs off to maturity. It represents the economic worth of equity under the current interest rate environment. Formula: EVE = PV(Assets) − PV(Liabilities), where PV(Assets) = Σ_t CF_t^A × V_t and PV(Liabilities) = Σ_t CF_t^L × V_t, and V_t is the discount factor at time t. A bank is said to be *liability-sensitive* when its liabilities have shorter duration than assets — in this case a rate rise reduces EVE (liability costs rise faster than asset yields). Basel III requires banks to measure the impact of six prescribed rate shock scenarios on EVE as part of Pillar 2 IRRBB reporting. #flashcard #topic/irrbb #difficulty/easy #date/2026-05-02

## Card 9 — EVE vs NII: The Core IRRBB Trade-off · Medium

Explain why managing EVE and NII stability simultaneously creates an inherent tension in IRRBB management, and describe the structural position that stabilises each.
?
EVE and NII represent two different perspectives on IRRBB — long-run economic value vs short-run earnings — and the portfolio structures that stabilise each are opposing:

**To stabilise NII** (earnings over a short horizon, typically 1–2 years): the bank should match the repricing profile of assets and liabilities closely, so that when rates change, both asset income and liability costs reprice at approximately the same time. This is achieved with a *short-duration, floating-rate structure* — assets and liabilities both reprice quickly, so NII is insulated from rate moves. However, this also means the bank has minimal maturity transformation and earns little from the yield curve shape.

**To stabilise EVE** (long-run economic value): the bank should hold longer-dated fixed-rate assets funded by longer-dated fixed-rate liabilities (or by shorter-duration, stable liabilities). A *long-duration asset structure with short-duration liabilities* (liability-sensitive) is the typical banking model — it earns the term premium but creates EVE volatility when rates rise sharply, since the higher discount rate compresses the PV of long-dated assets more than the PV of shorter liabilities.

**The tension**: a liability-sensitive bank (typical mortgage/retail bank) earns more NII in stable or falling-rate environments (the yield curve spread is captured) but suffers EVE deterioration when rates rise — as seen in 2022–23 at US regional banks (e.g. SVB). Conversely, an asset-sensitive bank has rising NII in rate hikes but lower NII in rate cuts. A bank cannot simultaneously maximise structural protection of both metrics, so IRRBB management involves calibrating an acceptable balance between them, expressed as limits on ΔEVE and ΔNII under prescribed shock scenarios. Basel III requires both to be reported under Pillar 2.

## Card 10 — Repricing Gap Analysis and NII Sensitivity · Medium

A bank's 1-year repricing gap in 3-month bands shows the following data: 0–3m gap = −R30m, 3–6m gap = +R20m, 6–9m gap = −R10m, 9–12m gap = +R5m. (a) State the NII sensitivity formula. (b) Calculate total NII sensitivity for a 100bp parallel rate increase.
?
(a) **NII Sensitivity in a time band** = (Repricing gap × Remaining term in months × Δr) / 12

Where:
- Repricing gap = rate-sensitive assets minus rate-sensitive liabilities in that time band (Rm)
- Remaining term = approximate midpoint months remaining in the year (use end of band)
- Δr = rate change expressed as a decimal

(b) Calculation (100bp = 0.01):

| Band | Gap (Rm) | Remaining months | NII Sensitivity (Rm) |
|------|----------|------------------|----------------------|
| 0–3m | −30 | 12 | −30 × 12 × 0.01 / 12 = **−0.300** |
| 3–6m | +20 | 9 | +20 × 9 × 0.01 / 12 = **+0.150** |
| 6–9m | −10 | 6 | −10 × 6 × 0.01 / 12 = **−0.050** |
| 9–12m | +5 | 3 | +5 × 3 × 0.01 / 12 = **+0.013** |
| **Total** | | | **−0.188** |

Interpretation: a 100bp parallel rate increase would reduce NII by approximately R187,500 over the next year. The net negative result reflects the bank's liability-sensitive position — more liabilities than assets reprice in the short run. To reduce this sensitivity, the bank could use pay-fixed-receive-floating interest rate swaps (which gain when rates rise, offsetting the liability repricing loss) or shorten the duration of fixed-rate assets.

## Card 11 — Earnings at Risk (EaR) · Easy

Define Earnings at Risk (EaR) and explain how it extends repricing gap analysis.::EaR is the VaR applied to the bank's net interest income (NII) over a given time horizon (typically up to 2 years) at a specified confidence level. Formula: EaR_(1−α) = VaR_(1−α)(ΔNII). It extends repricing gap analysis by introducing a stochastic element: instead of a single parallel rate shock, a stochastic interest rate model (e.g. Vasicek) is used to generate a distribution of possible rate scenarios via Monte Carlo simulation. The resulting distribution of NII outcomes allows the bank to quantify NII-at-risk — the level such that only (1 − α)% of simulations produce a worse NII outcome. For example, "95% EaR = R5m" means there is a 5% probability that NII over the horizon will be R5m worse than the base scenario. EaR captures short-to-medium-term earnings volatility; EVE captures long-run economic value risk. Both are required under Basel IRRBB standards. #flashcard #topic/irrbb #difficulty/easy #date/2026-05-02

## Card 12 — Basel IRRBB: Prescribed Shock Scenarios · Medium

State the six prescribed interest rate shock scenarios for EVE measurement and the two scenarios for NII measurement under the Basel IRRBB standard (BCBS 2016). Explain why non-parallel shocks matter.
?
Under the BCBS IRRBB standard (2016), banks must measure the impact of rate shocks on both EVE and NII using the following prescribed scenarios:

**Six EVE shock scenarios:**
1. Parallel upward shock — all tenors shift up by the same magnitude
2. Parallel downward shock — all tenors shift down
3. Steepener — short rates fall, long rates rise (flattening-reversal)
4. Flattener — short rates rise, long rates fall (yield curve flattening)
5. Short rate upward shock — short-end rises, long-end unchanged (bear flattener)
6. Short rate downward shock — short-end falls, long-end unchanged (bull steepener)

**Two NII shock scenarios:**
1. Parallel upward shock
2. Parallel downward shock

**Why non-parallel shocks matter**: Most IRRBB models are well-hedged against parallel shifts — banks use interest rate swaps referenced to benchmark rates. However, non-parallel shifts expose *basis risk* (e.g. the prime-JIBAR basis in SA) and *yield curve risk* across tenors that parallel hedges do not address. A flattener penalises banks that earn a spread by borrowing short and lending long (their spread compresses). A steepener can create mark-to-market losses on long-dated fixed-rate assets funded with short-dated liabilities if long rates rise sharply. Requiring non-parallel shocks ensures banks do not under-state IRRBB by holding only parallel-hedge instruments.

**SA context**: SARB's Directive 7/2018 implements the Basel IRRBB framework, requiring PA-supervised banks to report EVE and NII sensitivity under all six and two prescribed scenarios respectively, and to meet the supervisory outlier test thresholds.

## Card 13 — IRRBB Supervisory Outlier Test (SOT) · Hard

(a) Define the Supervisory Outlier Test (SOT) for IRRBB and state the EVE and NII threshold criteria. (b) Explain the economic motivation behind using Tier 1 capital as the denominator for the EVE SOT. (c) A bank has Tier 1 capital of R5bn. Under a parallel upward shock, ΔEVE = −R850m and ΔNII = −R300m (base NII = R1.5bn). Assess whether the bank passes each SOT, and describe the supervisory consequences if it fails.
?
(a) The Supervisory Outlier Test (SOT) is a threshold-based test used by supervisors to identify banks with potentially excessive IRRBB relative to their capital base, triggering mandatory supervisory dialogue and possible Pillar 2 capital add-ons.

**EVE SOT**: A bank is an "outlier" if |ΔEVE| under any of the six prescribed shock scenarios exceeds **15% of Tier 1 capital**.
Threshold: |ΔEVE| / T1 > 15%

**NII SOT** (introduced in the EBA guidelines — EBA/GL/2022/14, effective 2023): A bank is an "outlier" if |ΔNII| under any of the two prescribed NII shock scenarios exceeds **5% of Tier 1 capital** in the European regulatory context (SARB / BCBS standard focuses primarily on EVE SOT, with NII as a complementary measure).

(b) Tier 1 capital is used as the denominator because IRRBB losses ultimately erode economic capital. If EVE falls sharply (due to rising rates reducing the PV of long-dated fixed assets), this is economically equivalent to a permanent reduction in shareholder value — the bank has less equity to absorb future losses. Measuring the shock EVE loss relative to Tier 1 capital calibrates IRRBB against the capacity to absorb it without breaching going-concern minimum capital requirements. Using total assets or RWA would be less appropriate because they don't directly reflect loss-absorption capacity.

(c) Numerical assessment:

**EVE SOT**:
- 15% × R5bn = R750m
- |ΔEVE| = R850m > R750m ✗ **OUTLIER** — fails the EVE SOT

**NII SOT**:
- 5% × R5bn = R250m
- |ΔNII| = R300m > R250m ✗ **OUTLIER** — fails the NII SOT under EBA GL thresholds (NII / T1 = 20%)

Supervisory consequences:
1. The bank must immediately notify the Prudential Authority (SARB) of its outlier status.
2. The supervisor initiates an enhanced review of the bank's IRRBB governance, measurement methodology, and hedging strategy.
3. The supervisor may impose a **Pillar 2 capital add-on** (Pillar 2A / Pillar 2B) if the excess exposure reflects inadequate management controls rather than a temporary position.
4. The bank may be required to reduce its IRRBB exposure through hedging (interest rate swaps, swaptions) or by restructuring the balance sheet (e.g. issuing longer-dated liabilities or shortening asset duration).

## Card 15 — Operational Risk: Basic Indicator Approach (BIA) · Easy

State the BIA capital formula, define all terms, and identify one key weakness of the approach.::BIA capital formula: K_BIA = [Σ(n=1 to 3)(GI_n × α)] / 3, where GI_n = annual gross income in year n (net interest income plus non-interest income, before deduction of operational losses) and α = 15%. Key weakness: using gross income as a proxy for operational risk assumes a linear relationship between revenue and loss exposure — but this is empirically invalid. During periods of stress when operational loss events are most likely, revenue (GI) often falls, mechanically *reducing* the BIA capital charge at exactly the time when more capital is needed. This procyclicality was a key motivation for replacing the BIA with the New Standardised Approach (NSA) under Basel IV. #flashcard #topic/operational-risk #topic/regulation #difficulty/easy #date/2026-05-02

## Card 17 — Operational Risk: AMA Four Data Elements · Medium

List and describe the four data elements required under the Advanced Measurement Approach (AMA), and explain why each is necessary. Name one statistical method used to combine them.
?
Under Basel II AMA, banks must incorporate four data elements into their operational risk capital model:

**1. Internal loss data** — the bank's own historical record of operational loss events, categorised by business line and loss event type. Minimum 5-year history (3 years when first approved). Used to calibrate the body of the loss distribution for high-frequency, low-severity events. Limitation: rare events (tail losses) are by definition under-represented in even 10-year internal histories.

**2. External loss data** — operational loss data purchased from industry consortia (e.g. ORX — Operational Riskdata eXchange) or compiled from public disclosures. Most important for calibrating the *tail* of the severity distribution, where internal data is sparse. Adjustments must be made for differences in size, business mix, and geographic exposure between the bank and external loss sources.

**3. Scenario analysis** — structured expert-judgement processes (workshops) to estimate the frequency and severity of extreme or unprecedented loss events (e.g. major fraud, cyberattack, model failure). Used to extend the loss distribution beyond historical experience and to capture 1-in-1000-year tail events. Must be forward-looking, clearly defined, and independently validated.

**4. Business environment and internal control factors (BEICFs)** — objective indicators of the current control environment: KRIs (key risk indicators), KPIs (key performance indicators), and KCIs (key control indicators), expressed as counts, percentages, or values (e.g. unmatched trades, staff turnover rate, number of audit exceptions). Intended to make the capital assessment forward-looking and responsive to actual control quality, not only historical losses.

**Combining the four elements**: The Loss Distribution Approach (LDA) is the most common method. Separate frequency distributions (e.g. Poisson) and severity distributions (e.g. log-normal, generalised Pareto for the tail) are fitted by business line/loss type combination. Monte Carlo simulation draws from frequency, then draws that many losses from severity, to produce a simulated aggregate annual loss distribution. AMA capital = VaR_(99.9%) of the aggregate loss distribution.

## Card 18 — NSA: Why Basel IV Replaced AMA/BIA/TSA · Medium

Identify four shortcomings of the Basel II operational risk capital approaches that motivated their replacement by the New Standardised Approach (NSA) under Basel IV.
?
The Basel Committee's post-crisis review identified the following shortcomings that led to the NSA replacing BIA, TSA, and AMA from 1 January 2023:

**1. Procyclicality of revenue-based proxies (BIA and TSA)**: Gross income fell during the GFC, mechanically reducing BIA/TSA capital at the exact time that operational risk events were most frequent and severe. Capital requirements moved in the *wrong* direction relative to actual risk. A size-and-loss-based indicator is more stable and less procyclical.

**2. Invalid assumption of linear risk-revenue relationship (BIA and TSA)**: There is no empirical support for the assumption that operational risk losses scale proportionally with gross income. Large banks may achieve operational risk economies of scale; high-revenue periods (e.g. bull markets in trading) are not necessarily periods of high operational risk.

**3. Extreme variability in AMA RWAs**: The AMA's flexibility ("use your own model") produced severe inconsistency across banks. The same bank could produce materially different capital numbers depending on distributional assumptions, correlation assumptions, and scenario judgement. Supervisors and market participants could not compare AMA capital numbers across banks — undermining Basel's "level playing field" objective.

**4. AMA's theoretical rigour did not translate to better risk capture**: The hope that flexible internal modelling would converge on best practice did not materialise. Many banks struggled to collect sufficient internal data for the AMA, and scenario analysis was inconsistently applied. Despite the complexity, AMA capital estimates for the same risk profile varied enormously.

**NSA solution**: replaces the gross income proxy with the **Business Indicator (BI)** — a more stable, financial-statement-based measure combining interest/dividend income, service income, and trading P&L. For larger banks (BI > €1bn), an **Internal Loss Multiplier (ILM)** scales the capital upward if the bank's own historical losses exceed the industry average, incentivising loss reduction while retaining comparability.

## Card 19 — NSA: Capital Calculation Step-by-Step · Hard

(a) Write out the full NSA capital formula, defining BI, BIC, ILM, and the Loss Component (LC). (b) A bank has a Business Indicator of €12bn and average annual operational losses of €180m. Calculate the NSA capital requirement step-by-step. (c) Explain why the ILM is set to 1.0 for Bucket 1 banks and state the policy rationale.
?
(a) NSA capital formula:

**Step 1 — Business Indicator (BI):**
BI = ILDC + SC + FC
- ILDC = min(|II − IE|_avg, 0.035 × IEA_avg) + DI_avg
- SC = max(OOI_avg, OOE_avg) + max(FI_avg, FE_avg)
- FC = |NPLT|_avg + |NPLB|_avg
(all averages over the most recent 3-year period)

**Step 2 — Business Indicator Component (BIC):**
Applied marginally to BI according to buckets:
- Bucket 1 (BI ≤ €1bn): 12% marginal coefficient
- Bucket 2 (€1bn < BI ≤ €30bn): 15%
- Bucket 3 (BI > €30bn): 18%

**Step 3 — Internal Loss Multiplier (ILM)** (optional for Bucket 1, mandatory for Buckets 2 and 3):
ILM = ln(exp(1) − 1 + (LC / BIC)^0.8)
where LC = Loss Component = 15 × average annual operational losses over the past 10 years.

**Step 4 — Capital Requirement:**
K_NSA = BIC × ILM

**Step 5 — RWA:**
RWA = K_NSA × 12.5

(b) Worked example (BI = €12bn, average annual losses = €180m):

**BIC calculation:**
- Bucket 1: 1 × 12% = €0.12bn
- Bucket 2: (12 − 1) × 15% = 11 × 0.15 = €1.65bn
- BIC = €0.12 + €1.65 = **€1.77bn**

**LC calculation:**
LC = 15 × €180m = €2.70bn

**ILM calculation:**
ILM = ln(exp(1) − 1 + (2.70 / 1.77)^0.8)
= ln(e − 1 + (1.525)^0.8)
= ln(1.718 + 1.438)  [since e ≈ 2.718, e−1 ≈ 1.718; 1.525^0.8 ≈ 1.406]
= ln(3.124) ≈ **1.14**

**K_NSA:** €1.77bn × 1.14 = **€2.02bn**

**RWA:** €2.02bn × 12.5 = **€25.2bn**

(c) For Bucket 1 banks (BI ≤ €1bn), supervisors have discretion to set ILM = 1.0 (i.e. capital = BIC alone, with no loss adjustment). Policy rationale: small banks often have limited loss data — fewer than 10 years of internal history, low event frequency — making the ILM unreliable and potentially volatile. The ILM function is most meaningful when a bank has a large, credible loss database. Forcing small banks to use potentially noisy ILMs could *add* uncertainty to capital estimates rather than improve them. The flat 12% BI-based BIC is simpler, more comparable, and more appropriate for institutions whose operational risk profile is adequately captured by size alone.

## Card 22 — FATF Grey Listing and South Africa · Medium

Explain the process by which FATF grey-listed South Africa in 2023, the implications for SA banks, and the conditions under which a country is removed from the grey list.
?
**FATF grey-listing process**: FATF conducts mutual evaluation reviews (MERs) of member jurisdictions against its 40 Recommendations, assessing both technical compliance and effectiveness of AML/CFT controls. Countries with material deficiencies that do not demonstrate timely remediation are placed on the **FATF grey list** (formally: "Jurisdictions Under Increased Monitoring"). Grey-listing is not a black-list — it signals enhanced oversight rather than sanctions.

**South Africa's grey-listing (February 2023)**: FATF's Mutual Evaluation Report on SA identified deficiencies including: inadequate prosecution of money laundering and terrorism financing offences; weaknesses in supervision of non-bank financial institutions and designated non-financial businesses/professions (DNFBPs); insufficient beneficial ownership transparency; and under-resourced Financial Intelligence Centre (FIC). South Africa was given an **Action Plan** (typically 12–24 months) to address identified gaps.

**Implications for SA banks**:
1. **Enhanced correspondent banking due diligence**: International banks and correspondent banking partners are obliged under their own regulatory frameworks to apply enhanced due diligence to all SA-originating transactions. This increases compliance costs and may delay cross-border transactions.
2. **Reputational and cost risk**: Some foreign financial institutions may reduce their South African correspondent relationships — reducing access to dollar and euro clearing.
3. **Increased SARB/FIC scrutiny**: SA banks face heightened domestic supervisory expectations on AML/CFT programmes, particularly transaction monitoring, STR quality, and beneficial ownership verification.
4. **Regulatory investment**: Banks must invest in upgraded transaction monitoring systems, AML training, and enhanced governance structures to demonstrate compliance.

**Removal conditions**: A country is removed from the grey list after FATF's follow-up review confirms that the Action Plan has been substantially implemented — typically requiring demonstrable *effectiveness* in enforcement (prosecutions, convictions, asset confiscations), not just legislative compliance. South Africa exited the grey list in **October 2024** after addressing the key Action Plan items, including amendments to the FICA and enhanced prosecution of predicate crimes.

## Card 25 — Climate Risk Transmission to Bank Risk Types · Medium

Explain how physical and transition climate risks transmit into each of the four main bank risk categories (credit, market, liquidity, operational risk), with a South Africa-specific example for at least two.
?
Climate risk is a *cross-cutting* risk driver — it does not sit in isolation but manifests through the transmission channels of conventional bank risk types:

**1. Credit Risk**
Physical: weather events and chronic climate shifts increase PD (borrowers' revenue is disrupted) and increase LGD (collateral — especially property and agriculture — is damaged). Example (SA): persistent Western Cape drought reduces the value of farmland used as mortgage collateral, increasing both EAD and LGD on agri-sector loans. Transition: carbon-intensive sectors face regulatory costs (carbon tax) or stranded assets (obsolete coal infrastructure), increasing PD for energy-sector borrowers.

**2. Market Risk**
Physical and transition risks affect the market value of securities and derivatives. Banks with trading positions in brown-sector equities face valuation losses as investor preferences shift. Transition: a sudden policy announcement (e.g. accelerated coal plant closures) can cause rapid mark-to-market losses on fossil fuel bonds. In SA, banks with significant government bond holdings face repricing risk if SA sovereign bonds are repriced to reflect climate fiscal risks.

**3. Liquidity Risk**
Funding risk: institutional investors increasingly apply ESG screens — a bank with a high brown-loan-book exposure may find its bond issuance facing wider spreads or reduced demand, increasing funding costs and liquidity risk. Physical: a natural disaster could trigger sudden deposit withdrawal in affected regions, straining intraday and short-term liquidity.

**4. Operational Risk**
Physical damage to bank infrastructure (data centres, branches) from floods or fires creates operational disruption. Business continuity risk in SA is heightened by load-shedding (power outages affecting transaction processing). Transition: regulatory change (e.g. mandatory TCFD disclosures, carbon reporting obligations) increases compliance and legal risk if disclosures are inadequate — creating reputational and conduct risk.

**Measurement approach**: Banks increasingly use **scenario analysis** (e.g. NGFS scenarios) to model climate risk transmission across these channels under different warming pathways. The SARB has engaged in the Climate Risk Assessment project and expects Pillar 2 climate risk considerations to be incorporated in ICAAPs by major SA banks.

## Card 26 — Fund Transfer Pricing (FTP): Core Purpose · Easy

Define Fund Transfer Pricing (FTP) and state BCBS 144's Principle 4, which underpins the regulatory mandate for FTP.::FTP is the internal pricing mechanism through which a bank's treasury function acts as a central counterparty to every business line — charging assets a funding cost and crediting liabilities a funding benefit — at rates corresponding to the tenor and liquidity characteristics of each transaction. Its purpose is to strip interest-rate and liquidity risk out of business line P&L, concentrate those risks in treasury where they can be hedged, and leave business units accountable only for credit, service, and operational performance. BCBS 144 Principle 4 (September 2008) states: "A bank should incorporate liquidity costs, benefits and risks in the internal pricing, performance measurement and new product approval process for all significant business activities (both on- and off-balance sheet), thereby aligning the risk-taking incentives of individual business lines with the liquidity risk exposures their activities create for the bank as a whole." The GFC demonstrated that banks without FTP systematically over-accumulated illiquid assets and off-balance-sheet commitments whose true funding cost only became apparent when wholesale markets closed. #flashcard #topic/pricing #topic/liquidity-risk #difficulty/easy #date/2026-05-02

## Card 27 — FTP Curve: Four Components · Medium

Write out the FTP curve equation, define each component, and explain why a pure risk-free-rate FTP is insufficient for long-dated assets.
?
The FTP rate for a transaction of tenor T is built as:

**r_FTP(T) = r_swap(T) + LP(T) + CC(T) + Optionality(T)**

**Component breakdown:**

1. **r_swap(T)** — the risk-free (or near-risk-free) base rate at tenor T. Post-2023, this is the relevant overnight rate (SOFR in USD, SONIA in GBP, JIBAR-linked in SA) or OIS rate. Captures the pure time-value-of-money cost of committing funds for tenor T.

2. **LP(T)** — term liquidity premium. The additional cost that markets charge for committing funds for a longer term vs overnight. Even under identical credit quality, a 5-year commitment is worth more to the borrower than overnight rolling — LP(T) quantifies this. LP must be explicitly priced because post-GFC, bank term funding trades at 50–200+ bps over the overnight rate. Omitting LP causes systematic under-pricing of long-dated assets and incentivises balance sheet illiquidity.

3. **CC(T)** — the bank's own credit spread at tenor T. Reflects the credit risk that investors demand for lending to *this* bank (as opposed to the risk-free sovereign). At short tenors this is small; at longer tenors it embeds the bank's probability of default and recovery rate.

4. **Optionality(T)** — cost of any embedded options in the transaction, such as prepayment options on mortgages, drawn/undrawn commitment risk on revolving credit facilities, or early redemption options on fixed-term deposits. These create contingent liquidity risk that must be priced.

**Why pure risk-free FTP is insufficient**: If only r_swap is used, long-dated loans appear profitable even when term liquidity is scarce and expensive. This was the pre-GFC failure: banks like SVB priced off near-zero term premiums. When wholesale markets closed in 2007–08 (and again during SVB's 2023 deposit run), long-term assets could not be funded at the assumed cost — the term liquidity premium should have been charged all along. BCBS 144 explicitly requires LP and contingent liquidity costs to be incorporated; SR 16-3 formalises this for US banks with ≥$250bn in assets. An LP-inclusive FTP creates the correct incentive: long-dated illiquid lending must pass a higher hurdle, naturally limiting balance sheet extension beyond what the bank can stably fund.

## Card 28 — FRTB IMA: Approval, Back-Testing, and P&L Attribution · Hard

(a) State the minimum qualitative conditions for supervisory approval of the IMA under FRTB. (b) Explain the back-testing traffic-light approach and the regulatory consequences of VaR exceptions. (c) Define the P&L Attribution (PLA) test, explain why it is new to FRTB, and state what happens if a desk fails it.
?
(a) Minimum qualitative conditions for IMA approval under FRTB (BCBS FRTB standard):
1. Supervisory authority must be satisfied that the risk management system is conceptually sound and implemented with integrity — models reflect actual risk-taking behaviour, not just regulatory compliance.
2. The bank must employ sufficient skilled staff in trading, risk control, product control, audit, and back office — ensuring model development and validation functions are adequately resourced.
3. The trading desk's risk model must have a proven track record of reasonable accuracy — demonstrated by historical back-testing performance over a meaningful period.
4. Regular stress testing must be conducted and results integrated into risk limits.
5. Positions included in IMA must be held in trading desks explicitly approved for IMA use — desk-level approval replaces the old bank-wide approval. A bank may have a mix of IMA and SA desks.
6. A documented set of internal policies, controls, and procedures governing model use must exist.
7. All models must be independently validated — by a team independent from development — at initial deployment, after significant changes, and periodically thereafter.

(b) Back-testing traffic-light approach:
Back-testing compares the daily 99% 1-day VaR estimate against actual daily P&L. A *VaR exception (break)* occurs when actual loss exceeds the VaR estimate. Regulatory consequences are based on the number of exceptions per rolling 250-day window:
- **Green zone (0–4 exceptions)**: no supervisory action; model is performing within acceptable tolerance.
- **Yellow zone (5–9 exceptions)**: increased regulatory scrutiny; supervisors may impose a capital multiplier add-on (scaling factor k increases above 3, up to a maximum of 4) on the VaR-based capital requirement. The bank must explain the source of exceptions (clean P&L vs full P&L attribution; model vs market factors).
- **Red zone (10+ exceptions)**: presumption that the model is inadequate; supervisors likely require the desk to revert to the standardised approach until the model is remediated. The bank may face immediate capital surcharges.

Note: Under FRTB, back-testing is conducted at both desk level (daily) and bank-wide level, and uses two P&L concepts: actual P&L (all revenues) and hypothetical P&L (position-based, excluding fees and reserves).

(c) P&L Attribution (PLA) test — new to FRTB:
The PLA test compares the *risk-theoretical P&L* (RTPL — P&L explained by the IMA risk model's risk factors) to the *hypothetical P&L* (HPL — P&L based on actual end-of-day positions repriced with new market data). If the model explains the actual P&L well, RTPL ≈ HPL. Two metrics are tested: the Spearman correlation between RTPL and HPL, and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test of distributional differences.

**Why it is new**: Pre-FRTB models could pass back-testing through luck or by using overly conservative VaR estimates, even if the model didn't actually capture the correct risk factors. PLA directly tests whether the model's risk decomposition explains observed trading outcomes — not just whether the loss exceeded a threshold.

**Consequences of failing PLA**: A desk that fails the PLA test is classified as "ineligible" for IMA and must switch to the **standardised approach (SA)** for capital purposes. This typically increases capital significantly for complex trading desks. The desk may appeal by demonstrating that the PLA failure was due to a specific, identifiable gap that has been remediated, and seek re-approval.

## Card 30 — Cross-Topic Synthesis: Climate Risk, IFRS 9, and IRB Capital · Hard

(a) Explain three channels through which physical climate risk on a mortgage portfolio can simultaneously affect IFRS 9 ECL, IRB regulatory capital, and bank liquidity. (b) Describe how a bank should incorporate climate risk into its ICAAP under Pillar 2. (c) Discuss the key limitation of current IFRS 9 and IRB frameworks in capturing long-horizon climate risk, and how scenario analysis partially addresses this gap.
?
(a) Three transmission channels from physical climate risk to IFRS 9 / IRB capital / liquidity:

**Channel 1 — Collateral value deterioration (floods/drought)**:
- **IFRS 9 ECL**: Falls in property value directly reduce LGD in ECL models (LGD = (EAD − Collateral Value at default) / EAD). For Stage 2/Stage 3 mortgages, ECL provision increases as expected recovery on collateral diminishes. Catastrophic events (e.g. Cape Town drought-related property devaluation in high-risk zones) may trigger a Stage 2 migration if the *significant increase in credit risk* criterion is met.
- **IRB capital**: Under A-IRB, LGD is a downturn estimate. Climate-related collateral stress events may qualify as "downturn conditions," requiring LGD uplift — increasing RWA. If PD also rises (borrowers' cash flows impaired), the IRB capital formula produces higher K = EAD × LGD × [WCDR − PD].
- **Liquidity**: A portfolio repriced lower (higher ECL provisions → lower book value) reduces the collateral available for repo transactions and secured funding, tightening liquidity headroom.

**Channel 2 — Borrower income disruption (agricultural drought)**:
- **IFRS 9**: PD rises for affected borrowers → forward-looking PD models (which incorporate macro scenarios including drought-related variables) trigger ECL increases across all stages. Acute shocks may trigger mass Stage 2 or Stage 3 migrations in regional agricultural portfolios.
- **IRB**: Through-the-Cycle PDs must eventually be recalibrated to reflect permanently elevated default rates in climate-exposed geographies. This increases RWA across the portfolio. Under A-IRB, historical observed default rates (ODRs) in affected regions enter the PD estimation dataset.
- **Liquidity**: Large provisions reduce retained earnings and CET1 capital. Falling CET1 tightens capital ratios, potentially triggering regulatory constraints on dividend payments — increasing market concern and widening funding spreads (liquidity risk from investor confidence channel).

**Channel 3 — Physical asset damage (flooding)**:
- **IFRS 9**: Catastrophic property damage may cause immediate Stage 3 migration for flooded properties, with a large IFRS 9 specific credit adjustment. Recovery becomes harder post-flood (lower market liquidity for damaged assets).
- **IRB**: If a significant cluster of defaults occurs simultaneously (correlated physical event), realised default rates in affected vintage/geographic buckets feed into PD model recalibrations, eventually raising TTC PDs.
- **Liquidity**: Large IFRS 9 provisions reduce P&L → erode CET1 → reduce headroom over capital floors → potentially trigger AT1/T2 conversion triggers, affecting wholesale funding costs.

(b) Incorporating climate risk into the ICAAP under Pillar 2:
The ICAAP (Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process) is the bank's own view of the capital needed to cover all material risks, including those not fully captured under Pillar 1. Climate risk integration under Pillar 2 involves:
1. **Risk identification**: Mapping the loan book by sector and geography to identify climate-exposed concentrations (e.g. coastal real estate, SA agricultural regions, fossil fuel-linked corporates).
2. **Scenario analysis**: Using NGFS (Network for Greening the Financial System) climate scenarios (e.g. "Current Policies," "Net Zero 2050," "Delayed Transition") to stress-test PD and LGD under different warming and policy pathways over 10–30-year horizons — far longer than typical stress test horizons.
3. **Capital quantification**: Estimate the incremental capital required beyond Pillar 1 IRB to cover climate-amplified credit losses, using scenario-conditional ECL and RWA projections.
4. **Governance**: Board-level approval of climate risk appetite; ALCO/CRO oversight of climate risk positions; TCFD-aligned reporting.
5. **SARB expectations**: The SARB has engaged SA banks through the Climate Risk Assessment project and expects Pillar 2 ICAAP submissions to increasingly reflect climate risk quantification, in alignment with NGFS scenario guidance.

(c) Limitations and role of scenario analysis:
- **IFRS 9 limitation**: ECL is calibrated on a 12-month or lifetime horizon using historical PD/LGD data and forward-looking adjustments for economic scenarios (IFRS 9 para B5.5.46–55). But climate impacts are typically material on 10–30-year horizons, well beyond IFRS 9's life-of-loan horizon for most retail products. Historical data contains little signal about future climate-driven default patterns — models trained on past data will systematically underestimate climate risk.
- **IRB limitation**: TTC PDs are calibrated on historical ODR data from periods that predate significant climate impacts. The ASRF model assumes time-invariant correlations — but correlated climate events (simultaneous regional droughts, flood events) create higher-than-modelled systematic risk that the single-factor ASRF framework cannot capture. Long-horizon transition risk (e.g. the stranding of fossil fuel assets over 10–20 years) falls outside the typical 1-year IRB capital horizon entirely.
- **How scenario analysis addresses the gap**: Climate scenario analysis allows banks to project forward-looking PD/LGD estimates under specific climate pathways without requiring historical calibration data. NGFS-aligned scenarios provide standardised assumptions about temperature trajectories, carbon pricing, and technology adoption. Banks can apply these to sector-level models (e.g. agriculture, real estate, energy) to estimate stressed ECL and RWA under each scenario. This is a Pillar 2 management tool rather than a Pillar 1 formula — it supplements but does not replace IFRS 9 or IRB estimates. The limitation of scenario analysis itself is that it relies on expert-driven narrative assumptions and model uncertainty is very high at long horizons.
