## Card 1 — Capital Management · Easy

What are CET1, AT1, and Tier 2 capital, and what is each tier's primary characteristic?::CET1 (Common Equity Tier 1): highest quality capital — ordinary shares plus retained earnings. Fully loss-absorbing on a going-concern basis. AT1 (Additional Tier 1): includes perpetual, non-cumulative instruments (e.g. contingent convertible bonds / CoCos) that absorb losses before resolution. Tier 2: includes subordinated debt with maturity ≥ 5 years; absorbs losses only in gone-concern (liquidation/bail-in). Minimum requirements (Basel III): CET1 ≥ 4.5%, Tier 1 (CET1+AT1) ≥ 6%, Total Capital ≥ 8% of RWA. #flashcard #topic/capital-management #topic/basel #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-13

## Card 2 — IRRBB · Easy

Define Interest Rate Risk in the Banking Book (IRRBB) and name the two primary sensitivity metrics used to measure it.::IRRBB is the risk that changes in market interest rates adversely affect a bank's economic value or net interest income arising from its non-trading (banking book) positions. The two primary metrics are: (1) EVE sensitivity (Economic Value of Equity) — change in the NPV of all banking book cashflows under a rate shock; a long-run, balance-sheet solvency measure. (2) NII sensitivity (Net Interest Income) — change in expected net interest income over a short horizon (typically 1–2 years) under a rate scenario; a short-run earnings measure. #flashcard #topic/irrbb #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-13

## Card 3 — Climate Risk · Easy

Distinguish physical risk from transition risk in the context of climate-related financial risk.::Physical risk: financial losses arising from the physical effects of climate change. Includes acute risks (extreme weather events — floods, fires, cyclones) and chronic risks (rising sea levels, long-run temperature increases). Affects collateral values and borrower repayment capacity. Transition risk: losses arising from the shift to a lower-carbon economy via policy changes (e.g. carbon taxes), technology disruption, or changes in market sentiment. Affects the viability of carbon-intensive sectors. NGFS and BCBS both recognise these as the two primary channels through which climate change becomes a financial risk driver. #flashcard #topic/climate-risk #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-13

## Card 4 — Pricing · Easy

What is Funds Transfer Pricing (FTP) and why is it important for a bank?::FTP is an internal mechanism that allocates the cost of funds and the benefit of deposits across business units. A central treasury sets transfer rates for each product/maturity; lending units "buy" funds at the FTP rate, and deposit-gathering units "sell" funds at the FTP rate. Importance: (1) isolates each business unit's true margin, (2) centralises interest rate risk and liquidity risk in treasury, (3) incentivises correct loan pricing by embedding the marginal cost of liquidity, (4) creates accountability — a unit running a liquidity mismatch pays for it via the FTP charge. Without FTP, individual units may misprice risk and cross-subsidise one another. #flashcard #topic/pricing #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-13

## Card 5 — Stress Testing · Easy

Distinguish sensitivity analysis, scenario analysis, and reverse stress testing.::Sensitivity analysis: shocks one variable at a time (e.g. +200 bps rate shift) while holding all else constant. Identifies the most important individual risk drivers. Scenario analysis: applies a coherent, simultaneous set of shocks (e.g. a macroeconomic recession scenario: GDP −3%, unemployment +5%, house prices −20%). More realistic; reflects correlations. Reverse stress testing: starts from a pre-defined adverse outcome (e.g. bank becomes unviable) and works backwards to identify which plausible scenarios could cause it. Mandated by the PRA/PA for ICAAP. Purpose: identifies vulnerabilities and tail risks not captured by forward-looking scenarios. #flashcard #topic/stress-testing #topic/capital-management #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-13

## Card 6 — Basel / Capital · Easy

What is the Capital Conservation Buffer (CCB) and what happens when a bank breaches it?::The CCB is a Pillar 1 buffer of 2.5% of RWA composed entirely of CET1, required on top of the 4.5% CET1 minimum. Total minimum CET1 including CCB = 7%. If a bank's CET1 falls into the buffer zone (i.e. between 4.5% and 7%), automatic constraints apply to distributions — dividends, share buybacks, and discretionary bonus payments are restricted. The closer the bank's CET1 is to the 4.5% floor, the greater the proportion of earnings that must be retained. Regulatory purpose: ensure banks build capital in good times to absorb losses in downturns without breaching minimum requirements. #flashcard #topic/basel #topic/capital-management #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-13

## Card 7 — Credit Risk · Easy

What is Exposure at Default (EAD) and how is it estimated for off-balance sheet items under the Standardised Approach?::EAD is the estimated total exposure a bank has to a counterparty at the moment of default. For on-balance-sheet assets, EAD equals the drawn balance. For off-balance-sheet items (e.g. undrawn credit facilities, guarantees), EAD = drawn balance + CCF × undrawn amount. CCF (Credit Conversion Factor) converts off-balance-sheet exposures to on-balance-sheet equivalents. Under the SA, Basel prescribes CCFs: 100% for direct credit substitutes, 40% for committed revolving credit lines > 1 year, 20% for trade-related contingencies, 10% for short-term self-liquidating trade letters of credit, and 0% for unconditionally cancellable commitments. #flashcard #topic/credit-risk #topic/basel #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-13

## Card 8 — Market Risk · Easy

Define Expected Shortfall (ES) and explain why Basel's FRTB replaced VaR with ES as the primary internal models measure.::ES (also called Conditional VaR or CVaR) is the expected loss given that the loss exceeds the VaR threshold: ES = E[Loss | Loss > VaR_α]. At 97.5% confidence, 10-day ES ≈ 99% VaR for normal distributions but captures more tail risk for fat-tailed distributions. FRTB replaced VaR because: (1) VaR is not sub-additive — it can incentivise portfolio concentration; (2) VaR ignores the severity of losses beyond the threshold; (3) ES is coherent (sub-additive, monotone, positively homogeneous, translationinvariant); (4) 2008 GFC revealed VaR underestimated tail losses catastrophically. FRTB uses 97.5% ES for IMA and 99% VaR for backtesting comparison. #flashcard #topic/market-risk #topic/basel #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-13

What triggers a Suspicious Activity Report (SAR) in South Africa and to whom is it submitted?::Under the Financial Intelligence Centre Act (FICA), an accountable institution (e.g. a bank) must submit a SAR to the Financial Intelligence Centre (FIC) when it knows, suspects, or has reasonable grounds to suspect that a transaction involves proceeds of unlawful activities, is connected to money laundering or terrorist financing, or has no apparent business or lawful purpose. ## Card 11 — IRRBB · Medium

Explain the Basel IRRBB standardised framework for EVE sensitivity measurement, including the prescribed rate shocks and the regulatory outlier threshold.
?
Under the Basel IRRBB Standards (2016, incorporated into SA's Directive 7/2018), banks must calculate EVE sensitivity under six prescribed rate shock scenarios:
1. Parallel up
2. Parallel down
3. Short rate up (steepener)
4. Short rate down (flattener)
5. Long rate up (bear steepener)
6. Long rate down (bull steepener)

For each scenario, EVE is computed as: ΔEVE = NPV(stressed cashflows) − NPV(base cashflows), where all banking-book cashflows are mapped to repricing time buckets. Key assumptions include: (a) behavioural modelling for non-maturity deposits (NMDs) — stable vs. volatile split, with a cap of 5 years on average repricing for stable retail NMDs; (b) prepayment modelling for fixed-rate loans; (c) interest rate floor at 0% for scenarios with negative rates (unless regulators explicitly allow negative rates).

Regulatory outlier criterion: if the maximum ΔEVE across all six scenarios exceeds 15% of Tier 1 capital, the bank is an IRRBB outlier and must disclose this publicly. The PA/PRA may impose additional Pillar 2 capital requirements.

SA context: SARB Directive 7/2018 mandates standardised or enhanced approach for all significant banks, with EVE and NII reporting quarterly to the SARB.

## Card 12 — Climate Risk · Medium

Explain how transition risk translates into credit risk in a bank's corporate loan book, and how a bank should incorporate it into credit risk models.
?
Transition risk materialises as credit risk through two primary channels:

1. Revenue impairment — carbon-intensive borrowers (e.g. coal miners, fossil fuel producers) face declining revenues as carbon pricing (e.g. a carbon tax) increases costs, demand falls for their products, or stranded asset risk crystallises (reserves/assets become worthless). This directly deteriorates PD.

2. Collateral value erosion — assets linked to high-emission activities (e.g. commercial property with poor energy ratings, heavy machinery) lose market value, increasing LGD when these are held as security.

Incorporation into credit risk models:
- Sector-level scenario analysis: map NGFS transition scenarios (Orderly, Disorderly, Hothouse) to sector-specific revenue/cost shocks and derive PD and LGD overlays.
- Carbon intensity scoring: augment internal credit ratings with a borrower-level Scope 1/2/3 emissions intensity metric.
- Climate-adjusted PD: apply conditional downward revisions to PD estimates for exposed sectors under stressed carbon price paths.
- Concentration risk reporting: identify sectoral concentrations in high-transition-risk industries (energy, mining, utilities, transport) in the ICAAP.

Under BCBS Pillar 2 guidance (June 2022) and SARB guidance (2022), climate risk must be incorporated into ICAAP risk appetites and stress testing frameworks. Banks are expected to disclose climate risk exposures under TCFD-aligned frameworks.

## Card 13 — Pricing · Medium

Describe the full components of risk-adjusted loan pricing and how each links to regulatory capital or risk measurement frameworks.
?
A correctly priced loan must cover four components:

1. Cost of funds (FTP rate): reflects the marginal cost of raising the funds for the loan, including the liquidity premium (NSFR/LCR costs). This is set by the FTP desk and varies by tenor and currency.

2. Credit spread (expected loss premium): covers EL = PD × LGD × EAD. Priced as an annual spread over the FTP rate. If pricing < EL rate, the loan is immediately loss-making in expectation.

3. Capital charge: covers the cost of holding regulatory capital against UL. Computed as: Capital charge = RWA × Capital ratio × Cost of equity (Ke). Under IRB, RWA = K × 12.5 × EAD, where K is the IRB capital requirement formula. For SA banks using SA approach, RWA are prescribed by the risk weight lookup tables.

4. Operating costs and profit margin: overheads allocated to the facility plus the target economic profit.

Full loan rate = FTP + Credit spread + Capital charge + Operating costs + Profit margin.

The target metric is Risk-Adjusted Return on Capital (RAROC): RAROC = (Revenue − EL − Costs) / Economic Capital. A loan is only value-creating if RAROC ≥ Ke (hurdle rate). Banks using RAROC correctly eliminate cross-subsidisation between high- and low-risk lending units.

## Card 14 — Capital Management · Medium

Explain the buffer stack above the Pillar 1 minimum CET1 requirement, naming each buffer and who sets it.
?
The total CET1 requirement under Basel III / CRR is built up in layers:

1. **Pillar 1 minimum**: 4.5% of RWA — set by Basel (law).
2. **Capital Conservation Buffer (CCB)**: +2.5% of RWA, mandatory CET1. Set by Basel. Breaching triggers automatic distribution restrictions.
3. **Countercyclical Capital Buffer (CCyB)**: 0–2.5% of RWA, CET1 only. Set by the national designated authority (in SA: SARB/PA) based on aggregate credit growth relative to GDP. Currently 0% in SA. Released rapidly in stress to avoid credit crunch.
4. **G-SIB / D-SIB buffer**: 1–3.5% (G-SIB) or 0–3.5% (D-SIB) of RWA. In SA, the PA designates Domestic Systemically Important Banks (D-SIBs) — the "Big 4 + Nedbank" — and applies a 1–2% surcharge. CET1 only.
5. **Pillar 2A add-on**: Firm-specific requirement set by the PA after ICAAP review. Covers risks not fully captured in Pillar 1 (e.g. IRRBB, pension risk, concentration risk). Can be met with a blend of CET1, AT1, and T2.

Total maximum stacked requirement = 4.5% + 2.5% + 2.5% + 3.5% = 13% CET1 before Pillar 2. SA D-SIBs typically target CET1 ratios of 11.5–13% in practice.

## Card 15 — Stress Testing · Medium

Explain reverse stress testing: its purpose, mechanics, and why it is considered superior to forward stress tests for identifying tail risks.
?
**Purpose**: Reverse stress testing starts from a defined adverse outcome — typically "the business model becomes unviable" — and works backwards to identify which scenarios could cause that outcome. Mandated by the PRA (UK) and SARB (SA) as part of the ICAAP.

**Mechanics**:
1. Define the failure endpoint: e.g. CET1 ratio falls below 4.5%, liquidity cannot be maintained for 30 days, or the bank becomes unable to operate.
2. Identify the risk drivers and their threshold values that would produce this outcome (e.g. credit losses > X, NII falls by Y, funding withdrawal > Z).
3. Construct plausible scenarios that would generate these driver values simultaneously.
4. Assess probability of those scenarios and identify mitigating actions.

**Why superior to forward stress testing**:
- Forward tests assume specific scenarios; they miss risks from scenarios not imagined by management.
- Reverse tests surface structural vulnerabilities — if the bank fails with only a moderate scenario, its business model is fragile.
- Encourages honest confrontation of tail risk and informs recovery and resolution planning (BRRD/SARB Resolution Framework).
- Identifies which combinations of risks (correlated tail events) are most dangerous, rather than treating risks independently.

Key limitation: The result is a set of scenarios, not a probability — boards must apply judgement about plausibility.
#flashcard #topic/stress-testing #topic/capital-management #difficulty/medium #date/2026-04-13

## Card 16 — Credit Risk · Medium

Explain how EAD is modelled for revolving credit facilities under the IRB Advanced approach, distinguishing between drawn and undrawn components and the key regulatory constraints.
?
For revolving credit facilities (e.g. credit cards, overdrafts, revolving business loans), EAD has two components:

**Drawn balance**: the amount currently utilised — EAD contribution = drawn amount.

**Undrawn balance**: the unused portion of the committed limit. EAD contribution = CCF × undrawn balance, where the CCF (Credit Conversion Factor) — also called LEQ (Loan Equivalent Factor) or EAD% — models the proportion of the undrawn facility that will be drawn by the time of default.

**Why CCF > 0 matters**: Defaulting borrowers tend to draw down facilities aggressively as their financial position deteriorates (the "flight to the facility" effect). Historical data consistently shows EAD/limit ratios rising sharply prior to default, particularly for corporate revolvers.

**IRB Advanced approach**:
- Banks may estimate own CCFs using internal data (at least 5 years for retail, 7 for corporate).
- The CCF is typically modelled as a function of current utilisation rate, product type, obligor risk grade, and time to default.
- Key regulatory constraint (CRR Art. 182): estimated CCF must be based on a period of economic downturn if more conservative than long-run average. A CCF floor of 0% applies (negative CCF is disallowed).
- For some retail products (e.g. credit cards), banks are allowed to use the "fixed CCF" approach where CCF = 1 (i.e. limit, not current balance, is used as EAD).

## Card 17 — Market Risk / FRTB · Medium

Explain the FRTB Internal Models Approach (IMA) backtesting framework: the traffic light zones, consequences of breaches, and why this is considered more rigorous than the Basel II approach.
?
Under FRTB (BCBS 2019, implemented in SA from 2023 phased), IMA backtesting is conducted at the trading desk level (not just firm-wide), comparing the desk's daily P&L against its 99% 1-day VaR.

**Traffic light system (over 250 trading days)**:

- Green zone (0–4 exceptions): IMA approved; no capital add-on.
- Amber zone (5–9 exceptions): IMA provisionally approved; capital multiplier (k) increased from 1.5 to up to 2.0, depending on number of exceptions.
- Red zone (10+ exceptions): IMA disapproved; the desk must revert to the Standardised Approach (SA) for that desk's capital calculation.

**Key improvements over Basel II**:
1. Desk-level granularity — a single poor-performing desk cannot hide within a well-performing aggregate.
2. P&L attribution test (PLAT) — the desk must also demonstrate that its risk-theoretical P&L aligns with actual P&L within prescribed tolerances (R² and Spearman tests). Failure triggers SA fallback independently of backtesting.
3. ES replaces VaR as the primary capital metric, but VaR is retained as the backtesting benchmark.
4. Liquidity horizons (10–120 days) replace the uniform 10-day horizon, better capturing illiquid risk factor risk.

**SA context**: SA banks (Absa, Standard Bank) using IMA must maintain FRTB-compliant risk infrastructure, approved by the PA.
#flashcard #topic/market-risk #topic/basel #difficulty/medium #date/2026-04-13

## Card 18 — IRRBB · Hard

(a) Define the repricing gap and explain how it determines a bank's EVE exposure to a parallel rate shock. (b) A bank has RSA = R500m (average repricing in 2 years) and RSL = R600m (average repricing in 1 year). Estimate the approximate ΔEVE for a +200 bps parallel rate shock, using the duration approximation. (c) What SARB reporting obligations arise if this bank is an IRRBB outlier?
?
**(a) Repricing gap and EVE exposure:**
The repricing gap (Gap = Rate-Sensitive Assets − Rate-Sensitive Liabilities in a given time bucket) determines whether the bank is asset-sensitive (positive gap → benefits from rising rates) or liability-sensitive (negative gap → benefits from falling rates). For EVE, each cashflow is discounted at the stressed rate, so the sensitivity depends on the duration of assets versus liabilities. A bank with longer-duration assets than liabilities (asset-sensitive) will see EVE fall when rates rise (asset NPV falls more than liability NPV). ΔEVE ≈ −[Duration_A × RSA − Duration_L × RSL] × Δr / (1+r).

**(b) Numerical estimation:**
Using the approximation: ΔEVE ≈ −(D_A × RSA − D_L × RSL) × Δr
Assume duration ≈ average repricing maturity (rough approximation):
- D_A × RSA = 2 years × R500m = R1,000m·yr
- D_L × RSL = 1 year × R600m = R600m·yr
- Net position = R1,000m − R600m = R400m·yr
- ΔEVE ≈ −R400m × 0.02 = −R8m

So a +200 bps shock reduces EVE by approximately R8m. If Tier 1 capital is say R50m, ΔEVE/Tier 1 = 16% > 15% outlier threshold.

**(c) SARB / outlier reporting obligations:**
Under SARB Directive 7/2018 and Basel IRRBB Standards, if the maximum ΔEVE across all six prescribed scenarios exceeds 15% of Tier 1 capital, the bank must:
1. **Disclose** its outlier status in its Pillar 3 report (publicly).
2. **Report** the breach to the Prudential Authority quarterly via the DI 370 return.
3. **Explain** to the PA the drivers of the outlier breach and the management actions planned.
4. Be subject to **Pillar 2 capital add-on** at the PA's discretion if the outlier breach is structural and not mitigated by hedging.
The PA may also require enhanced IRRBB governance — senior ALCO accountability and quarterly board reporting.

## Card 19 — Capital Management / ICAAP · Hard

(a) Explain the structure of the ICAAP capital adequacy assessment, distinguishing Pillar 1, Pillar 2A, and Pillar 2B capital requirements and who sets each. (b) A SA D-SIB has CET1 = 11.2% of RWA. Its Pillar 1 stack (including D-SIB buffer) requires 8.5% CET1. The PA has set a Pillar 2A add-on of 1.5%. Stress testing shows a peak drawdown of 2.0% CET1 over 3 years. Assess whether the bank is adequately capitalised and what actions management might consider. (c) What is the role of management actions in ICAAP stress testing and how should they be presented?
?
**(a) ICAAP capital structure:**
- **Pillar 1 minimum** (4.5% CET1): Formula-based Basel requirement for credit, market, and operational risk. Set by Basel/CRR, implemented by PA.
- **Pillar 1 buffers** (CCB 2.5% + D-SIB 0–2% + CCyB 0–2.5%): Stack above the minimum, also CET1. Set by Basel (CCB) and PA/SARB (D-SIB, CCyB).
- **Pillar 2A**: Firm-specific add-on set by PA after ICAAP review; covers risks not in Pillar 1 (IRRBB, pension, concentration, model risk). Can be part-met with AT1/T2.
- **Pillar 2B** (SA equivalent of PRA Buffer): PA-imposed buffer based on stress test results; represents capital needed to absorb peak losses under the ICAAP stress scenario. CET1 only. Not publicly disclosed.

**(b) Capital adequacy assessment:**
Required CET1 stack = Pillar 1 min (4.5%) + CCB (2.5%) + D-SIB buffer (1.5%) + Pillar 2A (1.5%) = 10.0%.
Stress drawdown = 2.0% CET1. Post-stress CET1 = 11.2% − 2.0% = 9.2%.
Post-stress surplus over required stack = 9.2% − 10.0% = **−0.8%** — a deficit.

The bank's current headline CET1 (11.2%) exceeds the required stack (10.0%) by 1.2%, so it is adequately capitalised at present. However, under stress it dips below the requirement by 0.8%, suggesting the Pillar 2B buffer implied by the PA stress test is not fully covered. Management must either raise capital, reduce RWA, or demonstrate credible management actions.

**(c) Management actions in ICAAP stress testing:**
Management actions are steps available to the board/management team to mitigate capital drawdown in stress. They must be:
- **Credible**: proven executable in the scenario (e.g. a rights issue may not be feasible in a systemic crisis).
- **Timely**: capable of being implemented within the stress horizon.
- **Pre-approved**: signed off by the board as part of the Recovery Plan.

Typical management actions: dividend suspension, bonus deferral, RWA reduction (book run-off, securitisation), asset disposals, new AT1 issuance (if market open), drawdown of credit lines.

Presentation: stress tests should show results in two tranches — (i) before management actions and (ii) after management actions — so the PA can assess the true severity of the stress independently of management optimism.

## Card 20 — Climate Risk · Hard

(a) Explain how physical climate risk can impair a bank's mortgage portfolio, identifying the channels through which both PD and LGD are affected. (b) Describe how a bank would incorporate a NGFS "Hothouse World" scenario into its credit risk stress testing. (c) What are the PA/SARB's current expectations for climate risk management in South African banks?
?
**(a) Physical risk → mortgage PD and LGD impairment:**

**PD channel**: Physical events (floods, storms, wildfires) destroy or damage the borrower's home/property, impairing their finances and ability to service debt. Chronic physical risks (rising temperatures, drought) reduce agricultural and economic output in exposed regions, lowering household incomes and increasing unemployment — both of which raise PD.

**LGD channel**: Collateral value (the mortgaged property) declines for two reasons:
1. Direct damage to the property reduces its market value or insurability.
2. Climate-exposed locations (flood zones, coastal areas) face systemic repricing as buyers and lenders become unwilling to transact — analogous to "stranded asset" risk but for real estate.
Additionally, if insurance becomes unavailable or unaffordable in high-risk areas (a growing trend), recovery in default becomes more difficult, directly raising LGD.

South African context: Cape Town (drought), KwaZulu-Natal (flooding — April 2022 floods caused ≈R5.9bn in insured losses), and Johannesburg (hail) represent key acute physical risks.

**(b) NGFS Hothouse World scenario — application to credit risk stress testing:**
The NGFS Hothouse World scenario assumes current policies only — no additional climate action — leading to 3–4°C warming by 2100. It represents the highest physical risk scenario. Application:
1. Map the bank's geographic loan exposures to climate-hazard maps (flood zones, wildfire risk, coastal inundation).
2. Apply hazard-specific damage functions (% asset value destroyed per event) for acute risks.
3. Estimate frequency of extreme events under the scenario using climate science outputs (e.g. 1-in-50-year floods become 1-in-10).
4. Translate property value declines and income disruptions into PD and LGD overlays per exposure segment.
5. Aggregate into portfolio-level EL under the scenario; compare to base-case EL to derive the stress capital requirement.
6. Run over a 30-year horizon (standard for climate scenarios), which requires integrating the credit risk model with long-horizon macroeconomic projections.

**(c) SARB/PA expectations (2022–2026):**
The SARB published its Guidance Note on Climate-Related Financial Risk Management (2022), aligned with BCBS Principles for Climate-Related Financial Risk (2022). Key expectations:
- Governance: Board must own climate risk; integrate into risk appetite statement.
- Risk identification: Banks must identify material climate-related exposures across all risk types (credit, market, liquidity, operational).
- Scenario analysis: Banks must conduct climate scenario analysis (physical and transition), using NGFS scenarios as a reference. Not yet required to hold capital against climate risk, but must demonstrate capabilities.
- Disclosure: Align with TCFD; JSE-listed banks must publish TCFD-aligned disclosures.
- Integration into ICAAP: Climate risks must be incorporated into the ICAAP risk universe and reflected in stress testing by 2025–2026.
The PA has indicated that Pillar 2 capital add-ons for material unmanaged climate risk are a medium-term possibility as measurement methodologies mature.
