## Card 6 — Market Risk · Easy

What does Value at Risk (VaR) measure and how is it interpreted?::VaR measures the maximum expected loss over a given time period that will not be exceeded with a given probability (confidence level). ## Card 11 — Credit Risk · Medium

Explain the difference between Point-in-Time (PiT) and Through-the-Cycle (TTC) PD estimates, and when each is used.
?
PiT PDs reflect the current state of the economic cycle and all available information at the observation date. Ratings migrate as risk drivers change; observed default rates per grade are semi-cyclical. PiT PDs are volatile — rising in downturns and falling in expansions. Used for IFRS 9 provisioning and short-term risk management.
<!--SR:!2026-05-07,5,245-->

TTC PDs average the probability of default across a full economic cycle, stripping out cyclical variation. They are more stable, reducing capital volatility and RWA pro-cyclicality, but less responsive to current borrower conditions. Used for long-term capital planning.

The UK market presents a challenge: a complete economic cycle is difficult to identify from bank data, making Dynamic PiT (PiT + periodic recalibration) operationally complex and pro-cyclical in RWA terms, even though it is the dominant approach in the US.
#flashcard #topic/credit-risk #difficulty/medium #date/2026-04-07

## Card 12 — Credit Risk · Medium

Outline the four objective methods for estimating LGD and note when each is appropriate.
?
1. Market LGD — inferred from market prices of defaulted bonds shortly after default. Reflects market views; preferred by rating agencies. Limitation: defaulted bond data is scarce.
2. Workout LGD — estimated from actual post-default recovery cashflows, discounted at an appropriate rate. Most accurate when recovery data is available; requires extensive historical workout records.
3. Implied Market LGD — derived from credit spreads of high-risk (non-defaulted) bonds. Benefits from large market data pools; commonly used in derivatives. Least developed methodology.
4. Implied Historical LGD — estimated from long-run average losses on similar asset types. Basel only permits this for retail portfolios and requires supplementation with current information where available.

## Card 13 — Credit Risk · Medium

Explain the EAD model framework and the regulatory output floor formula.
?
EAD is estimated at account level as: EAD = EADF × Limit at Observation, where EADF is the expected Credit Conversion Factor at the time of default.

Four estimates are produced:
- PiT EADF: estimated directly from observed data; sensitive to the economic cycle; consistent with IFRS 9.
- LRA EADF: long-run average calibrated over the full historical period (per CRR Art. 182).
- DT EADF: downturn estimate calibrated to a stress period (e.g. 2008 GFC) per risk grade.
- Regulatory EADF = max(LRA EADF + MoC_LRA, DT EADF + MoC_DT)

Margin of Conservatism (MoC) is applied to both LRA and DT estimates to account for model uncertainty, data limitations, and sampling bias. Separate MoCs are computed per grade and aggregated at portfolio level.

## Card 14 — IFRS 9 · Medium

How does the PD comparison approach work for determining SICR, and what are its two main challenges?
?
A SICR event is declared when the magnitude of PD change since origination exceeds a threshold u:
- Absolute: m(x, t'_r) = p(x, t'_r) − p(x, t'_i) > u
- Relative: m(x, t'_r) = p(x, t'_r) / p(x, t'_i) > u

If m > u, the account migrates to Stage 2. The converse applies for cure (Stage 2 → Stage 1).

Challenge 1 — Threshold selection: No regulatory prescription for u. The EBA suggests 200% (relative) but offers no justification; the PRA has observed multiple threshold values across UK banks and portfolios. The choice is highly subjective.

Challenge 2 — PD accuracy: Reliance on point-estimate PDs requires modelling accuracy. Poor methodology, failure to capture the time-dynamic nature of lifetime PD, inadequate variable selection, or data quality issues all introduce classification error — either over-staging (costly) or under-staging (risky).
#flashcard #topic/ifrs9 #topic/credit-risk #difficulty/medium #date/2026-04-07

## Card 15 — Liquidity Risk · Medium

Explain the purpose of the NSFR and distinguish between ASF and RSF weights.
?
NSFR = ASF / RSF ≥ 100%. The Basel III long-term structural liquidity metric (1-year horizon) promotes resilience by requiring banks to fund activities with stable sources; it reduces reliance on short-term wholesale markets and limits rollover/maturity mismatch risk.

ASF (Available Stable Funding) weights by funding stability:
- 100%: Regulatory capital; instruments > 1 year
- 95%: Stable retail/SME demand deposits < 1 year
- 90%: Less stable retail/SME deposits < 1 year
- 50%: Non-financial corporate funding < 1 year; operational deposits

RSF (Required Stable Funding) weights by asset liquidity/tenor:
- 0%: Central bank reserves, cash
- 5%: Level 1 HQLA (excl. cash/reserves)
- 15%: Level 2A HQLA
- 65%: Residential mortgages > 1yr, risk weight ≤ 35%
- 100%: All other assets

## Card 16 — Market Risk · Medium

Describe the four structural sources of IRRBB mismatch and the three forms of interest rate risk they generate.
?
Four sources of mismatch:
1. Term — short-term liabilities funding long-term assets (e.g. demand deposits funding 20-year mortgages).
2. Volume — many small deposits funding fewer large loans.
3. Interest rate type — fixed vs floating mismatches on both sides of the balance sheet.
4. Product features — early deposit redemption and loan prepayment create embedded behavioural options.

Three resulting forms of IRRBB:
- Gap risk: term structure mismatch; measured via gap analysis; managed by cash or derivative hedges.
- Option risk: automatic (contractually embedded, e.g. caps/floors) and behavioural (customer-driven, e.g. prepayment, early redemption).
- Basis risk: assets and liabilities priced off different benchmarks or different tenors of the same benchmark (e.g. prime vs JIBAR 3M in South Africa). Not captured by gap analysis.

## Card 17 — Market Risk · Medium

Why is VaR not a coherent risk measure, and how does Expected Shortfall (ES) address this?
?
VaR fails the subadditivity axiom: ρ(L₁ + L₂) ≤ ρ(L₁) + ρ(L₂) need not hold. This means combining two positions can produce a higher combined VaR than the sum of individual VaRs — penalising diversification rather than rewarding it. VaR also captures only the loss threshold, not the severity of losses beyond it, underestimating tail risk for fat-tailed (leptokurtic) and skewed distributions.

ES = E[−X | X ≤ VaR[α]] — the average loss in the worst (1−α)% of scenarios. ES satisfies all four coherence axioms including subadditivity. FRTB (effective January 2023) replaced VaR with ES for trading book capital requirements to better capture tail risk.

Disadvantage of ES: less intuitive and harder to communicate to non-technical stakeholders.

## Card 18 — Operational Risk · Medium

Describe the three lines of defence model for AML/CFT risk management and the role of each.
?
1st Line — Business units (e.g. retail banking, corporate banking): front-line identification, assessment, and control of money laundering risks through Customer Due Diligence (CDD), transaction monitoring, and filing Suspicious Transaction Reports (STRs).

2nd Line — Chief AML/CFT Officer, compliance function, HR, technology: independently assess effectiveness of AML/CFT controls; design AML policies, systems, and training; report to senior management.

3rd Line — Internal or external auditors: provide an independent opinion on the adequacy and effectiveness of AML/CFT practices. Ultimate accountability rests with the board of directors.

Risk-based approach: the intensity of due diligence is commensurate with risk. Standard CDD for low-risk customers; Enhanced Due Diligence (EDD) for high-risk customers such as Politically Exposed Persons (PEPs) and clients in FATF-grey-listed jurisdictions.

## Card 19 — Regulation · Medium

How are G-SIBs bucketed by the FSB and what requirements apply to them?
?
The FSB identifies G-SIBs annually using the BCBS methodology (based on end-year data). They are allocated to five buckets corresponding to additional CET1 loss absorbency requirements:

| Bucket | Buffer | Examples |
|--------|--------|---------|
| 5 | 3.5% | Empty |
| 4 | 2.5% | Empty |
| 3 | 2.0% | Citi, HSBC, JPMorgan |
| 2 | 1.5% | BofA, Barclays, BNP, Deutsche |
| 1 | 1.0% | Majority of the 30 G-SIBs |

Beyond capital buffers, G-SIBs must: meet the TLAC standard (total loss-absorbing capacity for resolution); comply with enhanced resolvability requirements (recovery and resolution plans, Crisis Management Groups); and meet higher supervisory expectations for risk data aggregation (BCBS 239) and risk governance.

## Card 20 — Liquidity Risk · Medium

How does the LCR treat retail deposit run-off rates, and why are South African banks structurally disadvantaged?
?
LCR run-off rates by retail deposit type:
- Stable (deposit insurance + transactional relationship): 3–5%
- Less stable (no deposit insurance): 10%

South Africa does not have a functioning deposit insurance scheme (CoDI is being developed, targeting R100,000 coverage). Consequently, all SA retail and SME deposits are classified as "less stable" — a 10% run-off factor. This makes LCR compliance more demanding for SA banks than for peers in jurisdictions with established deposit insurance schemes: UK (£85,000 via FSCS), EU (€100,000). The structural effect is a permanently higher HQLA requirement relative to the balance sheet for an equivalent deposit base.

## Card 21 — Credit Risk · Hard

(a) Outline the three PD rating philosophies under IRB. (b) Discuss the UK market challenge for Dynamic PiT. (c) Explain why Dynamic PiT was rejected and PiT chosen in the design decision in your notes.
?
(a) Three philosophies:
- TTC (Through-the-Cycle): Grades based on static characteristics; no rating migration; ODR varies with the cycle. Simple but non-compliant if it ignores current information (CRR Art. 180(2)(a)).
- PiT (Point-in-Time): Ratings driven by segmentation and scorecard outputs; ratings migrate as risk drivers change; ODR semi-cyclical. Reflects current risk profile; supports partner onboarding.
- Dynamic PiT: PiT model plus periodic recalibration to track the cycle exactly; ODR per grade flat across the cycle; all cycle variation captured through rating migrations. Strongest PiT alignment.

(b) UK challenge: a complete economic cycle is difficult to identify from UK bank-specific data. In the US, complete cycles are identifiable, making Dynamic PiT more feasible. In the UK, recalibration relies on backward-looking factors and introduces pro-cyclical RWA volatility.

(c) Rejected Dynamic PiT: (1) operationally burdensome — frequent recalibration required; (2) pro-cyclical capital impact — RWA rises in downturns precisely when banks need capital most; (3) backward-looking calibration factors reduce forward-looking accuracy. Rejected TTC: non-compliant with CRR Art. 180(2)(a). Chosen PiT (without dynamic recalibration): CRR-compliant by incorporating current risk drivers; avoids recalibration burden; semi-cyclical via meaningful risk driver changes; partner-resilient.
#flashcard #topic/credit-risk #difficulty/hard #date/2026-04-07

## Card 22 — Credit Risk · Hard

(a) Outline the LGD decomposition model structure from your notes. (b) State the formula and define each payer-type component. (c) Explain the role of MoC in producing regulatory LGD.
?
(a) The model decomposes LGD into probability and severity components using logistic regression and GLM (CLM) frameworks. Two sub-models: LGD1 (non-defaulted, performing book) and LGD2 (defaulted, non-performing book). Three payer types: Zero Payer (zero recoveries), Full Payer (100%+ recoveries), Partial Payer (some recoveries, neither zero nor full).

(b) LGD = 1 − [(R_Zero + R_Full + R_Partial) + E[r(Debt Sale)] − CoR]

Where:
- R_Zero = P(Zero) × E[r(Zero)]  — 0% undiscounted recovery assumed
- R_Full = (1−P(Zero)) × P(Full|not Zero) × E[r(Full)]  — 100% undiscounted recovery, adjusted for discounting
- R_Partial = (1−P(Zero)) × (1−P(Full)) × E[r(Partial)]
- CoR = total direct and indirect cost of recoveries

(c) PiT LGDs are grouped into risk grades and calibrated to the empirically observed Long Run Average (LRA) and Downturn (DT) LGD. Margin of Conservatism (MoC) is then added — computed per grade, aggregated at portfolio level — to account for model uncertainty, residual estimation errors, and data limitations. Regulatory LGD = LRA/DT + MoC + Appropriate Adjustments (AA).

## Card 24 — Credit Risk · Hard

(a) Define EE, EPE, and EEPE in counterparty credit risk. (b) Explain the IMM EAD formula and the purpose of the alpha factor. (c) Describe wrong-way risk.
?
(a) EE(t) = E[max(V(t), 0)] — average exposure to a counterparty at future time t; only positive MtM values matter (if MtM is negative, the bank owes the counterparty, so no credit exposure exists).

EPE(t) = max_{s≤t} EE(s) — a non-decreasing transformation of EE that prevents artificial exposure decline over time due to modelling artefacts; reflects that credit risk does not mechanically decrease as maturity approaches.

EEPE = Σ EPE(t_k) × Δt_k — the time-weighted average EPE over the shorter of 1 year or the netting set maturity; the core input to the IMM EAD formula.

(b) IMM EAD = 1.4 × EEPE. The alpha factor of 1.4 is a regulatory scaling factor that partially compensates for: (i) model uncertainty not captured in the simulation, and (ii) wrong-way risk not fully reflected in the EEPE.

(c) Wrong-way risk: the counterparty's probability of default is positively correlated with the size of the bank's exposure to that counterparty. Example: a bank selling CDS protection on a reference entity to a counterparty whose own creditworthiness is correlated with the reference entity — the counterparty is most likely to default precisely when the bank's exposure is at its largest. EEPE partially addresses this by focusing on positions that could become exposures in the future.

## Card 25 — Market Risk · Hard

(a) Define asset-sensitive and liability-sensitive banks. (b) Explain how each responds to a rising rate environment across NII and EVE. (c) Outline two tools for managing gap risk.
?
(a) Asset-sensitive: interest-earning assets (loans, investments) reprice more quickly than interest-bearing liabilities (deposits, wholesale funding). Liability-sensitive: liabilities reprice faster than assets — typical when long-term fixed-rate assets are funded with short-term deposits.

(b) Rising rate environment:
- Asset-sensitive: NII benefits as asset yields reset upward faster than funding costs increase. EVE is less affected as assets have shorter duration. Net position: rising rates are broadly positive.
- Liability-sensitive: NII is compressed as deposit rates rise faster than loan yields can reset. EVE declines as the discount rate applied to long-duration fixed-rate assets increases, reducing their present value. Net position: rising rates are broadly negative — the structural risk of maturity transformation.

(c) Gap risk management:
1. Cash hedges — write assets or liabilities at specific repricing maturities to reduce gap (e.g. offer 5-year fixed-rate deposits to offset 5-year fixed-rate mortgages). Natural hedge; no counterparty risk.
2. Derivative hedges — FRAs, futures, and interest rate swaps synthetically alter repricing profiles. More flexible and scalable than cash hedges but introduce basis risk and require collateral management.

## Card 26 — Basel Framework · Hard

(a) Outline the three tiers of regulatory capital and their eligibility criteria. (b) Explain the Basel IV output floor and its significance. (c) Distinguish Pillar 1 from Pillar 2 capital.
?
(a) CET1 (Common Equity Tier 1): highest quality capital — ordinary shares, retained earnings, accumulated OCI. Fully loss-absorbing, permanently available, no fixed charges or stated maturity. Subject to deductions (e.g. goodwill, deferred tax assets).
AT1 (Additional Tier 1): perpetual instruments with principal write-down or conversion triggers (contingent convertibles — CoCos). Discretionary coupons; no fixed maturity.
Tier 2: subordinated debt with maturity ≥ 5 years; lower quality; loss-absorbing only in resolution or winding-up. Includes general provisions (limited).

(b) Output floor (Basel IV): IRB-calculated RWAs must be no lower than 72.5% of Standardised Approach RWAs. This limits the capital benefit banks derive from internal models, prevents excessive RWA reduction, and restores cross-institution comparability. Significant capital impact for banks whose internal model RWAs fall well below the SA floor.

(c) Pillar 1: Formula-driven minimum — 8% total capital / RWAs (≥ 4.5% CET1, ≥ 6% Tier 1). Covers credit, market, and operational risk via prescribed approaches.
Pillar 2: Supervisor-set additional capital based on ICAAP review, covering risks not fully captured in Pillar 1 (e.g. concentration risk, IRRBB, pension risk, strategic risk). Bank must demonstrate internal capital adequacy; result can be bank-specific.
#flashcard #topic/basel #difficulty/hard #date/2026-04-07

## Card 27 — Pricing · Hard

(a) Outline the asymmetric option risk embedded in fixed-rate mortgages. (b) Explain how UK banks manage this through deal periods and SVR. (c) Describe what happened to US S&L institutions and the structural lesson it teaches.
?
(a) Fixed-rate mortgages embed an asymmetric borrower option: if rates fall, borrowers prepay or refinance — reducing the bank's fixed-rate asset duration and forcing reinvestment at lower rates. If rates rise, borrowers stay — extending duration at a below-market rate, compressing NIM. The bank bears the full downside of the rate cycle with none of the corresponding upside repricing.

(b) UK banks use deal periods (2 or 5 years fixed). After expiry, the mortgage reverts to the bank's Standard Variable Rate (SVR) — a bank-controlled rate that can be reset with adequate notice. SVR creates a forced repricing point, restoring NIM management capability. Duration mismatch is therefore limited to the initial deal period rather than the full mortgage term.

(c) US S&L crisis: US regulations historically required 15-year fixed-rate mortgages. During the 1970s–1980s inflation surge, rising short-term rates caused S&Ls to pay escalating deposit rates while earning fixed, low mortgage rates — creating persistently negative margins. Approximately one-third of US S&Ls failed. Resolution: US banks now securitise mortgages (Freddie Mac, Fannie Mae, ABS), transferring duration risk and prepayment risk to capital market investors. Structural lesson: product design must incorporate repricing mechanisms or the bank becomes a one-sided bearer of the interest rate cycle.
#flashcard #topic/pricing #topic/market-risk #difficulty/hard #date/2026-04-07

## Card 28 — Credit Risk · Hard

(a) Distinguish the Standardised Approach (SA) from Foundation IRB and Advanced IRB. (b) Explain the Basel IV output floor. (c) Describe the IRB use test requirement and why it exists.
?
(a) Standardised Approach: uses fixed regulatory risk weights by exposure category (e.g. 75% for retail, 100% for unrated corporates, 150% for past due). Simple; no internal modelling required; less risk-sensitive.
Foundation IRB (F-IRB): banks estimate PD internally; LGD and EAD are prescribed by regulators (e.g. 45% LGD for senior unsecured corporate). Intermediate complexity.
Advanced IRB (A-IRB): banks estimate PD, LGD, and EAD internally, subject to regulator approval. Greatest risk sensitivity; highest data and governance requirements. Retail portfolios must use A-IRB.

(b) Basel IV output floor: IRB RWAs ≥ 72.5% × SA RWAs. Prevents excessive capital benefit from internal models; restores cross-institution comparability; significant impact for banks with very low model-derived RWAs.

(c) Use test (CRR Art. 144): IRB ratings and risk estimates must be materially used in internal decision-making — credit approval, risk-based pricing, capital allocation, ICAAP, and performance measurement. Banks cannot develop models purely for regulatory capital while using different systems internally. The use test ensures models are robust, maintained, connected to business reality, and not gamed — regulators review actual usage as part of model approval.

## Card 29 — Regulation · Medium

Describe the five core functions of a central bank, and explain quantitative easing (QE) and repos.
?
Five core functions:
1. Currency issuance — issuing notes and coins.
2. Foreign reserve management — managing the country's foreign exchange reserves.
3. Monetary policy — controlling inflation by setting policy rates (raising rates to reduce borrowing demand; lowering to stimulate). Also setting the countercyclical capital buffer (CCyB).
4. Payment system oversight — ensuring effectiveness and integrity of national payment systems.
5. Bank regulation and supervision — often via a subsidiary authority (PRA in UK, Prudential Authority in SA).

QE: when policy rates approach zero, central banks stimulate by purchasing large amounts of government bonds and other securities from banks, increasing the money supply and reducing long-term yields. Used post-2008 and post-COVID.

Repos: central banks purchase securities from banks under an agreement to resell (repurchase agreement / repo), providing temporary liquidity. Reverse repos remove liquidity. Both maintain appropriate liquidity in the banking system day-to-day.

## Card 30 — Credit Risk · Hard

(a) Distinguish provisions and regulatory capital in purpose and balance sheet treatment. (b) Explain the IFRS 9 ECL calculation and the role of forward-looking information (FLI). (c) Identify one key difference between IFRS 9 and IRB approaches to credit loss estimation.
?
(a) Provisions (impairments): income forfeited today (income statement: impairment charge) held as a balance sheet reserve (reduction in asset carrying value). Designed to smooth earnings volatility and pre-empt write-offs. Cover Expected Loss (EL).
Regulatory capital (CET1, AT1, T2): stock of equity and qualifying instruments absorbing Unexpected Loss (UL) — the deviation of actual losses from EL. Required as a buffer for tail-risk events. The two work jointly: provisions → EL; capital → UL.

(b) IFRS 9 ECL = PD × LGD × EAD, discounted to the reporting date. Three stages set the ECL horizon (12-month vs lifetime). Critically, FLI (Forward-Looking Information) must be incorporated — PDs must be adjusted for macro scenarios (base, upside, downside), probability-weighted. This makes IFRS 9 provisioning explicitly forward-looking and scenario-dependent.

(c) Key difference: IRB calibrates PDs to a Long Run Average (LRA) over a full economic cycle — TTC-like anchoring reduces RWA pro-cyclicality. IFRS 9 requires PiT estimates with FLI, causing provisions to fluctuate sharply with the economic cycle — rising steeply in downturns. This creates a potential double-hit in recessions: higher provisions reduce capital, while RWA may also increase, compressing the capital ratio from both sides.
