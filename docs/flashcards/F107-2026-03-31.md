## Card 1 — Business Model · Easy

What is maturity transformation and why does it create structural vulnerability for banks?::Maturity transformation is the simultaneous pursuit of lending long (e.g. mortgages) while accepting deposits on demand (short-dated liabilities). This creates a structural maturity mismatch: assets are illiquid and long-dated; liabilities are payable on demand. If depositors collectively withdrew simultaneously (a bank run), the bank could not fund its long-dated assets and would become insolvent — making liquidity [[01-liquidity_framework|risk management]] and capital requirements essential. #flashcard #topic/business-model #difficulty/easy #date/2026-03-31

Stage 2 — credit risk has increased significantly but asset is not yet credit-impaired; lifetime ECL recognised. Stage 3 — credit-impaired (defaulted/non-performing); lifetime ECL recognised and interest revenue calculated on amortised cost (net of loss allowance), not gross carrying amount. #flashcard #topic/ifrs9 #topic/credit-risk #difficulty/easy #date/2026-03-31

## Card 3 — Operational Risk · Easy

What is the Basel II definition of operational risk and what are its four sources?::Basel II defines operational risk as "the risk of direct or indirect loss resulting from inadequate or failed internal processes, people and systems or from external events." Legal risk is included; reputational and strategic risks are excluded. The four sources are: (1) Internal processes — unclear or incomplete processes; (2) People — poor culture, conflicts of interest, inadequate enforcement of rules; (3) Systems — technology failures, security breaches; (4) External events — fraud, natural disasters, external crimes. #flashcard #topic/operational-risk #difficulty/easy #date/2026-03-31

## Card 6 — Risk Management · Medium

Outline the three lines of defence in bank risk management and identify a key committee associated with each.
?
1st Line (Risk Ownership) — Business/revenue-generating units. Perform granular transaction-level controls daily; own the risk they take on.
2nd Line (Risk Control) — Independent risk and compliance functions. Key committees include: Credit Risk Committee (monitors credit exposures and provisions); ALCO/Treasury Committee (balance sheet, capital, and funding); Model Risk Committee (validates and monitors internal models); Group Risk Committee (board-level risk governance per King Code).
3rd Line (Independent Assurance) — Internal audit and Audit Committee; independent review of control adequacy; board members must confirm adequacy of internal controls in the AFS.
4th Line — External auditors and regulatory authorities.

## Card 7 — Pricing · Medium

Explain the difference between fundamental pricing and tactical pricing, and define the formula for loan pricing.
?
Fundamental pricing derives a theoretically fair price using a DCF model that captures all expected income, costs, credit losses, and the required return on CET1 capital — it answers "what rate makes this product economically viable?"
Tactical pricing is the adjusted, market-facing price incorporating competitive dynamics, strategic objectives, and mechanisms such as customised pricing, non-linear pricing, or revenue management.
Loan pricing formula:
Loan rate = FTP cost + credit risk premium + operating cost margin + profit margin
The bank targets NIACC-positive pricing (Net Income After Cost of Capital > 0) and uses credit cut-off scores to exclude borrowers where even risk-adjusted rates cannot break even.

## Card 8 — IRRBB · Medium

Describe the four forms of interest rate risk in the banking book (IRRBB) and distinguish automatic from behavioural option risk.
?
Four forms:
(1) Gap risk — maturity mismatch between assets and liabilities at different repricing dates; managed via cash hedges (fixed-rate deposits to offset mortgages) or derivative hedges (FRAs, swaps).
(2) Option risk — customers alter the level or timing of cashflows:
  - Automatic: contractually embedded; cap risk (loan rate floor compresses margin when rates rise) and floor risk (deposit rate floor compresses margin when rates fall).
  - Behavioural: driven by customer behaviour within product terms; prepayment risk (early loan repayment) and early redemption risk (deposit withdrawal before maturity).
(3) Basis risk — assets and liabilities priced off different benchmarks (e.g. prime vs JIBAR 3-months); not captured in the gap report.
(4) Yield curve risk — non-parallel yield curve shifts affect borrow-short/lend-long strategies differently.

## Card 9 — Liquidity · Medium

Rank the five most stable deposit types in the liability stability hierarchy and explain why retail current accounts are the most stable despite being contractually on-demand.
?
Most stable to least stable (top 5):
1. Retail current accounts
2. Retail deposit accounts
3. Corporate operational cashflow / call accounts
4. Retail savings accounts
5. Retail fixed-term deposits

Retail current accounts are contractually payable on demand, but exhibit sticky behavioural tenor — customers maintain large, stable balances over long periods. The lead time required to open a new account makes rapid transfer difficult during a 30-day stress window. Regulators permit banks to treat a proportion of these balances as term funding, with the exact amount determined by statistical analysis of historical account behaviour.

## Card 10 — Liquidity · Medium

State the LCR formula, its minimum requirement, and explain why South Africa has no stable retail deposit classification.
?
LCR = Stock of HQLA ÷ Total net cash outflows over next 30 calendar days ≥ 100%
Net cash outflows = total expected outflows − total expected inflows (inflows capped at 75% of outflows).

Purpose: ensure sufficient HQLA to survive a combined idiosyncratic and market-wide 30-day stress scenario.

South Africa context: A "stable" retail deposit classification requires an effective deposit insurance scheme (DIS) — one that guarantees prompt pay-outs and has legal powers, operational independence, and public awareness. South Africa does not yet have a functioning DIS (CoDI was established in 2021 targeting R100 000 coverage per depositor, but is not yet operational). Therefore all South African retail and SME deposits are classified as "less stable" (10% run-off factor), making LCR compliance structurally more demanding than in deposit-insured jurisdictions.

## Card 11 — Liquidity · Medium

Compare LCR and NSFR: purpose, formula, time horizon, and the South Africa-specific NSFR dispensation.
?
LCR — short-term (30-day) stress resilience: HQLA ÷ Net outflows ≥ 100%.
NSFR — one-year structural funding resilience: Available Stable Funding (ASF) ÷ Required Stable Funding (RSF) ≥ 100%.

NSFR addresses rollover and mismatch risk by requiring stable funding for all assets held. ASF weights range from 100% (regulatory capital, liabilities >1yr) down to 0% (short-term wholesale liabilities). RSF weights reflect how much stable funding each asset type requires.

SA dispensation: Under standard Basel III, ZAR-denominated financial corporate funding with residual maturity <6 months carries an ASF factor of 0% (no stable funding value). The SARB granted a SA-specific exception assigning 35% ASF to this funding category, recognising that exchange controls and the closed rand system prevent capital from leaving the domestic economy — making outflows structurally less volatile than implied by the Basel rule.

## Card 12 — Market Risk · Medium

Define Value at Risk (VaR), explain what a VaR break is, and contrast VaR with Stressed VaR (sVaR).
?
VaR[α] = the maximum loss not exceeded with probability α over a given holding period.
Example: 1-day VaR of R25m at 95% confidence means there is an estimated 5% probability of losing more than R25m in a single trading day (~1 in 20 days).

A VaR break occurs when actual losses exceed the VaR threshold. In a well-calibrated model, breaks occur (1 − α)% of trading days. Banks back-test daily; regulators impose capital add-ons for models with excessive breaks.

sVaR (Stressed VaR) — uses the most stressful consecutive 1-year period from historical data (typically the 2008 crisis) to calculate VaR. sVaR is used alongside current VaR to set regulatory capital levels and cannot be lower than point-in-time VaR. Both VaR and sVaR feed economic capital calculations.

## Card 13 — Market Risk Hedging · Medium

Compare FRAs, futures, and OIS swaps as interest rate hedging tools — highlight key structural differences.
?
All three instruments allow a bank to lock in or hedge an interest rate for a future period without necessarily borrowing or lending the underlying funds. The differences are structural — how they trade, how they settle, and what risk they carry.

1. Forward Rate Agreements (FRAs)

A bilateral OTC contract where two parties agree today on a fixed interest rate to be applied to a notional amount over a specified future period.

Quoted as e.g. a "3×6 FRA" — the hedge starts in 3 months and covers the period to month 6. Cash-settled at the start of the covered period — the net difference between the FRA rate and the prevailing reference rate (e.g. JIBAR/SOFR) is discounted and paid upfront. Fully customisable — notional, dates, and reference rate are negotiated bilaterally. Counterparty credit risk exists because it's bilateral (unless a Credit Support Annex is in place). No daily margining unless specifically agreed.

Best for: hedging a specific future borrowing or lending rate where the bank needs tailored dates and amounts.

2. Interest Rate Futures

Exchange-traded, standardised contracts referencing an implied short-term rate. 

Traded on exchanges (e.g. CME for SOFR futures) with standardised notional sizes and expiry dates (typically IMM dates — March, June, September, December) Priced as 100 minus the implied rate — so if the implied rate is 5%, the contract trades at 95. Daily mark-to-market — gains and losses are settled in cash every day via variation margin; initial margin is also required upfront. No counterparty credit risk — a central counterparty (CCP) sits between buyer and seller. Very liquid — easiest instrument to enter and exit quickly. Basis risk: the standardised contract may not perfectly match the bank's actual exposure dates or notional

Best for: liquid, short-dated hedges where speed of execution and no credit risk matters; also used for speculative positioning on rate direction.

3. Overnight Index Swaps (OIS)

An OTC swap where one party pays a fixed rate and receives a compounded overnight rate (e.g. SOFR in the US, SONIA in the UK, SABOR in South Africa) on a notional amount.

The floating leg compounds the overnight rate daily over the swap period; net settlement occurs at maturity. No exchange of principal — only the net interest difference is paid. Tenors range from one week to 30+ years. Post-LIBOR transition, OIS has become the primary benchmark derivative because overnight rates (SOFR, SONIA) replaced term rates (LIBOR) as the standard reference. Also used as the discount rate for collateralised derivatives (OIS discounting replaced LIBOR discounting after the financial crisis). Low counterparty risk; increasingly centrally cleared under post-crisis regulation

Best for: hedging exposure to central bank policy rate movements; managing overnight funding costs; longer-dated rate hedges in the post-LIBOR world.
<!--SR:!2026-04-15,1,230-->

Futures — Exchange-traded, standardised terms (e.g. JIBAR futures on the JSE). Margined daily (initial + variation margin), eliminating counterparty credit risk. Strips of consecutive contracts create long-dated hedges. Cleared via SAFCOM in South Africa.

OIS (Overnight Index Swap) — One party pays fixed rate; other pays the daily compounded overnight index rate on a nominal amount. No exchange of principal. Allows treasury to separate interest rate risk from liquidity risk. A pay-fixed-receive-JIBAR 3-months OIS offsets floating-rate liabilities referencing JIBAR 3-months.

Key challenge for all instruments: basis risk — the hedging instrument may not move perfectly with the bank's underlying exposure (e.g. prime-JIBAR basis).

## Card 14 — Capital / Stress Testing · Medium

Define the Pillar 2B Capital Planning Buffer (CPB) and list the four key drivers modelled in a stress scenario.
?
The CPB is the peak shortfall in CET1 capital observed over the stress horizon relative to Pillar 1 + Pillar 2A requirements:
CPB = max over t of (Pillar 1 + Pillar 2A − CET1(t))

CET1(t) evolves as: CET1(0) + Σ[Revenue(s) − Impairments(s) − Dividends(s)] − ΔRWA(s)

Four key stress drivers:
(1) Impairment charges — PD migrations and LGD increases from macro shocks (GDP decline, unemployment rise, property price falls); these flow through IFRS 9 Stage 2/3 migrations.
(2) Revenue compression — NIM compression from lower rates or higher funding costs; non-interest income falls with lower business volumes.
(3) RWA inflation — portfolio downgrades shift borrowers to higher risk weight buckets.
(4) Dividend and payout policy — most banks suspend or reduce dividends during stress.

## Card 15 — Capital / Stress Testing · Medium

Describe the three types of stress scenario used in Pillar 2B stress testing.
?
(1) Macroeconomic downside — GDP contraction, unemployment spike, property price decline, interest rate shock. The most common scenario type, often prescribed by supervisors (PRA in UK, PA in South Africa) as a baseline.
(2) Idiosyncratic stress — a scenario specific to the bank, such as a major operational failure, a reputational event, or a concentrated sector loss. Tailored to the bank's own risk profile.
(3) Reverse stress test — instead of asking "what happens if X occurs?", asks "what scenario would cause the business model to fail?" and then assesses whether that scenario is plausible. This tests resilience at the edge of viability rather than within it.

## Card 16 — Credit Risk · Medium

State the Expected Loss (EL) formula, define each component, and explain the difference between EL and Unexpected Loss (UL).
?
EL = Σ PD_i^TTC × EAD_i × LGD_i

Components:
PD (Probability of Default) — average percentage of obligors defaulting over a 1-year horizon (through-the-cycle).
EAD (Exposure at Default) — estimated outstanding balance if the borrower defaults.
LGD (Loss Given Default) — proportion of EAD not recovered after default (expressed as a % of EAD).

EL represents the normal cost of doing business — covered by provisions and pricing (credit risk premiums in loan rates).
UL (Unexpected Loss) — the variability of losses around EL; measured by the standard deviation of the portfolio loss distribution. Unlike EL, UL is not a simple sum of individual ULs — it depends on loss correlations. Capital is held to absorb UL at a 99.9% confidence level under Basel II (i.e. the bank should remain solvent in all but 1-in-1000-year events).

## Card 17 — Capital / IRB · Hard

(a) Write out the IRB capital requirement formula K and define each term. (b) Explain what the WCDR represents and how it is derived from the Vasicek model. (c) Contrast F-IRB and A-IRB approaches in terms of which parameters are bank-supplied vs supervisory.
?
(a) K = EAD_Reg × LGD_Reg × [N((G(PD) + √R × G(0.999)) / √(1−R)) − PD] × MA
where: N = standard normal CDF; G = inverse standard normal; PD = through-the-cycle PD; R = asset correlation; MA = maturity adjustment = (1 + (m−2.5)b) / (1−1.5b); b = [0.11852 − 0.05478 × ln(PD)]²; m = effective maturity.
RWA = K × 12.5 (conversion from capital to RWA using the Basel I equivalence).

(b) WCDR (Worst-Case Default Rate) — the PD that occurs at the 99.9th percentile of the systematic risk factor. Derived from the Vasicek single-factor ASRF model: WCDR = N((N⁻¹(PD) + √ρ × N⁻¹(0.999)) / √(1−ρ)). It represents the default rate exceeded in only 1 in every 1,000 bank-years. The capital charge K = LGD × (WCDR − PD) × MA captures only unexpected losses since EL is assumed to be covered by pricing.

(c) F-IRB: bank supplies PD only. Supervisory values apply for LGD (45% senior, 75% subordinated), EAD (credit equivalent approach), and M (set at 2.5). A-IRB: bank supplies PD, LGD, EAD, and M for corporate/sovereign/bank exposures — subject to regulatory approval and validation.

## Card 18 — Capital / ICAAP · Hard

(a) Outline the two types of capital assessment in the ICAAP. (b) List four Pillar 2A risk categories and describe how credit concentration risk is quantified. (c) Explain how economic capital differs from regulatory capital on three dimensions.
?
(a) ICAAP has two components:
  PiT Capital Assessment — Pillar 1 (8% × RWA for credit, market, operational) + Pillar 2A (add-ons for risks not in Pillar 1) + MIRA (material risk assessment across all risk types).
  Forward-Looking Assessment — Pillar 2B stress testing: quantitative (stress scenarios) and qualitative (loss absorption assessment); results in the Capital Planning Buffer (CPB).

(b) Four Pillar 2A categories:
  Credit Concentration Risk — uses Gordy-Lütkebohmert granularity adjustment (GA) for name concentration, or multi-factor Vasicek extension for sector concentration.
  IRRBB — quantified via EVE sensitivity (ΔEVEmax across six Basel rate shock scenarios; outlier if >15% of Tier 1 for 200bp shock) and NII sensitivity.
  Pension Risk — stressed pension deficit less the base deficit and existing CET1 deduction.
  Model Risk — percentage uplift α to Pillar 1 RWAs reflecting model validation findings, back-testing failures, MoC analysis.

(c) Three differences between EC and regulatory capital:
  Confidence level: EC uses ~99.95–99.97% vs Basel's fixed 99.9%.
  Diversification: EC recognises inter-risk correlation benefits; ASRF assumes infinite granularity and no sector concentration.
  Risk coverage: EC covers all material risks (IRRBB, pension, model, strategic, reputational); Pillar 1 covers only credit, market, and operational risk.

## Card 19 — Regulatory Capital · Hard

(a) Outline the evolution from Basel I to Basel 3.1 in terms of credit risk RWA methodology. (b) Explain why a bank would prefer A-IRB over Standardised Approach. (c) State the output floor introduced under Basel 3.1 and explain its purpose.
?
(a) Basel I — simple risk buckets (0%, 20%, 50%, 100%); corporate = 100%; retail mortgage = 50%; single 8% CAR; only credit risk initially (market risk added via 1996 Amendment).
Basel II — granular SA using external ratings + IRB approaches (F-IRB and A-IRB) based on internal PD/LGD/EAD estimates; 99.9% confidence, Vasicek/ASRF framework; new capital charge for operational risk.
Basel III — strengthened SA; introduced capital buffers (capital conservation, countercyclical); higher quality CET1 capital requirements; added leverage ratio and liquidity standards (LCR/NSFR).
Basel 3.1 (Basel IV) — tightened SA to reduce RWA variability; revised IRB rules limiting which portfolios can use A-IRB; introduced output floor.

(b) A-IRB benefits: internal models can incorporate supplementary customer information unavailable to ECAIs; more risk-sensitive weights mean lower capital for high-quality borrowers; capital optimisation incentivises better credit risk management. Example: R393m RWA for corporate loans under A-IRB vs R1bn under SA or Basel I.

(c) Output floor (Basel 3.1): A-IRB RWAs cannot be less than 72.5% of the equivalent SA RWAs. Purpose: prevent excessive RWA reduction through modelling choices; maintain a minimum capital floor across banks regardless of model sophistication; reduce RWA variability and restore confidence in capital ratios.

## Card 20 — AML/KYC · Hard

(a) Outline the five KYC areas a bank must maintain. (b) Discuss two categories of high-risk customer requiring enhanced due diligence. (c) Explain what grey-listing means and its consequences for a bank operating in a grey-listed jurisdiction.
?
(a) Five KYC areas:
  1. Customer Acceptance Policy — define risk-based criteria for accepting or declining customers.
  2. Customer and Beneficial Owner Identification, Verification, and Risk-Profiling — apply CDD; if verification fails, do not open account and raise an STR.
  3. Ongoing Monitoring — continuously monitor transactions against customer risk profile; flag unusual patterns.
  4. Management of Information — retain customer records and transaction history for at least five years after the relationship ends (FATF Recommendation 11).
  5. Reporting of Suspicious Transactions — transparent STR process; all staff trained; reports submitted to the relevant authority (e.g. FIC in South Africa under FICA).

(b) Two high-risk categories:
  Politically Exposed Persons (PEPs) — high-profile public officials whose position creates elevated risk of bribery, corruption, or misuse of public funds. Warrant enhanced due diligence and ongoing heightened monitoring.
  High cash-usage businesses (e.g. casinos) — cash-intensive environments make placement of illicit funds easier to disguise; require enhanced transaction monitoring and source-of-funds verification.

(c) Grey-listing: FATF places a country on the "increased monitoring" list for non-compliance with its 40 Recommendations. Consequences: banks in grey-listed jurisdictions face enhanced due diligence from correspondent banks globally; transaction processing becomes slower and more expensive; some international banks may reduce or sever correspondent relationships; banks may face higher compliance costs, reputational damage, and reduced access to international capital markets. South Africa was grey-listed by FATF in 2023.
