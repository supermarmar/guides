## Card 1 — Capital Structure · Easy

State the three tiers of Basel III regulatory capital, classify each as going-concern or gone-concern, and give the minimum ratio for each relative to RWA.::CET1 (going-concern): ordinary shares + retained earnings + AOCI + share premium. Minimum 4.5% of RWA. AT1 (going-concern): perpetual subordinated instruments with no step-up, discretionary coupons (e.g., contingent convertibles / CoCos). Tier 1 = CET1 + AT1 ≥ 6% of RWA. Tier 2 (gone-concern): subordinated debt with ≥5-year original maturity, amortised over final 5 years. Total capital = T1 + T2 ≥ 8% of RWA. The distinction matters because only going-concern capital absorbs losses before insolvency; gone-concern capital absorbs losses in resolution. In SA, the Banks Act regulation sets the floor at max(R250 million, 10% of RWA), a higher floor than the Basel minimum 8%. #flashcard #topic/capital-management #topic/basel #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-15

## Card 2 — Credit Risk · Easy

State the Expected Loss formula and explain the economic intuition behind each of its three components.::EL = PD × LGD × EAD. PD (Probability of Default): the likelihood the borrower fails to repay within a 1-year horizon — estimated as a through-the-cycle (TTC) average to smooth cyclicality. LGD (Loss Given Default): the fraction of EAD not recovered after default — influenced by collateral quality, seniority, and recovery process. EAD (Exposure at Default): the outstanding balance at the time of default — for revolving facilities includes an estimate of undrawn amounts likely to be drawn before default (CCF × commitment). Economic intuition: EL is the average cost of credit risk embedded in normal business — banks recover EL through loan pricing and provisions. Capital is held only for Unexpected Loss (UL = VaR − EL). #flashcard #topic/credit-risk #topic/ifrs9 #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-15

## Card 3 — Basel IRB · Easy

Define the Worst-Case Default Rate (WCDR) and state its role in the Basel IRB capital formula.::The WCDR is the conditional probability of default at the 99.9th percentile of the systematic risk factor — i.e., the default rate expected to be exceeded only once in 1,000 years. Derived from the Vasicek/ASRF model: WCDR = N([N⁻¹(PD) + √R × N⁻¹(0.999)] / √(1−R)), where R is the asset correlation and N is the standard normal CDF. Role in capital formula: K = EAD × LGD × (WCDR − PD) × MA — the capital charge covers the gap between the stress-scenario loss (WCDR × LGD) and the expected loss (PD × LGD). The WCDR links regulatory capital directly to the model's view of systemic risk: higher asset correlation R → higher WCDR → more capital required. #flashcard #topic/credit-risk #topic/capital-management #topic/basel #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-15

## Card 4 — IFRS 9 · Easy

Define IFRS 9 Stage 1 and state the loss allowance and interest revenue rule that applies.::Stage 1 applies to financial assets that have not experienced a significant increase in credit risk (SICR) since initial recognition. Loss allowance = 12-month ECL — the expected credit loss from default events that could occur within the next 12 months only (not over the instrument's lifetime). Interest revenue is calculated on the gross carrying amount (i.e., before deducting the loss allowance) using the original effective interest rate (EIR). Economic rationale: Stage 1 assets are performing normally; the 12-month ECL serves as a proxy for the initial expectation of credit losses built into pricing. Newly originated assets always start in Stage 1 regardless of credit quality. #flashcard #topic/ifrs9 #topic/credit-risk #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-15

## Card 5 — IFRS 9 · Easy

State the interest revenue rule for Stage 3 assets and explain why it differs from Stage 1 and 2.::In Stage 3, the borrower is credit-impaired (defaulted / non-performing). Interest revenue is calculated on the amortised cost (gross carrying amount MINUS the lifetime loss allowance), not on the gross carrying amount. This differs from Stages 1 and 2 where interest is always on the gross amount. Rationale: once a borrower has defaulted, continuing to accrue interest on the full gross balance would overstate income — the bank effectively cannot expect to collect all future interest. Applying the EIR to the lower amortised cost produces a more conservative, economically realistic interest income figure. This interaction between Stage 3 treatment and write-off (para 5.4.4) directly affects a bank's net interest margin reported in the income statement. #flashcard #topic/ifrs9 #topic/credit-risk #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-15

## Card 6 — IFRS 9 Classification · Easy

State the two tests an asset must pass to be classified at amortised cost under IFRS 9, and give a bank example.::Test 1 — Business model test: the asset is held within a business model whose objective is to collect contractual cash flows (hold-to-collect). Test 2 — SPPI test (Solely Payments of Principal and Interest): the contractual cash flows are solely payments of principal and interest on the principal outstanding — no exotic return features. Bank example: a standard home loan or corporate term loan held on the banking book. The bank intends to collect monthly P&I repayments and does not plan to trade the loan. Result: measured at amortised cost, with IFRS 9 ECL applied as a loss allowance reducing the net balance sheet carrying amount. If either test fails, the asset moves to FVOCI (hold-to-collect-and-sell) or FVPL (trading / residual). #flashcard #topic/ifrs9 #topic/business-model #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-15

## Card 7 — IFRS 9 Classification · Easy

Distinguish FVOCI from FVPL under IFRS 9 classification, and state which measurement category applies to derivatives.::FVOCI (Fair Value through Other Comprehensive Income): applied when the business model is "hold to collect AND sell". SPPI test must also be met. Fair value changes flow to OCI (not P&L) until disposal, at which point cumulative gains/losses are recycled to P&L. ECL is recognised in P&L but does not reduce the carrying amount on the balance sheet. Example: bonds held for liquidity management that may be sold under stress. FVPL (Fair Value through Profit or Loss): residual category — applies when hold-to-collect and FVOCI conditions are not met, or the bank designates the asset at FVPL to eliminate an accounting mismatch. Derivatives: always FVPL — their cash flows are not solely P&I, and they are held for trading or hedging, not to collect contractual cash flows. #flashcard #topic/ifrs9 #topic/market-risk #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-15

## Card 10 — Basel IRB · Easy

Distinguish F-IRB from A-IRB: which parameters does the bank supply under each approach?::F-IRB (Foundation IRB): banks supply only PD (subject to a floor of 0.03% for corporate/bank exposures). LGD, EAD, and Maturity M are supervisory values set by BCBS (LGD = 45% senior / 75% subordinated; M = 2.5 years). A-IRB (Advanced IRB): banks supply their own estimates of PD, LGD, EAD, and M for corporate, sovereign, and bank exposures — subject to regulatory approval. For retail exposures all banks using IRB supply their own PD, LGD, and EAD (there is no F-IRB for retail; no maturity adjustment is applied). The A-IRB advantage: capital can be optimised if internal models produce more risk-sensitive estimates. The cost: significantly higher model development, validation, and governance requirements (use test, data history ≥5–7 years). SA banks applying for IRB approval must satisfy the SARB/PA. #flashcard #topic/credit-risk #topic/capital-management #topic/basel #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-15

## Card 12 — Maturity Adjustment · Easy

What does the Basel IRB maturity adjustment (MA) correct for and what is the formula?::The maturity adjustment accounts for the fact that for instruments lasting longer than one year, there is additional credit exposure from a counterparty's potential credit deterioration (migration risk) during the instrument's life — not just the risk of outright default in year 1. Formula: MA = (1 + (m − 2.5) × b) / (1 − 1.5 × b), where m = effective maturity (years) and b = [0.11852 − 0.05478 × ln(PD)]². When m = 2.5, MA = 1. For longer-dated instruments (e.g., 5-year loans), MA > 1, increasing the capital charge. For retail exposures, no maturity adjustment is applied — maturities within a pool are assumed homogeneous. The MA links directly to the IRB formula: K = EAD × LGD × (WCDR − PD) × MA. #flashcard #topic/credit-risk #topic/capital-management #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-15

## Card 13 — Downturn LGD · Easy

Distinguish through-the-cycle (TTC) LGD from downturn LGD and state which is used for Basel IRB capital vs IFRS 9 provisioning.::TTC LGD: long-run average loss rate across the credit cycle — reflects average economic conditions. Used as a starting point for model calibration. Downturn LGD: LGD estimated under adverse economic conditions, where collateral values fall and recovery costs rise. Basel IRB capital uses downturn LGD — the ASRF model conditions PDs on a 99.9% stress scenario, so LGD must reflect stress conditions consistently. IFRS 9 provisioning uses a probability-weighted, forward-looking LGD that incorporates multiple macroeconomic scenarios — typically a blend of base, upside, and downside scenarios weighted by probability. It is usually a point-in-time (PIT) LGD. This divergence — DT LGD for capital, PIT LGD for provisions — means that even if a bank's EL-provision comparison is favourable, it does not imply the Basel IRB EL (using DT LGD) equals the IFRS 9 ECL. #flashcard #topic/credit-risk #topic/ifrs9 #topic/capital-management #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-15

## Card 16 — EL vs UL · Medium

Explain the distinction between Expected Loss (EL) and Unexpected Loss (UL), their treatment in bank financial management, and how IFRS 9 provisions and Basel capital interact at the boundary.
?
**Expected Loss (EL)** = PD × LGD × EAD — the mean of the credit loss distribution. EL represents the normal cost of doing business: banks embed EL recovery into loan pricing (e.g., the credit spread component of the lending rate) and hold accounting provisions (IFRS 9 ECL allowances) against it. EL is not the purpose of capital.

**Unexpected Loss (UL)** = VaR₉₉.₉% − EL — the deviation of losses from the mean at an extreme confidence level. UL is what capital is held for: the Basel IRB formula sets K = EAD × LGD × (WCDR − PD) × MA, where (WCDR − PD) × LGD is the UL component.

**IFRS 9 ↔ Basel interaction at the boundary**:
- If IFRS 9 ECL provisions > Basel EL (= PD_reg × LGD_reg × EAD): the excess provisions (up to 0.6% of IRB credit RWA) can be included in Tier 2 capital.
- If IFRS 9 ECL provisions < Basel EL: the shortfall is deducted from CET1 — reducing capital quality.
- This creates an incentive for banks to align provisioning with the IRB model's expected loss, and for provisioning models and capital models to be developed in an integrated framework.
- Under stress, IFRS 9 Stage 2/3 migrations sharply increase ECL, which flows through retained earnings and reduces CET1 — amplifying the capital depletion modelled in Pillar 2B stress tests.

## Card 17 — IFRS 9 Modification · Medium

Explain the IFRS 9 accounting treatment when a loan is modified but not derecognised, covering the modification gain/loss, carrying amount recalculation, and the interaction with SICR assessment.
?
**When a loan's contractual cash flows are renegotiated** (e.g., interest rate reduced due to borrower hardship), the bank must assess whether derecognition applies. If the modification is not substantial (quantitative 10% test and qualitative factors), the loan is **not derecognised**.

**Carrying amount recalculation**: The new gross carrying amount = PV of modified cash flows discounted at the **original EIR** (not the revised rate). Any difference between the old carrying amount and the recalculated amount is recognised immediately as a **modification loss (or gain) in P&L**.

Example (from IFRS 9 para 5.4.3): Original loan R1,000,000, EIR = 10%, modified rate = 6%. New annual payment = R237,397. PV at 10% over remaining 5 years = R899,252. Modification loss = R100,748 recognised in P&L immediately.

**SICR re-assessment**: After modification, the bank must compare the default risk under the modified terms (at the reporting date) with the default risk at **original recognition under original terms**. The modification does not reset the SICR clock — a loan that was in Stage 2 before modification remains subject to lifetime ECL assessment unless credit risk has genuinely decreased.

**Write-off interaction**: If modification reflects that the bank has no reasonable expectation of full recovery, a write-off (para 5.4.4) may be required instead — directly reducing the gross carrying amount and constituting a derecognition event.

## Card 18 — BCBS 239 Data Principles · Medium

Describe the four risk data aggregation principles of BCBS 239 (Principles 3–6) and explain why each is especially critical for G-SIBs during a stress event.
?
**Principle 3 — Accuracy and Integrity**: Risk data must be accurate and reliable, reconciled to accounting data where appropriate, and produced largely via automated systems to minimise manual errors. Critical for G-SIBs under stress: inaccurate risk data in 2008 (e.g., misstated derivative exposures) prevented regulators and management from understanding true losses; automation also speeds up the production cycle.

**Principle 4 — Completeness**: All material risk data must be captured across every legal entity, business line, asset type, geography, and risk factor. G-SIBs operate across dozens of jurisdictions — completeness ensures that a concentration in one legal entity or geography is visible at the group level, supporting resolution planning under FSB Key Attributes.

**Principle 5 — Timeliness**: Aggregate risk data must be available on a time scale appropriate to the risk's volatility. For market risk this may be intraday; for credit risk it may be daily or weekly. G-SIBs must be able to produce an enterprise-wide risk picture within hours during a crisis — the GFC showed that some banks took days or weeks to consolidate exposures.

**Principle 6 — Adaptability**: Systems must flexibly produce ad-hoc risk reports, stress test outputs, and regulatory queries — including new risk types or scenarios not anticipated in original system design. In a crisis, regulators and boards request non-standard cuts of data (e.g., "what is our exposure to counterparties in country X?") — adaptable architecture delivers these without multi-week IT projects.

**Link to governance**: All four rely on Principles 1–2 (governance and IT architecture) being sound. BCBS 239 non-compliance is a Pillar 2 supervisory concern — regulators may impose additional capital charges for inadequate MIS.

## Card 19 — Vasicek / ASRF Model · Medium

Explain the key assumptions of the Basel ASRF (Asymptotic Single Risk Factor) model and derive the interpretation of the WCDR formula.
?
**Core assumptions of the ASRF model**:
1. **Single systematic risk factor**: All default correlations arise from one common macroeconomic factor S (the "state of the economy"). Each borrower's asset value is: Xᵢ = √ρ × S + √(1−ρ) × Zᵢ, where S is systematic (common) and Zᵢ is idiosyncratic.
2. **Asymptotic portfolio**: The portfolio is infinitely granular — no single obligor contributes meaningfully to portfolio loss. This removes idiosyncratic risk entirely via diversification; only systematic risk remains.
3. **Gaussian distribution**: Both S and Zᵢ are standard normal, and the asset value process follows geometric Brownian motion (Merton framework).
4. **Default threshold**: Borrower i defaults when Xᵢ < N⁻¹(PD) — i.e., when its asset value falls below the liability threshold corresponding to its unconditional TTC PD.

**WCDR derivation**: Conditional on the systematic factor S = N⁻¹(0.999) (the 99.9th percentile "worst state of the world"):
WCDR = N([N⁻¹(PD) + √ρ × N⁻¹(0.999)] / √(1−ρ))

This is the conditional default rate at the stress scenario — the proportion of the portfolio expected to default in the worst 1-in-1000-year year. Basel IRB capital = LGD × (WCDR − PD) × MA — covering the stress loss above EL.

**Limitation**: The single-factor assumption underestimates tail risk in portfolios with genuine multi-sector correlations. Concentration risk (Pillar 2A) must be separately captured, as the ASRF model assumes away all idiosyncratic concentration.

## Card 20 — Stress Testing · Medium

Explain Pillar 2B stress testing mechanics, covering the CPB formula, the three key drivers of CET1 depletion under stress, and the SA-specific Individual Capital Requirement (ICR).
?
**Pillar 2B** is the forward-looking component of Pillar 2, determined via stress testing rather than point-in-time model estimation.

**Capital Planning Buffer (CPB)**:
CPB = max over t in [0,T] of (Pillar 1 + Pillar 2A − CET1(t))

Where CET1(t) = CET1(0) + Σ[Revenue(s) − Impairments(s) − Dividends(s)] − ΔRWA(s)

**Three key CET1 depletion drivers under stress**:
1. **Impairment surge**: IFRS 9 Stage 2/3 migrations spike under adverse macro scenarios (GDP contraction, unemployment rise, property price falls) → higher ECL → higher impairment charges → lower retained earnings → lower CET1.
2. **Revenue compression**: NIM narrows (rate cuts or higher funding costs); fee income falls (lower transaction volumes) → less retained earnings to absorb losses.
3. **RWA inflation**: Portfolio downgrades → higher risk weights under SA or IRB (stressed PDs increase RWA). Denominator increases while numerator (CET1) falls → double-pressure on CET1 ratio.

**SA Individual Capital Requirement (ICR)**:
In South Africa, the PA combines Pillar 2A (model-based risks not in Pillar 1) and Pillar 2B (stress capital) into the ICR — a bank-specific, non-public add-on above the Pillar 1 minimum. The ICR integrates IFRS 9 stressed impairments with Basel IRB capital projections, making it the key intersection of accounting and regulatory capital frameworks.

## Card 21 — AT1 Eligibility · Medium

Describe the AT1 eligibility criteria under Basel III CAP10 and explain why these criteria serve the going-concern loss-absorption purpose.
?
**AT1 eligibility criteria (CAP10.11 — key requirements)**:
1. **Perpetual**: No maturity date, no step-up or redemption incentive — ensures the instrument remains available throughout the bank's life.
2. **Fully discretionary coupons**: The bank can cancel interest payments at any time without triggering default. Discretion ensures no cash drain during stress when the bank needs to preserve capital.
3. **Subordinated**: Ranked below depositors, general creditors, and subordinated debt (Tier 2). This ensures AT1 holders absorb losses before depositors in a going-concern wind-down.
4. **Not secured or guaranteed**: Cannot have credit enhancements that would elevate the instrument's claim above its face seniority — must be a true loss absorber.
5. **Conversion or write-down trigger**: Must include a contractual mechanism to convert to CET1 or be written down at or before a CET1 ratio of 5.125% — ensuring loss absorption happens while the bank is still a going concern (not just at resolution).

**Why these criteria serve going-concern purposes**: Unlike Tier 2 (which absorbs losses in resolution/insolvency), AT1 must absorb losses while the bank continues operating. The conversion/write-down trigger ensures that AT1 is genuinely available to recapitalise the bank before regulatory minimum breaches cascade into failure. In SA, CoCos (contingent convertible bonds) are the most common AT1 instrument issued by D-SIBs.

## Card 22 — Basel Approach Comparison · Medium

Using the numerical examples from the source material, compare the RWA produced by Basel I, the Standardised Approach, and the A-IRB approach for a R1 billion portfolio of BBB-rated corporate loans (PD = 0.1%, LGD = 60%, M = 2.5 years).
?
**Basel I**: Risk weight for corporate loans = 100% flat (regardless of credit quality). RWA = 100% × R1 billion = **R1,000 million**. Capital = 8% × R1bn = R80 million. No credit differentiation — a BBB and a CCC loan receive identical RWA.

**Standardised Approach (Basel II/III)**: Risk weights vary by external rating. BBB-rated corporate → typically 100% under SA. RWA ≈ **R1,000 million** (similar to Basel I for BBB; benefit only emerges for higher ratings or with eligible collateral).

**A-IRB (Basel II, with PD = 0.1%, LGD = 60%, ρ = 0.24 for corporate)**:
- WCDR ≈ 3.4% (at 99.9% confidence with ρ ≈ 0.4 in this example's notation)
- MA = 1.59 (for m = 2.5)
- K = 1,000 × 0.6 × (0.034 − 0.001) × 1.59 ≈ R31.5 million per R1bn
- RWA = K × 12.5 ≈ **R393 million**

**Conclusion**: A-IRB produces 60% less RWA than Basel I for a well-rated portfolio — a significant capital relief incentive for banks with resources to implement IRB. The Basel IV output floor (72.5% of SA-equivalent RWA) limits this benefit, preventing RWA falling below ≈ R725 million for this portfolio. This corrects the concern that IRB optimisation creates artificially low capital requirements that understate true systemic risk.

## Card 23 — IFRS 9 FVOCI Impairment · Medium

Explain how the loss allowance for FVOCI assets differs from amortised cost assets under IFRS 9, and state the balance sheet and P&L impact of each.
?
**Amortised cost assets (IFRS 9 para 5.5.1)**:
- Loss allowance recognised on the balance sheet as a deduction from the gross carrying amount → net carrying amount = gross amount − ECL.
- ECL changes flow through **P&L** as impairment gains/losses.
- Balance sheet: Net loan = R90,000 if gross loan is R100,000 and ECL = R10,000.

**FVOCI assets (IFRS 9 para 5.5.2)**:
- Loss allowance is calculated on the same ECL basis (12-month or lifetime ECL depending on stage).
- Critically: the loss allowance **does not reduce the carrying amount on the balance sheet** — the asset continues to be measured at full fair value.
- Instead: the ECL is recognised in **OCI** (Other Comprehensive Income, in equity) as a cumulative impairment loss — it reduces OCI, not the carrying amount.
- However, the ECL change flows through **P&L** as an impairment gain/loss (same P&L impact as amortised cost).

**Practical implication**: For FVOCI assets, the balance sheet shows fair value gross of ECL, but equity is reduced by accumulated ECL through OCI. This distinction means analysts must look at OCI reserves — not just the asset value — to understand the true loss position on FVOCI portfolios (e.g., a bank's bond portfolio held for liquidity management).

## Card 24 — IFRS 9 Write-Off · Medium

Describe the write-off mechanism under IFRS 9 para 5.4.4, its balance sheet impact, and how Basel IRB capital interacts with write-offs on defaulted assets.
?
**IFRS 9 write-off (para 5.4.4)**: A write-off occurs when the bank has **no reasonable expectation of recovering** the financial asset in full or in part. The bank directly reduces the gross carrying amount — it is a derecognition event. Write-offs are not a P&L event at the point of write-off (the P&L impact was already recognised through ECL impairment charges when the asset moved to Stage 3 and accumulated lifetime ECL). The write-off simply removes both the gross asset and the associated loss allowance from the balance sheet simultaneously.

**Example**: Gross loan R100,000; Loss allowance R80,000; Net carrying amount R20,000. If recovery prospects collapse → write off R80,000 → gross loan reduces to R20,000; loss allowance reduces to zero. Net carrying amount = R20,000. Any post-write-off recoveries → recognised as income (reversal).

**Basel IRB interaction — defaulted assets**: For defaulted assets (PD = 100% by definition), the IRB capital formula simplifies to: K = max(0, LGD_reg − BEEL), where BEEL (Best Estimate of Expected Loss) is the bank's specific provision or current internal loss estimate. This creates a link: the IFRS 9 Stage 3 provision (the BEEL equivalent) directly feeds the Basel defaulted-asset capital calculation. If the provision fully covers the regulatory LGD-based loss, capital for that asset = 0. If provisions are inadequate, additional capital is required — reinforcing the importance of accurate IFRS 9 Stage 3 provisioning.

## Card 25 — G-SIB Resolution · Medium

Explain the resolution framework for G-SIBs: distinguish TLAC from Basel III capital requirements, and describe the role of bail-in in preventing taxpayer exposure.
?
**Purpose of the distinction**: Basel III minimum capital (CET1 4.5%, T1 6%, Total 8%) ensures loss absorption on a going-concern basis — the bank continues operating while absorbing losses from its capital cushion. TLAC is additional: it ensures that even after Basel III capital is exhausted, sufficient further resources exist to **recapitalise the resolved entity** without public funds.

**TLAC minimum**: From 2022: ≥18% of RWA AND ≥6.75% of leverage ratio exposure. These must be **pre-positioned** in the resolution entity — not locked in subsidiaries. TLAC instruments must be subordinated, with maturity > 1 year and capable of being bailed in by the resolution authority.

**Bail-in mechanism**: When a G-SIB enters resolution, TLAC instruments are written down or converted to equity in the resolved entity, recapitalising it to above minimum operating requirements. Critical functions (payments, deposit taking) continue uninterrupted. Bond investors absorb losses; depositors are protected; taxpayers are not called upon. This directly addresses the "too-big-to-fail" moral hazard exposed in 2008.

**G-SIB capital surcharge interaction**: CET1 used to meet TLAC cannot simultaneously meet capital conservation or G-SIB surcharge buffers (no double-counting). A G-SIB in Bucket 3 (e.g., JP Morgan, HSBC, Citigroup) must hold 2% additional CET1 surcharge ON TOP of meeting TLAC.

**SA context**: SA does not formally implement TLAC (no SA-domiciled G-SIBs), but the SARB's Resolution Framework (2021) introduces similar concepts for D-SIBs, including gone-concern loss-absorbing capacity (GLAC) requirements.

## Card 26 — Integrated Capital Framework · Hard

(a) Derive the Basel A-IRB capital formula K step by step from first principles (EL, UL, Vasicek/ASRF). (b) Explain how the IFRS 9 ECL provisioning shortfall/excess modifies the effective capital position. (c) Demonstrate with numbers: PD = 0.5%, LGD_reg = 40%, EAD = R100m, ρ = 0.20, M = 2.5 years; calculate WCDR, K, and RWA.
?
**(a) Derivation from first principles**:

Step 1 — Loss distribution: Credit losses are not normally distributed — they are right-skewed (most years: small losses; rare years: catastrophic losses). VaR₉₉.₉% = the 99.9th percentile of the loss distribution.

Step 2 — ASRF model (Vasicek): Under a single systematic risk factor, conditional on the worst-case state of the world (S = N⁻¹(0.999)), the conditional default rate is:
WCDR = N([N⁻¹(PD_TTC) + √ρ × N⁻¹(0.999)] / √(1−ρ))

Step 3 — Capital must cover UL = Stress loss − EL:
K (per unit of EAD) = LGD_reg × (WCDR − PD_reg) × MA

Full formula:
K = EAD × LGD_reg × [N((N⁻¹(PD) + √R × N⁻¹(0.999)) / √(1−R)) − PD] × MA

Step 4 — RWA conversion: RWA = K × 12.5 (the reciprocal of the 8% minimum).

SA applies a scaling factor of 1.06 per SARB regulation, consistent with international practice.

**(b) IFRS 9 provision interaction**:
- Basel EL = PD_reg × LGD_reg × EAD (using regulatory parameters).
- IFRS 9 ECL = probability-weighted, forward-looking estimate (PIT parameters, multi-scenario).
- If ECL > Basel EL: excess (up to 0.6% of credit RWA) → eligible for Tier 2 capital inclusion.
- If ECL < Basel EL: shortfall → deducted from CET1, reducing the highest-quality capital.
- This shortfall deduction directly links the IFRS 9 provisioning model's adequacy to the bank's CET1 ratio.

**(c) Numerical example**:
PD = 0.005, LGD = 0.40, EAD = R100m, ρ = 0.20, M = 2.5 years.

WCDR = N([N⁻¹(0.005) + √0.20 × N⁻¹(0.999)] / √0.80)
= N([−2.576 + 0.447 × 3.090] / 0.894)
= N([−2.576 + 1.381] / 0.894)
= N(−1.195 / 0.894)
= N(−1.337) ≈ 9.06%

b = [0.11852 − 0.05478 × ln(0.005)]² = [0.11852 − 0.05478 × (−5.298)]² = [0.11852 + 0.2903]² = [0.4088]² = 0.1671
MA = (1 + (2.5 − 2.5) × 0.1671) / (1 − 1.5 × 0.1671) = 1 / 0.749 = 1.335

K = 100 × 0.40 × (0.0906 − 0.005) × 1.335 = 100 × 0.40 × 0.0856 × 1.335 = **R4.57 million**
RWA = 4.57 × 12.5 = **R57.1 million** (vs. R100m under Basel I).

## Card 27 — IFRS 9 Staging + Stress Testing Integration · Hard

(a) Explain the three-stage IFRS 9 model: the triggers for migration, the ECL measure, and the interest revenue rule at each stage. (b) Explain how IFRS 9 staging flows feed into a Pillar 2B stress test CET1 projection. (c) Why does IFRS 9 pro-cyclicality create a regulatory concern and what mechanisms exist to mitigate it?
?
**(a) Three-stage model**:

Stage 1 — No SICR since origination:
- ECL = 12-month ECL (proxy for initial credit loss expectation)
- Interest revenue on gross carrying amount at EIR
- All newly originated assets start here

Stage 2 — SICR since origination (but not yet credit-impaired):
- Trigger: significant increase in lifetime PD since origination; OR 30+ DPD (rebuttable presumption); OR qualitative triggers (e.g., covenant breach, sector stress, forward-looking macro indicators)
- ECL = lifetime ECL (full remaining life of the instrument)
- Interest revenue still on gross carrying amount at EIR
- The jump from 12-month to lifetime ECL can be 3–5× the Stage 1 allowance — a material P&L impact

Stage 3 — Credit-impaired / defaulted:
- Trigger: objective evidence of default (90+ DPD, forbearance, distress restructuring, legal action)
- ECL = lifetime ECL (individually assessed for large exposures; collectively for retail)
- Interest revenue on amortised cost (gross minus loss allowance) — lower revenue recognition
- Write-off (para 5.4.4) once no reasonable recovery expectation exists

**(b) Pillar 2B integration**:

Under a stress scenario, GDP contracts → unemployment rises → property prices fall → PD and LGD worsen.
- Stage 1 → Stage 2 migrations: immediate ECL jump (12m to lifetime) → impairment charges spike
- Stage 2 → Stage 3 migrations: full individual assessment → larger ECL and reduced interest income
- Stressed ECL flows reduce retained earnings → CET1 falls

CPB = max_t(Pillar 1 + Pillar 2A − CET1(t)), where CET1(t) is reduced by stressed impairment charges, reduced NIM, and RWA inflation. The IFRS 9 staging model is therefore the bridge from macroeconomic scenarios to P&L impairment projections within the Pillar 2B stress test.

**(c) Pro-cyclicality concern and mitigants**:

IFRS 9 creates a "cliff effect": when economic conditions deteriorate, mass Stage 1→2 migrations force simultaneous large ECL increases across the banking system → impairment charges surge → capital falls → lending contracts → recession deepens. This amplifies the cycle.

Mitigants:
- Basel CCyB: released during downturns to provide CET1 headroom, partially offsetting IFRS 9 ECL spikes
- IASB/BCBS dialogue: guidance against mechanical model application; encourages management overlays and forward-looking smoothing
- Post-COVID: regulators explicitly asked banks to use economic scenario weightings that avoided cliff-edge migrations (PA / PRA guidance 2020)
- Capital buffers (CCB 2.5% CET1): designed to be usable in downturns — banks can operate below the combined buffer without immediate supervisory intervention

## Card 28 — BCBS 239 + G-SIB Governance · Hard

(a) Explain why BCBS 239 was motivated by G-SIB failures specifically observed during the 2008 GFC. (b) State all six risk data principles (Principles 1–6) with their core requirement. (c) Explain how BCBS 239 compliance links to Pillar 2 capital adequacy and G-SIB resolvability under the FSB framework.
?
**(a) GFC motivation**:

During 2007–2009, several G-SIBs (e.g., Lehman Brothers, RBS, UBS) demonstrated that their MIS could not produce accurate, consolidated risk exposure data under stress:
- Counterparty credit risk concentrations (especially to monolines and AIG-type structures) were underreported because data existed in silos across business lines
- Aggregate position-level data took days or weeks to produce — too slow for crisis management
- Regulators could not obtain timely, reliable data to assess systemic risk or plan resolution
- This contributed to the disorderly failure of some institutions and amplified contagion

**BCBS 239 was introduced in January 2013, with G-SIB compliance required by January 2016.**

**(b) Six principles**:
1. **Governance** — Board-owned framework; fully documented; independent validation; applies group-wide regardless of legal structure
2. **Data architecture and IT infrastructure** — designed to support aggregation in normal AND stress conditions; single authoritative data source per risk type; automated where possible
3. **Accuracy and integrity** — risk data is accurate, reliable, reconciled to accounting data; bank has a data "dictionary" ensuring consistent definitions
4. **Completeness** — all material risk data captured across every entity, geography, business line, asset type; no material risk excluded
5. **Timeliness** — risk data available within timeframes appropriate to the volatility of each risk; intraday for market risk; near real-time for liquidity
6. **Adaptability** — systems produce ad-hoc reports, scenario outputs, and stress test cuts without major IT effort

**(c) Link to Pillar 2 and FSB resolvability**:

**Pillar 2 (SRP standard)**: Inadequate risk data aggregation is a Pillar 2 risk — supervisors (e.g., PA in SA, PRA in UK) may impose additional capital requirements (Pillar 2A add-ons) if they assess that a bank's MIS cannot reliably measure or aggregate risk. BCBS 239 compliance is therefore a precondition for Pillar 2 adequacy assessments.

**FSB resolvability**: The FSB Key Attributes of Effective Resolution require that resolution authorities have access to accurate, granular, rapidly produced risk data. Without BCBS 239-compliant systems, G-SIBs cannot support the recovery and resolution planning (RRP) process — their resolution would require more time and improvisation, increasing the risk of disorderly failure. BCBS 239 is therefore directly embedded in G-SIB higher supervisory expectations (one of the four G-SIB-specific requirements alongside TLAC, capital surcharge, and resolution planning).

## Card 29 — Basel I → IV Evolution + SA Implementation · Hard

(a) Trace the evolution of credit risk capital requirements from Basel I through Basel IV, identifying the key innovation at each stage. (b) Describe South Africa-specific implementation dates and deviations. (c) Explain the Basel IV output floor and its impact on IRB capital optimisation.
?
**(a) Basel evolution**:

**Basel I (1988)**: Flat risk weights (0%, 20%, 50%, 100%) assigned by broad asset class — no credit quality differentiation. All corporate loans = 100% RW. Capital = 8% × RWA. Simple but crude — regulatory arbitrage rife. 1996 Market Risk Amendment added market risk capital. Some SA subsidiary exposures still governed by Basel I.

**Basel II (2004, SA from 2008)**: Three-pillar structure introduced. Pillar 1: risk-sensitive credit RWA via SA (external ratings) or IRB (internal PD/LGD/EAD/M). Pillar 2: ICAAP/SREP — capital for risks not in Pillar 1. Pillar 3: public disclosure. New operational risk capital charge (BIA / TSA / AMA). The A-IRB approach allows 40–60% RWA savings for well-rated portfolios vs Basel I.

**Basel III (2010, SA from 2013)**: Post-GFC response. Raised capital quality: CET1 minimum 4.5%; Tier 1 6%; Total 8%. Added conservation buffer (2.5%), CCyB (0–2.5%), G-SIB surcharge. Introduced LCR (2015) and NSFR (2018). Leverage ratio backstop ≥3%. TLAC added by FSB for G-SIBs.

**Basel IV / Basel III finalisation (2017, effective 2025 phased to 2030)**: Constrained IRB usage (some asset classes F-IRB only; CVA risk floor); revised SA (more granular risk weights); new operational risk SMA replacing AMA/BIA/TSA; output floor (72.5% of SA-equivalent RWA); leverage ratio surcharge for G-SIBs.

**(b) SA-specific implementation**:
- Basel II: effective 1 January 2008
- Basel III: effective 1 January 2013 (with transitional arrangements to 2023)
- SA minimum Tier 1: at least 50% of total capital base
- SA total capital floor: max(R250 million, 10% of RWA) — more conservative than Basel's 8%
- PA scaling factor for IRB: 1.06× applied to IRB capital, consistent with BCBS practice
- SA D-SIBs designated by PA: Absa, Standard Bank, FNB/FRB, Nedbank, Investec — subject to D-SIB surcharges
- SA Committed Liquidity Facility (CLF): SARB backstop facility counts as HQLA for LCR, compensating for structural HQLA shortage

**(c) Output floor impact**:

Without floor: A well-rated IRB bank might compute credit RWA of 30–40% of SA-equivalent. Floor: effective RWA = max(IRB RWA, 72.5% × SA RWA). For a R100bn SA-equivalent RWA, floor = R72.5bn; if IRB = R40bn, the bank must hold capital on R72.5bn. This eliminates up to 32.5 percentage points of the IRB capital benefit, significantly reducing the incentive to invest in advanced modelling purely for capital relief, while preserving the risk-sensitivity benefit of IRB for internal management purposes.

## Card 30 — Integrated Cross-Topic: IFRS 9 + Basel IRB + Pillar 2 + SA Regulation · Hard

(a) A bank's mortgage portfolio has: EAD R10bn, TTC PD 0.8%, Downturn LGD 25%, ρ = 0.15, M (retail, no MA). IFRS 9 ECL = R120m. Calculate Basel IRB capital and RWA, determine the EL-provision comparison, and state the Tier 2 / CET1 impact. (b) In a stress scenario PD rises to 3% and 15% of the portfolio migrates Stage 1→2. Explain the CET1 impact through IFRS 9, Basel RWA, and Pillar 2B CPB channels. (c) State two SA-specific regulatory actions the PA could take if the bank's CET1 falls below the combined buffer requirement.
?
**(a) Normal conditions — Basel IRB capital and IFRS 9 comparison**:

Retail mortgages: no maturity adjustment (MA = 1).

WCDR = N([N⁻¹(0.008) + √0.15 × N⁻¹(0.999)] / √0.85)
= N([−2.409 + 0.387 × 3.090] / 0.922)
= N([−2.409 + 1.196] / 0.922)
= N(−1.315) ≈ 9.42%

K = EAD × LGD × (WCDR − PD) = R10bn × 0.25 × (0.0942 − 0.008) = R10bn × 0.25 × 0.0862 = **R215.5m**
RWA = 215.5 × 12.5 = **R2,694m ≈ R2.7bn** (vs. R10bn under Basel I; SA scaling factor 1.06 → RWA = R2,855m)

Basel EL = R10bn × 0.008 × 0.25 = **R20m**
IFRS 9 ECL = **R120m** (> Basel EL by R100m)

ECL excess over EL = R100m.
Maximum Tier 2 inclusion = 0.6% × IRB credit RWA = 0.6% × R2,694m = **R16.2m**. The remaining R83.8m excess does not qualify for capital credit (above the cap) — but it does not reduce CET1. Result: Tier 2 increases by R16.2m.

**(b) Stress scenario — 3 channels of CET1 impact**:

**Channel 1 — IFRS 9 impairment (P&L → retained earnings → CET1)**:
15% Stage 1→2 migration = R1.5bn portfolio. Lifetime ECL replaces 12-month ECL. Assume Stage 1 ECL was ~0.5% and Stage 2 ECL rises to ~3.5% (lifetime). ECL increase = (3.5% − 0.5%) × R1.5bn = R45m additional impairment → reduces retained earnings → CET1 falls by R45m. Remaining portfolio under stressed PD = 3% also increases Stage 1 ECL: R8.5bn × (new 12m ECL − old 12m ECL). Net: total impairment charge could be R80–120m, funded from capital.

**Channel 2 — RWA inflation**:
Stressed WCDR with PD = 3%, ρ = 0.15:
= N([N⁻¹(0.03) + 0.387 × 3.090] / 0.922) = N([−1.881 + 1.196] / 0.922) = N(−0.743) ≈ 22.9%
New K = 10bn × 0.25 × (0.229 − 0.03) = R497.5m
New RWA ≈ R6,220m. RWA has more than doubled → CET1 ratio denominator grows, numerator shrinks simultaneously.

**Channel 3 — Pillar 2B CPB**:
CPB = max_t(Pillar 1 + Pillar 2A − CET1(t)). The simultaneous impact of impairment surges (depressing CET1 numerator) and RWA inflation (inflating denominator) drives CET1 ratio sharply lower. The Pillar 2B CPB quantifies the peak shortfall — the bank must pre-hold capital equal to this buffer above Pillar 1 + Pillar 2A.

**(c) PA actions below combined buffer**:

1. **Distribution restrictions (automatic)**: Once CET1 falls below the combined buffer (Pillar 1 + CCB + D-SIB surcharge + CCyB), the Banks Act Regulations and PA directives trigger automatic constraints on dividend payments, share buybacks, and discretionary AT1 coupon payments — conserving CET1 mechanically.

2. **Supervisory intervention / SREP action**: The PA can issue a directive under Section 6 of the Banks Act / FSRA requiring the bank to submit and implement a capital restoration plan, suspend further lending growth, or raise additional capital within a specified timeframe. For severe cases, the PA may designate the bank as being in recovery under the Resolution Framework, triggering the ICR review and potentially activating GLAC instruments.
