## Card 1 — IRB Capital Formula Derivation · Hard

(a) Write out the full A-IRB capital requirement formula K for a non-defaulted corporate exposure, naming every term. (b) Explain the economic intuition for why capital = UL − EL rather than UL alone. (c) State what happens to the formula for retail exposures and why the maturity adjustment is dropped.
?
(a) The A-IRB capital requirement is:

K = EAD × LGD × [N((G(PD) + √R × G(0.999)) / √(1−R)) − PD] × MA

Where:
- EAD = Exposure at Default (bank's own estimate under A-IRB)
- LGD = Downturn Loss Given Default (economic-downturn estimate, bank's own estimate under A-IRB)
- N(·) = Cumulative standard normal CDF
- G(·) = Inverse standard normal CDF (probit)
- PD = Through-the-Cycle average PD (1-year horizon, minimum floor of 0.03% for corporates)
- R = Asset correlation parameter (prescribed by Basel: ranges 12%–24% for corporates, inversely related to PD)
- G(0.999) = probit at 99.9th percentile ≈ 3.09
- MA = Maturity Adjustment = (1 + (m − 2.5)b) / (1 − 1.5b), where b = [0.11852 − 0.05478 × ln(PD)]²
- m = effective maturity (1–5 years; set to 2.5 years under F-IRB)

RWA = K × 12.5 (× 1.06 scaling factor applied by SARB)

(b) Expected losses EL = LGD × PD × EAD should be covered by loan pricing (the interest rate charged incorporates a credit spread to recover EL in expectation) and by provisions (IFRS 9 ECL). Capital is not the right instrument to cover losses that are already "priced in" to revenue and absorbed by provisions. Capital is expensive — holding it against EL would inflate lending costs unnecessarily and reduce ROE. Capital is the buffer for *unexpected* losses — the tail of the credit loss distribution that exceeds EL. Setting K = UL − EL correctly reflects that EL is a cost of doing business, not a risk to absorb via equity.

(c) For retail exposures: (i) the maturity adjustment is dropped entirely (MA = 1); (ii) F-IRB and A-IRB are merged — banks must provide their own PD, LGD, and EAD estimates (no supervisory LGD or EAD). The rationale is that retail exposures are managed at portfolio/segment level rather than individually; loan maturities within each segment are approximately homogeneous, so a maturity adjustment adds no meaningful differentiation. Supervisory LGDs are also impractical at retail scale due to product-level collateral and loss variation.

## Card 2 — ASRF Model Assumptions and Limitations · Hard

(a) State the four key assumptions of the Asymptotic Single Risk Factor (ASRF) model underlying the Basel IRB capital formula. (b) Identify two consequences of violating each of the first two assumptions in practice. (c) Explain how the Basel scaling factor of 1.06 (1.0 under SARB implementation) partially compensates for these violations.
?
(a) The four ASRF assumptions:
1. **Single systematic factor**: All default correlations between obligors arise from their common sensitivity to one aggregate macroeconomic factor S. There is no sector, geographic, or product-specific systematic risk beyond this single factor.
2. **Asymptotic portfolio**: The portfolio is assumed infinitely granular — no single obligor contributes a material share of portfolio loss. This allows idiosyncratic risk to diversify away completely.
3. **Normality**: Both the systematic factor S and idiosyncratic factor Z are independent standard normal variables. Firm asset value follows lognormal Brownian motion (Merton/Vasicek).
4. **Constant asset correlation**: R is fixed and prescribed by the regulator (as a function of PD for the asset class) rather than empirically estimated from the actual portfolio.

(b) Violations in practice:
- Assumption 1 violated (single factor): Real portfolios exhibit sector-specific and geographic clustering (e.g. property sector, sovereign exposures). A single-factor model understates correlation in concentrated portfolios. Consequence 1: capital underestimates tail risk for concentrated books. Consequence 2: diversification benefits are overstated — a mortgage-heavy SA bank faces property-cycle risk not captured in the ASRF.
- Assumption 2 violated (granularity): Large corporate books have significant name concentration — one counterparty can represent 5–15% of RWA. Consequence 1: idiosyncratic risk does not diversify away, so actual loss variance exceeds what the formula implies. Consequence 2: the ASRF capital formula may materially understate the UL for large-exposure portfolios, which is why the Granularity Adjustment (GA) was proposed (but not mandated).

(c) The Basel Committee recognised that the ASRF model produces aggregate capital estimates that may be too low due to model conservatism gaps. The 1.06 scaling factor was applied as a simple multiplicative uplift to IRB RWAs (not Standardised) to offset the expected aggregate shortfall. The SARB also applied 1.06. This is a blunt instrument — it does not correct individual exposure miscalibrations but raises average capital requirements to a level deemed adequate in aggregate.

## Card 3 — Downturn LGD vs BEEL · Hard

(a) Define Downturn LGD and Best Estimate of Expected Loss (BEEL) and state which is used in the capital formula for performing exposures vs defaulted exposures. (b) Explain why it would be inappropriate to use a PIT LGD in the capital formula for performing exposures. (c) State the capital formula for defaulted assets and explain the economic meaning of max(0, LGD_Reg − BEEL).
?
(a) **Downturn LGD** (DT-LGD): an estimate of LGD reflecting loss severity during adverse economic conditions (typically the worst 10–20% of the economic cycle). It is the LGD term in the IRB capital formula K for performing exposures (K = EAD × LGD_DT × [WCDR − PD] × MA). Basel does not prescribe a mapping function for downturn LGD; A-IRB banks must estimate their own DT-LGD using internal data on loss severity during historical downturns (e.g. property price troughs for mortgages, corporate default cycles for wholesale).

**BEEL** (Best Estimate of Expected Loss): the bank's current best estimate of expected loss on a defaulted facility, reflecting its current economic condition and recovery status. It is essentially the specific provision (IFRS 9 Stage 3 ECL or individual impairment) on that facility. BEEL is used in the capital formula for *defaulted* exposures: K = max(0, LGD_Reg − BEEL).

(b) A PIT LGD reflects current loss severity under prevailing economic conditions — it fluctuates over the credit cycle, being low during booms (high collateral values, active workout markets) and high during downturns. Using a PIT LGD in the capital formula would make capital procyclical: capital would fall just as economic conditions deteriorate, exactly when a bank most needs its buffer. This would amplify systemic risk and is the opposite of what a capital buffer is meant to achieve. The downturn LGD provides a stable, conservative floor that ensures capital is held through the cycle.

(c) For defaulted assets: **K = max(0, LGD_Reg − BEEL)**

Economic meaning: once an asset has defaulted, PD = 100% — there is no longer uncertainty about whether a loss will occur. The remaining uncertainty is in the magnitude of recovery. LGD_Reg is the regulatory estimate of what will be lost in the worst-case 99.9th percentile scenario (or simply the downturn LGD). BEEL is what the bank currently expects to lose (akin to the provision already held). Capital is therefore the potential *additional* loss above what is already provisioned — the gap between the expected loss (BEEL) and the severe-scenario loss estimate (LGD_Reg). If BEEL already fully covers or exceeds LGD_Reg, no additional capital is required — the max(0,...) ensures the floor of zero is maintained.

## Card 4 — WCDR Derivation · Hard

(a) Starting from the Vasicek single-factor model, derive the expression for the Worst-Case Default Rate (WCDR) at the 99.9th percentile. (b) Explain why lower-PD obligors have higher asset correlations R in the Basel framework, and what this implies for WCDR behaviour. (c) Illustrate numerically: compute the WCDR for PD = 1% and R = 0.15.
?
(a) In the Vasicek framework, firm i defaults if its normalised asset value X_i falls below the default threshold G(PD):

X_i = S√R + Z_i√(1−R)

where S is the common systematic factor and Z_i is the idiosyncratic factor (both N(0,1)).

The conditional default rate (conditional on S = s) is:
WCDR(s) = N((G(PD) − s√R) / √(1−R))

For capital purposes, we seek the worst-case scenario at the 99.9th percentile. Since a *bad* economic scenario corresponds to S taking a *low* (negative) value (more firms default when economic conditions worsen), the conservative value is S = G(0.001) = −G(0.999). Substituting:

WCDR = N((G(PD) − G(0.001)√R) / √(1−R))
     = N((G(PD) + G(0.999)√R) / √(1−R))

This is the fraction of the portfolio expected to default in a 1-in-1000-year economic environment.

(b) Basel prescribes the corporate correlation formula (simplified):
R = 0.12 × (1 − e^(−50×PD)) / (1 − e^(−50)) + 0.24 × [1 − (1 − e^(−50×PD))/(1 − e^(−50))]

This yields R ≈ 24% as PD → 0 and R ≈ 12% as PD → 1. **Rationale**: High-quality (low-PD) obligors are typically large, investment-grade corporates whose fortunes are more closely tied to macroeconomic conditions — they are fundamentally sound businesses that only default in broad systemic crises (high R). Junk-rated (high-PD) obligors are more idiosyncratic — they may default due to firm-specific factors even in good times (lower R). This inverse relationship is an empirical finding from historical default data. **Implication for WCDR**: Higher R amplifies the sensitivity of WCDR to the systematic shock G(0.999). For a given PD, a high-R obligor will have a higher WCDR (more defaults under the stress scenario) because more of its risk is systematic. This makes large, investment-grade corporate books surprisingly capital-intensive under IRB.

(c) For PD = 1% = 0.01 and R = 0.15:
- G(0.01) = N⁻¹(0.01) ≈ −2.326
- G(0.999) ≈ 3.090
- Numerator: −2.326 + √0.15 × 3.090 = −2.326 + 0.3873 × 3.090 = −2.326 + 1.197 = −1.129
- Denominator: √(1 − 0.15) = √0.85 ≈ 0.9220
- Argument: −1.129 / 0.9220 ≈ −1.225
- WCDR = N(−1.225) ≈ 11.0%

Interpretation: In a 1-in-1000-year scenario, 11% of this portfolio would default — eleven times the expected 1% default rate.

## Card 5 — PIT vs TTC: Capital and Procyclicality · Hard

(a) Distinguish Point-in-Time (PIT), Through-the-Cycle (TTC), and Dynamic PIT PD philosophies, including rating migration behaviour and ODR per grade across the cycle. (b) Explain the procyclicality problem under each approach from a capital management perspective. (c) State why the South African banks and the PRA's SS4/24 guidance prefer a PIT approach and how the SARB manages procyclicality.
?
(a) **TTC**: Risk grades are based solely on static borrower characteristics (balance sheet strength, industry). PDs per grade are constant across the cycle. As the economy deteriorates, more borrowers migrate *downward* into higher-risk grades — the ODR per grade remains approximately flat but aggregate default risk shifts via migration. Rating stability is high; recalibration is infrequent.

**PIT**: Grades and PDs incorporate current economic conditions (arrears levels, utilisation, current macro variables). PDs per grade rise in downturns and fall in booms. Rating migration is high — the same borrower receives a worse grade in recession. ODR per grade tracks more closely over time. The approach is compliant with CRR Art. 180(2)(a) and reflects current risk.

**Dynamic PIT**: A PIT model *plus* periodic recalibration of PD scalars to match realised ODRs. ODR per grade is intentionally kept flat across the cycle — all cyclicality is expressed through migration, not through changing PDs for a given grade. Most faithful to the true credit cycle but operationally burdensome.

(b) Procyclicality risk:
- **TTC**: Capital is relatively stable across the cycle (PDs per grade don't change) but potentially understates current risk in downturns — a bank may appear well-capitalised just before stress peaks. Little procyclicality.
- **PIT**: Capital rises in downturns (PDs increase) and falls in booms (PDs decrease). This is *procyclical*: banks are forced to raise capital exactly when it is most expensive to do so (equity markets stressed, credit spreads wide), potentially amplifying credit contraction. RWA volatility creates P&L and strategic planning challenges.
- **Dynamic PIT**: Highest procyclicality — capital demand closely tracks the realised default cycle, creating maximum RWA volatility.

(c) **PRA SS4/24** (and implicitly SA SARB guidance) prefers PIT for compliance with CRR Art. 180(2)(a): models must reflect current relevant risk information. TTC is considered non-compliant if it ignores current economic conditions. PIT is adopted at South African banks because it provides the best forward-looking risk discrimination for pricing, limit-setting, and IFRS 9 staging — it is more useful for actual risk management decisions.

Procyclicality management: Basel III introduced **capital conservation buffers** (2.5% CET1) and **countercyclical capital buffers** (CCyB, 0–2.5% CET1, set by SARB based on credit-to-GDP gap) precisely to absorb the procyclical capital demands of PIT models. In downturns, buffers can be drawn down, offsetting the mechanical rise in IRB capital from higher PDs.

## Card 6 — Definition of Default: Regulatory Triggers · Hard

(a) List all six UTP (Unlikeliness to Pay) triggers under CRR Article 178(3) and explain each in one sentence. (b) Explain the contagion (pulling effect) rule, its regulatory basis, and the 20% materiality threshold used in practice. (c) Distinguish between technical past due and genuine past due, and explain the regulatory treatment of each.
?
(a) Six UTP triggers under CRR Art. 178(3):
1. **Non-accrued status** (Art. 178(3)(a)): The bank has put the credit obligation on non-accrued status — i.e. interest is no longer recognised in P&L due to deterioration in credit quality.
2. **Specific credit risk adjustment** (Art. 178(3)(b)): The bank has recognised a specific credit adjustment (SCRA) — an IFRS 9 impairment or specific provision — reflecting a significant perceived decline in credit quality since origination.
3. **Sale at credit-related loss** (Art. 178(3)(c)): The institution sells the credit obligation at a material credit-related economic loss exceeding the 5% materiality threshold.
4. **Distressed restructuring** (Art. 178(3)(d)): The bank consents to a restructuring that likely results in a diminished financial obligation — e.g. NPV reduction >1%, rate reduction, principal forgiveness, or extension with concession.
5. **Bank-initiated bankruptcy** (Art. 178(3)(e)): The institution has filed for the obligor's bankruptcy or a similar order.
6. **Obligor-initiated bankruptcy** (Art. 178(3)(f)): The obligor has sought or been placed in bankruptcy or similar protection that would avoid or delay repayment.
(b) **Contagion / pulling effect**: If an obligor defaults on one facility, all other facilities to the same obligor should also be flagged as default, given that UTP indicators under Art. 178(3)(e) and (f) relate to the *obligor's* condition, not a specific facility. SS3/24 §7.8 references a "significant proportion of exposures" as the trigger. In practice, the 20% materiality threshold is used: if the balance in default exceeds 20% of the obligor's total exposure, all remaining facilities are pulled into default. This threshold was developed to align with IFRS 9 Stage 3 contagion reporting. The pulling effect prevents banks from treating isolated facility defaults as ring-fenced when the obligor's overall creditworthiness is impaired.
(c) **Technical past due**: Occurs when a past-due flag arises due to system/data errors, payment timing lags, factoring arrangements, or failed payment execution — not genuine credit deterioration. CRR Art. 178(1D) and SS3/24 §2.8–2.9 require banks to identify and correct technical defaults and suspend DPD counting during genuine disputes about the repayment amount. Exception processes must exist to remove erroneous flags; IT teams correct system or process errors. **Genuine past due**: Material credit obligations unpaid beyond the due date where the materiality threshold is met. Regulatory minimum threshold for retail = £0/0% (zero materiality) under PRA guidance — any past-due amount counts, regardless of size. The DPD counter starts from the contractual due date of the oldest unpaid amount and continues for 90 days before the default trigger fires.

## Card 7 — Economic Loss Calculation · Hard

(a) Write out the economic loss formula for a defaulted credit card account, naming every component. (b) Explain why indirect costs are included in capital LGD but excluded from IFRS 9 LGD. (c) Explain the treatment of post-default interest and fees in the economic loss calculation and cite the relevant regulatory paragraph.
?
(a) Economic Loss_i = [EAD_i − NPV(Cash Recoveries)_i − NPV(Debt Sale Proceeds)_i + NPV(Cost of Recoveries)_i] / EAD_i

Where:
- EAD_i = Outstanding balance at default date, including all principal, accrued interest, and fees up to default
- NPV(Cash Recoveries) = Post-default cash payments from collections (agency, legal, direct), discounted to default date using discount rate = SOFR + 5% (or max(SOFR+5%, 9%) for downturn LGD)
- NPV(Debt Sale Proceeds) = Net proceeds from forward-flow debt sale (e.g. 12–36 months post charge-off), discounted to default date
- NPV(Cost of Recoveries) = Direct (third-party commission fees, legal fees) and indirect (overhead allocation) costs, discounted to default date
- Discounted Payment = Undiscounted Payment × (1 + r/12)^(−t), where t = months since default

The formula yields a **realised LGD** as a proportion of EAD. Realised LGDs are floored at zero (SS4/24 §14.14) — where recoveries exceed EAD, no negative loss is recorded.

(b) SS4/24 §13.21 requires all material direct *and indirect* costs to be included in LGD for capital purposes. This reflects regulatory conservatism: the true cost of managing and ultimately recovering a defaulted facility includes overhead allocation (case management time, systems, monitoring), not just out-of-pocket expenses. For IFRS 9, the standard (IFRS 9.B5.5.55) requires only incremental costs directly attributable to the credit loss event. Indirect overhead costs are excluded from IFRS 9 ECL because the accounting standard aims to measure the *accounting* loss from the financial instrument, not the total operational cost burden to the bank. This creates a systematic wedge: LGD_capital > LGD_IFRS9 due to the indirect cost loading.

(c) **Post-default interest**: No post-default interest is charged by the bank (credit charging privileges are revoked at default). Therefore, no adjustment is needed to EAD for post-default interest. Consistent with SS4/24 §13.10 — any interest capitalised pre-default is included in default balance; post-default interest charges do not increase economic loss.

**Post-default fees**: SS4/24 §13.9 states that fees capitalised *after* the moment of default should not increase the amount of economic loss or the outstanding amount at default. Pre-default fees are included in EAD. The regulatory rationale is that post-default fees are not genuine credit losses — they are contractual charges that the bank may or may not be able to collect; including them would create inconsistency in EAD measurement across banks.

## Card 8 — Maturity Adjustment · Hard

(a) Write out the maturity adjustment (MA) formula and define all terms. (b) Explain the economic rationale for the MA — why does a 5-year corporate exposure require more capital per unit of EAD than a 1-year exposure with the same PD and LGD? (c) Calculate the MA for PD = 2% and m = 4 years. What does this imply for K relative to a 2.5-year exposure?
?
(a) MA = (1 + (m − 2.5) × b) / (1 − 1.5 × b)

Where:
- m = effective maturity of the exposure in years (floor: 1 year, cap: 5 years; set to 2.5 under F-IRB for most exposures)
- b = [0.11852 − 0.05478 × ln(PD)]²
- PD = through-the-cycle PD for the obligor

At m = 2.5: MA = (1 + 0 × b) / (1 − 1.5b) ≠ 1 unless b = 0 (i.e. MA ≈ 1 only when PD is very high, so b → 0)
At m = 2.5 and b > 0: MA > 1 (the denominator < 1)

(b) The IRB formula in isolation models a 1-year credit horizon — it captures the risk of default over the next year. But for a 5-year loan, the bank faces *migration risk* across years 2–5: even if the counterparty doesn't default in year 1, it might be downgraded during the remaining life, reducing the market value of the loan (mark-to-market loss or increased provisioning need). A longer-maturity instrument has greater exposure to this mark-to-market deterioration risk. The MA is designed to convert the 1-year default model into an approximation of the full multi-year credit risk, scaling capital upward for longer maturities. It is calibrated on empirical credit spread data showing that longer-maturity credit spread risk increases roughly linearly with residual maturity.

(c) PD = 0.02:
- b = [0.11852 − 0.05478 × ln(0.02)]²
- ln(0.02) = ln(2×10⁻²) ≈ −3.912
- b = [0.11852 − 0.05478 × (−3.912)]²
- = [0.11852 + 0.2143]²
- = [0.3328]²
- = 0.1108

MA(m=4) = (1 + (4 − 2.5) × 0.1108) / (1 − 1.5 × 0.1108)
         = (1 + 1.5 × 0.1108) / (1 − 0.1662)
         = (1 + 0.1662) / 0.8338
         = 1.1662 / 0.8338
         ≈ 1.398

MA(m=2.5) = (1 + 0) / (1 − 1.5 × 0.1108) = 1 / 0.8338 ≈ 1.199

Implication: A 4-year exposure requires capital approximately 1.398/1.199 ≈ 17% higher per unit of EAD×LGD than a 2.5-year exposure with the same PD — purely due to the maturity effect. This is material for long-dated wholesale lending.

## Card 9 — F-IRB vs A-IRB: Parameter Ownership and Capital Implications · Hard

(a) Distinguish F-IRB from A-IRB in terms of which parameters are bank-supplied vs supervisory for each exposure class. (b) Explain why banks seek A-IRB approval and what the likely capital impact is relative to F-IRB. (c) State two key supervisory standards that constrain A-IRB LGD estimates and explain their purpose.
?
(a) Parameter ownership by approach and exposure class:

| Parameter | F-IRB (Corporate/Bank/Sovereign) | A-IRB (Corporate/Bank/Sovereign) | Retail (always A-IRB equivalent) |
|-----------|----------------------------------|----------------------------------|----------------------------------|
| PD | Bank estimate (floor 0.03%) | Bank estimate (floor 0.03%) | Bank estimate |
| LGD | Supervisory: 45% senior unsecured; 75% subordinated | Bank estimate (downturn) | Bank estimate (downturn) |
| EAD | Supervisory CCF applied to off-balance items | Bank CCF estimates | Bank estimate |
| Maturity (M) | Fixed at 2.5 years (mostly) | Bank estimate (1–5 years) | Not applicable (MA dropped) |

(b) Banks seek A-IRB approval because their internal LGD and EAD models, calibrated to their actual portfolio, typically produce *lower* estimates than the conservative supervisory proxies in F-IRB. A senior unsecured F-IRB LGD of 45% may be materially higher than the bank's observed historical LGD of 25–35% on a well-collateralised portfolio. Similarly, bank-estimated CCFs for revolving facilities often come in below the supervisory credit conversion factors. Lower LGD and EAD estimates directly reduce K, cutting RWA and freeing up regulatory capital — improving ROE and creating capacity for additional lending. Importantly, A-IRB also allows banks to optimise pricing more precisely: capital-to-risk alignment improves when all four parameters reflect actual portfolio characteristics.

(c) Two key constraints on A-IRB LGD:
1. **Downturn requirement**: SS4/24 (PRA) / Basel CRE36 requires that LGDs reflect economic-downturn conditions — the period during which loss severities are expected to exceed those seen in normal times. Banks must demonstrate that their LGD estimates incorporate the impact of declining collateral values, reduced recovery market liquidity, and increased workout costs during downturns. This prevents banks from using boom-time LGD observations as the basis for a permanently low regulatory LGD estimate. The purpose is to ensure LGD is cycle-neutral and conservative.
2. **Discount rate floor**: SS4/24 §13.17 specifies that for downturn LGD estimation, the discount rate applied to post-default recoveries should be max(SOFR+5%, 9%). This floor prevents banks from using a low discount rate that inflates the NPV of distant recoveries, artificially reducing realised LGD. The 9% floor was set to represent a rate that reflects both the risk-free rate and an appropriate credit/operational risk premium for uncertain recovery cash flows.

## Card 10 — Standardised Approach: Risk Weight Mechanics · Hard

(a) State the risk weight schedule for (i) sovereign exposures, (ii) bank exposures, and (iii) retail mortgage exposures under the Basel II/III Standardised Approach, including the ECAI recognition requirement. (b) Explain the comprehensive collateral approach and how it differs from the simple approach. (c) SA-specific: how does the SARB diverge from the standard Basel SA for domestic sovereign and bank exposures?
?
(a) Risk weight schedule (Basel II/III SA):

**Sovereign exposures**: Rated AAA to AA− → 0%; A+ to A− → 20%; BBB+ to BBB− → 50%; BB+ to B− → 100%; below B− → 150%; unrated → 100%. Supervisor may apply preferential 0% risk weight to claims on home-country sovereign in domestic currency.

**Bank exposures** (Option 2 — based on own rating): AAA to AA− → 20%; A+ to A− → 50%; BBB+ to BBB− → 50%; BB+ to B− → 100%; below B− → 150%; unrated → 50%. Short-term claims (<3 months) receive one category more favourable, floored at 20%.

**Retail mortgage (residential)**: 35% risk weight for claims secured by residential property occupied by the borrower. Commercial real estate: 100% (poor historical loss experience). Standard retail claims (unsecured): 75%.

**ECAI requirement**: Banks must use ratings from ECAIs formally recognised by the national supervisor. Critically, banks must conduct independent due diligence; they may not reduce a risk weight based on an ECAI rating — the rating can only increase (not decrease) a risk weight relative to the due diligence result.

(b) **Simple approach**: The risk weight of the counterparty is replaced by the risk weight of the eligible collateral for the collateral-covered portion of the exposure (minimum 20% RW applies). The uncovered portion retains the counterparty risk weight. Collateral must be revalued at minimum every 6 months and pledged for the full exposure life.

**Comprehensive approach**: Applies volatility-adjusted haircuts to both the exposure (upward adjustment for potential exposure increase) and the collateral (downward adjustment for potential value decline). A net exposure = adjusted exposure − adjusted collateral value is computed; the counterparty's risk weight applies only to this net figure. The haircuts can be supervisory standard haircuts or own-model haircuts (subject to approval). The comprehensive approach must be used for counterparty credit risk in the trading book; banks choose between approaches in the banking book.

(c) **SA-specific**: The SARB allows a **preferential 0% risk weight** for claims on the South African sovereign (National Treasury and SARB) denominated and funded in ZAR. For banks incorporated in SA, supervisors may apply the 0% home-country preferential weight. The SARB also applies the standard Basel SA rule for foreign-currency sovereign claims (which follow the external rating schedule). SA's National Treasury carried investment-grade ratings (though Moody's downgraded SA sovereign to sub-investment grade in 2020), complicating the application of the SA preferential treatment for foreign-currency exposures.

## Card 11 — EL vs Provisions: Capital Interaction · Hard

(a) Explain the relationship between Expected Loss (EL), accounting provisions (IFRS 9 ECL), and Tier 1/Tier 2 capital under the Basel III framework. (b) State the capital treatment for an EL shortfall (EL > provisions) and an EL excess (provisions > EL). (c) Explain why PIT LGD is typically used for IFRS 9 and DT-LGD for capital, and how this interacts with the EL/provision comparison.
?
(a) Under Basel III IRB:
- **EL** = PD_TTC × LGD_DT × EAD (regulatory, 1-year horizon)
- **ECL (IFRS 9)** = PD_PIT × LGD_PIT × EAD × Discount Factor (accounting, multi-stage, lifetime for Stage 2/3, 12-month for Stage 1)
<!--SR:!2026-05-06,4,270-->

These are calculated independently and are not directly comparable. The Basel framework acknowledges that EL represents the long-run average credit cost per exposure, while IFRS 9 ECL represents the current expected accounting loss. The interaction determines how provisions affect regulatory capital.

(b) **EL shortfall (EL > provisions)**: Where regulatory EL exceeds accounting provisions, the bank is considered under-provisioned for regulatory purposes. The shortfall = EL − provisions is deducted from **Tier 1 capital** (specifically CET1), reducing the bank's capital base. This reflects that provisions are the first line of loss absorption and, if insufficient, equity must absorb the gap.

**EL excess (provisions > EL)**: Where provisions exceed EL, the excess represents a regulatory capital benefit — the bank has set aside more against expected losses than the model requires. Under Basel III, up to **0.6% of credit RWA** of this excess may be included in **Tier 2 capital**. This is because any excess provision buffers against unexpected losses and strengthens the bank's overall loss-absorbing capacity, though Tier 2 inclusion is capped to prevent banks from inflating capital through excessive provisioning.

(c) **PIT LGD for IFRS 9**: IFRS 9 requires *unbiased* estimates of losses given current conditions. A PIT LGD reflects current collateral values, current workout market conditions, and current macroeconomic forecasts. Using DT-LGD (conservatively elevated) for IFRS 9 would overstate provisions relative to a genuine best estimate, violating the unbiased requirement.

**DT-LGD for capital**: The capital formula requires conservatism — it must hold capital against the worst-case scenario, not the expected scenario. DT-LGD ensures capital is maintained even when collateral values collapse and recovery markets seize.

**Interaction**: Because DT-LGD > PIT-LGD (especially at the bottom of the cycle), regulatory EL is typically higher than IFRS 9 ECL for secured portfolios. This tends to create **EL shortfalls** in stressed conditions — precisely when the EL/ECL gap widens the most — requiring Tier 1 deductions that amplify capital pressure in downturns. This is a known procyclical feature of the Basel/IFRS 9 interaction.

## Card 12 — Probation Period and Return to Non-Default · Hard

(a) State the minimum probation period requirements under CRR Art. 178(5) and SS3/24 §5 for (i) non-distressed restructuring defaults and (ii) distressed restructuring defaults. (b) Explain the concept of the LGD independence period under SS4/24 §12.2 and how it interacts with the probation period. (c) Describe how a bank determines the optimal probation period length and the trade-off involved.
?
(a) Minimum probation periods:
- **(i) Non-distressed restructuring defaults** (90+ DPD exits): CRR Art. 178(5)(a) — minimum **3 months** after the default conditions cease to apply before reclassification to non-defaulted.
- **(ii) Distressed restructuring defaults** (forbearance exits): CRR Art. 178(5A) — minimum **12 months** of satisfactory performance on the restructured terms before reclassification to non-defaulted.

In practice, most banks apply a uniform 12-month probation period across both types, which is conservative but administratively simpler and compliant with both minimum standards. The 12-month period is also intuitively defensible: it covers a full annual cycle, reducing the risk of seasonal or temporary improvement masking persistent default risk.

(b) **LGD independence period** (SS4/24 §12.2): For LGD model development, where a previously defaulted account returns to non-default and then re-defaults within **9 months**, the two default events are treated as a *single, continuous default event* (default date = first default). The rationale: a re-default within 9 months strongly suggests the first "cure" was spurious — the account never genuinely recovered, and treating it as two separate defaults would artificially inflate the cure rate and depress observed LGDs (since the first default appears to have zero loss if recoveries are not allocated correctly). A bank may extend this independence period beyond 9 months if economically justified for specific exposure types (SS4/24 §12.2).

**Interaction with probation**: The probation period is a regulatory classification rule (when does an account re-enter performing?); the independence period is a model development rule (when are two defaults treated as one for LGD training data). The 9-month independence period applies from the *start* of the probation period — meaning accounts that re-default during probation (before the 12-month probation is complete) have never technically exited default and are trivially treated as continuous default events. The independence period only becomes binding for accounts that completed probation but re-defaulted quickly thereafter.

(c) The optimal probation period balances two competing forces:
- **Too short**: Many accounts that "cure" re-default shortly thereafter — data quality degrades (high re-default rate contaminates the performing book), and PD models trained on this data underestimate true default probability. Capital and provisions are understated.
- **Too long**: Cured accounts remain in default for an extended period, artificially inflating the default book and understating the performing book size. Capital requirements are overstated; EL/provision calculations are distorted.

Banks conduct probation period optimisation by: (1) identifying previously defaulted accounts; (2) tracking re-default rates at N months (3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 24) after all default criteria cease; (3) plotting re-default rate as a function of N. The optimal period is where the marginal reduction in re-default rate from extending the period diminishes substantially — typically observed at 12 months in retail credit card data, where re-default rates stabilise.

## Card 13 — Guarantees: Capital Treatment and PD Substitution · Hard

(a) Explain how guarantees affect PD, LGD, and EAD in the IRB framework for a guaranteed corporate exposure. (b) Distinguish the credit risk mitigation (CRM) treatment under SA vs IRB for guarantees. (c) Explain the double-default framework and when it applies.
?
(a) **PD**: A guarantee reduces PD — the bank replaces the obligor's PD with the *guarantor's* PD if the guarantor is more creditworthy. Under the SA, this is a direct risk weight substitution. Under IRB, the adjusted PD should reflect the probability that *both* the obligor defaults *and* the guarantee fails to pay — though in the standard IRB approach (without double-default), the bank simply substitutes the guarantor's PD (treating the guarantee as full credit protection).

(b) **SA treatment**: The bank substitutes the risk weight of the guarantor for the risk weight of the obligor for the guaranteed portion. It applies only when: the obligor is corporate/bank/sovereign rated; the guarantor is a bank or other regulated entity with its own PD estimate; and the guarantee is documented as satisfying Basel criteria. The effect is that K_double_default < min(K_obligor, K_guarantor), capturing the diversification benefit of the joint default requirement. This is rarely used in practice due to data and approval complexity.

## Card 14 — Rating Mobility and Capital Procyclicality · Hard

(a) Explain the concept of rating mobility and how it interacts with a bank's PD philosophy to drive regulatory capital volatility. (b) Describe how a PIT-rated portfolio would behave in terms of RWA during the 2007–2009 Global Financial Crisis, compared to a TTC-rated portfolio. (c) Explain the countercyclical capital buffer (CCyB) mechanism in SA and how it addresses the procyclicality problem.
?
(a) **Rating mobility** is the frequency and magnitude of obligor rating migrations — movements from one risk grade to another — over time. Under a TTC philosophy, ratings are anchored to long-run average financial strength; the same obligor will receive the same grade regardless of the economic environment, so migration is low. Under a PIT philosophy, ratings incorporate current economic conditions (e.g. arrears, utilisation, macro variables), so obligors are downgraded during recessions and upgraded during booms — migration is high.

Rating mobility drives capital volatility because: (i) under IRB, RWA per obligor is an increasing function of PD; (ii) PIT downgrades increase PD per obligor in a downturn → RWA rises mechanically. Conversely, PIT upgrades during booms reduce RWA just as lending is expanding. The combination creates procyclical capital demands.

(b) A PIT-rated portfolio during 2007–2009:
- Late 2007: property collateral values begin falling; utilisation rises as corporates draw revolving credit facilities; arrears increase. PIT grades deteriorate → PDs assigned to existing exposures rise sharply. RWA spikes.
- 2008: mass corporate and retail downgrades following Lehman collapse. WCDR rises, LGDs increase (property values decline), MA rises as markets compress maturities. RWA increases by 30–60% on some portfolios.
- Banks face capital ratio breaches — forced to raise equity (at maximum cost) or reduce lending (amplifying the credit crunch).

A TTC-rated portfolio would see minimal rating migration — the same obligor grades, hence modest RWA increase during 2008 — but would systematically understate current risk and delay provisioning recognition until defaults crystallised.

(c) **CCyB in SA**: The Prudential Authority (acting through SARB) sets the CCyB rate for South African banks, guided by the credit-to-GDP gap (the deviation of actual credit growth from its long-run trend) and other systemic risk indicators. The range is 0–2.5% of CET1. When credit grows rapidly above trend (a signal of building systemic risk), the CCyB is increased — banks must hold more CET1 against the same RWA, dampening credit supply. During a downturn, the CCyB can be *released* (reduced to 0%), freeing up capital exactly when PIT models are demanding more — partially offsetting the procyclical IRB effect. The CCyB is a macroprudential tool complementing the microprudential IRB capital framework. In 2020 (COVID), the SARB reduced capital buffer requirements precisely to provide headroom for banks to absorb losses and continue lending through the pandemic.

## Card 15 — Specialised Lending and Covenant Triggers · Hard

(a) Explain the three key financial covenant types used in project finance (DSCR, LLCR, LTV) and describe what each measures. (b) Explain the cash waterfall concept in specialised lending and how seniority interacts with LGD estimation. (c) Describe two circumstances under which covenant breach should trigger a default classification and one circumstance where it should not.
?
(a) **DSCR (Debt Service Coverage Ratio)**: DSCR = (Revenue − Operating Expenses) / (Interest + Principal). Measures whether current cash flows are sufficient to service debt in the current period. Applied to all types of structured lending. A DSCR covenant is set at the minimum acceptable level — breach signals that cash generation has fallen below the level needed to service debt, providing an early warning of repayment risk. Lenders typically require DSCR ≥ 1.2x–1.5x.

**LLCR (Loan Life Coverage Ratio)**: LLCR = NPV(Future cash flows available for debt service) / Total outstanding debt. Covers the entire remaining loan life — more forward-looking than DSCR. Particularly useful in project finance (e.g. toll roads, power plants) where cash flows are modelled over a 15–30 year horizon. LLCR is harder to monitor than DSCR if project cash flows are non-uniform.

**LTV (Loan-to-Value Ratio)**: LTV = Total outstanding loan / Market value of underlying asset. Central to residential mortgage and commercial property lending. Indicates how far asset prices can fall before the lender is in a loss position on foreclosure. A rising LTV (property value falling) triggers additional collateral requirements (margin calls) or restricts further drawdowns.

(b) **Cash waterfall**: Post-default, a project's asset-based cash flows and proceeds from asset sale are distributed in strict priority order — senior lenders first, then subordinated (mezzanine) lenders, then equity holders (residual claim). The waterfall determines the effective LGD for each tranche: senior lenders lose little if asset coverage is adequate (LGD near zero); mezzanine lenders lose proportionally more as recovery shortfalls flow down. Banks estimating LGD for project finance must model the waterfall — the seniority of their debt position directly determines expected recovery. LGD for senior secured project finance (asset coverage > loan balance) can be very low (5–20%); LGD for mezzanine/subordinated tranches is typically very high (60–90%).

(c) **Covenant breach → default classification**: (i) **Financial covenant breach** where the breach reflects genuine credit deterioration (e.g. DSCR < 1.0x — the project cannot service debt from current cash flows; this is substantively equivalent to UTP under CRR Art. 178(3)). (ii) **Cross-default clause triggered** by default on another facility (especially relevant in group lending — default of the borrowing entity on a different counterpart triggers all covenanted facilities).

**Covenant breach → NOT default**: Where the covenant is breached due to a technical/administrative cause — e.g. late filing of audited accounts (a reporting covenant breach), or a temporary DSCR breach due to scheduled maintenance shutdown that has already been resolved, or where the bank formally waives the breach after satisfying itself that repayment remains highly likely. Covenant breaches are an early-warning mechanism, not an automatic default trigger — banks retain discretion. The key question is whether the breach signals genuine UTP or inability to pay, or is merely procedural/temporary.

## Card 16 — ELGC (Economic Loss Given Cure) · Hard

(a) Define ELGC and state the formula used to calculate it at the account level. (b) Explain the concept of the "artificial cash flow" in SS4/24 §13.7 and its components. (c) Explain why ELGC is typically low for credit card portfolios and how this affects long-run average LGD estimation.
?
(a) **ELGC** (Economic Loss Given Cure) measures the losses incurred by the bank from the moment of default to the subsequent cure of an account, as a proportion of EAD. It captures (i) delays in receiving customer payments relative to the contractual schedule (which, when discounted to the default date, reduce the value of those payments) and (ii) costs incurred in facilitating the cure process.

ELGC_i = (EAD_i − NPV(Observed Payments)_i − NPV(Artificial Cash Flow)_i + NPV(Costs)_i) / EAD_i

Where:
- EAD = Outstanding balance at default date (including all accrued interest and fees)
- Observed Payments = All repayments made while the account was in default (discounted to default date)
- Artificial Cash Flow = Balance at the "moment of cure", adjusted for post-default drawings (discounted to default date)
- Costs = Direct and indirect costs incurred from default to cure
- "Moment of cure" = Start of the independence period (i.e. when probation ends and no default triggers remain active)

(b) The **artificial cash flow** is an analytical construct introduced by SS4/24 §13.7. It is not an actual customer payment — it represents the outstanding balance at the point of cure, serving as a synthetic "payoff" of the remaining obligation. It is "artificial" because: when an account cures, the customer does not actually repay the full balance on the cure date; instead, they resume scheduled payments. However, for LGD calculation purposes, the post-cure cash flows are outside the default observation window, so the remaining balance at cure is treated as a recovery inflow on the cure date. This avoids the need to model post-cure repayment behaviour within the LGD framework.

Components of the artificial cash flow (SS4/24 §13.7(a)):
- Principal: full outstanding loan at cure (only past-due amounts discounted)
- Interest: accrued from default to cure
- Fees: accrued from default to cure
- Observed recoveries: deducted (already captured separately)
- Post-default drawings: included with same treatment as principal
- Costs: accrued from default to cure

(c) For credit card portfolios, ELGC is typically very low because:
1. Cards are revolving, with relatively small per-account balances at any point.
2. Many curing accounts repay their arrears quickly once the cause of distress resolves (e.g. job found, short-term hardship ends).
3. The discount effect on delayed payments is small relative to the balance — the time from default to cure is often only 2–6 months, so discounting at SOFR+5% has minimal NPV impact.

This means cured accounts contribute minimal LGD to the long-run average — the LGD distribution is bimodal: cured accounts cluster near 0%, while charge-off accounts dominate the loss tail. Long-run average LGD (LRA-LGD) is therefore heavily weighted by the charge-off population. For capital purposes, this is captured by the recovery rate model structure: cure probability is estimated separately, and the overall LGD = P(cure) × ELGC + P(charge-off) × LGD_charge-off.

## Card 18 — On-balance-sheet Netting and Collateral in IRB · Hard

(a) State the three conditions required for on-balance-sheet netting to be recognised for capital purposes. (b) Explain how collateral reduces LGD under A-IRB versus under the SA comprehensive approach — and identify where the two methodologies are structurally aligned. (c) Why is netting not permitted in the Basel leverage ratio calculation, and what problem does the leverage ratio address that RWA-based capital ratios do not?
?
(a) For on-balance-sheet netting to reduce EAD in capital calculations, three conditions must be satisfied:
1. **Legal enforceability**: The bank must have a well-founded legal basis — supported by legal opinions — that the netting agreement is enforceable in each relevant jurisdiction under all conditions, including insolvency. The bank must hold a legal right of set-off (LROS) against the counterparty's deposit/loan balance.
2. **Tenor match**: The maturity of the deposit used for netting must be at least as long as the maturity of the loan. A short-dated deposit cannot be used to net a long-dated loan — the deposit could mature and be withdrawn before the loan is repaid, destroying the netting protection.
3. **Monitoring systems**: The bank must have adequate reporting and monitoring systems to continuously identify and track the relevant assets and liabilities (loans and deposits subject to netting) and manage rollovers to maintain the netting position.

(b) Under **A-IRB**: Collateral reduces LGD directly. The bank models the expected recovery value from realising collateral upon default — adjusting for time to realisation, costs of repossession/legal proceedings, and volatility in collateral value during the workout period. The downturn LGD requirement means the bank must stress collateral values to their nadir (e.g. property values at the bottom of the cycle for mortgage LGD). The CRR (Art. 181) allows collateral to be reflected in either EAD or LGD estimates, with the choice determined at model development time.

Under **SA comprehensive approach**: Collateral adjusts EAD through volatility-adjusted haircuts. The adjusted net exposure (EAD_adj = exposure_adj − collateral_adj) is then risk-weighted at the counterparty's standard risk weight. The methodology is formulaic — supervisory haircuts are prescribed; the bank does not model its own recovery estimates. There is no concept of "downturn" in the SA collateral framework.

**Structural alignment**: Both approaches require collateral to be (i) legally enforceable in all relevant jurisdictions, (ii) regularly revalued, (iii) held for at least the life of the exposure, and (iv) monetisable under adverse conditions. These conditions reflect the economic principle that collateral only reduces credit risk if it can actually be seized and liquidated when default occurs.

(c) The leverage ratio = Tier 1 capital / Total unweighted exposures ≥ 3% (Basel III minimum; SARB applies 4% for D-SIBs). Netting is not permitted in the exposure denominator — derivatives and repo exposures must be counted gross.

**Problem the leverage ratio addresses**: RWA-based capital ratios (Tier 1/RWA) allow banks to internally model risk weights and, through model optimisation, reduce RWA without reducing the actual volume of assets. This creates a denominator that is endogenous and manipulable. During 2007–2009, some large banks had Tier 1/RWA ratios above 8% while running total leverage of 30–50x their equity — because trading book RWAs were compressed by internal models. The leverage ratio uses a simple, model-independent exposure measure that cannot be gamed through internal modelling. It acts as a backstop constraint: even if IRB models produce very low RWAs (especially for derivatives and low-credit-risk portfolios), the leverage ratio prevents extreme balance sheet leverage. Netting exclusion in the leverage ratio ensures the gross systemic exposure of large derivatives books is captured.

## Card 19 — Use Tests and Model Governance · Hard

(a) State what the IRB use test requires and explain why it was introduced by the Basel Committee. (b) Describe three specific use-test failures that a regulator would identify in a model review, and the likely capital consequence of each. (c) Explain how the use test interacts with PIT vs TTC philosophy in the context of IFRS 9 staging.
?
(a) The **IRB use test** (CRR Art. 144(1)(b); Basel CRE36.1) requires that the risk estimates used in the regulatory capital calculation must also be *materially used* in the bank's credit decisions, risk management, and internal capital allocation. A bank may not build a separate "regulatory model" that exists solely to calculate capital while making actual lending decisions using different estimates. The bank must demonstrate that: IRB PD, LGD, and EAD estimates feed into credit approval (accept/decline, limit-setting), pricing (risk-adjusted pricing), portfolio monitoring (watchlist triggers), stress testing, ICAAP, and provisioning.

**Rationale**: The Basel Committee wanted to incentivise banks to build genuinely accurate internal risk models rather than ones calibrated purely to minimise regulatory capital. If the models are used in real business decisions, banks have a natural incentive to make them accurate — biased models lead to mispricing and bad lending decisions that cost the bank money.

(b) Three use-test failures:
1. **Capital model not used in pricing**: The bank uses a spreadsheet-based pricing tool that ignores PD/LGD estimates from the IRB model. Capital costs in pricing are based on flat percentages by product rather than obligor-specific risk. *Capital consequence*: Regulator may require a higher Pillar 2 add-on under ICAAP review, or revoke A-IRB approval for the affected portfolio — forcing reversion to SA (typically higher capital).
2. **IRB PDs not used in credit approval**: Business units use relationship manager judgment for approval and set credit limits without reference to model PDs. IRB estimates sit in a risk model tool but are not consulted in the origination workflow. *Capital consequence*: This is a direct use-test breach; the regulator may impose a Pillar 2 capital add-on equal to the difference between IRB and SA capital until remediation is demonstrated.
3. **PD model not updated when significant portfolio shifts occur**: A bank acquires a new retail portfolio with materially different characteristics (e.g. higher-LTV mortgages) but continues applying the existing model without recalibration or performance monitoring. The model is no longer representative. *Capital consequence*: Potential RWA understatement; regulator may require conservative PD/LGD overlays or model rebuild, with capital add-on in the interim.

(c) Under a **PIT approach**: IFRS 9 Stage 2 classification requires a "significant increase in credit risk" (SICR) since origination. PIT PDs, which track current economic conditions, provide a natural trigger: if PD has increased materially since origination (both in absolute terms and relative to lifetime expected), the account migrates to Stage 2 (lifetime ECL). The PIT model thus integrates naturally into IFRS 9 staging because the grade migration embeds current risk information.

Under a **TTC approach**: TTC PDs are stable across the cycle and do not migrate with economic conditions. Using a TTC PD directly as the SICR trigger would fail to identify deteriorating borrowers until defaults crystalise — violating the IFRS 9 requirement for forward-looking, unbiased ECL measurement. Banks using TTC IRB PDs must derive a separate PIT adjustment (e.g. macroeconomic conditional PD) for IFRS 9 staging purposes. The use test does not require IFRS 9 and IRB to use identical models — but it does require that IRB models inform risk management decision-making. Maintaining entirely siloed IFRS 9 and IRB risk frameworks without cross-reference would likely be challenged by the regulator as evidence that IRB models are not genuinely "used."

## Card 20 — Conditional Expected Loss and ASRF Capital Interaction · Hard

(a) Derive the relationship: Conditional Expected Loss = UL + EL, and show how this implies that total capital resources (capital + provisions) must equal Conditional EL for the ASRF model to hold. (b) Explain the role of the 99.9% confidence level in this framework and critique it in light of the GFC. (c) Show how the treatment diverges for defaulted vs performing assets in the ASRF framework.
?
(a) **Derivation**:
Unexpected Loss (UL) = Conditional Expected Loss − Expected Loss (EL)
⟹ Conditional Expected Loss = UL + EL

Under the ASRF model, Conditional EL = LGD × WCDR (the expected loss conditional on the worst-case systematic factor at the 99.9th percentile).

Capital K is set to cover UL: K = Conditional EL − EL = LGD × (WCDR − PD)

For the bank to remain solvent at the 99.9th percentile:
- Provisions must cover EL (the expected accounting loss under normal conditions)
- Capital must cover UL (the deviation of 99.9th percentile losses from EL)
- Total resources = Provisions + Capital = EL + (Conditional EL − EL) = Conditional EL ✓

This is the fundamental ASRF result: the sum of regulatory provisions and regulatory capital exactly equals the expected loss in the worst-case 1-in-1000-year systemic scenario. If EL > provisions (EL shortfall), the difference is deducted from Tier 1 because the bank is relying on capital to cover what provisions should have covered — undermining the clean partition.

(b) The 99.9% confidence level (1-in-1000-year) was chosen for three reasons: (i) bank insolvency carries systemic externalities requiring a very high solvency standard — a 99.9% solvency probability per bank per year implies a very small probability of systemic failure across the entire banking system; (ii) Tier 2 capital has lower loss absorbency than Tier 1, creating uncertainty in effective capital quality, requiring extra conservatism; (iii) model parameter estimation error (in PD, LGD, and R) introduces uncertainty that justifies pushing to the tail of the distribution.

**GFC critique**: The 99.9% confidence level proved insufficient in 2007–2009 for several reasons:
- The ASRF single-factor model understated cross-sector and cross-asset correlations; the GFC was triggered by tail correlations far exceeding the Basel-prescribed R for mortgage and structured credit exposures.
- PD and LGD models were estimated on a benign 2001–2007 credit cycle — through-the-cycle calibration turned out to be badly miscalibrated relative to actual downturn severity (especially for US subprime mortgages).
- Stress loss (the tail beyond 99.9%) was not captured in capital but proved to be the relevant risk in the crisis — systemic, correlated losses exceeded the 1-in-1000-year model assumptions by orders of magnitude.
- The result: institutions with Tier 1/RWA ratios above 8% became insolvent or required state support because actual unexpected losses in 2008–2009 far exceeded K_ASRF.

(c) **Performing assets** (WCDR formula applies):
K = EAD × LGD_DT × (WCDR − PD_TTC) × MA
Capital reflects the *incremental* uncertainty above EL — the gap between conditional EL (stress scenario) and average EL.

**Defaulted assets** (special ASRF treatment):
K_defaulted = max(0, LGD_Reg − BEEL)
For defaulted exposures, PD = 1 — there is no further default uncertainty; N(...) = 1 and WCDR = 1. The formula collapses to: K = EAD × LGD_Reg × (1 − 1) × MA = 0, were it not for the separate treatment. Instead, Basel assigns capital directly as the potential additional loss above the BEEL provision. This is conceptually different from the ASRF derivation for performing assets: for defaulted assets, it is LGD uncertainty (not default probability uncertainty) that drives capital. The ASRF model was designed for performing portfolios — its application to defaulted assets is a practical approximation rather than a model derivation.
