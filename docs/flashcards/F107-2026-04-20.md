## Card 1 — Liquidity Risk Definition · Easy

State the three distinct types of liquidity and explain which is the primary concern of bank liquidity risk management.::The three types are: (1) **Funding liquidity** — the ability of a bank to fund assets throughout their life and meet demands for withdrawal of liabilities as and when they arise; this is the primary concern of liquidity risk management. (2) **Trading liquidity** — the ease with which an asset can be bought or sold in the market at scale without materially impacting its price; a liquid asset trades at a narrow bid-offer spread. (3) **Redemption liquidity** — similar to trading liquidity but relevant only as an asset approaches maturity. Funding liquidity is primary because a bank that cannot meet depositor withdrawal demands or fund committed facilities faces insolvency, regardless of whether its assets are theoretically valuable. #flashcard #topic/liquidity-risk #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-20

## Card 2 — Maturity Transformation · Easy

Define maturity transformation and explain why it is the structural source of liquidity risk in banking.::Maturity transformation is the core banking activity of funding long-dated assets (e.g. residential mortgages with 20–30 year maturities, project finance loans, undrawn credit facilities) using shorter-duration liabilities (e.g. current accounts, short-term deposits). This creates a maturity mismatch: asset cash inflows are distant while liability cash outflows can be immediate. Liquidity risk arises from the uncertainty underlying this mismatch — banks must continuously roll over funding and cannot assume depositors will not withdraw on demand. Were this assumption ever to fail, banks would never originate long-dated illiquid assets in the first place. #flashcard #topic/liquidity-risk #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-20

## Card 3 — Loan-to-Deposit Ratio · Easy

State the LDR formula and the three interpretation bands used in practice.::LDR = Net loans / Customer deposits. Interpretation: (1) LDR > 100% — early warning of excessive asset growth or deposit loss; implies potentially risky reliance on wholesale funding, especially if that funding is short-dated. (2) LDR 85%–95% — generally considered best practice, though the ideal range varies by business model and jurisdiction. (3) LDR < 70% — may imply excessive liquidity and inadequate returns if surplus funds are deployed in low-yielding assets. A level above 100% is not automatically bad where the bank holds a sufficient liquid asset buffer, but a number significantly above 100% is an indicator of funding stress risk in market instability. #flashcard #topic/liquidity-risk #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-20

## Card 4 — LCR Definition and Minimum · Easy

State the LCR formula, its minimum regulatory requirement, and its time horizon.::LCR = Stock of HQLA / Total net cash outflows over the next 30 calendar days ≥ 100%. The LCR was introduced under Basel III and measures short-term liquidity resilience: the bank must hold sufficient unencumbered HQLA to survive a combined idiosyncratic and market-wide stress scenario lasting 30 days without central bank support. The 30-day horizon reflects the assumption that management and supervisors would have time within that window to take corrective action. The SARB phased in the LCR from 60% in 2015 to 100% from 2019. In South Africa, the Committed Liquidity Facility (CLF) supplements HQLA where the domestic stock of eligible government securities is structurally insufficient. #flashcard #topic/liquidity-risk #topic/basel #topic/regulation #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-20

## Card 5 — NSFR Definition and Minimum · Easy

State the NSFR formula, its minimum regulatory requirement, and its time horizon.::NSFR = Available Stable Funding (ASF) / Required Stable Funding (RSF) ≥ 100%. The NSFR measures structural liquidity risk over a one-year horizon. ASF represents the funding that is reliably available over 12 months under both normal and stressed conditions — weighted by stability (e.g. CET1 = 100%, stable retail deposits = 95%, short-term wholesale deposits = 0–50%). RSF represents the stable funding required to support assets over 12 months, weighted by their liquidity (e.g. HQLA Level 1 = 5% RSF; loans to corporates > 1 year = 65–85% RSF; undrawn committed facilities = 5–30% RSF). The NSFR was phased in from 2018 under Basel III and has largely superseded the Liquidity Risk Factor as the primary regulatory measure of structural funding. #flashcard #topic/liquidity-risk #topic/basel #topic/regulation #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-20

## Card 6 — HQLA Eligibility Requirements · Easy

State the three mandatory characteristics of a Liquid Asset Buffer (LAB) and explain why central bank eligibility is not one of them.::A LAB must comprise assets that are: (1) **Good credit quality** — they must retain value under stress, ruling out sub-investment-grade or distressed securities. (2) **Funded by stable or term funding** — the buffer cannot itself be subject to maturity rollover risk; it must not be financed by the very short-term liabilities whose withdrawal would trigger a stress event. (3) **Readily convertible to cash at 1-day notice** — monetisable immediately via outright sale or repo, including under fire-sale conditions. Central bank eligibility is NOT a criterion because central bank funding is a last resort, not a business-as-usual source of liquidity. Using central bank access as a substitute for a genuine HQLA buffer would defeat the purpose of the LAB. #flashcard #topic/liquidity-risk #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-20

## Card 8 — SARB HQLA Level 2A and 2B · Easy

Distinguish Level 2A from Level 2B HQLA under the SARB framework, including credit rating thresholds and haircut implications.::Both Level 2A and 2B HQLA are subject to regulatory haircuts, reflecting their lower liquidity and credit quality relative to Level 1 assets. **Level 2A**: Corporate and SOE debt securities rated **AA– or higher** — higher credit quality, lower haircut (typically 15% haircut applied in LCR calculation). **Level 2B**: Corporate and SOE debt securities rated **BBB– or higher** — lower credit quality, larger haircut (typically 25–50% depending on instrument type). ## Card 9 — Lender of Last Resort · Easy

Define the lender of last resort role and explain why it must not be treated as a business-as-usual liquidity source.::In every jurisdiction the central bank (SARB in South Africa) acts as **lender of last resort**: it provides emergency liquidity assistance to a solvent but illiquid bank that cannot generate liquidity through asset liquidation or market funding. However, recourse to central bank funding signals a compromised standalone viability — it indicates the bank cannot independently fund itself through normal markets. A bank accessing emergency central bank facilities will face immediate market scrutiny, potential rating downgrades, and depositor concern, which can accelerate the very outflows the facility was meant to address. Post-2009, Eurozone banks used ECB repo facilities quasi-routinely — regulators acknowledged this reflected structural funding instability, not sound practice. Under normal conditions, central bank funding (other than daily open market operations) lies outside ordinary liquidity management. #flashcard #topic/liquidity-risk #topic/regulation #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-20

## Card 10 — Liability Stability Hierarchy · Easy

State the top three and bottom three categories in the liability stability hierarchy and explain what drives their ranking.::The hierarchy ranks liabilities from most to least stable under stress. **Top three (most stable)**: (1) Retail current accounts — contractually on-demand but behaviourally sticky; customers maintain large, persistent balances. (2) Retail deposit accounts. (3) Corporate operational cashflow / call accounts — businesses need these for day-to-day operations, reducing outflow probability. **Bottom three (least stable)**: (9) Wholesale market fixed-term deposits, (10) Money market term funding (CDs / commercial paper), (11) Money market deposits / interbank deposits. The driving factors are: stability of the customer relationship, awareness of bank-specific credit risk, and speed of withdrawal. Institutional / wholesale depositors are multi-banked, rate-sensitive, and market-aware — they move funds within hours in a stress event, whereas retail depositors move far more slowly due to friction, loyalty, and lower financial sophistication. #flashcard #topic/liquidity-risk #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-20

## Card 11 — Cumulative Liquidity Model · Easy

Define the Cumulative Liquidity Model (CLM) and state its primary purpose and horizon.::The CLM is a forward-looking daily model that projects cumulative cash inflows, outflows, and available liquidity across all maturity buckets over a **12-month horizon**. It extends the simple liquidity ratio concept by modelling the entire funding gap timeline — not just a single point. The CLM identifies forward-looking liquidity stress points on a cash basis before they crystallise, allowing ALCO and treasury to restructure funding proactively. It is typically prepared daily at legal entity and group level. Unlike the LCR (which is a 30-day stress scenario snapshot), the CLM is primarily a management tool for strategic funding decisions, capacity planning, and early-warning monitoring across a realistic planning horizon. #flashcard #topic/liquidity-risk #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-20

## Card 12 — Liquidity Risk Factor · Easy

State the LRF formula and explain its value and limitation as a monitoring metric.::LRF (also called the Maturity Transformation Ratio) = Weighted average tenor of assets (days) / Weighted average tenor of liabilities (days). Tenors are calculated as weighted averages using either behavioural or contractual maturities, weighted by nominal amounts or drawn balances. A higher LRF indicates a larger maturity gap and greater liquidity risk. Limitation: the LRF has limited value as a standalone number — its utility lies in **trend monitoring over time** against long-run averages, providing early warning of unsustainable funding structure build-up. The limit is set judgementally by the ALCO and varies by risk profile. The LRF has largely been superseded by the NSFR as the primary regulatory structural funding metric. #flashcard #topic/liquidity-risk #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-20

## Card 13 — Concentration Report · Easy

Define the purpose of the concentration and funding source report and state a typical single-counterparty limit.::The concentration report shows the extent of reliance on a single source of funds — measured by depositor name, sector, or country. Excessive concentration to one lender, sector, or country is an early-warning sign because the withdrawal of a concentrated funding source leaves an acute structural shortfall that cannot be quickly replaced in stress. Banks typically set a single-customer concentration limit of around **10% of the aggregate funding base**. The tenor of concentrated deposits matters: a long-dated concentrated deposit carries materially less risk than a short-dated one with the same counterparty. Where the limit is breached, the bank must either reduce the concentration, grow the overall deposit base, or maintain sufficient HQLA to cover the potential outflow. #flashcard #topic/liquidity-risk #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-20

## Card 14 — SA Committed Liquidity Facility · Easy

Explain the SA Committed Liquidity Facility (CLF) and why it exists as a structural feature of the South African LCR framework.::The CLF is a SARB-provided backstop facility that SA banks can count as HQLA for LCR purposes, up to an agreed committed amount. It exists because South Africa has a **structural shortage of domestic HQLA**: government securities outstanding are insufficient to allow all SA banks to simultaneously hold LCR-compliant HQLA buffers of adequate size. Unlike most developed markets, SA does not have sufficient supply of Level 1 eligible government bonds relative to the banking system's balance sheet. The CLF compensates for this structural gap — a SARB commitment to provide liquidity on request against eligible collateral is treated as equivalent to holding liquid assets. The CLF is subject to a fee paid to the SARB and is subject to periodic review of the eligible collateral posted. #flashcard #topic/liquidity-risk #topic/regulation #difficulty/easy #date/2026-04-20

## Card 15 — Wholesale vs Customer Funding · Easy

Distinguish customer funds from wholesale funds in terms of stability, rate-sensitivity, and SA-specific reliance.::**Customer funds**: sourced from retail and corporate depositors. More stable behaviourally — customers maintain balances for transaction and savings purposes rather than yield maximisation. Retail current and deposit accounts exhibit the highest stability. They form the preferred funding base for any bank. **Wholesale funds**: sourced from institutional depositors — asset managers, pension funds, other banks, money market instruments (CDs, commercial paper), and interbank deposits. ## Card 16 — LDR Limitations and Complementary Metrics · Medium

Explain the three key limitations of the LDR as a liquidity metric and describe which of the six baseline metrics addresses each limitation.
?
The LDR is widely reported and intuitively meaningful, but it has three structural weaknesses as a standalone metric:

**Limitation 1 — No tenor dimension**: The LDR does not capture the maturity of deposits or loans. A bank with a 90% LDR funded by 1-month wholesale deposits against 30-year mortgages is far more vulnerable than one with the same LDR funded by 3-year retail fixed deposits. The **LRF (maturity transformation ratio)** and **1-week/1-month liquidity ratios** address this by capturing gap risk at specific maturity points.

**Limitation 2 — No concentration dimension**: A 90% LDR could be achieved by a single large institutional depositor or by a diversified retail deposit base — the risk profiles are entirely different. The **concentration and funding source report** addresses this by monitoring reliance on individual depositors and sectors.

**Limitation 3 — No forward-looking dimension**: The LDR is a static snapshot — it does not model how the funding gap evolves as deposits mature and assets must be refinanced. The **CLM** addresses this by projecting cumulative cash flows forward 12 months and identifying future stress points before they crystallise.

Together, these six metrics — LDR, 1-week/1-month ratios, CLM, LRF, concentration report, and inter-entity lending report — provide a complete picture of the dimensions of liquidity risk the LDR alone cannot capture.
#flashcard #topic/liquidity-risk #difficulty/medium #date/2026-04-20

## Card 17 — Behavioural vs Contractual Tenor · Medium

Explain the distinction between contractual and behavioural tenor for deposits, and describe how banks model behavioural tenor for liquidity risk management.
?
**Contractual tenor** is the legally agreed maturity — the date on which the bank is obligated to repay the deposit. For current accounts, contractual tenor = 0 days (payable on demand immediately). For a 90-day fixed deposit, contractual tenor = 90 days.

**Behavioural tenor** reflects how deposits actually behave in practice. Retail current accounts are contractually repayable on demand but are behaviourally sticky — customers maintain large, stable balances over months or years. Banks observe that, historically, only a small proportion of current account balances are withdrawn in any given period; the "core" balance persists for years. Regulators permit banks to model this core balance as providing longer-dated funding than the contractual maturity implies, subject to statistical evidence.

**Behavioural modelling approach**: Banks analyse historical account data to estimate:
1. The proportion of balances that constitute "core" (stable) vs "transient" (volatile) balances.
2. The average behavioural tenor of the core component, typically using survival analysis or runoff rate analysis.
3. Calibration under stress — recognising that behaviour under a bank-specific stress event may differ significantly from BAU.

**SA nuances**: Low-income SA customers may withdraw entire salary payments in cash on pay day, creating predictable but concentrated outflow events that differ from the behavioural patterns seen in wealthier customer segments. Digital/mobile accounts may also exhibit different behaviour than branch-based accounts due to lower friction in withdrawal. Conservatism and prudence are the appropriate calibration approach given the rarity of severe stress events in any single bank's historical data.
#flashcard #topic/liquidity-risk #difficulty/medium #date/2026-04-20

## Card 18 — LCR Numerator and Denominator Mechanics · Medium

Describe how the LCR numerator (HQLA) and denominator (net cash outflows) are constructed, including the key haircuts and run-off rates applied under Basel III.
?
**Numerator — Stock of HQLA**:
- Level 1 assets: no haircut (counted at full face value). No cap.
- Level 2A assets: 15% haircut; subject to a cap such that Level 2 assets do not exceed 40% of total HQLA.
- Level 2B assets: 25–50% haircut (equities: 50%, RMBS: 25%); subject to a cap of 15% of total HQLA.
- Assets must be unencumbered — not pledged as collateral or otherwise restricted.

**Denominator — Net cash outflows over 30 days**:
= Total expected cash outflows − min(Total expected cash inflows, 75% × outflows)

Key **outflow run-off rates** (stressed assumptions of % withdrawn):
- Stable retail deposits (insured / relationship): 3–5%
- Less stable retail deposits: 10%
- Unsecured wholesale deposits (operational): 25%
- Unsecured wholesale non-operational: 75–100%
- Committed credit/liquidity lines to non-financials: 10% (credit) / 30% (liquidity)
- Committed lines to banks / other financials: 40%

The Basel III run-off rates reflect the severity of the combined stress scenario: a bank-specific rating downgrade + market-wide stress + partial run on retail deposits, all simultaneously over 30 days. By design the LCR stress scenario is more severe than any single historical liquidity crisis to ensure a genuine buffer.
#flashcard #topic/liquidity-risk #topic/basel #difficulty/medium #date/2026-04-20

## Card 19 — NSFR ASF and RSF Factor Logic · Medium

Explain the economic logic behind the ASF and RSF weighting systems in the NSFR, and give three examples of ASF and RSF factors with their economic rationale.
?
The NSFR asks: "Does the bank have enough stable funding (ASF) to cover the stable funding it needs over 12 months (RSF)?"

**ASF (Available Stable Funding)** weights each liability and capital source by the probability it remains available for ≥12 months under stress:
- CET1 and Tier 1/2 capital: **100% ASF** — equity is permanent and will not withdraw; subordinated debt matures in >1 year.
- Stable retail deposits (relationship, insured): **95% ASF** — high customer loyalty; low outflow even in stress.
- Less stable retail / small business deposits: **90% ASF** — slightly higher run risk.
- Non-financial corporate / PSE deposits ≥ 1 year: **50% ASF** — institutional depositors less sticky than retail.
- Short-term wholesale / interbank deposits <6 months: **0% ASF** — assumed to fully roll off under stress; cannot be counted as stable.

**RSF (Required Stable Funding)** weights each asset by the proportion that must be funded by stable sources:
- Level 1 HQLA: **5% RSF** — immediately convertible to cash; needs almost no stable funding backing.
- Residential mortgages (loan-to-value ≤ 80%): **65% RSF** — long-dated, illiquid; most of this asset must be stably funded.
- Loans to non-financial corporates ≥ 1 year: **65–85% RSF** — duration creates structural funding need.
- Undrawn committed facilities: **5–30% RSF** — some proportion likely to be drawn under stress, requiring pre-positioned stable funding.

The NSFR corrects a key GFC failure: banks were funding long-dated mortgage books with overnight commercial paper, which collapsed in 2007, precipitating the Northern Rock and Bear Stearns liquidity crises.

## Card 20 — 1-Week and 1-Month Liquidity Ratios · Medium

Define the 1-week and 1-month liquidity ratios, state the formula, and explain how they complement the LCR as management tools.
?
**Formula**:
Liquidity ratio[t] = (Cash inflows[t] − Cash outflows[t] + Liquid assets[t]) / Total liabilities

Where t = 1 week or 1 month maturity bucket, and liquid assets[t] represents the cash value of liquid securities that could be monetised within bucket t.

**What they measure**: Gap risk — the net cash position (adjusted for monetisable liquid assets) at a specific near-term maturity point, expressed as a proportion of total liabilities. A negative ratio means outflows exceed inflows plus available liquidity in that bucket.

**How they complement the LCR**:
1. They are measured against **internal regulatory limits** (not just Basel III stress scenarios) and can serve as early-warning triggers before LCR approaches its minimum.
2. They capture the actual **structural funding gap** — not just the stress-scenario run-off rates prescribed by Basel III — giving management information specific to the bank's actual funding composition.
3. They can be decomposed by currency, legal entity, and business line, providing granular visibility the LCR aggregate does not.

Although largely superseded by the LCR as the **regulatory** minimum short-term liquidity measure, the 1-week and 1-month ratios remain valuable ALCO management tools — especially for identifying stress points within the 30-day LCR window that a monthly LCR snapshot would miss.
#flashcard #topic/liquidity-risk #difficulty/medium #date/2026-04-20

## Card 21 — Wholesale Funding Run-Off Under Stress · Medium

Explain why short-term wholesale funding is the most systemically dangerous form of liquidity risk, using historical evidence and the mechanics of a wholesale run.
?
**The mechanism of a wholesale run**: Wholesale depositors — asset managers, pension funds, other banks, money market funds — are professional investors who monitor counterparty credit closely and maintain lines with multiple banks simultaneously. When any signal of credit deterioration emerges (e.g. a rating downgrade, a profit warning, a rumour), they can withdraw 100% of their short-term deposits at maturity without rollover within hours. Unlike retail depositors (protected by deposit insurance and impeded by inertia), wholesale depositors face no social or practical barrier to withdrawal.

**Historical evidence**:
- **Northern Rock (2007)**: Heavy reliance on short-term securitisation and interbank funding collapsed when counterparties refused to roll over. A UK bank that had been profitable failed within weeks of wholesale markets seizing.
- **Bear Stearns (2008)**: Repo funding of long-dated mortgage securities evaporated over days as counterparties refused overnight repo rollover — the classic "maturity mismatch + wholesale run" failure mode.
- **Eurozone sovereign stress (2010–2012)**: Cross-border interbank deposits withdrew from peripheral banks (Italy, Spain, Greece) to core banks (Germany, Netherlands), creating sudden structural shortfalls.

**Why it's systemically dangerous**: Wholesale runs are self-fulfilling and contagious. One bank's funding difficulty causes counterparties to cut all interbank lines simultaneously ("flight to quality"), turning an idiosyncratic stress into a systemic liquidity crisis. The LCR's high run-off rates for wholesale non-operational deposits (75–100%) directly reflect this history.

**SA context**: South African banks' structural reliance on wholesale funding (given low domestic savings rates) is a specific vulnerability that SARB/PA monitors closely via LCR, concentration reports, and the CLM.
#flashcard #topic/liquidity-risk #topic/regulation #difficulty/medium #date/2026-04-20

## Card 22 — Inter-Entity Lending Report · Medium

Explain the purpose of the inter-entity lending report and the regulatory dimension it introduces for group banking entities.
?
The inter-entity lending report is relevant for consolidated or multi-entity banking groups. It measures two things:
1. The **reliance of a specific subsidiary or branch on intra-group funding** — quantifying how much of its funding comes from the parent or other group entities, and what proportion of its balance sheet is funded internally.
2. **Proximity to regulatory limits** on cross-border and cross-legal-entity lending — providing early warning before limits are breached.

**Why it matters**: Intra-group lending is common and economically efficient (surplus liquidity in one entity is recycled to fund deficits in another). However, in a stress event this creates two risks:
- The providing entity may need to recall funding to meet its own liquidity needs, stripping liquidity from the receiving entity simultaneously with external market stress.
- Cross-border intra-group flows are subject to **regulatory ring-fencing**: host regulators (e.g. SARB for SA subsidiaries of foreign banks) may restrict the repatriation of capital or liquidity to the parent, creating trapped pools. Conversely, a parent in distress cannot always upstream funds to support a subsidiary.

**SA-specific**: The SARB requires SA-domiciled banks and branches of foreign banks to demonstrate **self-sufficiency** — the SA operation must be able to withstand a scenario in which intra-group support is unavailable. The inter-entity report provides the primary evidence for this assessment and feeds directly into the ICR liquidity component.

## Card 23 — LCR vs NSFR: Complementary Roles · Medium

Explain how the LCR and NSFR address different dimensions of liquidity risk and describe a scenario where a bank could breach one without breaching the other.
?
**LCR** addresses **short-term liquidity resilience**: Can the bank survive a severe 30-day combined stress scenario (market + idiosyncratic) using its HQLA buffer, without relying on central bank funding?

**NSFR** addresses **structural (medium-term) funding stability**: Is the bank's funding structure sustainable over 12 months — is it funded by stable sources consistent with the duration and liquidity of its assets?

**Complementary because**: The LCR does not capture structural funding mismatches that play out beyond 30 days — a bank could pass the LCR by holding a large HQLA stock funded by 35-day commercial paper, yet face a structural cliff when that CP matures and cannot be rolled. The NSFR catches this by requiring that stable assets be funded by stable liabilities over 12 months.

**Scenario — LCR breach without NSFR breach**: A bank holds a large HQLA stock but has experienced a sudden outflow of retail deposits (a run), reducing its immediately available liquid assets below 30-day outflows. Its long-term funding structure remains sound (NSFR > 100%) but the short-term stress has consumed the buffer. The LCR would show a breach; the NSFR would not, since 12-month ASF/RSF is unaffected.

**Scenario — NSFR breach without LCR breach**: A bank funds a large portfolio of 5-year corporate loans (high RSF) primarily with 6-month wholesale deposits (low ASF factor). Its HQLA stock is adequate for 30-day stress (LCR > 100%), but the structural mismatch means ASF < RSF — NSFR breaches. This was the typical Northern Rock structural profile: short-term funding, long-term mortgage assets.

## Card 24 — Behavioural Conservatism in Stress Calibration · Medium

Explain why pure liquidity stress events are rare and why this makes conservative assumptions essential for deposit behavioural modelling. Include SA-specific considerations.
?
**The rarity problem**: Pure liquidity stress events (not accompanied by a broader credit or solvency crisis) are extremely infrequent. A typical senior treasury professional may encounter at most one systemic liquidity event in a career. This means that historical data for calibrating deposit run-off rates under bank-specific stress is sparse and potentially unrepresentative of the next crisis, which will likely differ in character from prior events.
<!--SR:!2026-05-05,3,250-->

**Consequences for modelling**: Standard statistical techniques (regression, survival analysis, historical simulation) cannot reliably estimate tail-event outflow rates when the tail is literally unobserved in the available data. Extrapolating from BAU behaviour significantly underestimates outflows during genuine stress because:
1. Customer behaviour changes discontinuously at perceived "failure" thresholds.
2. Social media and digital banking have dramatically accelerated the speed of retail deposit withdrawal (Silicon Valley Bank, 2023: 25% of deposits withdrawn in one day via mobile app).
3. NBFIs and non-retail depositors have become a larger share of funding, increasing behavioural sensitivity.

**Appropriate response**: Conservatism and prudence — applying higher-than-historically-observed run-off rates, using multiple forward-looking stress scenarios (idiosyncratic, market-wide, combined), and supplementing with management overlays and peer-group analysis.

**SA-specific**: Low-income SA depositors withdrawing full salary balances in cash on pay day create predictable but concentrated outflow events that must be modelled explicitly. Digital banking penetration in SA is growing rapidly — mobile-enabled accounts may exhibit fundamentally different outflow behaviour than branch-based accounts, requiring demographic segmentation in behavioural models.

## Card 25 — Intraday Liquidity Risk · Medium

Define intraday liquidity risk and explain its relevance to systemically important banks in payment and settlement systems.
?
**Intraday liquidity risk** is the risk that a bank cannot meet its payment and settlement obligations throughout the business day — i.e. at the specific times during the day when its payment obligations fall due — even if it is solvent and has sufficient end-of-day liquidity.

**Why it differs from overnight liquidity risk**: Banks participating in large-value payment systems (RTGS — Real-Time Gross Settlement, such as SAMOS in South Africa) must fund each payment individually in real time. If a bank runs short of intraday liquidity (central bank reserves + daylight overdraft facilities), it cannot process outgoing payments, triggering gridlock across the system as other participants awaiting those payments are also delayed.

**Drivers of intraday risk**:
1. **Timing mismatches**: Inflows and outflows do not align within the day — large outflows (e.g. settlement of government bond purchases) may fall due before corresponding inflows arrive.
2. **Concentration**: A single large correspondent bank or CSD participant can create a concentrated intraday exposure.
3. **Stressed behaviour**: In a stress event counterparties may delay or hold back outgoing payments (hoarding liquidity), creating systemic gridlock.

**BCBS intraday liquidity monitoring tools** (2013): Banks are expected to monitor eight metrics including: peak intraday liquidity usage, available intraday liquidity at start of business day, and largest single-day net payment obligations. The SARB requires SAMOS participants to maintain sufficient intraday liquidity to avoid gridlock.

**Relevance for SA D-SIBs**: As SAMOS participants, SA D-SIBs must manage intraday positions carefully — a large D-SIB payment failure can cascade through the entire system, creating a systemic liquidity event from a single operational failure.

(b) Explain what management actions could restore LCR compliance within 30 days. (c) State two SA-specific features that affect the LCR calculation. Balance sheet data: Level 1 HQLA (government bonds) = R50bn; Level 2A HQLA (AA– corporate bonds, pre-haircut) = R20bn. ?
**(a) LCR Calculation**:

**Step 1 — HQLA Numerator**:
- Level 1: R50bn × no haircut = R50.0bn
- Level 2A: R20bn × (1 − 15% haircut) = R17.0bn
- Check cap: Level 2 ≤ 40% of total HQLA. Total HQLA = R50 + R17 = R67bn. 40% cap = R26.8bn. Level 2A = R17bn < R26.8bn → no cap binding.
- **Total HQLA = R67.0bn**

**Step 2 — Net Cash Outflows (30-day)**:
- Stable retail deposits: R200bn × 3% = R6.0bn
- Less stable retail deposits: R80bn × 10% = R8.0bn
- Wholesale non-operational deposits: R60bn × 75% = R45.0bn
- Committed credit lines (corporate undrawn): R40bn × 10% = R4.0bn
- **Total outflows = R63.0bn**

Assume no material inflows (conservative) → net outflows = R63.0bn.

**LCR = R67.0bn / R63.0bn = 106.3% ✓** — above the 100% minimum; bank complies, but the buffer is thin (only 6.3 percentage points above the floor).

**(b) Management actions to increase LCR headroom**:
1. **Extend wholesale funding tenor**: Replace <30-day non-operational deposits (75% run-off) with >30-day deposits (0% run-off in the LCR window) — this removes R45bn from outflows at the cost of higher funding expense.
2. **Purchase additional Level 1 HQLA**: Use available cash or short-term funding to buy SA government bonds — directly increases the numerator. Funded by >30-day wholesale or term retail deposits.
3. **Reduce committed facility exposure**: Reduce undrawn committed credit lines to corporates (or convert to uncommitted, reducing the 10% RSF) — reduces outflow without balance sheet shrinkage.
4. **Grow stable retail deposits**: Retail deposit growth (insured, relationship) improves the funding base at 3% run-off vs 75% for wholesale — structurally reduces LCR vulnerability.

**(c) SA-specific features**:
1. **Committed Liquidity Facility (CLF)**: Where SA banks face a structural shortage of Level 1 HQLA (insufficient government bond supply), the SARB CLF commitment can be included in the LCR numerator as if it were HQLA — up to the agreed CLF limit. This bank should check whether its CLF headroom can be applied.
2. **SARB HQLA tiering**: SA classifies SARB-issued securities and nominal/index-linked government bonds as Level 1 — this is consistent with Basel III but the specific instruments differ from, e.g., the ECB or Fed framework. SA's Level 2A is limited to AA– or better corporate/SOE debt, which is a narrower market than in Europe, reinforcing structural HQLA scarcity.

## Card 27 — NSFR Structural Stress Analysis · Hard

(a) A bank has the following funding and asset profile. Calculate the NSFR. (b) Explain which funding substitution would most efficiently improve the NSFR. (c) Explain how the NSFR interacts with the LCR during a liquidity stress event. Profile: CET1 + Tier 2 capital = R30bn; Stable retail deposits = R120bn; Less stable retail deposits = R50bn; Unsecured wholesale deposits <6 months = R80bn; Unsecured wholesale deposits 6–12 months = R30bn. Assets: Level 1 HQLA = R40bn; Retail mortgages (LTV ≤ 80%) = R150bn; Corporate loans > 1 year = R100bn; Undrawn committed facilities = R20bn.
?
**(a) NSFR Calculation**:

**ASF (Available Stable Funding)**:
- CET1 + Tier 2 capital: R30bn × 100% = R30.0bn
- Stable retail deposits: R120bn × 95% = R114.0bn
- Less stable retail deposits: R50bn × 90% = R45.0bn
- Wholesale deposits <6 months: R80bn × 0% = R0.0bn (assumed to fully roll off)
- Wholesale deposits 6–12 months: R30bn × 50% = R15.0bn
- **Total ASF = R204.0bn**

**RSF (Required Stable Funding)**:
- Level 1 HQLA: R40bn × 5% = R2.0bn
- Retail mortgages (LTV ≤ 80%): R150bn × 65% = R97.5bn
- Corporate loans > 1 year: R100bn × 65% = R65.0bn
- Undrawn committed facilities: R20bn × 5% = R1.0bn (credit line, non-financial)
- **Total RSF = R165.5bn**

**NSFR = R204.0bn / R165.5bn = 123.3% ✓** — well above 100%.

**(b) Most efficient NSFR improvement**:
The largest ASF drag is the R80bn wholesale deposits <6 months (ASF = 0%). If extended to >6 months:
- Moving R80bn from <6 months (0% ASF) to 6–12 months (50% ASF) adds R40bn of ASF.
- NSFR numerator: R204 + R40 = R244bn. New NSFR = R244 / R165.5 = 147.4%.

This is the most efficient lever because: (1) it targets the largest zero-weighted funding block; (2) the ASF gain is R40bn per R80bn extended — 50 cents per rand of funding extended; (3) no balance sheet growth or HQLA purchase is required. The cost is paying a longer-dated spread, but this simultaneously reduces LCR vulnerability by removing short-dated outflows from the 30-day window.

**(c) LCR–NSFR interaction during stress**:

During a funding stress, the two ratios pull in different directions:
- **LCR pressure**: Rising deposit outflows deplete HQLA. The bank must monetise Level 1/2 assets to fund outflows → HQLA stock falls → LCR numerator falls → potential LCR breach.
- **NSFR pressure**: HQLA monetisation (selling government bonds for cash) reduces Level 1 RSF (5%) — a small RSF reduction. But if the bank replaces outflowing deposits with <6-month wholesale funding (0% ASF), the NSFR numerator deteriorates sharply. Simultaneously, if the bank is forced to draw on committed facilities (increasing assets → increasing RSF), the NSFR denominator rises.

**Net effect**: LCR tends to breach faster and more visibly in a short-term stress event. NSFR deterioration follows if the bank's response to LCR pressure (e.g. taking short-term wholesale funding) worsens the structural funding position. This is why SARB monitors both ratios and why a bank repairing LCR compliance via short-term wholesale funding may inadvertently worsen its NSFR.

## Card 28 — Liquidity Crisis Cascade · Hard

(a) Trace the sequence of events in a bank-specific liquidity crisis from initial trigger to potential resolution, mapping each stage to the Basel III / SARB regulatory framework. (b) Explain how HQLA monetisation, the CLF, and the lender of last resort form successive lines of defence. (c) Describe the conditions under which the SARB would activate resolution under the SA Resolution Framework.
?
**(a) Sequence of a bank-specific liquidity crisis**:

**Stage 1 — Trigger** (Day 0–1): An adverse credit event (e.g. large loan loss announcement, unexpected earnings miss, counterparty default) raises doubts about the bank's solvency. Credit analysts downgrade the bank; counterparties withdraw overnight interbank lines.

**Stage 2 — Wholesale run** (Days 1–5): Non-operational wholesale depositors (money market funds, pension funds, corporate treasurers) refuse to roll over short-term deposits at maturity. LCR monitoring shows daily HQLA stock declining. The CLM flags that cumulative cash outflows will exceed inflows + liquid assets within days.

**Stage 3 — Retail run** (Days 5–15): News spreads via media/social media; retail depositors begin withdrawing (historically slower than wholesale, but SVB 2023 showed digital channels compress this to hours). LCR approaches 100% → supervisory notification threshold.

**Stage 4 — HQLA monetisation** (Days 1–30): The bank activates its first line of defence — selling HQLA (SA government bonds) outright or pledging them in repo markets to generate cash. Under extreme stress, bid-offer spreads widen and prices fall, making Level 2A/2B assets harder to monetise at par — reinforcing the Basel haircut rationale.

**Stage 5 — CLF draw** (Days 15–30): If HQLA exhaustion is imminent, the bank draws on its Committed Liquidity Facility from the SARB (posted eligible collateral in exchange for cash). This is the second line of defence and signals to the market that the bank requires SARB support.

**Stage 6 — Lender of Last Resort** (Day 30+): If the CLF is insufficient or collateral is exhausted, the bank approaches the SARB for Emergency Liquidity Assistance (ELA) — the third and final line of defence. ELA requires SARB board approval and is conditioned on the bank being assessed as solvent-but-illiquid. ELA is public — announcement typically confirms the crisis and may accelerate outflows.

**(b) Three lines of defence**:

1. **HQLA buffer (LAB)**: Self-insurance — the bank's own unencumbered liquid assets. Designed to last 30 days without external support (LCR design). First call in any stress event. Weakness: fire-sale discounts in market-wide stress reduce actual monetisation value below face value.

2. **CLF**: SARB commitment — counts as HQLA for LCR purposes. Second line. Activated by posting pre-agreed eligible collateral (government bonds, SARB-eligible corporate bonds). Structural backstop compensating for SA HQLA scarcity. Subject to a fee; signals SARB awareness.

3. **Lender of Last Resort (ELA)**: Emergency central bank credit — no predetermined collateral pool; negotiated case-by-case. Requires SARB solvency assessment. Last resort — signals failure of the bank's independent funding capacity.

**(c) SARB resolution activation conditions**:

Under the SA Resolution Framework (2021), the SARB's Resolution Function activates when:
1. The bank **fails or is likely to fail** — meaning it cannot meet its obligations as they fall due, or regulatory capital falls below minimum thresholds, and
2. **No reasonable private-sector solution** (recapitalisation, sale, merger) is available within the timeframe needed to prevent disorderly failure, and
3. **Resolution is necessary in the public interest** — i.e. the bank provides critical functions (deposit taking, payment services) whose disruption would cause systemic harm.

Resolution tools available to SARB: bail-in of TLAC-eligible liabilities, sale of business (purchase and assumption), bridge institution, or asset separation. SA D-SIBs are subject to **GLAC** (Gone-concern Loss-Absorbing Capacity) requirements analogous to TLAC, ensuring that bail-in instruments exist before resolution is needed.

## Card 29 — Integrated: Liquidity Risk + IFRS 9 + Capital · Hard

(a) Explain how a sudden wholesale funding run creates a reinforcing feedback loop between IFRS 9 provisioning, LCR, and CET1. (b) A bank has CET1 = R40bn, HQLA = R80bn (before stress), 30-day net cash outflows (pre-stress) = R60bn. Under stress: 40% of wholesale deposits (R100bn) withdraw; 10% of retail deposits (R200bn) withdraw; IFRS 9 Stage 2 migrations increase ECL by R8bn; RWA increases by R15bn. Calculate post-stress LCR and CET1 ratio (assuming pre-stress RWA = R400bn) and identify all regulatory breaches. (c) State the PA's automatic and discretionary responses.
?
**(a) The reinforcing feedback loop**:

**Step 1 — Wholesale outflows trigger HQLA monetisation**: The bank sells government bonds to fund cash outflows → HQLA stock falls → LCR numerator falls → LCR weakens.

**Step 2 — Market stress raises asset impairment**: The same macroeconomic deterioration that triggers deposit outflows (e.g. recession fear) causes borrower PDs to rise → IFRS 9 Stage 1→2 migration → lifetime ECL replaces 12-month ECL → impairment charge hits P&L → retained earnings fall → CET1 falls.

**Step 3 — Credit spreads widen → HQLA fire-sale discount**: As the bank is forced to sell HQLA quickly, widening credit spreads reduce the market value of Level 2A/2B assets → effective HQLA recovery is less than face value → worse-than-modelled LCR.

**Step 4 — Rating downgrade → further wholesale outflows**: Falling CET1 ratio triggers rating agencies → downgrade → covenant trips on wholesale funding → further withdrawal of deposits (LCR run-off rates effectively increase) → more HQLA consumed.

**Step 5 — RWA inflation + CET1 fall = double-pressure on capital ratio**: Stressed PDs inflate IRB RWA → RWA denominator rises as CET1 numerator falls → capital ratio deteriorates faster than either change alone would imply.

This feedback loop is the mechanism that converted idiosyncratic stress events in 2008 into systemic crises — each step amplified the next, and multiple banks hit the same loop simultaneously.

**(b) Post-stress calculations**:

**LCR**:
- Pre-stress HQLA: R80bn
- Wholesale outflows: R100bn × 40% = R40bn withdrawn → HQLA consumed to fund = R40bn
- Retail outflows: R200bn × 10% = R20bn → further R20bn HQLA consumed
- Post-stress HQLA: R80bn − R40bn − R20bn = **R20bn**

- Pre-stress net outflows: R60bn
- Stressed additional outflows: R40bn (wholesale) + R20bn (retail) = R60bn additional
- Post-stress 30-day net outflows: R60 + R60 = **R120bn** (simplification: assume outflows are all within 30-day window)

**Post-stress LCR = R20bn / R120bn = 16.7%** — catastrophic breach of 100% minimum.

**CET1**:
- Pre-stress CET1: R40bn
- IFRS 9 ECL increase: −R8bn (flows through P&L → retained earnings)
- Post-stress CET1: R40 − R8 = **R32bn**

- Pre-stress RWA: R400bn
- RWA increase: +R15bn
- Post-stress RWA: **R415bn**

**Post-stress CET1 ratio = R32bn / R415bn = 7.7%**
vs. minimum CET1 ratio (4.5% Pillar 1 + 2.5% CCB + D-SIB surcharge ~1% = 8% combined buffer)
→ **CET1 ratio of 7.7% is below the combined buffer requirement of 8%** → distribution restrictions triggered.
→ CET1 above Pillar 1 minimum of 4.5% (R32bn / R415bn > 4.5%) → not yet at Pillar 1 minimum breach.

**Regulatory breaches**: (1) LCR breach (16.7% vs 100% minimum) — immediate supervisory notification required. (2) Combined CET1 buffer breach (7.7% < 8%) — automatic distribution restrictions triggered.

**(c) PA responses**:

**Automatic (triggered by buffer breach)**:
- Distribution restrictions under the Banks Act Regulations: dividends, share buybacks, and discretionary AT1 coupons are prohibited or capped at a maximum distributable amount (MDA) formula — CET1 is conserved mechanically.

**Discretionary (PA-initiated)**:
1. **LCR breach directive**: The PA issues a formal directive requiring an immediate liquidity restoration plan — timeline for restoring LCR to ≥100%, interim reporting requirements (potentially daily), and restrictions on new lending or wholesale funding concentration.
2. **SREP capital action**: PA can require the bank to raise additional capital (rights issue, AT1 issuance) within a specified period, reduce RWA, or suspend lending growth — under Section 6 of the Banks Act.
3. **Resolution consideration**: If LCR cannot be restored within the 30-day window and SARB ELA has been activated, the PA's Resolution Function assesses whether the bank "fails or is likely to fail" — triggering GLAC bail-in or other resolution tools under the SA Resolution Framework.

## Card 30 — Liquidity Risk in the SA Context: Structural Vulnerabilities · Hard

(a) Identify three structural features of the South African banking system that create heightened liquidity risk relative to a developed-market peer. (b) Explain how the PA and SARB regulatory framework specifically addresses each of these features. (c) Describe the interaction between SA exchange controls and cross-currency liquidity risk management for SA D-SIBs.
?
**(a) Three SA-specific structural vulnerabilities**:

**Vulnerability 1 — Low domestic savings rate and HQLA scarcity**: SA has low discretionary savings — contractual savings flow through pension funds and asset managers (institutional, wholesale depositors) rather than bank deposits. This means: (i) the bank deposit base is structurally less stable (dominated by institutional rather than retail deposits); and (ii) the supply of domestic HQLA (government bonds) is insufficient for all SA banks to simultaneously hold LCR-compliant buffers — a market shortage unique among major economies.

**Vulnerability 2 — High wholesale funding reliance**: SA banks' loan-to-deposit ratios are structurally higher than peer markets (often above 90–95%) because the deposit base is inadequate to fund all lending. Wholesale market funding — from pension funds, asset managers, and money market funds — is essential. This creates acute sensitivity to institutional depositor confidence and repo market conditions.

**Vulnerability 3 — Currency and exchange control risks**: SA is an emerging market with exchange controls. Cross-border capital flows are restricted — SA banks cannot freely convert rands to foreign currency or transfer capital across borders without SARB approval. In a stress event, foreign currency liquidity (USD, EUR) may be unavailable domestically, and rand depreciation increases the effective cost of foreign currency liabilities, potentially creating currency-specific LCR breaches.

**(b) Regulatory responses to each vulnerability**:

**Response to HQLA scarcity**: The SARB introduced the **Committed Liquidity Facility (CLF)** — a committed SARB facility that banks can pledge eligible collateral against for a fee, which counts as HQLA for LCR purposes. This compensates for the structural HQLA shortage without requiring SA government to issue more bonds purely for regulatory compliance purposes. The CLF amount per bank is agreed annually with the SARB.

**Response to wholesale funding reliance**: The PA requires SA banks to demonstrate a credible **NSFR ≥ 100%** and monitors concentration reports closely. D-SIBs must demonstrate self-sufficiency scenarios in their recovery plans — showing the bank can survive the withdrawal of its largest wholesale depositor without external support. The PA also monitors the inter-entity lending report to prevent group-level liquidity from masking subsidiary fragility.

**Response to FX liquidity risk**: Basel III requires LCR compliance to be monitored **by material currency** — SA banks must maintain HQLA in each currency in which they have significant outflows, not just in aggregate rands. The PA requires SA banks to report FX liquidity positions separately and demonstrate that cross-currency liquidity cannot be assumed (currencies are not freely convertible in stress). SA exchange controls mean rand HQLA cannot be converted to USD in a stress event at will — banks must pre-position FX HQLA (USD government securities, USD HQLA) separately.

**(c) Exchange controls and cross-currency liquidity**:

SA exchange controls (administered under the Currency and Exchanges Act and the SARB's Exchange Control Regulations) restrict:
1. Capital outflows — SA residents cannot freely transfer capital offshore without SARB approval.
2. FX funding access — SA banks cannot freely borrow in foreign currency and convert to rands to fund domestic lending.

**Implications for cross-currency liquidity management**:
- SA D-SIBs with offshore operations (e.g. Standard Bank's African subsidiaries, Absa's international book) must manage **trapped liquidity**: rand liquidity held in SA cannot be upstreamed to fund a foreign subsidiary's shortfall, and vice versa, without exchange control approval that may take days in a stress event.
- The SARB's exchange control dispensation process is not designed for crisis speeds — this creates a structural gap between consolidated group liquidity (apparently adequate) and legal entity liquidity (actually unavailable across borders).
- SA banks manage this by **pre-positioning FX liquidity buffers** in each jurisdiction, maintaining separate FX LCR calculations, and maintaining standby FX facilities with international correspondent banks, subject to exchange control compliance.
- The **FATF grey-listing of SA in 2023** (for AML/CFT deficiencies) increased correspondent banking risk — some international banks reduced or repriced correspondent relationships, increasing the cost and reducing the availability of offshore USD clearing, which has a direct bearing on FX liquidity management capacity.
